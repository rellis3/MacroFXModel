Notation Glossary

A lookup page, not a lesson. When a symbol in any Theory Lab page stops you cold — the triangle, the weird curly-o, "isn't that variance?" — come here. Every entry is written for someone who's never seen it before: what it's called, what it actually measures or does, and where in this curriculum it shows up.

Read this first — the single biggest trap in all of this notation: a handful of Greek letters (α, β, λ, θ, γ, π, φ, ρ) mean completely different things in different lessons. The same squiggle that means "GARCH's shock sensitivity" in one lesson means "a regression's intercept" in another and "risk aversion" in a third. There is no universal decoder ring — every lesson defines its own symbols locally in a "the math, step by step" section. This page tells you the full range of what a symbol could mean and which lesson uses which meaning, so you can tell them apart instead of assuming they're all the same thing. Entries below are flagged ⚠ multi-meaning where this applies.

Greek letters — one consistent meaning

These show up in many lessons, but they mean the same thing everywhere in this curriculum.
\(\sigma\)
Sigma (lowercase)Standard deviation / volatility
The single most common symbol in this whole curriculum. Plain English: how big the typical wiggle away from average is. Not the direction of a move — just how jumpy the thing is. A calm currency pair has a small σ; a wild one has a large σ. It's always in the same units as the thing it's measuring (e.g. "%", or price points).
Technically it's the square root of variance — variance is the average squared distance from the mean (squared so negative and positive swings don't cancel out), and σ takes the square root to get back into normal, human-readable units.
Defined from scratch in: Descriptive Statistics primer. Central to: GARCH, Black-Scholes, Kelly Criterion, Extreme Value Theory, and almost everything else.
\(\mu\)
MuMean / average / "drift"
The typical or expected value — the number everything else is measured as a distance from. In a price-path formula (like an SDE), μ is the steady, predictable pull in one direction, before any random noise gets added on top. If σ is "how jumpy," μ is "which way is it steadily leaning, on average."
Defined in: Descriptive Statistics primer. Used as "drift" in: Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (the long-run mean it reverts to), Black-Scholes, Heston. Used as "expected return" in Kelly Criterion.
\(\rho\)
RhoCorrelation between two random things
A number between -1 and +1 saying how much two things tend to move together. +1 = perfectly together, -1 = perfectly opposite, 0 = no linear relationship. In this curriculum ρ specifically shows up as the correlation between two different sources of randomness inside one model (e.g. "does volatility spike exactly when price falls?").
Used in: Heston and SABR (correlation between the price process and the volatility process — a negative ρ is what generates a realistic volatility skew). The everyday version of this same idea (correlation between two variables generally) is taught as plain r in the Correlation & Regression primer.
\(\nu\)
Nu"Vol of vol"
Not the volatility of the price — the volatility of the volatility itself. How much does the wiggliness keep changing? A low ν means volatility itself stays fairly steady; a high ν means volatility spikes around unpredictably in its own right, which is exactly what produces a curved (not flat) volatility smile.
Used in: Heston and SABR — in SABR specifically, ν controls how curved the smile is.
\(\kappa\)
KappaSpeed of mean reversion (for variance)
How fast something snaps back to its long-run average once it strays. Bigger κ = snaps back faster; smaller κ = wanders further before correcting. This is the exact same job θ does in the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck lesson — Heston just uses a different letter because it's applying the idea to variance instead of price.
Used in: Heston (variance's speed of reversion). Compare directly to θ's identical role in Ornstein-Uhlenbeck.
\(\xi\)
XiTail "fatness" (GPD shape parameter)
Controls how fat an extreme tail is. Positive ξ = a genuinely heavy tail (extreme losses bigger than a normal-curve guess would ever predict); ξ = 0 = a milder, exponential-style tail; negative ξ = a tail that's actually bounded (there's a hard floor to how bad it gets).
Used only in: Extreme Value Theory, fitted to the Generalized Pareto Distribution.
\(\varepsilon\)
EpsilonError / noise / "the leftover"
Whatever a model doesn't explain. If you fit a line through some points, ε is the gap between the line's prediction and what actually happened at each point — the part your formula can't account for, assumed to average out to zero over many observations.
Used in every regression/AR/MA formula: Correlation & Regression primer, ARIMA & VAR, Regression Diagnostics.
\(\Delta\)
Delta (capital)"The change in ___"
Just means "the change in whatever comes after it." Δt = a step forward in time. ΔS = how much the price moved. It's the plain, everyday-sized version of "d" in calculus (which means an infinitely tiny change) — Δ is a real, finite-sized change you could actually measure.
Separately, traders also use the word "Delta" for an option's sensitivity to the underlying price moving (how much the option's value changes per $1 move) — a specific number, not this general symbol, covered as \(dC/dS\).
General usage throughout. Option "Delta" specifically discussed in: Calculus for Finance, Black-Scholes.
\(\tau\)
TauA lag, or time remaining
Usually "how many steps back are we comparing to" (e.g. comparing today to τ days ago) or "how much time is left" (equivalent to writing T−t as one symbol).
Used loosely in: Stationarity, ACF & PACF (as a lag).
\(\Omega\)
Omega (capital)The sample space
The set of literally every possible outcome. Roll a die: Ω = {1,2,3,4,5,6}. It's the "universe" a probability problem is defined inside.
Defined in: Probability & Random Variables primer.

Greek letters — ⚠ multi-meaning (context matters)

Same letter, genuinely different jobs depending on which lesson you're in. Always trust the local definition in that lesson's "symbols" table over anything below.
\(\beta\)
Beta⚠ Changes meaning by lesson
In regression (Correlation primer, Regression Diagnostics, ARIMA/VAR): the slope of a fitted line — how much Y moves per one unit of X.
In GARCH: "persistence" — how much of yesterday's volatility carries into today.
In SABR: the "backbone" exponent controlling how volatility scales with the price level itself.
Correlation & Regression primer · GARCH · SABR
\(\alpha\)
Alpha⚠ Changes meaning by lesson
In regression: the intercept — where the fitted line crosses zero.
In GARCH: how sensitive volatility is to yesterday's shock.
In Multiple Testing: the significance threshold you're willing to accept (usually 0.05 — a 5% chance of a false alarm).
In SABR: today's volatility level.
In trading slang (not a formula here): "alpha" just means edge/excess return — a completely different, informal usage.
Correlation & Regression primer · GARCH · Multiple Testing · SABR
\(\lambda\)
Lambda⚠ Three unrelated meanings
In Market Microstructure (Kyle's Lambda): how much price moves per unit of order size — a measure of market depth/liquidity.
In Merton Jump-Diffusion: how often price jumps happen (e.g. average jumps per year) — nothing to do with price impact.
In PCA / Random Matrix Theory: an eigenvalue — how much of the total variance one particular "direction" or factor explains.
Market Microstructure · Merton Jump-Diffusion · PCA · Random Matrix Theory
\(\theta\)
Theta⚠ Changes meaning by lesson
In Ornstein-Uhlenbeck: speed of mean reversion — bigger θ snaps back to the mean faster.
In Heston: sometimes the long-run variance level itself (paired with κ for the speed).
In Hidden Markov Models: loose shorthand for "the model's whole parameter set," not one specific number.
On a real options desk (not a lesson here): "theta" also names an option's time-decay Greek — a different, informal use of the same letter.
Ornstein-Uhlenbeck · Heston · Hidden Markov Models
\(\gamma\)
Gamma⚠ Two unrelated meanings
In Merton's Portfolio Problem: risk aversion — bigger γ means a more cautious investor who should rationally bet a smaller fraction of full Kelly.
On a real options desk (touched on in Calculus for Finance/Black-Scholes): "Gamma" also names an option's second-order price sensitivity — how fast Delta itself changes. "Being short gamma" is a common trading phrase, unrelated to the risk-aversion meaning.
Merton's Portfolio Problem · Black-Scholes (Greeks section)
\(\pi\)
Pi⚠ Two totally unrelated meanings
In the Taylor Rule: inflation (a percentage, like 3%).
Everywhere else: just the mathematical constant 3.14159… — it shows up inside the normal distribution's formula for reasons that have nothing to do with economics. The context always makes it obvious which one you're looking at.
The Taylor Rule (inflation) · Descriptive Statistics primer (the constant, inside the bell-curve formula)
\(\phi\)
Phi (lowercase)⚠ Two meanings
In OU / ARIMA / Stationarity: the AR(1) coefficient — how much of yesterday's value carries forward into today (φ close to 1 = very persistent, φ close to 0 = barely persistent).
In statistics generally: sometimes denotes the standard normal bell-curve formula itself (its capital version, Φ, is the cumulative version — see below).
Ornstein-Uhlenbeck · Stationarity, ACF & PACF · ARIMA & VAR
\(\omega\)
Omega (lowercase)⚠ Two unrelated meanings
In GARCH: the baseline/floor constant in the variance formula — roughly, "the variance you'd settle at with no recent shocks."
In Probability: one single specific outcome drawn from the sample space Ω (e.g. "the die landed on 4" is one ω from Ω={1..6}).
GARCH · Probability & Random Variables primer

Capital letters, functions & operators

The non-Greek notation that shows up just as often — expectation brackets, the summation sign, square roots.
\(E[\cdot]\)
E[ ]Expectation / expected value
"The average result you'd get if you could repeat this forever." Not a guess about any single outcome — a statement about the long-run average across infinitely many repeats. \(E[X]\) reads as "the expected value of X."
Defined in: Probability & Random Variables primer. Used throughout — Kelly Criterion, Bayesian Inference, Merton's Portfolio Problem.
\(Var(\cdot)\)
Var( )Variance
The average squared distance from the mean — squared specifically so that swings above and below the average don't cancel each other out, and so bigger swings count more than proportionally. σ (sigma) is just the square root of this, back in normal units.
Defined in: Descriptive Statistics primer, Probability primer.
\(Cov(\cdot,\cdot)\)
Cov( , )Covariance
Do two things tend to move together (positive), move apart (negative), or move independently of each other (near zero)? Unlike correlation, covariance's size also depends on each variable's own units — that's exactly why correlation \(r\) exists, to rescale it into a clean, comparable -1-to-+1 number.
Defined in: Correlation & Regression primer. Used in: Linear Algebra, Markowitz & Black-Litterman.
\(N(\cdot)\) / \(\Phi(\cdot)\)
N( ) or Phi (capital)The normal-curve "percentile lookup"
"What fraction of a bell curve sits to the LEFT of this point?" Feed it a number, get back a probability between 0 and 1. This is the exact tool Black-Scholes uses to turn \(d_1\)/\(d_2\) into an actual price — \(N(d_2)\) is literally "the probability the option finishes in the money."
Central to: Black-Scholes. The z-score version of this same idea (how many standard deviations away, and what percentile that implies) is taught in the Descriptive Statistics primer.
\(\Sigma\)
Sigma (capital)⚠ Two unrelated meanings
As a big operator (\(\sum\), often drawn slightly differently but easy to confuse): "add up everything that follows, across a list." E.g. \(E[X]=\sum_i x_i P(x_i)\) means "multiply each outcome by its probability, then add all those products together."
As a bolded/matrix symbol (covariance matrix): a whole grid of numbers — every pair of variables' covariance, all at once. Not a sum at all, despite reusing the same-looking capital letter.
Summation: Probability primer. Covariance matrix: Linear Algebra, PCA, Random Matrix Theory, Markowitz & Black-Litterman.
\(\sqrt{\cdot}\)
Square rootUndoes squaring
Turns variance (which is in "squared units," like %² — an awkward unit nobody thinks in) back into standard deviation (real, human-readable units, like %). Also shows up in the famous "volatility scales with the square root of time" rule — a 20-day σ is roughly the 1-day σ times √20, not ×20.
Descriptive Statistics primer, Returns & Compounding primer (√time scaling).
\(\int\)
IntegralTotal area under a curve
The running total/accumulation of something over a range — literally the area trapped between a curve and the x-axis. In finance this is how you turn "a range of possible future prices, each with some probability" into "one expected value."
Introduced in: Calculus for Finance.
\(\partial\)
Partial derivativeRate of change, one input at a time
"How fast does this change if I nudge JUST this one input, freezing every other input exactly where it is?" The curly d (as opposed to a plain d) is the signal that there are multiple inputs and you're only wiggling one of them.
Central to: Black-Scholes's pricing equation, Itô's Lemma.
\(dW_t\)
dW, dS, dX (with a "d")An infinitely tiny random nudge
A "d" in front of a variable means "an infinitely small change in it, over an infinitely small step of time." \(dW_t\) specifically is the unpredictable, purely-random nudge from Brownian motion at each instant — the "noise engine" bolted onto a drift term (μ) to build almost every stochastic model in this curriculum. It's the mathematical object behind "the price wiggles randomly."
The heart of: Ornstein-Uhlenbeck, Black-Scholes, Merton Jump-Diffusion, Heston, Itô's Lemma, Girsanov's Theorem.

Subscripts, hats, bars & other shorthand

Small marks attached to a letter that change its meaning — easy to miss, easy to misread.
\(X_t\)
Subscript t"Which point in time"
A small \(t\) after a variable just means "the value at time t" — today, in most contexts. \(X_{t-1}\) is yesterday's value, \(X_{t+1}\) is tomorrow's. It's a label, not a multiplication or an exponent — don't read it as "X times t."
Used in essentially every lesson involving a time series.
\(\hat{\beta}\)
Hat (▲ over a letter)"This is an estimate, not the truth"
A hat means "this is the number you actually calculated from real, limited data" — as opposed to the true, unknowable value it's trying to approximate. \(\hat\beta\) = the slope you computed from your sample; \(\beta\) (no hat) = the true, never-fully-knowable population slope. Every backtested statistic in this curriculum is technically a "hat" version of something.
Used throughout regression contexts: Correlation & Regression primer, Regression Diagnostics.
\(\bar{X}\)
Bar (▬ over a letter)The average of your actual data
"The average of the specific numbers I have in front of me" — the sample mean, computed directly rather than a theoretical/true value.
Used in: Descriptive Statistics primer, Returns & Compounding primer.
\(\tilde{X}\)
Tilde (~ over a letter)A transformed/adjusted version
Marks "this is a modified version of the plain variable" — exactly what's been done to it (de-meaned, rescaled, risk-adjusted, etc.) always gets spelled out locally in whichever lesson uses it.
Context-specific — check the local lesson's own symbol table when you see it.
\(X \sim N(0,1)\)
~ (tilde between two things)"Is distributed as"
Read the whole expression as a sentence: "X is distributed as a standard normal." It's a completely different symbol/use from the tilde-over-one-letter above — this one sits between two things and means "follows this probability distribution."
Probability & Random Variables primer, and used casually everywhere after.
\(\approx\)
Approximately equal"Close enough, with a stated simplification"
Flags that a formula is a simplification, not exact — usually because a small term was dropped (e.g. \(\ln(1+R)\approx R - R^2/2\), the exact version has more terms that matter less as R gets smaller). Worth asking "small compared to what?" whenever you see it.
Used constantly, e.g. Returns & Compounding primer, Kelly Criterion's volatility drag.
\(n \to \infty\)
Arrow (→)"Tends to" / "approaches"
Describes what happens as something grows or shrinks without bound. \(n\to\infty\) means "as your sample size gets bigger and bigger, without limit" — used to describe a theoretical result that holds exactly only in that limit, but is a useful approximation well before you actually get there.
Probability primer (Law of Large Numbers, CLT), Random Matrix Theory.
