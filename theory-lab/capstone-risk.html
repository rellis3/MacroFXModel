A One-Page Risk Report

A risk report for a small multi-asset portfolio that a manager could act on: what it holds in risk terms, how bad a bad month gets, and what would break the assumptions.

CAPSTONE WORKBENCH~30 minYour notes stay in this browser
How to use it. Step 1 describes the portfolio. Every later step reuses those numbers, so change a weight or a correlation and the whole report follows. The defaults are illustrative, not a recommendation. Write each sentence in plain words a manager could act on, then download the page as your report.
Step 1 of 6

Describe the portfolio in risk terms

Three holdings, their weights, volatilities and correlations. The defaults are an illustrative 40/40/20 book: overwrite them with yours. Everything after this step uses these numbers.

Learn it in: Correlation & Linear Regression · Markowitz & Black-Litterman
Step 2 of 6

Allocate by risk contribution, not capital, and show the two side by side

Capital weights say where the money is. Risk contributions say where the swings come from. Equal risk contribution (risk parity) is the allocation where every holding adds the same share of risk.

Learn it in: Risk Parity & Equal Risk Contribution · Hierarchical Risk Parity
Step 3 of 6

Report expected shortfall as well as VaR, with a fat-tail adjustment

Value at risk is the loss you exceed on the worst 1 month in 20 (95%) or 1 in 100 (99%). Expected shortfall is the average loss on those bad months. The Cornish-Fisher line bends the normal answer for skew and fat tails.

Learn it in: Coherent Risk Measures: VaR, CVaR & the Artzner Axioms · Cornish-Fisher Expansion · Extreme Value Theory & Tail Risk
Step 4 of 6

Run one historical and one hypothetical stress scenario

Historical: pick a real episode and enter what each holding did in it. Hypothetical: invent the one that worries you most. The defaults are illustrative, not a record of any episode. The second default shows stocks and bonds falling together, the case a negative correlation assumption hides.

Learn it in: Stress Testing, Scenario Analysis & Reverse Stress Tests
Step 5 of 6

Show how many independent bets the book really holds, and what that falls to in a crisis

The effective number of bets reads the correlation matrix: three holdings that move together are closer to one bet. In a crisis correlations tend to rise towards one. Enter the level you expect them to reach.

Learn it in: Principal Component Analysis & Factor Regimes · Random Matrix Theory & the Marchenko-Pastur Law
Step 6 of 6

Name the one assumption whose failure would hurt most, and how you would see it coming

Every number above rests on assumptions: volatilities stay near today's, correlations hold, returns are roughly normal. Name the one that would hurt most if it broke, and the early sign you would watch for.

Learn it in: Copulas — Modeling Dependence Beyond Correlation · Stress Testing, Scenario Analysis & Reverse Stress Tests
