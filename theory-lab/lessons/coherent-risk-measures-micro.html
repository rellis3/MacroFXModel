Coherent Risk Measures: When Diversification Looks Riskier

Every risk limit, capital charge, and "how much could we lose" conversation reduces to one number pulled from a loss distribution. Value-at-Risk, the industry's default answer, sometimes fails a test that sounds like it should be automatic: combine two positions, and the risk number should never get worse than the sum of the parts. It can. Conditional VaR — the tail average, not just the cutoff — is the fix, and the four axioms that expose the difference are the entire subject of this deck.

Section 1

One number, everywhere

Value-at-Risk (VaR) is the most widely reported single risk statistic in finance. Here's what it actually promises.

🎯

The industry default

Risk limits, capital charges, and desk-level "how much could we lose" conversations almost all start here — VaR is the number everyone already has an intuition for.

📏

Answers exactly one question

Where does "fine" end and "bad" begin? VaR draws a single line: the loss level you should only expect to exceed (1−p) of the time.

🧮

Cheap, fast, easy to say out loud

"A 1-in-20-day loss level" fits in one sentence — which is exactly why it never left risk reports, even after its flaw became well known.

🌉

But it's silent past the line

Two portfolios can share an identical VaR while one barely crosses it on a bad day and the other collapses many multiples past it — VaR can't tell them apart.

Two bridges can share the exact same "1-in-100-year flood" rating while one shrugs off worse water and the other fails the moment the level rises another inch. VaR is that rating — it marks the waterline, and says nothing about what's on the other side of it.
Section 2

VaR, precisely

Let L be a random loss (positive L means you lost money). Fix a confidence level p — typically 0.95 or 0.99.

The threshold, drawn
VaRp(L) is the smallest loss threshold ℓ such that P(L > ℓ) ≤ 1−p. Equivalently, VaR₀.₉₅ is the 95th percentile of the loss distribution — the loss level you expect to exceed only 5% of the time.

That's the whole definition. VaR answers exactly one question — where is the cutoff — and says nothing at all about how bad things get once you're past it.

Section 3

CVaR: averaging what VaR ignores

Instead of stopping at the threshold, Conditional VaR (CVaR) keeps going — averaging every loss beyond it.

The tail, shaded
CVaRp(L) = E[L | L > VaRp(L)] — the average loss, conditional on already being in the tail beyond VaR. Also called Expected Shortfall (ES): same quantity, two names.
A flood rating tells you the water level for a "1-in-100-year" event. It says nothing about whether, on the unlucky year it's exceeded, you get a light overflow or a total collapse. CVaR is the more useful engineering question: given that the flood exceeds the rated level, how high does the water typically actually get?
Two names, one number

Conditional VaR and Expected Shortfall refer to the identical calculation here. If a regulatory document says "ES" and an academic paper says "CVaR," in the continuous-distribution case this deck uses throughout, they're the same thing.

Section 5

Artzner's four axioms

Artzner, Delbaen, Eber & Heath (1999) asked: what properties should any sensible risk measure ρ have, regardless of the formula computing it? A measure satisfying all four is called coherent.

1

Monotonicity

If position 1 can never lose more than position 2, in every outcome, it can't be rated riskier. L₁ ≤ L₂ always ⟹ ρ(L₁) ≤ ρ(L₂).

2

Sub-additivity

Combining two positions can never make measured risk exceed the sum of the parts. ρ(L₁+L₂) ≤ ρ(L₁) + ρ(L₂) — the formal version of "diversification shouldn't look worse than concentration."

3

Positive homogeneity

Scale a position by λ ≥ 0, its risk scales by exactly λ. ρ(λL) = λρ(L) — doubling a bet doubles its score, no bonus or penalty from pure size.

4

Translation invariance

Add a certain, non-random amount c to the loss, the risk score moves by exactly c. ρ(L+c) = ρ(L) + c.

Citation

Philippe Artzner, Freddy Delbaen, Jean-Marc Eber & David Heath, "Coherent Measures of Risk," Mathematical Finance, Vol. 9, No. 3, 1999 — the paper that turned "coherent" into a technical term, not just a compliment.

Section 6

The one axiom that actually bites

VaR satisfies three of the four axioms without much trouble — monotonicity, positive homogeneity, and translation invariance all hold under normal conditions. The one it fails, sub-additivity, is exactly the axiom that matters most for portfolios: VaR only reports where a single quantile sits and throws away everything about the distribution's shape beyond that point. Combine two positions, and the combined distribution's shape near its own quantile can look qualitatively different from either position's shape alone.

Two friends each have only a small chance of catching a cold this week — individually, neither looks "risky" by a simple cutoff rule. But the chance that at least one of them catches a cold is bigger than either alone. VaR, which only ever looks at one cutoff point, gets caught off guard by that combined possibility. CVaR doesn't, because it's already averaging over every bad outcome — including the ones where both happen together.

The next two slides make this completely concrete with two independent defaultable bonds — the textbook counterexample for exactly this reason.

Section 8

Same setup, now check CVaR

VaR — broke
  • VaR(A) = VaR(B) = $0 → sum $0
  • VaR(A+B) = $100
  • $100 > $0 — sub-additivity fails
CVaR — held
  • CVaR(A) = CVaR(B) = $100 → sum $200
  • CVaR(A+B) = E[loss | loss > $100] = $200 (the only outcome past $100 is "both default," costing exactly $200)
  • $200 ≤ $200 — sub-additivity holds, with equality

Section 11

Where this shows up — and a check against this repo's code

🏛

Regulatory capital moved to ES

The Basel Committee's Fundamental Review of the Trading Book (FRTB) replaced VaR-based market-risk capital with Expected Shortfall at 97.5% confidence, citing exactly this blind spot.

🧩

Convex risk measures optimize cleanly

CVaR is provably convex — a direct consequence of coherence — so "minimize CVaR subject to a return target" is well-posed. Minimizing VaR directly is harder and can have multiple local optima.

🏢

Aggregation across desks needs sub-additivity

A firm-wide risk number built by summing desks' individual numbers needs the aggregation itself to behave sensibly — exactly where a naive VaR-only rollup can silently mislead a risk committee.

📋

VaR didn't disappear — it got a companion

Still simple, cheap, and intuitive to report, so it's typically shown alongside CVaR/ES today, not replaced by it.

Checked against this repo's actual code

The full lesson describes this material as "conceptual… not a claim that any specific VaR/CVaR implementation is built or validated anywhere in this project." That's now out of date: js/metricsCore.js ships real histVaR and histCVaR functions — historical-quantile VaR and the mean-loss-beyond-that-quantile CVaR — which js/backtestStats.js actually calls to compute var95, var99, and cvar95 on real strategy P&L series. Separately, js/evtTail.js's potVarCvar() computes GPD/POT-based VaR and CVaR for extrapolating past the sample. So VaR and CVaR — in both a plain historical form and a fitted-tail form — are built and running in this codebase today; a full coherence audit of this specific pipeline is the part that remains "conceptual."

This is the first Risk Deep Dive in the curriculum, but not the first place these ideas surface. Extreme Value Theory, or How Bad It Gets, Given That It's Already Bad already builds the peaks-over-threshold machinery behind evtTail.js's fitted VaR/CVaR, and The Sharpe Ratio Doesn't Know How Many Times You Tried covers the companion question of whether a risk-adjusted return number can be trusted at all.

Section 12

Ways coherence gets misread

✗

"VaR is wrong" — no, it's incomplete

The arithmetic in the worked example isn't a bug. VaR, by construction, only ever reports one quantile and cannot see co-occurring tail events across positions — a different question from "is the number computed correctly."

📉

The failure needs a lumpy tail

Perfectly correlated positions, or a smooth unimodal distribution like a plain normal, often leave VaR behaving sub-additively in practice. The violation needs a discrete, "mostly fine, occasionally catastrophic" shape — defaults, jump risk, binary payoffs.

⚠

Coherent ≠ well-estimated

Coherence describes aggregation behavior only. A CVaR built on a thin tail sample is still an unstable, poorly estimated number — coherence and estimation quality are separate questions.

🧭

The axioms assume a known probability space

Extending the same intuitions to a strategy whose return distribution is itself uncertain — model risk stacked on top of the risk measure — needs more care than the four axioms alone provide.

Section 13

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

A portfolio's VaR₉₅ is $2M. What can you conclude?

VaR marks a quantile, not a ceiling. It says nothing about how much worse a loss beyond $2M could get.
Question 2

Which of the four Artzner axioms does plain VaR typically fail?

VaR generally satisfies monotonicity, positive homogeneity, and translation invariance. Sub-additivity — the one with the most direct portfolio-management consequence — is the one it can violate.
Question 3

In the two-bond example (4% default, $100 face, p=95%), why is each bond's individual VaR exactly $0?

P(default) = 4% ≤ 1−p = 5%, so the threshold ℓ=$0 already satisfies P(L>ℓ)≤5% — the definition of VaR.
Question 4

What does CVaR₉₅ measure that VaR₉₅ does not?

CVaR conditions on being in the tail and averages over the whole thing — that's the structural fix that gives it sub-additivity.
Question 5

A colleague says "CVaR is coherent, so it must be the more accurate number." What's the flaw?

A coherent formula fed a thin tail sample is still an unstable estimate. Coherence and estimation quality are separate questions.
Section 14

What to carry forward

01

VaR marks a threshold; CVaR averages everything beyond it — that structural difference explains everything else in this deck.

02

Coherence is four testable axioms — monotonicity, sub-additivity, positive homogeneity, translation invariance (Artzner, Delbaen, Eber & Heath, 1999).

03

VaR fails sub-additivity — the two-bond example shows two individually "safe" ($0 VaR) positions combining into a $100 VaR, the opposite of what diversification should do.

04

CVaR is coherent — same example, CVaR sums exactly ($200 ≤ $200), which is why post-2008 regulation (FRTB) shifted capital requirements from VaR toward Expected Shortfall.

05

The failure needs a lumpy, tail-heavy distribution — smooth unimodal losses or perfectly correlated positions usually don't trigger it; defaults, jumps, and options are where it bites.

06

Coherence ≠ good estimation — a coherent formula fed a thin tail sample is still an unstable number; see Extreme Value Theory for the machinery built to stabilize it.

What this now lets you do

Read a VaR headline for what it actually promises — a threshold, not a ceiling. Know which axiom to check before trusting an aggregated risk report across desks or strategies. And reach for CVaR/Expected Shortfall whenever positions are being combined and diversification's effect on risk needs to be verified, not just assumed.
