Active Portfolio Management

Alpha vs. Beta Separation showed how to pull a return stream apart into a market bet and a residual. This lesson asks the next question: how good is that residual, really — and is there even a way for the "average active dollar" to win at all?

CONCEPT — theory only, no manager, forecast series or portfolio in this repo has an IR, IC, breadth or Active Share computed for it ~17 min read Prereqs: Alpha vs. Beta Separation, Sharpe/Sortino/Calmar & the Deflated Sharpe Ratio, Factor-Based Portfolio Construction
TL;DR
  • The Information Ratio, \(IR = \alpha/\omega\), is the Sharpe ratio's benchmark-relative cousin: excess return over a chosen benchmark, divided by tracking error (the volatility of that excess return) — not excess return over cash divided by total volatility.
  • The Information Coefficient \(IC\) is the raw per-forecast skill number — the correlation between what a manager predicted and what actually happened. In practice an \(IC\) of just 0.05–0.10 is considered genuinely good.
  • The Fundamental Law of Active Management, \(IR \approx IC \times \sqrt{BR}\), says a modest edge repeated across many independent bets compounds into a strong aggregate Information Ratio — the same logic that makes a diversified portfolio's Sharpe ratio beat any single position's. The trap: breadth (\(BR\)) means independent bets, and correlated bets are not independent bets, no matter how many trade tickets they generate.
  • Active Share (Cremers & Petajisto) measures how different a portfolio's holdings are from its benchmark's; tracking error measures how different its returns have been. These are not the same axis, and a portfolio can score high on one and low on the other — the mismatch is often the most useful diagnostic in the whole lesson.
  • The mandatory honest caveat: before costs, the average actively-managed dollar's return must equal the market's return, because active managers collectively hold the market portfolio — a pure accounting identity (Sharpe, 1991), not a claim about anyone's skill. After costs, the average active dollar underperforms. Everything else here is about finding the manager on the right side of that split, not proof that doing so is easy.
Section 01

Why this matters

The Alpha vs. Beta Separation lesson built the tool for splitting any return stream into a market bet (beta, cheap and hedgeable) and a residual (alpha, the part actually worth paying for). It stopped there, at the split itself — it never asked how to judge whether a given alpha is good, or how a manager who repeats small, imperfect calls many times over can still end up with a strong track record. That's the gap this lesson fills: once alpha is isolated, how skilled does a manager have to be, per call, to produce a given level of risk-adjusted outperformance, and how many independent calls does that manager actually need to make?

It also introduces a second, entirely different lens on the same question — not "how much better did the returns look," but "how different are the actual holdings." Two portfolios can report the same tracking error against a benchmark yet be built in completely opposite ways: one from a handful of concentrated, high-conviction stock or currency picks, the other from a benchmark-hugging position with one large factor tilt layered on top. Telling those two apart — and knowing which one you're actually paying an active fee for — is the second half of this lesson, and it closes with the single most important caveat in the whole topic: a rigorous accounting identity that says the average active dollar cannot win before costs, and must lose after them.

💡
Plain-English intuition

A weather forecaster doesn't need to be right every day

Imagine a forecaster who predicts, every single trading day for a year, whether tomorrow will be slightly better or slightly worse than average for a given currency pair. On any one day, that forecaster is barely better than a coin flip — maybe right 52% of the time instead of 50%. That's a tiny edge, and useless if it's the only call being made. But repeat that tiny edge across 250 trading days, on genuinely independent information each time, and the accumulated record starts to look unmistakably skilled — not because any single call got much better, but because the noise in 250 small, uncorrelated bets washes out while the tiny edge keeps compounding. That's the entire intuition behind the Fundamental Law below: skill per bet (\(IC\)) and number of independent bets (\(BR\)) trade off against each other, and a manager can build a strong track record from either a rare high-conviction insight or a large number of small, genuinely uncorrelated ones. The catch — and it's a catch that trips up real portfolios constantly — is that "independent" is doing all the work in that sentence, not "many."

Section 02

The math, step by step

1

The Information Ratio — risk-adjusted skill against a benchmark

Where the Sharpe ratio measures excess return over cash per unit of total volatility, the Information Ratio measures excess return over a chosen benchmark per unit of the volatility of that excess return alone:

\[ IR = \frac{\alpha}{\omega} \]
IRInformation Ratio — risk-adjusted skill at beating this specific benchmark
αannualized benchmark-relative excess return
ω(omega) tracking error — annualized std. dev. of that excess return

Contrast this directly with the Sharpe ratio, \(S = (r_p - r_f)/\sigma_p\), covered in the Sharpe, Sortino, Calmar & the Deflated Sharpe Ratio lesson: Sharpe is benchmark-agnostic — it only asks whether a portfolio beat cash, using the portfolio's total volatility as the denominator. The Information Ratio is explicitly benchmark-relative — it asks whether a portfolio beat a specific comparison (an index, a peer group, a passive allocation), using only the volatility of the gap between the two as the denominator. A manager can have a mediocre Sharpe ratio (their total portfolio wasn't especially smooth) while still having an excellent Information Ratio (whatever bumpiness there was, it consistently beat the benchmark) — the two ratios are answering genuinely different questions, not two versions of the same one.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

The Sharpe ratio asks "did I beat just sitting in cash, given how bumpy my whole ride was." The Information Ratio asks a narrower, different question: "did I beat this one specific yardstick I said I'd be measured against, given how bumpy the gap between me and that yardstick was." You could be a smooth investor overall but a mediocre yardstick-beater, or a bumpy investor overall who still consistently edges out the yardstick — they're genuinely not measuring the same thing.

2

The Information Coefficient — raw skill per forecast

The Information Coefficient is the correlation between a manager's forecasted returns (or scores, rankings, signals) and the returns that were subsequently realized:

\[ IC = \operatorname{corr}(\text{forecast},\, \text{realized return}) \]
ICInformation Coefficient — a correlation, so it runs from −1 to +1
+1forecasts perfectly ranked the realized outcomes
0forecasts carried no information at all about what actually happened

\(IC\) is the single most sobering number in active management: it is a correlation over an entire universe of forecasts, not a per-trade win rate, and in practice an \(IC\) of just 0.05 to 0.10 is considered genuinely good — a working professional forecasting edge, not a rounding error. That concrete number matters for what follows, because it's the input the Fundamental Law multiplies by \(\sqrt{BR}\): a manager never needs anywhere close to \(IC=0.5\) to build a strong track record, provided \(BR\) is large enough and genuinely independent.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine grading a weather forecaster not on "did they nail tomorrow's exact temperature," but on "across a whole year of forecasts, did their guesses lean the right direction even slightly more often than random chance." A tiny lean in the right direction, repeated across hundreds of calls, is what a "good" IC of 0.05–0.10 actually looks like — it sounds unimpressively small, but it's real, professional-grade skill, not noise.

3

The Fundamental Law of Active Management

Grinold & Kahn's Fundamental Law connects the two numbers above to the Information Ratio through breadth — the number of independent bets made per year:

\[ IR \approx IC \times \sqrt{BR} \]
IRthe resulting Information Ratio
ICskill per forecast — how good each individual call is
BRbreadth — the number of statistically independent bets made per year

The \(\sqrt{BR}\) term is the same diversification arithmetic behind why a portfolio of many uncorrelated positions has a higher Sharpe ratio than any one position alone: uncorrelated noise partially cancels across bets while a genuine, repeated edge does not, so the signal-to-noise ratio improves with the square root of the number of independent trials. A manager doesn't need a rare, high-conviction insight to post a strong Information Ratio — modest skill (\(IC \approx 0.05\text{–}0.10\)), applied across enough truly independent calls, compounds into a respectable \(IR\) the same way many small, uncorrelated positions compound into a respectable portfolio Sharpe.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

A forecaster who's only slightly better than a coin flip on any single call can still build an impressively good track record — as long as they make that same slightly-better-than-a-coin-flip call many, many times, on genuinely separate, unrelated situations. The catch is "genuinely separate": if all your calls are secretly the same bet wearing different outfits, repeating it 50 times doesn't give you 50 independent shots — it gives you one bet dressed up 50 ways.

4

Active Share — a holdings-based measure, not a return-based one

Everything above is built from a portfolio's returns. Active Share, from Cremers & Petajisto (2009), instead measures how different the actual holdings are from the benchmark's:

\[ \text{Active Share} = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i} \left| w_{p,i} - w_{b,i} \right| \]
w_p,ithe portfolio's weight in holding i
w_b,ithe benchmark's weight in that same holding i
1/2prevents double counting — every overweight elsewhere is exactly offset by an underweight

Active Share runs from 0% (holdings identical to the benchmark, a literal index fund) to 100% (zero overlap with the benchmark at all). It is a fundamentally different axis from tracking error: tracking error is a statistical property of the return series, computed with no knowledge of what's actually held; Active Share is a mechanical property of the positions themselves, computed with no reference to how those positions' returns behaved. Two portfolios can post identical tracking error against a benchmark while holding almost completely different instruments, and two portfolios can hold almost identical positions while producing very different tracking error — the divergence between the two measures is the single most useful diagnostic this lesson has to offer, developed fully in the chart below.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Two students can get the exact same final grade (tracking error) in totally different ways — one by studying completely different material from the syllabus, the other by studying almost exactly the syllabus with one small extra topic tacked on. Active Share is like checking their actual notebooks (the holdings) instead of just their grade (the returns) — it tells you whether a fund is genuinely doing something different, or just quietly hugging the benchmark with one big side bet.

The breadth trap: \(BR\) counts independent bets, not trade tickets. Five separate G10 currency positions that all move together during a dollar-strength regime are much closer to one independent bet than five — inflating \(BR\) by counting correlated positions as though each were a fresh, uncorrelated call is a textbook misapplication of the Fundamental Law, and it makes an \(IR\) forecast look far more achievable than the manager's actual, correlation-adjusted opportunity set supports.

🧮
Worked example

Part 1 — checking the Fundamental Law's arithmetic

A manager's forecasts have an estimated \(IC = 0.10\) — at the top of the "genuinely good" range. The manager re-ranks 9 G10 currency pairs once per quarter, giving \(9 \times 4 = 36\) nominal forecasts per year; taken at face value as fully independent bets, \(BR = 36\). The Fundamental Law then predicts:

\[ IR \approx IC \times \sqrt{BR} = 0.10 \times \sqrt{36} = 0.10 \times 6 = 0.60 \]

Checking this is internally consistent with the \(IR=\alpha/\omega\) definition: an annualized benchmark-relative excess return of \(\alpha = 3.0\%\) against a tracking error of \(\omega = 5.0\%\) gives \(IR = 3.0/5.0 = 0.60\) — the same number, arrived at two different ways, which is exactly the point of the Fundamental Law: it connects the return-based \(IR\) to the forecast-based \(IC\) and \(BR\) that produced it.

The caveat immediately applies: \(BR=36\) only holds if those 36 quarterly calls are genuinely independent. If the manager's rankings are really one dominant "dollar strength vs. weakness" view applied across 9 correlated G10 crosses, the true breadth could be closer to \(BR=4\) (one independent view, refreshed quarterly) than \(BR=36\) — which would predict \(IR \approx 0.10 \times \sqrt{4} = 0.20\), a much less impressive number than 0.60. Nothing about the arithmetic itself flags this — only checking the actual correlation structure of the 9 pairs' forecasts does.

Part 2 — computing Active Share for a 6-name toy portfolio

A macro FX sleeve is benchmarked against a G10 currency-exposure index. Its portfolio weights and the benchmark's weights across six positions:

EURPortfolio 40% · Benchmark 25% · \(|w_p-w_b|=15\)
JPYPortfolio 10% · Benchmark 20% · \(|w_p-w_b|=10\)
GBPPortfolio 15% · Benchmark 15% · \(|w_p-w_b|=0\)
AUDPortfolio 25% · Benchmark 15% · \(|w_p-w_b|=10\)
CADPortfolio 0% · Benchmark 15% · \(|w_p-w_b|=15\)
Rest of G10Portfolio 10% · Benchmark 10% · \(|w_p-w_b|=0\)

Both sets of weights sum to 100% (40+10+15+25+0+10 = 100 for the portfolio; 25+20+15+15+15+10 = 100 for the benchmark). Summing the absolute differences: \(15+10+0+10+15+0 = 50\). Active Share is half of that sum:

\[ \text{Active Share} = \frac{1}{2}(50) = 25\% \]

A 25% Active Share is, on its own, a fairly modest number — this sleeve is holding real over/underweights in EUR, JPY, AUD and CAD, but three of six positions (GBP and "Rest of G10") sit exactly on the benchmark. Whether 25% counts as "active enough" depends entirely on what tracking error this same portfolio produced — which is precisely the second axis the 2×2 above exists to check, not something the holdings-based number alone can answer.

🎯
Real-world trading scenario

An allocator's due diligence turns up a closet indexer

An allocation committee is reviewing "Fund X," a G10 FX fund charging a full active management fee. On the surface the numbers look reasonable: a multi-year Information Ratio of roughly 0.3 against its stated benchmark, with a tracking error of about 3% — not spectacular, but not obviously a red flag either. The committee's first pass, using only \(IR\) and \(\omega\), would likely wave the fund through.

As a second, independent check, the committee requests Fund X's actual quarter-end holdings and computes Active Share against the same benchmark — the same \(\tfrac{1}{2}\sum|w_{p,i}-w_{b,i}|\) calculation as the worked example above, just run on the fund's real position file. The result comes back at 18% — deep in the low-Active-Share range. Fund X's modest 3% tracking error, it turns out, is being generated almost entirely by one persistent overweight to a single high-beta currency, sitting on top of a book that otherwise looks close to the benchmark itself.

That's the bottom-left/top-left boundary from the 2×2 above in practice: a fund with return-based numbers that look like modest, plausible active management is, on a holdings basis, close to a passive position with one factor bet layered on top — "closet indexing," in Cremers & Petajisto's own term for exactly this pattern, discovered only because the committee checked a holdings-based measure that a returns-only review would never have surfaced.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

Institutional manager selection teams use \(IR\), \(IC\) and Active Share as three separate, complementary due-diligence checks rather than a single combined score. \(IR\) answers "did this manager beat the stated benchmark, per unit of the risk taken to do it." \(IC\), where a manager's raw forecast history is available, answers the more fundamental "is there real, positive skill in the individual calls, independent of how concentrated or sized the resulting bets happened to be." And Active Share answers a question neither return-based number can: "is the fee being paid for a genuinely different portfolio, or for a benchmark-like book with a name attached." The Fundamental Law connects the first two — a plausible \(IR\) can come from either rare high-conviction skill or many small, independent edges, and knowing which one a manager is actually running matters for whether that \(IR\) is likely to persist as market conditions change.

The mandatory honest framing — this is a zero-sum accounting identity, not a claim about anyone's skill. Sharpe's 1991 "Arithmetic of Active Management" result: because every share of every security is held by someone, and passive investors by construction hold the market portfolio, active investors in aggregate must also collectively hold the market portfolio — there is nothing else left for them to hold. It follows immediately, before any costs are considered, that the average actively-managed dollar's return must equal the market's return, dollar for dollar. After costs (management fees, trading costs, the bid-ask spread crossed on every rebalance), the average active dollar must underperform the average passive dollar by exactly the amount of those costs. This is true by the market-clearing definition of "the market" — it holds regardless of whether active managers are collectively skilled, unskilled, or anything in between. Everything built above — \(IR\), \(IC\), breadth, Active Share — is machinery for identifying which manager sits on the winning side of that zero-sum split. None of it is evidence that doing so is easy, common, or that this repo has identified one.
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Picture a pizza that's already been completely divided among everyone at the table — the average slice size, by definition, is just one divided by the number of people, no matter who's "better" at getting pizza. Investing works the same way before fees: since every single share of every company has to be owned by someone, the average actively-managed dollar's return can't beat the market — it has to exactly equal it, purely because of how the ownership adds up. Once you subtract the cost of trying, the average active dollar actually does worse than just holding everything passively.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where active management measurement goes wrong in practice

Counting correlated bets as independent ones. As flagged in the solid callout above, inflating \(BR\) by treating five correlated positions as five independent bets is the single most common misapplication of the Fundamental Law — it makes a target \(IR\) look achievable with far less real skill than the strategy's true, correlation-adjusted opportunity set can support.

A high Information Ratio over a short history barely means anything. The same short-track-record problem the Sharpe, Sortino, Calmar & the Deflated Sharpe Ratio lesson raises for a headline Sharpe number applies identically to \(IR\) — a handful of quarters of strong benchmark-relative performance is close to indistinguishable from noise, and that lesson's minimum-track-record discussion is the right place to check how many observations are actually needed before trusting a reported ratio at all.

Conflating tracking error with Active Share. They answer different questions and can diverge sharply, as the 2×2 above shows — a low tracking error does not imply benchmark-like holdings, and a high Active Share does not imply large realized return deviation. Checking only one of the two, as is common in practice, misses exactly the closet-indexing pattern the scenario above is built around.

Forgetting the zero-sum floor. A positive \(IR\) or a high Active Share are descriptive facts about a specific manager over a specific history — neither one repeals the accounting identity above. The base rate for "the average active dollar, after costs" is underperformance, and any specific manager's numbers need to clear that bar convincingly, on genuinely out-of-sample history, before being treated as the exception.

Section 04

Key Takeaways

✓IR is Sharpe's benchmark-relative cousin. \(IR=\alpha/\omega\) measures skill at beating a specific benchmark using tracking error as the denominator, not skill at beating cash using total volatility.
✓An IC of 0.05–0.10 is genuinely good. The Information Coefficient is a correlation over an entire forecast universe, not a per-trade win rate — real, useful skill looks small on this scale.
✓The Fundamental Law, \(IR\approx IC\times\sqrt{BR}\), lets modest skill compound. Many small, independent edges can produce as strong an \(IR\) as one rare high-conviction insight — the worked example checks this arithmetic two ways and gets the same 0.60.
⚠Breadth means independent bets, not trade count. Correlated positions inflate \(BR\) and make a target \(IR\) look far more achievable than the manager's true opportunity set supports — the single most common misapplication of this formula.
✓Active Share (holdings) and tracking error (returns) are different axes and can diverge. Low Active Share plus high tracking error is a classic closet-indexing signature that a returns-only review misses entirely.
⚠The zero-sum floor is an accounting identity, not an opinion. Before costs the average active dollar equals the market; after costs it must underperform (Sharpe, 1991) — every tool in this lesson is about finding the exception, not proof that exceptions are common.
✗Not built or validated in this repo. No manager, forecast series, or portfolio here has an \(IR\), \(IC\), breadth, or Active Share number actually computed against real data.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. How does the Information Ratio differ from the Sharpe ratio — both in what goes in the numerator and what goes in the denominator?
The Sharpe ratio's numerator is excess return over the risk-free rate, and its denominator is the portfolio's total volatility — it is benchmark-agnostic, only asking whether a portfolio beat cash. The Information Ratio's numerator is excess return over a specific chosen benchmark, and its denominator is tracking error — the volatility of that benchmark-relative excess return alone, not the portfolio's total volatility. IR answers "did this beat a specific comparison, per unit of the risk taken relative to it," which is a different question from Sharpe's "did this beat cash, per unit of total risk."
Q2. Why is an Information Coefficient of 0.05–0.10 described as "genuinely good" rather than weak?
IC is a correlation computed across an entire universe of forecasts, not a per-trade accuracy rate — it measures whether forecasted rankings, on average, line up even slightly better than chance with what actually happened, across potentially hundreds of individual calls. A correlation that small is easy to dismiss as noise by intuition, but the Fundamental Law shows that a small, real, repeated edge compounds into a meaningful Information Ratio once enough independent bets are made — so 0.05–0.10 represents real, professional-grade forecasting skill, not a rounding error.
Q3. In the worked example, IC=0.10 and BR=36 gives IR≈0.60. What independently checks that this number is consistent, and why does that check matter?
The check is computing IR directly from IR=α/ω using the illustrative α=3.0% and ω=5.0%, giving 3.0/5.0=0.60 — the same value the Fundamental Law predicted from IC and BR alone. It matters because it demonstrates the Fundamental Law is not an independent, separately-fitted formula — it is an approximation that connects the forecast-level statistics (IC, BR) to the return-level statistics (α, ω) that they, in aggregate, produce.
Q4. Why would treating a manager's 9 correlated G10 currency positions as 9 independent bets overstate their predicted Information Ratio?
The Fundamental Law's √BR term only produces a genuine noise-cancellation benefit when the bets are actually statistically independent — if all 9 positions move together during, say, a dollar-strength regime, their combined outcome behaves much more like a single large bet than nine small, uncorrelated ones. Plugging in BR=36 (treating each of the 9 pairs' 4 quarterly calls as independent) when the true independent breadth is closer to BR=4 overstates the predicted IR substantially (0.60 vs. roughly 0.20 in the worked example), because the formula has no way to detect that the bets weren't actually independent — that check has to be done separately, on the correlation structure of the forecasts themselves.
Q5. A fund reports a modest, plausible Information Ratio and tracking error. Why might a due-diligence team still want to compute its Active Share separately?
Tracking error and Active Share are independent axes — tracking error is computed purely from the return series, with no reference to what is actually held, while Active Share is computed purely from the holdings, with no reference to how those holdings' returns behaved. A fund can generate a given tracking error either from broad, genuinely different individual positions or from a benchmark-like book with one or two large factor/beta bets layered on top — the return-based numbers alone cannot distinguish between those two very different portfolios, but Active Share, computed on the actual position file, can. This is exactly how the "closet indexing" pattern in the scenario above gets uncovered.
Q6. Why does Sharpe's zero-sum result hold regardless of whether active managers, on average, are skilled or unskilled?
It follows purely from the definition of "the market" as the sum of everything held by every investor. Passive investors, by construction, hold the market portfolio. Since every unit of every security must be held by someone, whatever is left over — held by everyone who is not passive — must, in aggregate, also equal the market portfolio; there is nothing else for it to be. That means the average active dollar's return equals the market's return before any costs are subtracted, purely as an accounting identity about who holds what, with no assumption anywhere in the derivation about whether any individual manager is skilled. Costs then push the average active dollar's return below the average passive dollar's, since passive investing has minimal costs by comparison.
