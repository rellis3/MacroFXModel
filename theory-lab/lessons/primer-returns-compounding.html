Returns, Log-Returns & Compounding

Two ways to measure "how much did the price move," why they disagree the moment volatility shows up, and why that disagreement is the single fact underneath option pricing and position sizing alike.

PRIMER — the last stop before Lesson 1 ~14 min read Prereqs: Correlation & Linear Regression
TL;DR
  • Simple returns (% change) compound multiplicatively and pick up a cross term when chained; log returns (\(\ln(P_t/P_{t-1})\)) are exactly time-additive — a \(T\)-period log return is just the sum of the one-period log returns, no approximation needed.
  • The arithmetic mean (\(\bar{R}\)) describes a typical single period; the geometric mean (\(g\)) describes what actually happened to compounded wealth, and the two coincide only when variance is zero: \(g \approx \bar{R} - \sigma^2/2\) (volatility drag), a gap that grows with variance, not with the mean.
  • Worked example: +50% then −50% has a 0% arithmetic mean but a real 25% loss ($100 → $150 → $75), because the −50% is taken off a bigger base than the +50% was added to — the whole primer distilled into one trade.
  • Volatility scales with the square root of time (\(\sigma_T = \sigma\sqrt{T}\)), not time itself, because variances of independent periods add while standard deviations don't — the exact reason this repo's engines scale daily σ by √5 for weekly and √20 for 20-day horizons instead of re-estimating from scratch.
  • The page's interactive chart lets you drag an annualized-volatility slider and watch the compounded-value line pull away from the fixed arithmetic-mean projection as σ²/2 drag grows quadratically with σ.
Section 01

Why this matters

Every lesson from here on describes a price series with a mean and a variance and calls the result "the return." That word is doing more work than it looks like it's doing. There are two mathematically distinct definitions of "return" in common use — the simple, percentage-change return you'd compute by hand, and the log return that every stochastic model in this series (Black-Scholes, GARCH, the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, Kalman filters on price) is secretly built on. They agree almost exactly for small moves and diverge in ways that matter for large ones, and the gap between them is not a technicality — it is the reason a volatile strategy compounds slower than its own average return suggests, the reason option pricing models assume prices are lognormal rather than normal, and the reason this repo's forecast engine scales its volatility bands by the square root of the horizon instead of the horizon itself.

This is the fourth and final math primer, and it closes the loop the first three opened. The statistics primer gave you mean, variance, and standard deviation as tools for describing a distribution. The probability primer gave you independence and the Central Limit Theorem — why sums of many small, independent things start looking Gaussian. The correlation and regression primer gave you the machinery for relating two series to each other. This primer applies all three to the one series every lesson after this one actually cares about: a price, evolving through time, and the arithmetic of what happens when you chain many periods of it together.

💡
Plain-English intuition

"Average return" is a trap if you don't say which average

Imagine a strategy that, over two periods, is up 50% in the first and down 50% in the second. Averaged the obvious way — add the two numbers, divide by two — the "average return" is exactly 0%. It sounds like you should have your money back. You don't. Start with $100: up 50% takes you to $150; down 50% of that new, larger $150 takes you to $75. You lost a quarter of your money on a strategy whose average return was zero. Nothing about that is a trick or a rounding error — it is what multiplying does that adding doesn't. A gain and an equal-sized loss are not opposites once you're compounding, because the loss is taken off a bigger base than the gain was added to a smaller one. This one example is the entire subject of this primer in miniature: it is why there are two different "averages" of a return series (arithmetic and geometric), why the gap between them is called volatility drag, and why that gap grows with variance, not with how good or bad the individual returns "average out" to look.

Section 02

The math, step by step

Two definitions of return. Let \(P_t\) be a price (or level, or NAV) at time \(t\), and \(P_{t-1}\) the price one period earlier. The simple return — the one everyone computes first, and the one that appears on a brokerage statement — is the percentage change:

\[ R_t = \frac{P_t - P_{t-1}}{P_{t-1}} \]
Pₜprice at the end of the period
Pₜ₋₁price one period earlier
Rₜthe plain percentage change

The log return is the natural logarithm of the price ratio instead:

\[ r_t = \ln\!\left(\frac{P_t}{P_{t-1}}\right) \]
ln(·)natural logarithm of the price ratio
rₜthe log return — additive across time, derived below

These are related by a simple identity worth holding onto: \(1+R_t = P_t/P_{t-1} = e^{r_t}\), so \(r_t = \ln(1+R_t)\) and \(R_t = e^{r_t}-1\). Neither definition is "more correct" in the abstract — a 10% simple gain is a real, literal 10% more money, and that's what a P&L statement should show. But the two behave completely differently once you start chaining periods together, and that difference is the whole reason quantitative models default to log returns almost everywhere.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

There are two ways to say how much something changed. A simple return is just "went up 10%" — easy to picture. A log return is a slightly different way of counting the same change using a special math trick, and it turns out to behave much more nicely when you chain a bunch of them together.

\(P_t\)Price (or index level, NAV, exchange rate) at time \(t\).
\(R_t\)Simple return over period \(t\): \((P_t-P_{t-1})/P_{t-1}\), a percentage of the starting price.
\(r_t\)Log return over period \(t\): \(\ln(P_t/P_{t-1})\).
\(\bar{R}\), \(\mu\)Arithmetic mean of a return series — the plain average, add and divide.
\(g\)Geometric mean (compounded per-period growth rate) — the constant per-period return that reproduces the same ending wealth.
\(\sigma^2\)Variance of the return series; \(\sigma\) is its standard deviation, the volatility.
\(T\)Number of periods (the holding horizon) a return or a volatility figure is measured or scaled over.

Why simple returns don't add across time. Chain two periods of simple returns together and the result is not their sum. Starting wealth \(W_0\) becomes \(W_0(1+R_1)\) after period 1, then \(W_0(1+R_1)(1+R_2)\) after period 2. The two-period simple return is \((1+R_1)(1+R_2) - 1 = R_1 + R_2 + R_1 R_2\) — the sum of the two individual returns plus a cross term \(R_1R_2\) that doesn't vanish unless one of the returns is zero. Simple returns compound multiplicatively, not additively, which is exactly what made the +50%/-50% example in the intuition box misleading if you tried to average the raw numbers.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

If you go up 10% one day and up 10% the next, you don't just get 20% total — you get a little extra bonus on top, because the second day's 10% grew off an already-bigger pile of money. That leftover bonus bit is the "cross term," and it's exactly why you can't just add simple percentages together across days.

Why log returns do add across time. This is the single most useful property in this primer, and it falls straight out of one logarithm rule: \(\ln(ab) = \ln a + \ln b\). Since \(P_2/P_0 = (P_1/P_0)\cdot(P_2/P_1)\), taking the log of both sides gives:

\[ \ln\!\left(\frac{P_2}{P_0}\right) = \ln\!\left(\frac{P_1}{P_0}\right) + \ln\!\left(\frac{P_2}{P_1}\right) \]
ln(P₂/P₀)the whole two-period log return
= sum of legseach one-period log return, added exactly — no cross term

which is exactly \(r_{0\to 2} = r_{0\to 1} + r_{1\to 2}\): the log return over the whole two-period stretch is simply the sum of the log returns of each leg — no cross term, no approximation, an exact identity for any size of move. This generalizes to any number of periods by the same telescoping argument: the log return from time \(0\) to time \(T\) is just \(r_1 + r_2 + \cdots + r_T\), the plain sum of the one-period log returns in between. This is what "log returns are time-additive" means, and it is the reason models that need to reason about returns over different horizons (a day, a week, a month, an option's time to expiry) almost always work in log-return space: addition is a far simpler operation to build statistics and probability theory on top of than the multiplicative compounding simple returns require.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Log returns are built specially so that leftover bonus bit never shows up — add up log returns from a whole bunch of days in a row, and you get exactly the log return for the whole stretch, no extra bit left over, like stacking blocks that always fit together perfectly.

Arithmetic mean vs. geometric mean. Given a series of simple returns \(R_1, \ldots, R_T\), there are two natural ways to ask "what was the average return?" The arithmetic mean is the plain average, \(\bar{R} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_t R_t\) — it answers "if I picked one period at random, what return would I expect?" The geometric mean \(g\) answers a different question: "what single, constant per-period return, applied every period, would have turned my actual starting wealth into my actual ending wealth?" Formally, it solves \((1+g)^T = \prod_t (1+R_t)\), so:

\[ g = \left(\prod_{t=1}^{T}(1+R_t)\right)^{1/T} - 1 \]
∏(1+Rₜ)chain-multiply every period's wealth factor
^(1/T)take the T-th root to get a per-period rate
gthe constant rate that reproduces actual ending wealth

The geometric mean is the number that actually describes compounded performance; the arithmetic mean is the number that describes the typical single period. They coincide only when every \(R_t\) is identical (zero variance) — the moment there is any spread at all, \(g\) falls below \(\bar{R}\), and it falls further the more variance there is. That gap is not a rounding artifact of the formula; it is a real, mechanical consequence of multiplying instead of adding, the same mechanism the +50%/−50% example showed directly.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine grading a video game player two ways: "what's their typical single round score" (that's the arithmetic mean) versus "if every round had scored exactly the same, what score would give the exact same final total" (that's the geometric mean). These give different numbers whenever the rounds bounce around a lot instead of being steady.

Deriving the gap: volatility drag. To see exactly where the gap comes from — and to see that it's driven by variance specifically, not by the mean — work in log returns and lean on a small piece of calculus: for small \(x\), \(\ln(1+x) \approx x - x^2/2\) (the second-order Taylor expansion of the natural log around zero; this is the same kind of local approximation used elsewhere in this series, and it gets more accurate the smaller \(x\) is). Apply it to each period's simple return:

\[ r_t = \ln(1+R_t) \approx R_t - \frac{R_t^2}{2} \]
Rₜthe period's simple return
−Rₜ²/2the second-order correction term — the seed of volatility drag

Average both sides over many periods. The average of \(R_t\) is just the arithmetic mean \(\bar{R}\). The average of \(R_t^2\) is, by the standard definition of variance (mean of the squared deviations from the mean, plus the mean squared — a fact from the statistics primer), approximately \(\sigma^2 + \bar{R}^2\); for the return sizes typical of a single trading period, \(\bar{R}^2\) is small enough relative to \(\sigma^2\) to drop, leaving a clean, useful approximation:

\[ \bar{r} \approx \bar{R} - \frac{\sigma^2}{2} \]
R̄the arithmetic mean return
σ²/2the drag subtracted off it

And because log returns are additive across time, the average log return over many periods is the compounded per-period growth rate — so \(\bar{r} \approx g\), and the relationship becomes the volatility-drag formula that also anchors the Kelly Criterion lesson:

\[ g \approx \bar{R} - \frac{\sigma^2}{2} \]

Read this literally: the compounded growth rate you actually experience is approximately the average per-period return you'd naively compute, minus half the variance. That subtracted term, \(\sigma^2/2\), is volatility drag (sometimes called variance drain). It costs you nothing if returns are perfectly steady (\(\sigma=0\)) and it costs you more, quadratically, the more the returns bounce around — doubling volatility while holding the arithmetic mean fixed doesn't double the drag, it quadruples it, because the drag term is \(\sigma^2\), not \(\sigma\). This is exactly why two strategies with identical average returns can have wildly different real-world outcomes: the steadier one keeps almost all of its arithmetic mean as real growth, and the choppier one gives a chunk of it back to its own variance before it ever reaches the compounding investor.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Bumpy, unpredictable growth quietly eats into your results compared to smooth, steady growth — even if they average out to look the same on paper. The bumpier things are, the bigger the bite taken out of your real, actual growth. This hidden bite is called volatility drag.

The trap: a gain and an equal-sized loss are not opposites once you're compounding. +50% then −50% is a 0% arithmetic average and a real 25% loss, because the loss is taken off a bigger base than the gain was added to. "Average return" without saying which average — arithmetic or geometric — is close to meaningless for anything you actually held through.

Why volatility scales with the square root of time. Log returns being additive across time buys one more result, and it's the one this repo's engines lean on daily (literally). Suppose one-period log returns \(r_1, \ldots, r_T\) are approximately independent of each other — a reasonable assumption for returns measured over non-overlapping windows with no strong serial correlation, and the same independence idea the probability primer built the Central Limit Theorem on. The \(T\)-period log return is their sum, \(r_{0\to T} = \sum_{t=1}^{T} r_t\). A basic property of variance — proved in the statistics primer — is that the variance of a sum of independent variables is the sum of their variances (this is not true of standard deviations, only of variances; it's why variance, not standard deviation, is the additive quantity). If every period has the same variance \(\sigma^2\):

\[ \text{Var}(r_{0\to T}) = \sum_{t=1}^{T}\text{Var}(r_t) = T\sigma^2 \]
ΣVar(rₜ)variances of independent periods simply add
Tσ²T periods of equal variance σ², summed

Standard deviation is the square root of variance, so the \(T\)-period volatility is:

\[ \sigma_T = \sqrt{T\sigma^2} = \sigma\sqrt{T} \]
σone-period volatility
√Tvolatility scales by the square root of the horizon, not the horizon itself

This is the famous "square-root-of-time" rule. It is not an empirical regularity someone noticed in market data — it's a direct algebraic consequence of (1) log returns adding across time and (2) variances of independent things adding across time, both derived above from definitions this series already introduced. It tells you, for instance, that if daily volatility is \(\sigma\), then 4-day volatility is \(2\sigma\) (not \(4\sigma\)) and 16-day volatility is \(4\sigma\) — the time horizon has to increase sixteenfold to double the volatility, because volatility scales with the square root of the horizon, not the horizon itself.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

If you know how wobbly something is in one day, you can't just multiply that wobbliness by the number of days to guess how wobbly it'll be over a month — you multiply by the square root of the number of days instead. So a month (about 20 days) isn't 20 times wobblier than a day, it's only about 4.5 times wobblier, since √20 ≈ 4.5.

🧮
Worked example

+50% then −50%: a 0% average return that loses you 25%

Take $100 through two periods: period 1 is \(R_1=+50\%\), period 2 is \(R_2=-50\%\). The arithmetic mean is \((0.50 + (-0.50))/2 = 0\%\) — by that measure, "nothing happened."

Now compound it for real. After period 1: \(100 \times 1.50 = \$150\). After period 2: \(150 \times 0.50 = \$75\). Starting from $100 and ending at $75 is a 25% loss — despite a 0% arithmetic average return. The geometric mean here is \(g = \sqrt{1.50\times 0.50} - 1 = \sqrt{0.75}-1 \approx -13.4\%\) per period, which correctly compounds: \((1-0.134)^2 \approx 0.75\), back to the actual $75 ending value. The arithmetic mean told you the typical single-period return; the geometric mean told you what actually happened to your money.

The log-return version confirms the additivity property derived above, exactly, not approximately. \(r_1 = \ln(1.50) \approx 0.4055\), \(r_2 = \ln(0.50) \approx -0.6931\). Their sum is \(0.4055 + (-0.6931) = -0.2877\). Compare that to the log return of the whole two-period move computed directly: \(\ln(75/100) = \ln(0.75) \approx -0.2877\) — the same number, because log returns add across time by construction, with no approximation needed for this identity (the Taylor-expansion approximation earlier was only for the arithmetic-mean shortcut formula \(g\approx\bar{R}-\sigma^2/2\), not for the exact log-additivity identity used here).

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

🧮

Quant modeling

Defaults to log returns for volatility, GARCH, regression, and price-path simulation — additivity across time and unbounded range make normality far more defensible.

📑

Performance reporting

Almost always uses simple returns and the geometric mean — a fund's "annualized return" is CAGR, because that's what reconciles with the dollars in the account.

📏

Risk desks & VaR scaling

Scale a 1-day VaR to a 10-day VaR by multiplying by √10 rather than re-estimating — a convenience that collapses if the independence assumption breaks.

Quant desks default to log returns for almost any modeling task — computing historical volatility, fitting a GARCH model, feeding a return series into a regression, simulating a price path — precisely because of the two properties derived above: additivity across time (so a daily model and a monthly model are the same math, just summed over more periods) and the fact that log returns can range from \(-\infty\) to \(+\infty\) while simple returns are bounded below at \(-100\%\), which makes a Gaussian (or any symmetric, unbounded) distributional assumption far more defensible for log returns than for simple ones. This is the exact justification behind the standard assumption that price levels are lognormally distributed — if log returns are approximately normal (a sum of many small, roughly independent shocks, which is the Central Limit Theorem argument from the probability primer applied to a price series), then the price level itself, being \(e^{\text{sum of normals}}\), is lognormal by definition. That single assumption is the foundation Black-Scholes is built on.

Performance reporting, by contrast, almost always uses simple returns and the geometric mean, not the arithmetic mean — a fund's "annualized return" is a compounded (geometric) figure, because that's the number that actually reconciles with the dollars in the account. Seeing both numbers side by side on a tearsheet — arithmetic mean return and CAGR (compound annual growth rate) — is a legitimate, quick volatility-drag check: the bigger the gap between them, the more variance is eating into real compounded performance, independent of whatever the win rate or average trade looks like in isolation.

The square-root-of-time rule is the standard, simplest way risk desks convert a volatility or Value-at-Risk figure between horizons — a 1-day 95% VaR is routinely scaled to a 10-day VaR by multiplying by \(\sqrt{10}\) rather than re-estimating the whole model at the longer horizon, precisely because it falls directly out of the independent-additive-variance argument above. It is a convenience, not a law of nature — the entire scaling collapses if the independence assumption it rests on breaks, which is a theme this series returns to more than once.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where returns-and-compounding reasoning quietly goes wrong

Averaging simple returns and reporting it as "the" return over a longer period. The arithmetic mean of a set of simple returns describes a typical single period; it does not describe what happened to compounded wealth, and the gap between the two — as the worked example shows starkly — can be the difference between "flat" and "down 25%." Any headline "average return" figure that doesn't specify arithmetic or geometric should be treated as ambiguous, and a strategy summary that only reports the arithmetic mean is, whether intentionally or not, reporting the more flattering of the two numbers.

Trusting log-return additivity for very large returns. The exact additivity identity \(r_{0\to T} = \sum r_t\) always holds — that part is algebra, not approximation, and the worked example confirmed it exactly. What breaks down for large moves is the separate small-\(x\) approximation \(\ln(1+x)\approx x - x^2/2\) used to derive the volatility-drag formula \(g\approx\bar{R}-\sigma^2/2\) — that shortcut degrades as individual-period returns get large (a genuine concern for daily crypto or single-name equity moves, less so for typical daily FX). When returns are large, use the exact geometric-mean formula rather than the Taylor-approximated shortcut.

Applying square-root-of-time scaling when returns aren't independent. The entire derivation above rests on periods being approximately independent, so their variances add cleanly. Real markets violate this routinely — volatility clustering (calm periods followed by calm periods, turbulent periods followed by turbulent periods, the exact phenomenon the GARCH lesson models) means today's variance is not independent of yesterday's, and under positive serial correlation in volatility, naive \(\sqrt{T}\) scaling understates true multi-period risk. The rule is a useful default, not a universal law, and it should be checked against realized multi-period volatility rather than assumed.

Confusing "zero average return" with "no loss." This is the intuition-box trap restated as a pitfall: a strategy or a position can show a 0% (or even positive) arithmetic mean return over some stretch while actually losing money in compounded terms, purely because of variance. Checking realized ending equity against the arithmetic-mean-implied projection is the simplest way to catch this before it's mistaken for a healthy, break-even system.

Section 05

Self-test

Q1. Why can two return series have the same arithmetic mean but wildly different compounded outcomes?
Because compounding is multiplicative, not additive: ending wealth is a product of \((1+R_t)\) terms, and the geometric mean that actually governs that product is approximately \(g \approx \bar{R} - \sigma^2/2\) — it depends on variance as well as the arithmetic mean. Two series can share the same \(\bar{R}\) while having very different \(\sigma^2\); the higher-variance one suffers more volatility drag and compounds to a lower ending value even though its "typical single period" looks identical on paper. The +50%/−50% example makes this concrete: the arithmetic mean is 0%, but a real 25% loss results.
Q2. Why are log returns additive across time while simple returns are not?
Chaining simple returns multiplies wealth factors together — \((1+R_1)(1+R_2)\) — which expands to \(R_1+R_2+R_1R_2\), a sum plus a nonzero cross term. Chaining log returns instead adds the logs of those same wealth factors directly, because \(\ln(ab)=\ln a+\ln b\) is an exact identity with no cross term: \(\ln(P_2/P_0)=\ln(P_1/P_0)+\ln(P_2/P_1)\). This holds exactly for any size of move, which is why log returns, not simple returns, are the natural unit for reasoning about returns across different time horizons.
Q3. Why does a strategy's volatility roughly quadruple if you extend the holding period from 1 day to 16 days?
Volatility scales with the square root of time, not time itself: \(\sigma_T = \sigma\sqrt{T}\), because variances of approximately independent periods add (\(\text{Var}(r_{0\to T}) = T\sigma^2\)) and standard deviation is the square root of variance. Going from 1 day to 16 days multiplies the horizon by 16, so volatility scales by \(\sqrt{16}=4\) — quadruples — not by 16. This is exactly the ×1 / ×√5 / ×√20 scaling this project uses for its daily / weekly / 20-day horizons.
Q4. In the +50%/−50% worked example, why does the log-return sum exactly equal the log return of the whole two-period move, while the simple-return average does not equal the whole-period simple return?
Log-return additivity, \(r_{0\to2}=r_1+r_2\), is an exact algebraic identity from \(\ln(ab)=\ln a+\ln b\) — no approximation, so \(\ln(1.5)+\ln(0.5)=\ln(0.75)\) matches to full precision. Simple returns compound multiplicatively: the two-period simple return is \((1+R_1)(1+R_2)-1\), which equals \(R_1+R_2\) only when the cross term \(R_1R_2\) is zero. Here \(R_1R_2 = 0.5\times(-0.5) = -0.25\), which is exactly why the "0% average" and the actual "−25% outcome" differ by 25 percentage points.
Q5. Why does Black-Scholes assume prices are lognormally distributed rather than normally distributed?
The model assumes log returns, not price levels, are approximately normal — a reasonable assumption if each log return is the sum of many small, roughly independent shocks (the Central Limit Theorem argument). Because log returns add across time (\(r_{0\to T}=\sum r_t\)), the price level itself is \(P_T = P_0 e^{\sum r_t}\) — an exponential of a (approximately) normal quantity, which is the definition of a lognormal distribution. A normal assumption directly on price levels would allow negative prices, which a lognormal assumption rules out by construction, matching the reality that prices (and FX rates) can't go below zero.
