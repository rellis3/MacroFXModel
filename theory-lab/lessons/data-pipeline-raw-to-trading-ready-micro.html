The Data Pipeline, From Raw Feed to Trading-Ready

A raw vendor feed and a backtest-ready dataset are not the same object. Between them sits a chain of stages — capture, validation, cleaning, normalization, bar construction, storage — and one particular choice buried inside that chain, how raw ticks get grouped into the bars a strategy actually sees, quietly decides whether the returns a model consumes behave anything like the well-mixed, independent draws most statistics secretly assumes.

Section 1

A raw feed is groceries, not a plated meal

Nobody eats straight off the delivery truck — and no strategy trades straight off the raw tick feed.

A raw market data feed is closer to a delivery of unwashed vegetables and uncut meat than to a finished meal. Between "raw ingredients arrive" and "plated dish goes to the table," a real kitchen runs a sequence of stations — and skipping one doesn't make the meal arrive faster. It makes a customer sick two stages later, usually somewhere much harder to trace back to the missed step.

Every lesson elsewhere in this Theory Lab that talks about a feature, a signal, or a backtest quietly assumes something upstream: that the price series feeding it is complete, correctly timestamped, free of corporate-action jumps, and dated to when the information was actually knowable. Get this layer wrong and no amount of discipline downstream rescues the result — a leak, a gap, or a stale value baked into the raw series propagates into every feature and every backtest built on top of it, silently.

What this deck is, and isn't

This is the map of the whole pipeline, condensed into slides. Cleaning, normalization, storage, and point-in-time serving are each large enough to need their own dedicated lesson later in this category — this deck gives you the shape of the whole journey and lingers longest on one specific, foundational choice: how raw ticks become bars.

Section 2

Six stages between raw and trading-ready

Each stage closes a specific way the earlier ones can still leave the data unfit to build a strategy on.

📡

1 · Ingestion

Capture raw ticks, quotes, and trades exactly as transmitted. Nothing gets fixed here — fixing anything before it's captured destroys the ground truth a later step needs to be checked against.

✓

2 · Validation

Schema checks, gap detection, sanity bounds. Validation flags what looks wrong — it never fixes anything itself.

🧼

3 · Cleaning

Bad ticks, stale quotes, halts, duplicates, and missing values get fixed here, one defensible method per failure mode — never a blanket rule.

🔗

4 · Normalization

Corporate actions and contract rolls each put a fake, non-tradeable jump in a raw series. Normalization turns that into one continuous, tradeable-return series.

🗄

5 · Storage

A single, versioned source of truth for the cleaned, normalized series — not five teams independently recomputing "the adjusted close" and slowly drifting apart.

🕰

6 · Point-in-time serving

Answers "as of date X" using only what was genuinely knowable on date X — no later revision, no restatement, no survivorship creeping in.

Only data that has passed all six stages is fit to build a feature on, run a backtest against, or drive a live signal from. A dataset that merely "looks clean" hasn't necessarily passed any of them.

Section 4

Before any of that: how do ticks become bars?

A "bar" is one open/high/low/close snapshot. The question is what triggers the shutter to click.

🕒

Time bars

Close a bar every fixed wall-clock interval — every minute, every hour — regardless of how much trading happened inside it. The default almost everyone starts with.

🔢

Tick bars

Close a bar after a fixed number of individual trade prints, however long or short that takes in wall-clock time.

📦

Volume bars

Close a bar once a fixed number of shares/contracts/units has traded, so every bar represents a constant amount of activity.

💵

Dollar bars

Close a bar once a fixed notional value (price × volume) has traded — stays comparable even as an instrument's price level drifts over years.

These four standard sampling clocks come from Marcos López de Prado's Advances in Financial Machine Learning (Wiley, 2018), Chapter 2. Bar construction is a choice made inside ingestion, before validation or cleaning ever touches the data — and it's the choice this deck spends the rest of its time on.

Section 5

Same 14 ticks, three different sampling clocks

A quiet 5 minutes, then a short burst of activity, then quiet again — a realistic 10-minute slice of an FX pair. Watch where each clock draws its bar boundaries.

Try it
Fixed clock, wildly uneven load. The 2-minute bar covering the burst swallows 7 of the 14 ticks and roughly 81% of the window's total volume — the exact same bar width as four other bars holding just one or two ticks each.
Fixed activity, uneven clock — on purpose. Bar 1 needed 270 seconds of quiet trading to fill its quota. Bars 2, 3 and 4 fill the exact same quota in about 40 seconds combined, because that's how long the burst takes to move the same amount of volume. Every completed bar represents a comparable slice of real turnover.
Same shape here, different reason. Within one session where price hardly moves, dollar bars and volume bars land at nearly the same boundaries — dollar's advantage over volume shows up across years, once an instrument's price level has drifted, not within a single ten-minute window like this one.
Section 6

The default is the weak one

Trading activity is not smooth across a session — it clusters around the open, the close, and news releases, and thins out in between. A fixed wall-clock interval doesn't know this: it samples the same way regardless. A time bar during a quiet stretch is built from a handful of ticks (oversampling a period with little genuine information turnover), while a time bar during a burst is built from a huge pile of ticks compressed into the same fixed window (undersampling a period where a great deal actually happened).

Volume bars and dollar bars sample by activity itself rather than by the clock, so each bar represents a comparable amount of genuine information turnover — which is why returns built from volume/dollar bars sit closer to independent and identically distributed, and closer to Gaussian, than returns built from time bars sampled at a comparable average frequency (López de Prado, AFML, Ch. 2). Many of the parametric statistical tools used elsewhere in this Theory Lab quietly assume something close to that — feeding them time-bar returns hands them a worse-behaved input than the same underlying data would have produced sampled a different way.
The trap

Time bars are not "wrong," and most of the industry still runs on them for good practical reasons — simplicity, universal tooling, human-readable intervals. But choosing them without knowing why volume/dollar bars exist means silently accepting a statistically worse-behaved return series, every single day, for every instrument sampled this way.

Section 8

One hour, one news release, two very different bar counts

A liquid FX pair, one trading hour with a scheduled data release 20 minutes in: ~200 ticks in the 15 minutes before, a burst of ~4,000 ticks in the 5 minutes around it, ~300 ticks in the remaining 40 minutes.

Time bars — 1-minute clock

Exactly 60 bars for the hour, no matter what. The 5 minutes around the release get exactly 5 bars — the same count as any other 5 quiet minutes — even though those 5 minutes carried roughly 4,000 of the hour's ~4,500 total ticks: about 89% of the hour's information content, squeezed into just 8% of its bars.

Volume bars — sized for ~60 bars/hour

The pre-release lull (200 ticks/15min) closes only 2–3 bars. The release burst (4,000 ticks/5min) closes roughly 40–45 bars on its own — the sampling clock speeds up exactly when genuine activity speeds up, so each bar keeps representing a comparable slice of real information turnover.

The time-bar version has quietly under-represented the single most informative five minutes of the hour, and over-represented the quiet stretches either side of it — the same oversampling/undersampling pattern from Section 6, made concrete with actual tick counts.

Section 10

Where this quietly breaks down in practice

📈

"I have a chart" ≠ "I have trading-ready data"

A chart renderer silently interpolates over gaps and rarely flags a stale quote — a series that looks fine on a chart can still fail every stage of this pipeline.

🚫

Fixing at ingestion destroys the audit trail

Correcting a suspicious tick before it's durably stored removes the original record needed to audit that correction later. Capture first, fix in a separate, logged stage.

⚖

Popularity isn't statistical soundness

Time bars being the default doesn't mean they're statistically neutral — the oversampling/undersampling effect is documented, not a matter of opinion.

🔁

One bar scheme doesn't fit every study forever

A scheme picked for a slow macro strategy may be a poor fit for a short-horizon microstructure study built later on the same infrastructure.

Section 11

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

What is the correct order of the six pipeline stages?

Each stage closes a specific gap left by the ones before it — capture first, then check, then fix, then adjust for corporate actions, then store, then serve only what was knowable as of a given date.
Question 2

Why is fixing a suspicious tick during ingestion a mistake, even if the fix is correct?

Ingestion's only job is durable capture. Correcting a record before that capture happens removes the ground truth a later validation or cleaning step could be audited against.
Question 3

Which sampling clock closes a bar once a fixed notional value (price × volume) has traded?

Volume bars hold a fixed quantity traded fixed; dollar bars hold a fixed traded value fixed, which stays comparable even as a price level drifts over years.
Question 4

Why do time bars produce statistically worse-behaved returns than volume or dollar bars?

A fixed wall-clock interval samples at the same rate regardless of real activity. Volume/dollar bars speed up exactly when genuine activity speeds up, keeping each bar's information content comparable — which pushes their returns closer to IID and Gaussian.
Question 5

In the worked example, roughly what share of the hour's ticks landed inside just 5 of its 60 one-minute time bars?

Roughly 4,000 of the hour's ~4,500 total ticks arrived during the 5-minute release burst — about 89% of the hour's activity, compressed into 5 of its 60 fixed bars.
Section 12

What to carry forward

01

Raw ≠ trading-ready — a tick passes through capture, validation, cleaning, normalization, bar construction, and storage before anything gets modeled on it.

02

Ingestion captures; it doesn't correct — fixing before durable capture destroys the audit trail a later fix needs to be checked against.

03

Bar construction is a foundational, separate choice inside ingestion — time, tick, volume, and dollar bars are the four standard sampling clocks (López de Prado, AFML, Ch. 2).

04

Time bars are the default — and the statistically weakest — because trading activity clusters unevenly across a session, and a fixed clock can't tell a quiet minute from a frantic one.

05

Volume and dollar bars sample by activity, not the clock, so each bar keeps representing a comparable slice of genuine turnover — pushing returns closer to IID and Gaussian.

06

This is the map, not the destination — cleaning, normalization, storage, and point-in-time serving each get their own dedicated lesson next in this category.

What this now lets you do

Recognize that "I have a chart" isn't the same claim as "I have trading-ready data." Ask which sampling clock a return series was built on before trusting its statistical behavior in a model that assumes IID, roughly-normal returns. Know which of the six pipeline stages a data problem actually belongs to, instead of patching it at the wrong one.
