Factor Models 4 of 5

Arbitrage Pricing Theory

CAPM derives its one factor from an equation about how every investor behaves. Fama-French hand-picks specific factors because they empirically work. APT does neither: one much weaker assumption — no arbitrage — is enough to force expected returns into an approximately linear function of exposures to some small number of pervasive risk factors, without ever saying which factors, or how many.

CONCEPT — this repo builds real macro-surprise indices and a real multi-factor regression engine, but never joins them the way Chen-Roll-Ross does ~18 min read Prereqs: Fama-MacBeth · The Taylor Rule · Central Bank Policy (for the macro-surprise framing)
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • Arbitrage Pricing Theory (Ross, 1976) is derived from a no-arbitrage argument alone — far weaker than CAPM's full-market-equilibrium assumption — and concludes that expected returns must be approximately linear in exposures to some small number of pervasive risk factors: \(E[R_i] \approx R_f + \sum_k \beta_{ik}\lambda_k\).
  • APT is a framework, not a fitted model: unlike CAPM (one factor, derived) or Fama-French (specific named factors, chosen empirically), APT makes no claim about which factors or how many — you plug in a concrete choice of factors before it becomes a testable, specific model.
  • The argument: if a diversified portfolio's idiosyncratic risk has been averaged away, two diversified portfolios with identical factor exposures but different expected returns let you build a zero-investment, zero-risk portfolio with a nonzero expected return — a free lunch competitive trading competes away.
  • Chen, Roll & Ross (1986) gave APT its most useful concrete form: observable macroeconomic surprises — unexpected industrial production growth, unexpected inflation, unexpected changes in the term spread and the default spread, and a market-return residual — as the priced factors. Only the surprise carries a risk premium; the anticipated component is already in the price, exactly the logic this curriculum's Central Bank Policy lesson uses for rate decisions.
  • Worked example below: a 2-factor toy model (an industrial-production-surprise factor and an inflation-surprise factor) prices three toy assets at 6.75%, 5.85%, and 7.50% — arithmetic you can rerun by hand or in the live calculator.
  • APT's generality is also its weakness: because it doesn't specify the factors, there is no single, universally agreed "the APT model" the way there is a specific, standard Fama-French three-factor model.
Section 01

Why this matters — three completely different ways to get a factor model

This is the fourth lesson in a five-lesson sequence on factor models, and it sits in a deliberately specific spot. CAPM derived a single factor — the market portfolio — from an equilibrium argument about how every investor in the economy behaves: if everyone is mean-variance optimizing and markets clear, the market portfolio is the only priced factor, full stop, no choices left to make. Fama-French / Carhart throws that derivation out entirely and goes empirical instead: sort stocks by size, by book-to-market, by momentum; keep whichever sorts earn a persistent excess average return; call the winners factors. Three (or four) specific, named, fixed-in-advance factors, chosen because they work, not because a theorem says they must exist.

Arbitrage Pricing Theory does neither of those things, and that is the entire point of this lesson. Ross (1976) starts from an assumption dramatically weaker than "every investor behaves like a mean-variance optimizer holding the market portfolio" — he assumes only that a well-diversified, zero-cost, zero-risk portfolio cannot have a nonzero expected return, because if it did, competitive arbitrage trading would immediately exploit it and compete the mispricing away. From that single assumption alone — no utility functions, no market-clearing condition, no claim about what any individual investor holds — he derives that expected returns must be approximately linear in exposures to some small number of pervasive, systematic risk factors:

\[ E[R_i] \approx R_f + \sum_{k=1}^{K} \beta_{ik}\,\lambda_k \]

Read that equation carefully and notice what it does not say. It does not say what \(K\) is. It does not say what the factors \(F_k\) actually are — macro variables, style portfolios, statistical principal components, anything satisfying the underlying assumptions would do. CAPM is a specific, one-factor theorem. Fama-French is a specific, three-factor empirical model. APT is neither specific nor a single model — it is a framework that becomes a specific, testable model only once someone plugs in a concrete choice of factors. Plug in the market portfolio alone and APT nearly degenerates back to something CAPM-like; plug in the Fama-French factors and APT rationalizes them from a different, weaker set of assumptions; plug in macroeconomic surprises, as Chen, Roll & Ross did in the lesson's most useful concrete example below, and you get a genuinely different, macro-flavored factor model. All three are "an APT model" — which is exactly the generality this lesson has to be honest about from the first paragraph.

CAPM — one factor, derived from equilibrium

Assumes every investor is mean-variance optimizing and, in equilibrium, holds some combination of the risk-free asset and the single market portfolio. That strong, economy-wide assumption is exactly what pins down one factor (market excess return) and one price of risk for it — no choice left for the researcher to make.

Fama-French/Carhart — specific factors, chosen empirically

No equilibrium derivation at all. Sort stocks by size, value, momentum; keep whichever sorted long-short portfolios earn a persistent excess average return; adopt them as factors. Specific and fixed in advance — three or four named factors, the same ones every time a paper says "Fama-French."

APT — no factors specified, derived from no-arbitrage alone

A far weaker assumption than full equilibrium: only that riskless, zero-cost arbitrage opportunities get competed away. Proves returns must be approximately linear in exposures to some small number of pervasive factors — but is silent on which ones or how many. A theorem about the shape of the pricing relationship, not a fitted model, until factors are chosen.

💡
Plain-English intuition

If two boxes hold the same risk but different price tags, buy the cheap one and sell the other

Imagine two vending machines, far apart, both selling the exact same candy bar. If one charges $1 and the other charges $1.50, you don't need a theory of vending-machine consumer behavior to know something is wrong — you just buy from the cheap machine and, if you could, resell at the expensive one, pocketing 50 cents with zero risk. That trade is so obviously profitable that if it were actually available at scale, people would do it until the prices converged. The "law of one price" is just this observation, stated generally: two things that are identical in every risk-relevant way cannot persistently trade at different (expected) prices, because the trade that exploits the gap has no downside.

APT applies exactly this logic to whole diversified portfolios instead of candy bars. Suppose two big, well-diversified portfolios happen to have identical exposure to every systematic risk factor that matters — same sensitivity to growth surprises, same sensitivity to inflation surprises, same everything — but one is priced to return 8% and the other only 6%. Buy the 8% portfolio, short the 6% one, in equal dollar amounts. The systematic risk exposures are identical, so they cancel exactly; the position is diversified, so the leftover asset-specific noise has already been averaged away. What's left is a zero-cost, (in the limit) zero-risk portfolio with a certain 2% profit — free money. That is not a state of the world competitive markets tolerate for long, and the trading that closes the gap is the entire mechanism behind the APT pricing equation.

Section 02

The math, step by step — from a factor structure to a pricing equation

Start from an assumption about how returns are generated, not priced — this part is purely descriptive. Suppose the return on any asset \(i\) can be written as a linear function of \(K\) common, pervasive factors plus an asset-specific residual:

\[ R_i = E[R_i] + \sum_{k=1}^{K} \beta_{ik} F_k + \varepsilon_i \]
E[R_i]asset i's expected return — what the pricing equation below solves for
β_ik F_kasset i's exposure to common factor k, times that factor's realized surprise
ε_iasset-specific noise — mean zero, and (crucially) uncorrelated across different assets

Each \(F_k\) is itself mean-zero — it represents the factor's surprise, the deviation of the realized factor from what was already expected, not the factor's raw level (this distinction becomes the entire point of Section 03). \(\varepsilon_i\) is the part of asset \(i\)'s return that has nothing to do with any common factor — a firm-specific earnings miss, a local supply disruption, pure noise. The one assumption doing the real work is that these \(\varepsilon_i\) terms are uncorrelated (or only weakly correlated) across different assets — which is exactly what makes the next step possible.

Step 1 — diversification erases the idiosyncratic term

Form a portfolio of \(N\) assets with weights \(w_i \approx 1/N\) each. Its return is:

\[ R_P = \sum_i w_i R_i = \sum_i w_i E[R_i] + \sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(\sum_i w_i \beta_{ik}\right) F_k + \sum_i w_i \varepsilon_i \]

The last term, \(\sum_i w_i \varepsilon_i\), is a weighted average of \(N\) uncorrelated, mean-zero residuals with weights shrinking like \(1/N\) each. Its variance shrinks like \(1/N\) as well, and as \(N\) grows large, this idiosyncratic term vanishes — exactly the same diversification mechanic covered in Decision Trees & Random Forests' random forest averaging, applied here to portfolio construction instead of tree predictions. What survives in a large, well-diversified portfolio is only the systematic part:

\[ R_P \approx E[R_P] + \sum_{k=1}^{K} \beta_{Pk} F_k \]

where \(\beta_{Pk} = \sum_i w_i \beta_{ik}\) is the portfolio's net exposure to factor \(k\).

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

If you flip one coin, the result is genuinely unpredictable. If you flip a thousand coins and look at the average number of heads, that average is extremely close to 50% almost every time — the individual randomness of each coin washes out in the crowd. A single asset's return has real asset-specific randomness baked in; a big, spread-out portfolio's return mostly doesn't, because each asset's own private noise is a different, unrelated coin flip, and averaging a thousand unrelated coin flips leaves you with something much more predictable than any one of them.

Step 2 — two identically-exposed diversified portfolios can't have different expected returns

Now suppose two large, well-diversified portfolios \(P\) and \(Q\) happen to share exactly the same factor exposures — \(\beta_{Pk} = \beta_{Qk}\) for every factor \(k\) — but different expected returns, \(E[R_P] \neq E[R_Q]\). Build a third portfolio: long $1 of \(P\), short $1 of \(Q\). Its properties are stark:

1

Zero net investment

The long and short legs are equal dollar amounts — this costs nothing to put on.

2

Zero net factor exposure

Because \(\beta_{Pk} = \beta_{Qk}\) for every \(k\), the long leg's exposure to each factor exactly cancels the short leg's exposure to that same factor.

3

Zero idiosyncratic risk

Both \(P\) and \(Q\) are already diversified — their own leftover asset-specific noise has already been averaged away by Step 1.

4

Nonzero expected return

The combined position earns \(E[R_P] - E[R_Q] \neq 0\) — with essentially none of the three ingredients (capital, factor risk, idiosyncratic risk) that would normally justify a nonzero expected return.

That is a free lunch: a costless, riskless position with a positive expected payoff. Competitive arbitrageurs would buy \(P\) and sell \(Q\) at essentially unlimited scale until the trade's own buying and selling pressure pushed \(E[R_P]\) down and \(E[R_Q]\) up, closing the gap. Ross's actual theorem is more careful than this three-line sketch — it is a limiting argument over economies with more and more assets, and the resulting pricing relation holds only approximately, with an error bound that shrinks as the number of assets grows, rather than holding as an exact equality the way CAPM's does for a single market portfolio. That asymptotic, "approximately" character is a real, load-bearing caveat, not fine print — it is precisely why the pricing equation below carries an \(\approx\) rather than an \(=\).

\[ E[R_i] \approx R_f + \sum_{k=1}^{K} \beta_{ik}\,\lambda_k \]
R_fthe risk-free rate — an asset with zero exposure to every factor should earn exactly this
β_ikasset i's estimated sensitivity to factor k — the same loading from the return-generating process above
λ_kfactor k's risk premium — the expected excess return earned per unit of exposure to that factor alone

Each \(\lambda_k\) is exactly analogous to CAPM's single market risk premium, generalized: it is the expected excess return of a portfolio built to have unit exposure to factor \(k\) and precisely zero exposure to every other factor — a "factor-mimicking portfolio." CAPM is the special case \(K=1\) with the market portfolio as the only factor; APT simply never commits to \(K=1\) or to what the factor is.

The no-arbitrage argument, drawn: same factor exposure, different expected return
Both portfolios carry the same factor exposure (β = 1.0), so a long-P / short-Q position is zero-cost and zero-net-exposure — yet it earns the full 2-percentage-point expected return gap with no offsetting risk. That mismatch is exactly what competitive arbitrage trading closes, pushing the two expected returns back toward each other. Illustrative numbers, not fitted to any real pair of portfolios.
Section 03

Chen, Roll & Ross (1986): putting a macro face on the factors

APT as derived above is silent on what \(F_k\) actually is — which is exactly why Chen, Roll & Ross's 1986 paper, "Economic Forces and the Stock Market," is the most useful concrete example for this curriculum. Rather than statistically extracting unlabeled factors or borrowing style-sorted portfolios from Fama-French, they proposed a specific, economically interpretable set of observable macroeconomic surprise variables as the priced factors:

🏭

Unexpected industrial production growth

The surprise component of real output growth — a direct read on unanticipated shifts in the business cycle, the same "how hot is the economy running" question the Taylor Rule's output gap asks, but expressed as a surprise rather than a level.

💵

Unexpected inflation

Actual inflation minus what was already priced in — not the inflation rate itself. An inflation print that lands exactly at consensus carries no new information and should move nothing.

📐

Unexpected change in the term spread

The surprise in long-minus-short government yields — a shift in what the market expects the future path of short rates to look like, beyond what was already anticipated.

⚠️

Unexpected change in the default spread

The surprise in the credit-risk premium — low-grade minus high-grade (or corporate minus government) yields — a read on unanticipated shifts in aggregate default risk and risk appetite.

📊

A market-return residual

Whatever exposure to broad equity-market risk isn't already captured by the four macro surprises above — included so the macro story isn't quietly smuggling in the entire market factor unlabeled.

Why "surprise," and not the raw level, is the right way to define a factor here

This is the single most important conceptual point in this section, and it is not a minor technicality. Markets are forward-looking: by the time a piece of macro data is released, the component of it that was already anticipated has, in an efficient market, already been priced in through whatever mechanism investors use to form expectations — futures curves, consensus surveys, a central bank's own guidance. If a factor entered the pricing equation as a raw level rather than a surprise, an asset's expected return would appear to depend on information the market already knew and had already acted on, which makes no sense in an equation whose whole purpose is to explain compensation for risk investors are still exposed to going forward. Only the unexpected component — the part nobody saw coming, and therefore the part that still represents genuine, un-hedged, un-priced risk at the moment it is realized — is capable of carrying a risk premium.

This is precisely the same logic this curriculum's own Central Bank Policy lesson spends an entire section establishing for interest-rate decisions: "it is the hawkish or dovish surprise relative to what was already priced — not the absolute hawkish/dovish stance itself — that moves a currency." A central bank hiking exactly as much as the market expected moves almost nothing, because the hike was already in the price; a smaller-than-expected hike can weaken a currency even though the bank still tightened. Chen-Roll-Ross applies the identical reasoning to a whole factor model at once: an inflation print, a term-spread move, an industrial-production release — none of it should carry a risk premium to the extent it was already anticipated, and all of it should to the extent it wasn't. The Taylor Rule lesson's own framing of "the gap between implied and actual, not the level" is the same idea again, one layer removed: what actually moves markets and carries a price is always the deviation from what was already known, not the raw number itself.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

If everyone already expects it to rain tomorrow and it rains, nobody's surprised and nobody changes their plans because of it — they'd already packed an umbrella. If everyone expects sunshine and it rains instead, that's the part that actually catches people off guard and makes them scramble. A weather forecast that already says "80% chance of rain" has already done the work of pricing rain in; only the gap between the forecast and what actually happens is news. Macro surprises work the same way for markets — a CPI print exactly matching consensus is a non-event, however high or low the number itself is, because the market already "packed the umbrella" for it.

Section 04

A worked example — a two-factor toy APT model

🧮
Worked example

Pricing three toy assets off two macro-surprise factors

Take a stripped-down, made-up 2-factor version of Chen-Roll-Ross: factor 1 is unexpected industrial production growth (an "IP surprise"), factor 2 is unexpected inflation (an "inflation surprise"). Suppose the risk-free rate is \(R_f = 3\%\), and the market has priced these two factor risk premia:

\(\lambda_{\text{IP}}\)+3% — a positive premium: assets more exposed to positive IP surprises are riskier in the sense of being more procyclical, so investors demand extra expected return for that exposure.
\(\lambda_{\text{INF}}\)−1.5% — a negative premium: assets that do better when inflation surprises to the upside are providing a partial inflation hedge, so investors accept a lower expected return for that exposure.

Three toy assets, with made-up factor loadings estimated (in a real application) from a time-series regression of each asset's own historical returns on the two factor-surprise series:

Asset X\(\beta_{\text{IP}}=1.5\), \(\beta_{\text{INF}}=0.5\) — high growth sensitivity, mild positive inflation-hedge exposure.
Asset Y\(\beta_{\text{IP}}=0.8\), \(\beta_{\text{INF}}=-0.3\) — modest growth sensitivity, and actually hurt by inflation surprises rather than hedged against them.
Asset Z\(\beta_{\text{IP}}=2.0\), \(\beta_{\text{INF}}=1.0\) — the most growth-sensitive and the strongest inflation-hedge exposure of the three.

Plug each asset's loadings into \(E[R_i] \approx R_f + \beta_{i,\text{IP}}\lambda_{\text{IP}} + \beta_{i,\text{INF}}\lambda_{\text{INF}}\):

\[ E[R_X] = 3 + 1.5(3) + 0.5(-1.5) = 3 + 4.5 - 0.75 = 6.75\% \]
\[ E[R_Y] = 3 + 0.8(3) + (-0.3)(-1.5) = 3 + 2.4 + 0.45 = 5.85\% \]
\[ E[R_Z] = 3 + 2.0(3) + 1.0(-1.5) = 3 + 6 - 1.5 = 7.5\% \]

Notice what each term is doing. Asset Z has the highest IP-surprise exposure, so its growth term contributes the most (+6pp) — but its large positive inflation-hedge exposure also costs it the most in the second term (\(-1.5\)pp), since that hedge exposure is priced with a negative premium. Asset Y has the smallest IP exposure and therefore the smallest growth term (+2.4pp), but its negative inflation exposure (it does worse, not better, on inflation surprises) actually turns the second term positive (+0.45pp) rather than costing it — the exact opposite effect from Asset Z's. None of these three numbers is a fudge; each is the linear combination of exposures times prices of risk that the pricing equation says it must be.

Section 05

How practitioners actually use this

In practice, applying APT means making three choices the theory itself refuses to make: which factors, how many, and how to estimate the \(\beta_{ik}\) loadings. A common two-step procedure — the same shape as Fama-MacBeth's second pass, covered in the prior lesson in this series — is to first run a time-series regression of each candidate asset's returns on the chosen factor-surprise series to estimate its \(\beta_{ik}\)s, then use those estimated loadings, cross-sectionally, to back out each \(\lambda_k\) as the price the market is actually paying for a unit of exposure to that factor. Desks that build a macro-factor APT model in the Chen-Roll-Ross spirit are, underneath the label, doing exactly this: constructing standardized surprise series for each candidate macro variable (actual minus consensus, scaled by that series' own historical surprise dispersion — a construction covered from a different angle in this curriculum's macro-surprise-index material), then regressing asset or portfolio returns on all of the surprise series jointly, in one multi-factor regression, to recover each asset's vector of factor loadings at once.

The word "jointly" is doing real work in that sentence. A genuine multi-factor APT regression estimates every \(\beta_{ik}\) at the same time, in a single regression with several factor columns side by side — not one factor's loading estimated in isolation, and not a sequence of separate univariate checks run one factor at a time. Isolating that distinction is exactly what the honest read of this repo's own code below turns on.

Verified against this repo's actual code

Real macro-surprise machinery and a real multi-factor regression engine — never joined the way this lesson describes

A repo-wide search for macro-surprise-based regression of FX returns turns up two genuinely real, but genuinely separate, pieces of machinery — and confirming that they are separate is the entire finding.

The surprise construction is real. js/econSurprise.js builds an honest, standard economic-surprise index: for each release series, it computes \(\text{actual}-\text{consensus}\), standardizes by that series' own historical surprise dispersion (so a payrolls miss and a CPI miss become comparable), and decay-weights by age into a per-currency surprise score — precisely the "surprise, not level" construction this lesson's Section 03 describes. js/macroRegimeFx.js's buildEventStudy then measures how much FX pairs actually move around individual scheduled releases, and its buildRegimeStudy buckets forward FX returns by macro regime (real yields rising/falling, credit spreads widening/tightening, curve inverted/normal) and reports the mean return in each bucket, complete with in-sample/out-of-sample splits and an "unstable" flag when a regime's sign flips between halves.

A genuine multi-factor OLS engine is also real — js/mve/ols.js's olsFit jointly estimates a vector of \(\beta\)s on a matrix of several factor columns at once (its own header calls it "multi-factor Ordinary Least Squares," generalizing a single-driver rolling regression to an arbitrary factor matrix), and server.js uses it for a BEER-lite FX fair-value model built on factors like the real-rate differential, DXY, and the yield curve.

What does not exist anywhere in this codebase is the two joined — a single regression that takes the standardized macro-surprise series js/econSurprise.js already builds and feeds several of them, jointly, as the factor matrix into a multi-factor OLS engine to estimate a vector of FX-return factor loadings, Chen-Roll-Ross style. The regime study buckets returns by macro state and reports means per bucket, one bucket at a time — it does not estimate joint linear loadings on several surprise variables at once. The multi-factor OLS engine runs on macro levels (a real-rate differential, DXY, curve) for fair-value estimation, not on surprise variables for expected-return decomposition. Both halves of this lesson's concrete example are individually real and well-built in this repo; the specific thing the lesson describes — a joint multi-factor regression of returns on macro surprises — is a real but genuinely different thing this codebase has not built.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

APT's generality is also its weakness

There is no single, universally agreed "the APT model." Fama-French has exactly one standard three-factor specification that every paper citing it means the same way. APT has no such anchor: because the theory itself never specifies which factors or how many, different researchers, applying the identical no-arbitrage theorem, routinely choose different macro variables, different numbers of statistical factors, or different estimation windows — and get materially different implied risk premia and different "priced" factors as a result. A paper that says "we test APT" and finds five significant factors, and another that says the same thing and finds three different ones, are not necessarily contradicting each other technically; they may simply have made different modeling choices the theory itself left entirely open.

"Approximately" is not a rounding error. Ross's own proof is an asymptotic, limiting-economy argument — the pricing relation holds increasingly well as the number of assets grows, not exactly for any finite set of real, tradable assets the way CAPM's relation holds exactly (within its own assumptions) for any single market portfolio. Reading a fitted APT model's pricing errors as pure noise, rather than partly an artifact of this approximation, overstates how tightly the theory actually pins things down.

Statistically extracted factors (from PCA, say) are often uninterpretable. One popular way to sidestep "which factors" is to extract statistical factors directly from the return covariance matrix rather than name them — technically valid under APT's own assumptions, but it trades one problem (which economic story explains the factor?) for another (does this factor still mean the same thing next year, once the covariance structure shifts and the extracted components rotate?).

Section 06

Key Takeaways

✓APT is derived from no-arbitrage alone — a far weaker assumption than CAPM's full-market-equilibrium requirement, which is exactly what makes APT more general and less specific at the same time.
✓A framework, not a fitted model. Unlike CAPM (one derived factor) or Fama-French (specific chosen factors), APT says nothing about which factors or how many — you have to plug in a concrete choice before it becomes testable.
✓The mechanism is a free-lunch argument. Two diversified portfolios with identical factor exposures but different expected returns let you build a zero-cost, zero-risk position with a nonzero expected return — competitive trading closes that gap.
✓Chen-Roll-Ross (1986) gave APT a genuinely useful macro face: unexpected industrial production, unexpected inflation, term-spread and default-spread surprises, and a market residual.
⚠Only the surprise carries a risk premium — never the level. An anticipated change is already priced in; the same logic this curriculum's Central Bank Policy and Taylor Rule lessons use for rate decisions.
✗There is no single, standard "the APT model." Because the theory never specifies the factors, different researchers routinely get different answers — treat any specific APT specification as one modeling choice among many, not a settled consensus.
Section 07

Self-test

Q1. What is the single key assumption APT is derived from, and how does it compare in strength to CAPM's assumption?
APT is derived from a no-arbitrage argument: a well-diversified, zero-cost, zero-risk portfolio cannot have a nonzero expected return, because competitive trading would immediately exploit it. This is a much weaker assumption than CAPM's, which requires every investor in the economy to be mean-variance optimizing and, in equilibrium, to hold some combination of the risk-free asset and the single market portfolio. APT needs nothing about how any individual investor behaves — only that riskless arbitrage opportunities get competed away.
Q2. Why is APT described as "a framework, not a fitted model" in this lesson?
Because the theory itself makes no claim about which factors belong in \(\sum_k \beta_{ik}\lambda_k\) or how many there should be (\(K\)) — it only proves that expected returns must be approximately linear in exposures to some small number of pervasive factors. It becomes a specific, testable model only once a researcher plugs in a concrete choice of factors (macro surprises, style-sorted portfolios, statistical principal components, or anything else satisfying the underlying assumptions).
Q3. Two large, well-diversified portfolios P and Q have identical exposure to every priced factor, but E[R_P] = 9% and E[R_Q] = 7%. Walk through why this cannot persist under APT's no-arbitrage assumption.
Build a position that is long $1 of P and short $1 of Q. It costs nothing (zero net investment), and because the two portfolios' factor exposures are identical, the long and short legs' exposures to every factor cancel exactly (zero net factor exposure). Both portfolios are already diversified, so their leftover asset-specific noise has already been averaged away (zero idiosyncratic risk). Yet the position earns \(E[R_P]-E[R_Q]=2\%\) — a costless, riskless position with a positive expected payoff. Competitive arbitrage trading (buying P, selling Q) would exploit this until the buying and selling pressure pushed the two expected returns back together.
Q4. Using this lesson's worked-example numbers (R_f = 3%, λ_IP = 3%, λ_INF = −1.5%), what is the APT-implied expected return of an asset with β_IP = 1.0 and β_INF = 0? Show the arithmetic.
\(E[R_i] = R_f + \beta_{\text{IP}}\lambda_{\text{IP}} + \beta_{\text{INF}}\lambda_{\text{INF}} = 3 + 1.0(3) + 0(-1.5) = 3+3+0 = 6\%\). With zero exposure to the inflation-surprise factor, only the IP-surprise term contributes anything beyond the risk-free rate — exactly what the equation says should happen when a loading is zero.
Q5. Why does Chen, Roll & Ross's framework use the SURPRISE component of inflation and industrial production, rather than their raw levels, as the priced factors?
Markets are forward-looking, and the anticipated component of any macro release has already been priced in by the time it's published. Only the unexpected component — the deviation from what was already forecast — represents genuine, still-unresolved risk at the moment it's realized, and only that component is capable of carrying a risk premium. This is the identical logic this curriculum's Central Bank Policy lesson uses for rate decisions: it's the hawkish or dovish surprise relative to what was already priced, not the absolute stance, that actually moves markets.
Q6. Why is it misleading to say "this repo runs a Chen-Roll-Ross-style APT regression" just because it has both a macro-surprise index and a multi-factor OLS engine?
Because the two pieces are real but never joined the way a genuine macro-factor APT test requires. The repo's surprise construction (js/econSurprise.js) and its regime/event studies (js/macroRegimeFx.js) analyze FX returns conditional on macro surprises one event or regime bucket at a time, reporting mean returns per bucket — not a joint linear regression estimating several factor loadings simultaneously. The repo's genuine multi-factor OLS engine (js/mve/ols.js) does estimate several loadings jointly, but on macro levels (real-rate differential, DXY, curve) for FX fair-value estimation, not on standardized macro surprises for expected-return decomposition. Having both ingredients separately is not the same claim as having built the specific joint regression this lesson describes.
