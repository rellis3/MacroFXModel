Swap Curves & OIS Discounting, Underneath the Fixed-for-Floating Trade

A government bond's yield carries the idiosyncratic scars of whoever happens to be issuing and repo-ing that specific bond. A swap has none of that baggage — it's a pure, unbundled bet on interest rates, which is exactly why swap curves, and increasingly OIS curves, quietly replaced government bond curves as the market's working definition of "risk-free," and why the 2008 crisis forced the industry to split one curve into two.

Section 1

A swap trades fixed for floating, nothing else

Two parties agree to exchange interest-rate cashflows on a notional amount. The notional itself never moves.

The mechanics
In practice only the net of the two legs is ever paid, once a period — nobody hands over the full fixed amount and the full floating amount separately.

Nobody's actual loan changes hands, and nobody has to buy or sell a bond. A company that borrowed floating and wants payment certainty can pay fixed on a swap instead of refinancing its real loan; a company that borrowed fixed and thinks rates are falling can pay floating instead. It's a pure side bet on the path of interest rates, stripped of every complication a real bond carries.

Section 2

Why each side is willing to do this

Pays fixed, wants certainty

Already exposed to a floating-rate liability (or wants exposure to falling rates) and would rather know exactly what next year's payment is. Entering the swap converts an uncertain floating obligation into a known fixed one, with no paperwork on the underlying loan itself.

Pays floating, wants optionality

Already locked into a fixed obligation but believes rates are heading down, or simply wants floating exposure without holding a floating-rate instrument. Entering the swap converts the fixed obligation into a floating one that moves with the market.

Because the notional is never at risk — only the net cashflow difference — a swap can grow to an enormous notional size without carrying anywhere near the counterparty risk of holding a bond of the same face amount. That's part of why the swap market is one of the largest derivatives markets in the world.

Section 4

The swap rate is a par rate — bootstrapped like a bond curve

The quoted swap rate at each maturity is the fixed rate K that makes that swap worth exactly zero today. Build a curve out of those rates the same way a bond curve gets built out of bond prices.

1

Start at the shortest maturity

The shortest-dated swap rate pins down the first discount factor directly — nothing else feeds into it yet.

2

Solve one new maturity at a time

The next swap rate, combined with the discount factors already solved, pins down exactly one new discount factor further out the curve.

3

Repeat out to the longest quoted tenor

Each additional liquid swap quote (2Y, 5Y, 10Y, 30Y…) extends the discount curve one more segment, exactly as bootstrapping a bond curve extends it one bond at a time.

4

The result: a full discount curve, built from swap quotes

No single bond's idiosyncratic richness or cheapness is baked into any point on it — every point comes from a liquid, standardized swap rate instead.

This is the same bootstrapping idea from Fixed Income 3 (bootstrapping a bond curve) and Fixed Income 4 (fitting Nelson-Siegel/Svensson to it) — just applied to swap rates instead of bond prices as the raw input.
Section 5

Why swap curves replaced government bond curves

A government bond's yield is never purely about the time value of money — a swap curve strips that noise out.

Illustrative shape
Swap curve — a smooth, pure read on rate expectations. Government curve — the same broad shape, but wiggled by which specific bond happens to be scarce, cheap-to-borrow, or newly issued that month.

A swap has nothing to issue, own, borrow, or repo — so the swap rate is a cleaner, more direct read on "what the market thinks rates will average out to" than any single bond's yield. That's why trading desks gradually adopted the swap curve, rather than the government bond curve, as their working definition of the risk-free discounting curve.

Section 7

Why OIS, not LIBOR: the discounting revolution

A curve's job when discounting isn't complete unless it reflects the actual cost of the collateral changing hands.

LIBOR — unsecured, embeds bank credit risk
  • An interbank unsecured lending rate — a self-reported estimate, not always a real transaction
  • Carries the credit risk of lending to a bank with no collateral
  • That spread over "true" risk-free is normally a few basis points — until banks stop trusting each other
OIS / SOFR — secured, overnight, near risk-free
  • References an overnight rate compounded over the period
  • SOFR is collateralized by Treasury repo — secured, not a bank's word
  • Carries essentially none of LIBOR's embedded bank credit risk

Through 2007–2008, the gap between LIBOR and OIS — the "LIBOR-OIS spread," normally a handful of basis points — widened dramatically as banks grew reluctant to lend to each other unsecured at any price they trusted, briefly exceeding 300 basis points around Lehman's collapse. That episode made concrete what had been a modeling footnote: discounting a fully collateralized derivative at LIBOR was quietly baking a bank credit-risk premium into a trade that, in practice, carried almost none. The fix — the OIS discounting revolution — was to discount collateralized derivatives at the rate actually earned on posted cash collateral: an OIS-family rate, not LIBOR.

Where this needs qualifying

Even OIS isn't literally risk-free — it's a practical approximation. Different collateral currencies imply different effective discount rates for the same cashflow (cross-currency basis), and OIS rates in different jurisdictions still embed that market's own repo and collateral dynamics.

Pre-2008, one curve did double duty: LIBOR both discounted cashflows to present value and forecast the floating leg's future resets — a convenient coincidence that held only because trades were largely uncollateralized, so LIBOR was a reasonable stand-in for the discount rate too.
OIS / SOFR

Today, two separate curves do two separate jobs: an OIS curve discounts every cashflow to present value (matching the rate actually earned on posted collateral), while a distinct SOFR-derived forward curve — bootstrapped from SOFR futures and swaps — projects the floating leg's future resets. They're built from different instruments and no longer move in lockstep.

That split is what the industry calls the multi-curve framework. It only exists because the single-curve shortcut broke: once collateralized trades were discounted at OIS specifically, discounting and forecasting stopped being the same job answered by the same number.

Section 9

The LIBOR-to-SOFR transition

Two separate problems converged on LIBOR — a discounting flaw, and a benchmark that could be gamed.

⚠

2008 — the discounting problem

The crisis exposed that LIBOR embeds bank credit risk — exactly the wrong thing to bake into the discount rate for a fully collateralized derivative.

🕵

2012 — the manipulation scandal

Panel banks were found manipulating their self-reported LIBOR submissions. LIBOR was an estimate with too little real transaction volume behind it to police reliably — regulators concluded it needed replacing, not patching.

🏦

2017–2018 — SOFR is chosen and published

The Fed-convened Alternative Reference Rates Committee selected SOFR — derived from actual, large-volume overnight Treasury repo transactions — as LIBOR's replacement. The New York Fed began publishing it in April 2018.

📅

2021–2023 — the switchover

New swaps and loans gradually moved to SOFR through the early 2020s; most LIBOR panel rates stopped publishing at end-2021; the last USD tenors ceased entirely on 30 June 2023.

For a rates desk, this meant years of running two curve worlds in parallel — legacy LIBOR trades on the old curve until they matured or converted, new trades on SOFR — on top of the OIS-vs-forecast-curve split already covered.
Section 10

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Why is no notional exchanged in an interest rate swap?

The notional is a reference amount used to size each leg's cashflow — it's never lent, borrowed, or repaid. Only the net difference changes hands each period.
Question 2

What makes a swap rate the "par rate"?

Par means neither side pays anything up front — the fixed leg's present value exactly equals the floating leg's present value at that specific K.
Question 3

Why did the industry move from LIBOR discounting to OIS discounting for collateralized derivatives?

A fully collateralized trade carries close to no counterparty credit risk. Discounting it at LIBOR was quietly baking in a bank credit-risk premium that didn't belong there — OIS/SOFR matches the trade's actual risk far more accurately.
Question 4

What replaced LIBOR as the standard USD reference rate, and why?

SOFR is secured, overnight, and transaction-based — it can't be gamed the way a panel of banks self-reporting an estimate could be, which is exactly what the 2012 manipulation scandal exposed about LIBOR.
Question 5

On the slider slide, when the floating rate observed rises above the fixed rate K, who receives the net cashflow?

Net to the fixed payer is N × (floating − fixed). When floating exceeds fixed, that's positive — cash flows to the fixed-rate payer.
Section 11

What to carry forward

01

A swap exchanges fixed for floating cashflows on a notional that's never itself exchanged — only the net difference changes hands each period.

02

The swap rate is the par rate — the fixed K that zeroes out the swap's value at inception — and a swap curve is bootstrapped from swap rates tenor by tenor, the same way a bond curve is bootstrapped from bond prices.

03

Swap curves replaced government bond curves as the standard "risk-free" benchmark because a swap has no issuance, repo, or scarcity noise the way a specific bond does.

04

Post-2008, OIS replaced LIBOR for discounting collateralized derivatives, because LIBOR embeds bank credit risk that a secured, overnight rate essentially doesn't.

05

The multi-curve framework splits discounting (OIS) from forecasting floating resets (a separate SOFR-forward curve) — pre-2008 these were, in effect, one curve.

06

LIBOR was retired for two separate reasons — its embedded credit risk, and a 2012 manipulation scandal — and replaced by SOFR, a transaction-based overnight rate, through a multi-year transition ending in mid-2023.

What this now lets you do

Read a swap curve as the market's cleanest available signal on where rates are heading, unpolluted by any single bond's issuance or repo quirks. Know why a collateralized derivative's cashflows get discounted at OIS/SOFR rather than a bank-quoted rate, and why that distinction only started to matter in 2008. Recognise, on sight, why an old contract still referencing LIBOR needed a fallback clause once LIBOR actually stopped publishing.
