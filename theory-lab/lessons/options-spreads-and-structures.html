Option Spreads & Structures

Verticals, straddles, butterflies, condors, calendars, collars and risk reversals are not new instruments. Each is the same four bricks combined to make a view cheaper, cap the loss, or get paid only for the exact move you expect.

CONCEPT — structures explained at Black-Scholes model prices; not a trade recommendation ~18 min read Prereqs: The Four Option Positions, Same Greeks, Different Risk
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
Risk. Educational, not advice. A long option can lose its whole premium. A short option can lose far more than the premium it collected, and a structure with an uncapped short leg has no limit on its loss. Every price here is a model price at stated inputs, not a quote you could trade.
TL;DR
  • Every spread, straddle, butterfly, condor, calendar, collar and risk reversal is the same four bricks: buy or sell, call or put, at chosen strikes and expiries. Add the legs' payoffs and you have the structure's payoff. Add their Greeks and you have its Greeks.
  • Structures exist for three reasons: to make a view cheaper by selling the part you do not need (a 100/105 bull call spread costs 2.01 where the 100 call alone costs 4.23), to cap the loss, or to be paid only for a precise move.
  • Nothing here is free. At model prices every leg is fair, so a structure changes where you win, not whether you have an edge. A short iron condor on 80/90/110/120 collects 1.49 for a worst case of 8.51: about a 75% model chance of profit, but a reward-to-risk of only 0.17 to 1.
  • A long 100 straddle costs 7.97. Spot must finish more than 7.97 points (about 8.0%) away from 100 in three months to profit. The at-the-money straddle is roughly \(0.8\,S\sigma\sqrt{T}\), the market's one-line "expected move".
  • Greeks add leg by leg. Straddles and strangles are long gamma and vega and pay theta. Condors, butterflies and credit spreads collect theta and are short gamma and vega. Before expiry a structure is worth far less than its expiry diagram: the 95/100/105 butterfly's best case is 4.03, but it is worth 0.38 halfway there with spot unchanged.
  • Real-world episode: in January 1995 a Barings trader sold straddles on the Nikkei 225, a structure with no wings. The Kobe earthquake hit on 17 January. Losses eventually reached £827 million and Barings was insolvent by 26 February.

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

I know what a call and a put are. But then people say iron condor and butterfly and I switch off. Is that a different product?

No, and that is the good news. There are only four bricks: buy a call, sell a call, buy a put, sell a put. A straddle is two of them. A butterfly is three or four. A condor is four. Learn the bricks and the rest is arithmetic.

Arithmetic how?

You add the payoffs. Say the stock is at one hundred, three months out, volatility twenty percent. The one hundred call costs about four point two three. That is your bought brick. Now you also sell the one hundred and five call, and you collect about two point two two. Your net cost is two point zero one.

So the call you sold pays for more than half of the one you bought.

Yes. And what did you give up? Everything above one hundred and five. The best you can make is the five points between the strikes, minus what you paid: two point nine nine. The worst is the two point zero one. You break even at about one hundred and two.

So it is a cheaper call with a ceiling.

Exactly. That is the first reason structures exist: you sell what you do not need in order to pay for what you want. The second is to cap the loss. The third is to shape the bet, for example to be paid only if the stock stays in a range.

The range one sounds safe. The iron condor?

Careful. In the lesson's example it collects about one point four nine and the worst case is about eight and a half. On the model's own numbers it makes money roughly three times in four. But the wins are small and the losses are big, and at fair prices that is simply a fair bet in a different shape. Defined risk is not low risk.

So where is the edge?

Nowhere, by construction. Every leg is fairly priced in the model, so no mix of fair legs is a bargain. A structure changes what you are paid for, not whether you have an edge. And every extra leg pays its own bid-offer spread.

What about before expiry?

That surprises people. The diagram at expiry is the end of the road. A butterfly's best case is about four point oh three, with the stock exactly at the middle strike. Halfway to expiry, with the stock sitting right there, it is worth about thirty-eight cents. Time pays the butterfly, and only at the end.

And the Greeks?

They add up, leg by leg. A straddle is long gamma, long vega, and pays time decay. A condor or a butterfly is the mirror image: it collects time decay and is short gamma and short vega. That is why one wants a big move and the other wants quiet.

Has this actually hurt anyone?

Barings, January 1995. A trader sold straddles on the Nikkei: collect the premium, no wings. The Kobe earthquake hit on the seventeenth and the market moved. Losses eventually reached eight hundred and twenty-seven million pounds. A bought option on each side would have put a ceiling on the loss from that one structure.

One line to take away?

A structure is four bricks added up. Before you trade it, ask what you pay, what you can lose, where you break even, and what you sold to make it cheap.

Section 01

Why this matters

A single option is a blunt tool. A bought call pays for every point of upside, including points you do not expect, and its price has to cover the chance of those points. A sold option collects a small premium for taking a loss that may be far larger. Most of the practical craft of options trading is about shaping a payoff so that you pay for what you believe and carry only the risk you chose.

That shaping is done with combinations of two or more options, often with a position in the underlying. They have names (vertical, straddle, butterfly, condor, calendar, collar, risk reversal), and the names make them sound like separate products. They are not. Each name is a recipe. Once you can read a recipe, you can work out for yourself the cost, the worst case, the break-even points and the Greek signs of any of them, and you can see what each one is really betting on.

This lesson is also the honest place to meet the main trap. Defined risk, "zero cost" and "high probability of profit" all sound safe, and each describes a real feature of a structure. None of them means the structure has an edge. We will keep returning to one question: what did you sell to make this look attractive?

💡
Plain-English intuition

You are not choosing a product. You are choosing a shape.

Think of the four positions from the first lesson as four bricks. A long call is a ramp that rises to the right. A short call is a ramp that falls to the right. A long put and a short put are the same two ramps on the left. Stacking bricks adds their heights at every price, so two ramps pointing in opposite directions cancel into a flat shelf, and a ramp up on the left joined to a ramp up on the right makes a V.

The shelf is the key trick. A bull call spread is a long call (ramp up) whose climb is stopped by a short call at a higher strike (ramp down). Above the higher strike the two cancel and the payoff goes flat. You have bought a capped upside, and the sold call has refunded part of the price. What is refunded is exactly what the market charges for the upside you gave away.

Section 02

The math, step by step

Watch the formula build
Where a structure's profit and loss comes from, one brick at a time
\[ \text{call}_K(S) = \color{#fbbf24}{\max(S-K,\,0)} \]

One brick. A call with strike \(K\) pays nothing below the strike and one for one above it. A put is the mirror image, \(\max(K-S,\,0)\).

\[ \Pi(S) = \color{#fbbf24}{\sum_i q_i}\,\text{leg}_i(S) \]

A structure is bricks with a quantity \(q_i\): plus one to buy, minus one to sell, plus or minus two for a doubled leg. The payoff is the sum.

\[ \pi(S) = \sum_i q_i\,\text{leg}_i(S) \;\color{#fbbf24}{-\,V_0}, \qquad V_0 = \sum_i q_i P_i \]

Profit and loss is the payoff minus what you paid. \(P_i\) is each leg's premium, so \(V_0\) is the net premium: positive is a debit you pay, negative is a credit you receive.

\[ \pi(S) = \max(S-K_1,0) \color{#fbbf24}{- \max(S-K_2,0)} - V_0 \]

Try it on a bull call spread: buy the call at \(K_1\), sell the call at the higher strike \(K_2\).

\[ \pi(S) = \color{#10b981}{\min\!\big(\max(S-K_1,0),\; K_2-K_1\big) - V_0} \]

The two ramps cancel above \(K_2\), so the payoff is capped at the width \(W = K_2-K_1\). Max gain is \(W - V_0\) and max loss is \(V_0\). For the 100/105 spread that is \(5 - 2.01 = 2.99\) and 2.01.

The same bet in different clothes. Put-call parity (\(c-p=S-Ke^{-rT}\), from the first lesson) says a call and a put at the same strike differ by a straight line, which has no curvature. Subtract two such pairs at different strikes and the straight lines cancel to a constant. So a debit call spread and a credit put spread on the same two strikes have payoffs that differ by a constant, and their prices must add up to the discounted width:

\[ D_{\text{call spread}} + C_{\text{put spread}} = W\,e^{-rT} \]

On the 100/105 strikes the call spread costs 2.01 and the matching put spread pays 2.97, which sum to 4.98, the discounted width. The same identity links the long call butterfly (0.97) and the short iron butterfly (4.00): they add to 4.98. Whether you build it from calls or puts is a choice about cash and margin, not about the bet. Likewise a collar is a bull call spread plus cash, because shares plus a put equal a call plus cash:

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Think of four kinds of Lego brick. Two of them make a ramp going up, two make a ramp going down. If you put an up-ramp and a down-ramp together, you can make a flat shelf, a V, or a roof. Every option "strategy" with a fancy name is just one of these builds. The only real question is what the build costs and how far you can fall off it.

Greeks add up too. Delta, gamma, theta and vega are all slopes of the structure's value, and slopes of a sum are the sum of the slopes. So the Greeks of any structure are the quantity-weighted sums of its legs' Greeks:

\[ \Delta=\sum_i q_i\Delta_i,\qquad \Gamma=\sum_i q_i\Gamma_i,\qquad \Theta=\sum_i q_i\Theta_i,\qquad \nu=\sum_i q_i\nu_i \]
Δ, Γprice sensitivity and how fast it changes
Θchange in value per day, all else equal (shown per calendar day)
νchange in value per volatility point (one point = 1% of vol)

A quick way to size an at-the-money straddle. For an at-the-money option with a small rate, the call value is close to \(S\sigma\sqrt{T}/\sqrt{2\pi}\). A straddle is a call plus a put, so:

\[ c + p \;\approx\; \frac{2\,S\sigma\sqrt{T}}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \;\approx\; 0.8\,S\,\sigma\sqrt{T} \]

0.8\(2/\sqrt{2\pi}=0.798\)

So the straddle price is the market's own estimate of how far spot will travel by expiry, which is why traders read it as the "expected move". It is also the break-even distance for the straddle buyer: the buyer needs a bigger move than the market has priced.

Worked example

The 100/105 bull call spread against the plain 100 call

Spot 100, three months, 20% vol. Buy the 100 call at 4.23 and sell the 105 call at 2.22:

\[ V_0 = 4.23 - 2.22 = 2.01 \qquad W = 105-100 = 5 \]
V₀ = 2.01net debit: 47% of the outright call's price
W = 5the most the spread can pay at expiry

From the rules: maximum loss is the debit, 2.01; maximum gain is \(5 - 2.01 =\) 2.99; break-even is \(100 + 2.01 =\) 102.01. The reward-to-risk is 1.49 to 1. The outright call breaks even at 104.23. Here is each position's profit and loss at expiry:

Spot at expiry95100102105110120
Buy the 100 call−4.23−4.23−2.23+0.77+5.77+15.77
100/105 bull call spread−2.01−2.01−0.01+2.99+2.99+2.99

The spread loses less when you are wrong and breaks even about two points sooner. In return it stops paying above 105. If the stock rallies to 120 the plain call makes +15.77 and the spread still makes only +2.99. Which is better depends entirely on how far you think the stock can go, which is the real content of the choice.

Section 03

The structures, one by one

Each card gives the build, the computed extremes at the model inputs, the Greek signature, when it is used, and why it exists. All figures are per share of the underlying, at the model inputs above.

1 · Vertical spread, debit: bull call spread and bear put spread

Buy one option and sell another of the same type further out of the money. You pay to be right, with a ceiling.

Build
Bull call: buy 100 call (4.23), sell 105 call (2.22). Bear put: buy 100 put (3.73), sell 95 put (1.74).
Numbers
Bull call: debit 2.01, max gain 2.99, max loss 2.01, break-even 102.01. Bear put: debit 1.99, max gain 3.01, break-even 98.01.
Greeks
Bull call: Δ +0.191, Γ +0.0027, Θ −0.002 per day, ν +0.013 per vol point. Small, because the legs offset: the lone 100 call has a delta of 0.540.
Used when
You have a direction and a rough target near the short strike, and a plain option feels expensive.
Exists because
The sold option pays for part of the bought one, and the loss is capped at the debit.

2 · Vertical spread, credit: bull put spread and bear call spread

Sell the option nearer the money and buy a cheaper one further out as protection. You are paid to be "not wrong", with a floor on the loss.

Build
Bull put: sell 95 put (1.74), buy 90 put (0.64). Bear call: sell 105 call (2.22), buy 110 call (1.04).
Numbers
Bull put: credit 1.10, max gain 1.10, max loss 3.90, break-even 93.90. Bear call: credit 1.18, max loss 3.82, break-even 106.18.
Greeks
Bull put: Δ +0.146, Γ −0.0126, Θ +0.006, ν −0.063: short gamma and vega, collects theta.
Used when
You expect the price to stay above (below) a level and want to collect premium with a known worst case, often when implied vol is high.
Exists because
It is the defined-risk version of selling a put: the bought 90 put turns "lose down to zero" into "lose at most the width less the credit". If every loser were a maximum loss, you would need to win 78.1% of the time to break even.

3 · Straddle and strangle

Buy (or sell) a call and a put together. A straddle shares one strike; a strangle uses an out-of-the-money call and put.

Build
Long straddle: buy 100 call (4.23) and 100 put (3.73). Long strangle: buy 105 call (2.22) and 95 put (1.74).
Numbers
Straddle: debit 7.97, break-evens 92.03 and 107.97, max loss 7.97 (at 100), gain on the upside without limit. Strangle: debit 3.96, break-evens 91.04 and 108.96. Selling either collects that premium and mirrors every number: the loss is then uncapped on the upside, and on the downside as large as the strike less the premium.
Greeks
Straddle: Δ +0.080, Γ +0.0794, Θ −0.044 per day, ν +0.397 per vol point. Long gamma and vega; pays theta from day one.
Used when
You expect a large move but not its direction (a data release, a decision, a breakout), or, sold, you think the market is overpaying for movement.
Exists because
It isolates movement from direction. You pay for the whole expected move up front, so you need a larger move than the market priced.

4 · Butterfly (and its twin, the iron butterfly)

A bet that price lands near one level. Buy the wings and sell twice the middle. The twin builds the same shape from puts and calls and is paid up front.

Build
Long call butterfly: buy 95 call (7.21), sell two 100 calls (4.23 each), buy 105 call (2.22). Short iron butterfly: sell 100 call and put, buy 95 put and 105 call.
Numbers
Butterfly: debit 0.97, max gain 4.03 at exactly 100, max loss 0.97, break-evens 95.97 and 104.03. Iron butterfly: credit 4.00, max loss 1.00, same shape. Their prices add up to the discounted width.
Greeks
Δ −0.001, Γ −0.0093, Θ +0.005, ν −0.047: short gamma and vega, collects theta, like the middle strike it is built around.
Used when
You have a "pin" view: price should settle near a level at expiry, for example around a heavily traded strike or after an event is priced out.
Exists because
Long odds for little money: 4.03 for 0.97, about 4.14 to 1. The catch is the narrow tent. The model gives about a 31% chance of any profit. Halfway to expiry, with spot at 100, it is worth only 0.38.

5 · Iron condor

Sell a put spread below the market and a call spread above it. A short strangle with a long option beyond each short strike, so the loss is capped.

Build
Sell 90 put (0.64), buy 80 put (0.03), sell 110 call (1.04), buy 120 call (0.17). It is a bull put spread plus a bear call spread.
Numbers
Credit 1.49 (also the max gain), max loss 8.51, break-evens 88.51 and 111.49. Reward to risk 0.17 to 1: the worst case is 5.72 times the best.
Greeks
Δ −0.040, Γ −0.0366, Θ +0.020 per day, ν −0.183 per vol point: collects theta, short gamma and vega.
Used when
You expect a range and want to sell premium with a defined worst case. Wider wings and wider bodies win more often but pay less.
Exists because
It lets you sell volatility without the open-ended risk of a naked short strangle. It still sells insurance: it pays steadily and gives it back in one move.

6 · Calendar spread

Same strike, different expiries. Sell the near option, buy the far one. The only structure here whose legs live to different dates.

Build
Sell 1-month 100 call (2.39), buy 3-month 100 call (4.23).
Numbers
Debit 1.85. At the near expiry with spot at 100 the position is worth about 1.57 if vol is unchanged; break-evens about 96.47 and 104.27. If vol falls 5 points the best case drops to 0.76; if it rises 5 points it grows to 2.38. These depend on the vol assumption.
Greeks
Δ +0.017, Γ −0.0293, Θ +0.016, ν +0.084: long theta and long vega, but short gamma. An unusual mix.
Used when
You expect price to stay near the strike for the near leg's life, or you want to buy the cheaper far-dated vol against rich near-dated vol, for example around an event.
Exists because
Near-dated options decay faster than far-dated ones. It is a bet on the difference, which is why it is a term-structure trade as much as a price trade.

7 · Collar

Own the shares, buy a put below the market, sell a call above it. The sold call helps pay for the put.

Build
Hold 100 shares at 100, buy 95 put (1.74), sell 105 call (2.22).
Numbers
At flat vol the options net a credit of 0.48. Loss is capped at 4.52 (the shares can fall to 95), gain at 5.48 (they can rise to 105), versus holding the shares alone. By parity this is a bull call spread plus cash.
Skew changes the price
Index and equity puts usually cost more vol than calls. Raising the put's vol and lowering the call's (the builder's skew control) shrinks the credit to 0.31 at a skew of −1 vol point per 10 strike points, 0.13 at −2, and flips it to a debit of 0.04 at −3.
Used when
You must keep a holding but want a floor: an executive with concentrated stock, a fund over an event, a corporate with an exposure to hedge.
Exists because
"Zero cost" protection is paid for with the upside you gave away. Hedging with options has its own lesson later in this series.

8 · Risk reversal

Buy an out-of-the-money call and sell an out-of-the-money put (the bullish version). A cheap directional position that behaves like a forward with a gap in the middle.

Build
Buy 105 call (2.22), sell 95 put (1.74).
Numbers
Debit 0.48 at flat vol, break-even 105.48, gain without limit, loss as large as 95.48 if spot went to zero. With downside skew the put is dearer and the debit shrinks: 0.04 (a credit) at −3.
Greeks
Δ +0.619, Γ +0.0039, Θ −0.006, ν +0.020: roughly a forward with a dead zone between the strikes.
Used when
You are directional, want little or no premium out of pocket, and accept the short leg's risk. Dealers also quote it as a package to trade skew itself.
Exists because
It finances one side with the other. In FX the "risk reversal" is quoted as the vol difference between a call and a put of the same delta, which reads the skew directly (FX Options Quoting).
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

A spread is two tickets stapled together: one you buy, one you sell. The one you sell pays for part of the one you buy, but it also means you stop winning past a point. A condor is two spreads stapled together. A butterfly is a spread and a mirror-image spread sharing the middle. Always ask: what did I sell, and what happens to me if that goes wrong?

You are not getting a better deal; you are choosing which outcomes to be paid for. If your estimate of the target is the part you trust least, the cheaper structure is the one that hurts less when that estimate is off.

Section 05

It actually happened: Barings, 1995

A structure with no wings has no limit on what it can lose, and the clearest historical illustration is a short straddle. The facts below are as widely reported; the primary account is the official inquiry's report.

It actually happened

Selling straddles on the Nikkei, with nothing to stop the loss

Barings was a London merchant bank. In January 1995 one of its traders in Singapore, Nick Leeson, was running positions on the Nikkei 225 index. On 16 January 1995 he sold a straddle, that is, both a call and a put at the same strike: in effect a bet that the Japanese market would not move much. That collects a premium and has no wings. If the index finishes near the strike you keep the premium. The further it moves in either direction, the more you lose.

The Great Hanshin (Kobe) earthquake struck early on 17 January 1995. The Nikkei fell, and the short put side of the straddle moved sharply against the position. Rather than reduce it, he added to his other positions in the hope that the market would recover, which it did not. His losses were also hidden by wider failures of control at the bank, and those are the larger part of the story. By late February the losses had reached about £827 million (US$1.4 billion), about twice Barings' available trading capital, and Barings was declared insolvent on 26 February 1995.

The point for this lesson is narrow and structural. A short straddle is the long straddle of the builder flipped upside down. Buy a put and a call further out and the same short straddle becomes an iron butterfly: a smaller premium, but a loss that cannot exceed the wing width less the credit.

⚠️
Where the textbook version breaks

The diagram is the end of the road, and the prices are a model

The payoff diagrams assume you hold to expiry, flat volatility, and prices at their fair model value. In practice you mark the position to market long before expiry; volatility differs by strike (skew), and moves after you enter; and each leg is quoted with a bid and an offer. Most of the ways these structures hurt are in the gap between the neat expiry diagram and that daily reality.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Selling a straddle is like promising to pay anyone who guesses whether the weather will turn, whichever way it turns, in return for a small fee. If it stays calm you keep the fees. If a storm comes, you pay and pay with no upper limit. A "wing" is buying cheap insurance so your promise has a ceiling.

Section 06

How practitioners actually use structures

Retail and small accounts. Mostly defined-risk verticals and the occasional condor or butterfly. The defined worst case matches how brokers set margin and which strategies they approve for an account. The risk is treating a "high probability of profit" as an edge: the iron condor above has a 75% model chance of profit, wins an average of 1.42 when it wins, and loses an average of 4.31 when it does not. That is an expected value of −0.01, which is what a fair price looks like.

Dealers and funds. They trade packages rather than legs: listed exchanges offer complex-order books so a multi-leg structure can be executed as one order at one net price, and over-the-counter markets quote standard packages directly. Butterflies and risk reversals are used to buy and sell the shape of the volatility smile (see The IV Smile & Skew); calendars to trade the term structure of vol around events. The point is a view on relative volatility with directional exposure netted out.

Corporates and holders of concentrated positions. Collars and risk reversals are the standard way to put a floor under a position or a currency exposure without paying full premium, and in FX the zero-cost versions of these are a staple of corporate hedging. The trade-off is the same one as in the card: the floor is paid for with the upside.

What the pros check before they trade one. The net price against the sum of the legs' mid-prices (the bid-offer cost of crossing four legs); the maximum loss as a share of the account, not of the margin; the Greek signature, in particular gamma and vega; and what happens to the position on the day before and the day of each event. A later lesson in this series, on choosing a trade, puts these checks to work.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where spreads and structures quietly go wrong

Treating "defined risk" as "low risk". The condor's maximum loss is 5.72 times its maximum gain. Defined means you know the worst case in advance; it does not make it small. Size by that worst case.

Forgetting what you sold. A cheap structure is cheap because you gave something up: the upside above the short call, the downside below the short put, the tail beyond a wing. "Zero-cost" collars and risk reversals are the clearest cases.

Ignoring the cost of legs. Four legs pay four bid-offer spreads. If each leg costs just 0.05 to cross on entry, the condor's credit falls from 1.49 to 1.29, a cut of 13% before commissions. Check the net price, and prefer a single multi-leg order to legging in, which also removes the risk of getting one leg filled and not the other.

Assignment and pin risk. On American-style options (most equity and ETF options) the short leg of a spread can be exercised before expiry, most often when it is deep in the money or just before a dividend. The long leg still caps your loss, but you can wake up with shares, a margin change and a leg to close. At expiry, with price close to a short strike, you cannot be sure whether you will be assigned. Cash-settled European index options avoid early assignment and delivery.

Reading the expiry diagram as today's value. The butterfly's 4.03 is for the very last day. Halfway there, with spot at 100, it is worth 0.38. A long straddle waiting for its move bleeds to time: marked at the halfway point with spot unchanged it is worth −2.33, while a 5-point rise in vol on day one would be worth +1.98 straight away. Both are position values, not expiry values.

Trusting flat vol. Real vol differs by strike. In the collar, the put is usually the dearer leg, which is why a "free" collar often costs something. The calendar's value depends on a vol forecast for a date that has not happened, so its maximum is an estimate, not a number you can bank.

Scenario drill · 16 January 1995

You want to be paid for the Nikkei sitting still

You trade Nikkei 225 index options and believe the market will not move much over the next few weeks. You want to collect premium for that view. Rather than a plain bought option, you are choosing a structure. Which do you use?

  1. Sell an at-the-money straddle: the most premium for the view.
    It collects the most, and nothing stops the loss in either direction. If the market is hit by a shock, the loss is a multiple of the premium with no ceiling. This is the structure that turned an ordinary range view into a catastrophe.
  2. Sell a wider strangle and rely on a stop-loss order.
    A wider strangle is safer on a normal day, but the loss is still uncapped. A stop order does not protect you from a gap: it is a trigger to trade, and if the market jumps through your level, you are filled at the next available price, far beyond it.
  3. Sell an iron butterfly or iron condor: the same short premium, with a bought option on each side as a wing.
    You collect less, but the worst case is fixed in advance at the wing width less the credit. A shock costs you that amount and no more. You pay for the protection out of the premium; that is the price of surviving the surprise.
  4. Skip the trade: your view of "quiet" is not strong enough to be paid for.
    Defensible and cheap. If you cannot say what you sold to collect the premium, and what the worst case is in points and in money, standing aside is a position. It earns nothing, but it loses nothing.

What happened. As widely reported, on 16 January 1995 a trader at Barings sold a straddle on the Nikkei 225. The Kobe earthquake struck early on 17 January and the market fell. The trader added to other positions hoping for a recovery. Losses reached about £827 million (US$1.4 billion) and Barings was declared insolvent on 26 February 1995. The losses were made far larger by hidden positions and failures of control, so this drill is about the option structure only. A wing on each side of that one option position would have capped its loss; it would not have fixed the controls.

Sources: Board of Banking Supervision, Report of the Board of Banking Supervision Inquiry into the Circumstances of the Collapse of Barings, HMSO, July 1995; Nick Leeson, Rogue Trader, Little, Brown, 1996. Section 07

Key Takeaways

✓Every structure is four bricks added up. Payoff, net premium and Greeks are sums over the legs, so you can work out any structure's cost, worst case and break-evens yourself: bull call 100/105 is debit 2.01, max gain 2.99, max loss 2.01, break-even 102.01.
✓Structures buy you cheapness, a cap, or precision, and each is paid for. The sold leg pays for the bought one but takes away a part of the payoff.
✓Same bet, different clothes. A debit call spread and a credit put spread on the same strikes add to the discounted width; so do a butterfly and an iron butterfly. A collar is a bull call spread plus cash.
✓Read the Greek signature. Straddles and strangles are long gamma and vega and pay theta; condors, butterflies and credit spreads are the mirror image. That signature says what the structure needs from the market.
⚠Defined risk is not low risk, and a high win rate is not an edge. The condor wins about 75% of the time in the model and still has an expected value near zero, because its average loss (4.31) is about three times its average win (1.42).
⚠The expiry diagram is not today's value. The butterfly is worth 0.38, not 4.03, halfway through its life with spot at the middle. Costs multiply with the number of legs, and short American-style legs can be assigned early.
✗A short structure with no wings has no ceiling on its loss. Barings' short straddle on the Nikkei, January 1995, is the standing example: the structure alone cannot lose less than the market moves against it.
Section 08

Self-test

Q1. A 100/105 bull call spread is built from a 100 call at 4.23 and a 105 call at 2.22. What are its maximum gain, maximum loss and break-even?
The net debit is \(4.23-2.22=2.01\) (more precisely 2.01 before rounding the legs). Maximum loss is the debit, 2.01, if spot finishes at or below 100. Maximum gain is the width minus the debit, \(5-2.01=2.99\), if spot finishes at or above 105. Break-even is \(100+2.01=102.01\).
Q2. Why do a 100/105 debit call spread and a 100/105 credit put spread behave like the same bet?
By put-call parity, a call and a put at the same strike differ by a straight line (\(S-Ke^{-rT}\)). Subtract two strikes and the straight lines cancel to a constant, so the two payoffs differ only by a constant and their prices sum to the discounted width: 2.01 plus 2.97 is 4.98, which is \(5e^{-rT}\). You choose between them for cash, margin and who is on the other side, not for the bet.
Q3. The short iron condor collects 1.49 with a worst case of 8.51, and the model says it makes money about 75% of the time. Is that an edge?
No. Under the model's own (risk-neutral) distribution, the wins average 1.42 and the losses average 4.31. \(0.75\times1.42-0.25\times4.31\approx-0.01\), which is the interest on the credit, so the expected value is about zero. A high win rate with a small average win is the shape of selling insurance. An edge would need the real world to pay less often than the model charges for, which is a claim about volatility, not about the structure.
Q4. A long 100 straddle costs 7.97. Where does it break even, and how can you estimate its price without a model?
It breaks even at \(100\pm7.97\), that is 92.03 and 107.97. For an at-the-money option, \(c+p\approx0.8\,S\sigma\sqrt{T}=0.8\times100\times0.20\times0.5=8.0\), close to the model's 7.97. The buyer needs the realised move to exceed what the market priced.
Q5. Why is the 95/100/105 butterfly worth 0.38, and not its 4.03 best case, halfway to expiry with spot at 100?
The 4.03 is the value on the last day only. With spot at exactly 100 then, just the bought 95 call is alive, worth 5, so the profit is \(5-0.97\). Before that, every leg carries time value. The two sold at-the-money calls carry the most, which offsets nearly all of the bought wings' value, so the net is small until the final weeks. Time pays a butterfly, and it pays at the end.
Q6. Why is a "zero-cost" collar not free, and why can it cost money in a market with downside skew?
Selling the upside call finances the protective put, so the premium nets near zero, but you give up everything above the cap. With downside skew the put's vol is higher than the call's, so the put costs more than the call brings in. In the builder, at −3 skew the collar's credit of 0.48 becomes a debit of 0.04. Free in cash is not free in outcomes.
