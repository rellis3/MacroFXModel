Linear & Quadratic Programming, When the Closed Form Breaks

Markowitz's textbook portfolio formula is pure linear algebra — invert a matrix, done. The instant you add a rule a real portfolio actually needs — no shorting, a position cap, a sector limit — that guarantee vanishes, because you no longer know in advance which rule will end up binding. Linear and quadratic programming are the two well-studied problem families that pick up exactly where the matrix formula gives out, and this deck is the geometry of why.

The whole idea
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

The last lesson said convex problems are the solvable kind. Which ones do I actually meet?

Mostly two families: linear programs and quadratic programs. In both, every rule is a straight line, like a cap or a budget. The difference is the thing you are trying to maximize or minimize.

Start with the linear one.

Picture a room with flat walls. Your rules are the walls. The thing you want, say profit, rises at a steady rate as you walk one way. Where do you stop?

When I hit a wall, I suppose.

Then you slide along the wall until you jam into a corner. So the best answer of a linear program is always at a corner. You never need to search the open floor.

Give me numbers.

Two strategies. Each unit of A earns three thousand dollars, each unit of B five thousand. A is capped at four units, B at six, and a shared margin budget limits the mix. The allowed region has five corners. Check them: zero, twelve, twenty-seven, thirty-six and thirty. The best is two units of A and six of B, for thirty-six thousand.

And the simplex method just walks those corners?

Yes. Start at one corner, step to a better neighbour, and stop when no neighbour is better. George Dantzig, 1947.

Now the quadratic one.

Same flat walls, but now you are minimizing a bowl. Portfolio variance is the classic bowl, so Markowitz mean-variance is exactly a quadratic program. A bowl's bottom can sit in the open middle of the room, so checking corners is no longer enough.

But Markowitz has a formula. Invert a matrix and you're done.

Only when every rule is an equality, like weights summing to one. Add no shorting or a position cap, and you no longer know in advance which rules will bind. The right equations depend on the answer. So a solver guesses which rules bind, solves, checks the guess, and adjusts.

Can't I just clip the weights to the cap?

It sounds fine, and it fails. Clip sixty-five percent to a fifty percent cap, leave the other asset at thirty-five, then rescale so they add up to one. Asset one comes back at about fifty-nine percent, over the cap again.

So what does the job look like in practice?

Nobody writes the solver; off-the-shelf libraries do that. Your job is writing the problem down correctly, and checking the rules are today's rules. A stale cap gives no error message. It gives a perfectly optimal answer to the wrong problem.

One line?

A ramp in a flat room ends at a corner, a bowl can end anywhere, and the solver is only as good as the walls you hand it.

Section 1 · How it reached trading

Making the optimiser fast enough for a real universe

How it reached trading

Markowitz's problem is a QP, and around 1960 it strained computers for a few hundred stocks. Wolfe's 1959 QP simplex solved it in general; Sharpe's 1963 single-index model cut the work.

LPs found their own jobs: cash-flow matching for pension liabilities, and minimising expected shortfall over scenarios (Rockafellar & Uryasev, 2000). Today QPs run index tracking and cost-aware rebalancing.

Section 2

One matrix inversion, until it isn't

The classical minimum-variance Markowitz problem — budget constraint plus a target-return constraint, nothing else — has a clean closed-form answer: write the stationarity condition as a linear system and invert a matrix. No search, no iteration.

⊘

No short-selling — wᵢ ≥ 0

You don't know in advance whether the unconstrained optimum actually wants a negative weight on any given asset — that depends on the specific μ and Σ, and is only known after solving.

▢

Position caps — wᵢ ≤ 0.1

A single-name limit turns one more equality-or-not question into an unknown: does this asset want more than 10% badly enough to hit the ceiling?

🏷

Sector limits

A cap on the summed weight of a whole group of assets is still a linear inequality — and still one more constraint whose binding status isn't known ahead of time.

↔

Turnover limits

Capping how far new weights may move from last period's holdings is a linear constraint on the change in w — same problem, one layer removed.

Every one of these is an ordinary linear inequality. What breaks isn't the math being harder — it's that the correct system of equations to solve now depends on information (which constraints bind) that's only available after the problem is already solved. There is no longer one fixed matrix to invert.
The intuition, compressed

With no shorting or caps, "solve for the minimum-risk portfolio" is a one-shot formula. Add "no shorting" and you don't know in advance which assets actually want to go negative and get stopped — so there's no single formula left to plug into. Something has to try, check, and adjust its way to the answer instead. That something is a QP solver.

Section 3

A linear program: flat walls, and the optimum is always a corner

Minimize (or maximize) a linear objective, cᵀx, subject to linear inequalities, Ax ≤ b. Every constraint is a straight line — in higher dimensions, a flat plane — so the region of points satisfying all of them at once is a polytope: a many-sided, flat-walled shape, the higher-dimensional cousin of a polygon.

A tiny 2-variable LP's feasible region
Maximize 3x + 5y over x≤4, y≤6, 3x+2y≤18, x,y≥0. Checking all five corners — (0,0), (4,0), (4,3), (2,6), (0,6) — the best is (2,6), Z=36. No point in the open interior beats it.

Because a straight line never curves, moving along any line inside the region changes the objective at a constant rate — so any interior point can always be improved by continuing to walk in whatever direction is helping, right up until a wall stops you. Repeat that argument and you can only ever get pushed all the way to a vertex, where two or more walls meet. The simplex method (Dantzig, 1947) turns this into an algorithm: hop from vertex to adjacent vertex, always improving, until no neighbor is better — never once checking the empty space in between.

Section 4

A quadratic program: same flat room, a bowl instead of a ramp

A QP keeps the exact same linear-constraint structure, Ax ≤ b, but swaps the linear objective for a quadratic one: minimize ½xᵀHx + cᵀx, with H positive semi-definite (so the bowl genuinely curves upward, never a saddle).

LP — linear objective

The objective changes at a constant rate everywhere. There's never a reason to stop in the open middle of the room — the optimum is always forced all the way to a vertex.

QP — quadratic objective

The objective curves — a bowl has a genuine bottom. That bottom can sit comfortably in the open interior of the feasible region, only getting pushed to a wall if the bowl's true minimum happens to fall outside it.

Constrained Markowitz mean-variance is exactly this template: minimize wᵀΣw (so H = 2Σ, the covariance matrix scaled by two, guaranteed PSD), subject to the budget constraint Σwᵢ=1, a target-return constraint wᵀμ = R*, and — the part that pushes it out of closed-form territory — box constraints like 0 ≤ wᵢ ≤ 0.4 on every weight. Same linear constraints an LP would have; a bowl-shaped objective instead of a ramp.

Why a QP needs a different algorithm than "check the corners"

Simplex only works because a linear objective is always improved by walking further, so the search never has to look anywhere but the boundary. A QP's bowl can bottom out mid-room — so a QP solver (active-set methods, or interior-point methods) has to be able to search the interior too, which is a structurally different kind of algorithm, not a faster version of the same one.

Section 7

Clipping isn't the same as solving the QP

A common shortcut: take the unconstrained weight, clip it to the cap, then renormalize so the two weights still sum to 100%. Try it — and watch what happens when renormalizing pushes a weight right back over the cap.

Renormalizing pushed Asset 1 back above the 50% cap — the naive clip-and-renormalize fix doesn’t actually satisfy the constraint. This is exactly why real portfolio construction hands the problem to a QP solver instead of a spreadsheet trick.

A real QP solver doesn't clip after the fact — it searches only among points that satisfy every constraint simultaneously from the start (an active-set method repeatedly guesses which constraints bind, solves the resulting equality system, and checks the guess was self-consistent). The heuristic above is a useful gut-check, not a substitute.

Section 8

Vertex-hopping vs. cutting through the middle

Two different algorithms, because LPs and QPs need to search different places.

Simplex — for LPs
  • Starts at any vertex of the polytope
  • Moves to whichever adjacent vertex improves the objective most
  • Never inspects a single non-vertex point
  • Finitely many vertices ⇒ guaranteed to terminate at the true optimum
Interior-point / active-set — for QPs
  • Can move through the open interior, not just the boundary
  • A barrier function discourages getting too close to a wall
  • Converges along a smooth path rather than discrete hops
  • Also the dominant modern method for very large, sparse LPs
Simplex remains highly competitive and is often preferred when a "warm start" from a nearby previous solution is available — resuming close to the new optimal vertex beats restarting an interior-point search from scratch. That's exactly the situation a fund re-solving a similar portfolio problem every night finds itself in.
Section 9

Nobody hand-rolls simplex on a trading desk

📦

Off-the-shelf solvers

OSQP, CVXOPT, and the GLPK/CLP family for open-source LP/QP work; Gurobi and CPLEX for large-scale or mixed-integer problems. The job is formulating H, c, A and b correctly — not implementing the algorithm.

🏦

Daily portfolio rebalancing

Any systematic multi-asset fund with position caps, sector limits, or no-shorting rules re-solves a fresh QP on a real schedule — commonly every single evening.

📉

Index & basis-trade replication

Minimizing tracking error to a benchmark, or finding the cheapest basket matching cash-flow targets, are routine LP/QP formulations well away from portfolio variance specifically.

Real-world scenario — a risk-parity fund's overnight rebalance

New forecasts and a refreshed covariance matrix land every evening; the fund needs new target weights that minimize risk for a return target, subject to fixed rules — no instrument over 15%, no asset class net short, prime-broker margin caps that shift day to day. Which rules actually bind changes night to night, so there's no fixed formula worth hard-coding — the solver runs fresh every time, and a stale position cap doesn't throw an error. It just silently hands back a solution that's optimal with respect to the wrong constraints.

Section 10

Where this actually goes wrong

Silent failure modes
  • Infeasible problem — constraints contradict each other (return target too high for a box-constrained universe). The fix is relaxing a constraint, not tuning the solver.
  • Unbounded objective — a missing upper-limit constraint can make a maximization LP "optimal" at infinity. A modeling bug, not a numerical one.
Numerical / interpretive traps
  • Degenerate vertices — extra constraints coincidentally binding at once leave the multipliers non-unique, even though x itself is well defined.
  • Near-singular Hessian — highly correlated assets make Σ technically PSD but numerically unstable. Shrinkage or a small ridge term is the standard fix.
Every one of these produces an answer that looks fine — a number comes back, constraints are satisfied — right up until someone checks whether the inputs handed to the solver were actually the correct ones for that day.

Section 12

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Why does adding a no-shorting constraint (wᵢ ≥ 0) eliminate Markowitz's closed-form solution?

The closed-form answer comes from inverting a fixed system of equality conditions. Which inequality constraints bind is only known after solving — so the correct system to invert isn't known in advance.
Question 2

A linear program's optimal solution is always found:

A linear objective changes at a constant rate along any line, so any interior point can always be improved by continuing to walk — the walk only stops at a vertex, where no improving direction remains inside the region.
Question 3

What turns constrained Markowitz into a QP rather than a plain matrix-inversion problem?

Pure equality constraints (budget, target return) keep the closed-form matrix solve intact regardless of asset count. It's the box/inequality constraints whose binding status is unknown in advance that require a QP solver.
Question 4

In the box-and-bowl picture, what happens to the constrained optimum when the box's cap shrinks below the bowl's natural minimum?

Shrinking the box past the bowl's center doesn't remove a solution — it moves the closest reachable point to the boundary. The optimum snaps to that edge rather than vanishing.
Question 5

A QP solver returns a solution where every KKT condition holds. Does that guarantee the portfolio is right for that day?

KKT conditions confirm the math was solved correctly for the given inputs. They say nothing about whether a position cap, margin limit, or return target was the right one for that day — a separate, non-mathematical check.

Scenario drill · an illustrative evening

The nightly rebalance says "optimal", with yesterday's cap

You run the overnight rebalance for a systematic multi-asset fund. Every night a QP sets new weights under fixed rules: no instrument above 15%, no asset class net short, and position caps from the prime broker that move with volatility. After a volatile day the prime broker has cut one instrument's cap to 10%. The solver ran with the old 15% cap, reports "optimal" and puts 15% in that instrument. What do you do before the orders go out?

  1. Send the orders. The solver converged and reported an optimal solution.
    The answer is optimal for the wrong rules. A stale cap never throws an error; it quietly changes what "optimal" means. The book would breach the prime broker's limit at the open.
  2. Clip that weight to 10% and rescale everything else to sum to 100%.
    Clip-and-rescale is not a QP solve. Rescaling can push other weights back over their own caps, and the result is no longer the minimum-risk portfolio under the real rules. It looks fixed and is not.
  3. Update the cap to 10% and re-run the QP, then add a nightly check of every constraint input against the mandate and the prime broker's current limits.
    Re-solving with the right walls gives the true optimum under tonight's rules; the solver works out which other constraints now bind. The check stops the same silent failure next time.
  4. Keep yesterday's weights until operations confirm the new caps.
    Safe from a breach only if yesterday's weights already respect the new 10% cap, and it skips a rebalance the fund wanted. Defensible as a stop-gap, not as the fix.

What happens. This is an illustrative situation built on the lesson's own rebalance scenario, not a specific historical event. Its lesson is general: a QP solver is only as trustworthy as the constraint set it is given, and which constraints bind changes from night to night, so there is no shortcut around solving again.

Section 13

What to carry forward

01

Markowitz's closed-form formula is matrix inversion — it only works with equality constraints. Add wᵢ≥0, a cap, or a sector limit, and the single fixed matrix to invert disappears.

02

A linear program (LP) minimizes a linear objective over linear constraints — the feasible region is a flat-walled polytope, and the optimum is always at a vertex.

03

A quadratic program (QP) keeps the same linear constraints but swaps in a bowl-shaped objective — its unconstrained minimum can sit anywhere, including deep in the interior.

04

Constrained Markowitz — minimize wᵀΣw subject to budget, return target, and box constraints — is exactly a QP, which is why real portfolios run on a QP solver, not the textbook formula.

05

When the unconstrained optimum falls outside the feasible region, the true constrained optimum gets pulled to the nearest boundary or corner — not to some compromise point in between.

06

Naive clip-and-renormalize heuristics can silently re-violate the very constraint they were trying to satisfy — exactly why a real solver, not a spreadsheet trick, does this properly.

07

How it reached trading: mean-variance is a QP (Wolfe, 1959); cash-flow matching and CVaR minimisation (Rockafellar & Uryasev, 2000) are LPs.

What this now lets you do

Recognize the moment a portfolio problem needs a QP solver rather than a formula — the instant a real-world limit like no-shorting or a position cap enters the picture. Correctly identify the pieces (H, c, A, b) a solver actually needs. Read a solver's boundary-snapping behavior as ordinary geometry, not a black box — and know that a "mathematically optimal" output is only as good as the constraints someone actually fed it.
