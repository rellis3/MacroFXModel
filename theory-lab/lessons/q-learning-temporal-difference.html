Q-Learning & Temporal-Difference Methods

How to learn the Bellman equation's values from raw experience, one step at a time, without ever needing to know the environment's transition probabilities or wait for an episode to end.

CONCEPT — explained here, not built or tested anywhere in this repo ~16 min read Prereqs: MDPs, Value Functions & the Bellman Equation
TL;DR
  • Temporal-difference (TD) learning updates a value estimate toward a target built from the next step's own estimate, rather than waiting for a full episode's actual return — the TD(0) update is \(V(s) \leftarrow V(s) + \alpha[r + \gamma V(s') - V(s)]\).
  • The bracketed quantity is the TD error — the gap between what you expected and what the very next step revealed — and it's the update signal every method in this lesson is built from.
  • Q-learning is the action-value version, and it's off-policy: \(Q(s,a) \leftarrow Q(s,a) + \alpha[r+\gamma\max_{a'}Q(s',a') - Q(s,a)]\) — the bootstrap term always uses the greedy action, regardless of which action the agent's actual (exploring) behavior took next.
  • SARSA is the on-policy sibling: it bootstraps off \(Q(s',a')\) for the action \(a'\) actually taken, not the best one — the two differ only in that one substitution, with real behavioral consequences.
  • Worked example in this lesson: explicit Q-table updates on the same 3-state flat/long/short MDP from the previous lesson, converging (with a constant step size) toward — but not exactly onto — the \(Q^*\) values solved for by hand there.
  • Model-free learning removes the previous lesson's biggest limitation (needing \(P\) and \(R\) up front) but trades it for a new one: you need enough sampled experience, and enough exploration, for the estimates to actually converge.
Section 01

Why this matters

The previous lesson solved a 3-state MDP by hand — but it could only do that because every transition probability and every reward was already written down in the diagram. No real trading environment hands you that table. What's actually available is a stream of experience: you were in some state, you took some action, you observed a reward and a next state. Temporal-difference learning is the family of methods that turns a stream of exactly that shape — \((s,a,r,s')\) tuples — into value estimates, without ever needing an explicit model of the environment.

This is the same generalization Kelly-style sizing eventually needs too: the clean formula \(f^*=\mu/\sigma^2\) assumes \(\mu\) and \(\sigma^2\) are known, and the honest caveat in that lesson was that a real trader only ever has estimates of them, built from data. TD learning is what estimating a value function directly from streamed data actually looks like, worked out as an explicit update rule rather than left as a caveat.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Why look one step ahead instead of waiting for the whole story?

Suppose you're estimating how good it is to hold a position, and you want to learn from experience rather than from a model. One option — Monte Carlo estimation — is to wait until the whole trade (or the whole episode) is over, see the total return it actually produced, and use that as a training signal. This works, but it's slow: you learn nothing until the entire episode finishes, and if episodes are long (or don't naturally end at all, like an ongoing trading account), you wait a long time between updates, and every update is noisy because it depends on the entire, highly variable path that happened to occur.

Temporal-difference learning takes a shortcut: instead of waiting for the actual final outcome, it uses your own current best guess about how good the very next state is, right after taking just one step. "I thought holding this position was worth roughly \(X\); I just took one step and I'm now in a state I estimate is worth \(Y\) — that one step's real reward plus my updated guess about \(Y\) should have matched my original guess about \(X\); to the extent it didn't, nudge \(X\) toward that new number." This is called bootstrapping — building an estimate partly out of another, more current estimate — and it's what lets a TD method update after every single step, using far less data per update, at the cost of leaning on an estimate that is itself still being learned.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine guessing how a road trip will go. Waiting for the whole trip to finish before learning anything is Monte Carlo — accurate, but slow, and one weird detour can throw the whole lesson off. Temporal-difference learning is like updating your guess after every single mile, using your best current guess about the rest of the trip — quicker feedback, even though that guess about "the rest of the trip" is itself still a work in progress.

Section 02

The math, step by step

TD(0) — learning the value of a state from one step of experience. Recall the Bellman equation from the previous lesson: \(V^\pi(s) = E[r + \gamma V^\pi(s')]\). If you only ever have samples of \((s,r,s')\) rather than the full expectation, the natural move is to treat one sampled transition as a noisy estimate of that expectation and nudge your current value estimate toward it — a little at a time, not all the way, since any single sample is noisy:

\[ V(s) \leftarrow V(s) + \alpha\big[\,r + \gamma V(s') - V(s)\,\big] \]
V(s)current value estimate for state s, before this update
α(alpha) the learning rate — how large a step to take toward the new information, 0 < α ≤ 1
r+γV(s')the TD target — one real reward plus the discounted current estimate of the next state
r+γV(s')−V(s)the TD error — how wrong the old estimate turned out to be, given this one sample

The bracketed term \(r+\gamma V(s') - V(s)\) is named the TD error, usually written \(\delta\). It's exactly the Bellman equation's identity, but with an equals sign turned into a "how far off is the left side from the right side, right now" residual — zero TD error, on average, is precisely the condition the true value function satisfies at the fixed point. Every update above nudges \(V(s)\) by a fraction \(\alpha\) of that gap, which is why a small \(\alpha\) makes learning stable but slow, and a large \(\alpha\) makes it fast but noisy — the exact same speed-versus-stability trade a learning rate makes in any gradient-based estimation procedure.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

The TD error is just "how surprised was I?" You thought a state was worth some amount; you take one step, get a real reward, and update your guess about what's next — and if that combination doesn't match what you originally expected, that gap is the TD error. The learning rate α decides how much you let one single surprise change your mind — a small α means "adjust a little, stay cautious," a big α means "believe this one data point a lot."

Compare this explicitly against the alternative: a Monte Carlo update would instead wait for the entire episode to finish, compute the actual realized return \(G_t = r_t + \gamma r_{t+1} + \gamma^2 r_{t+2}+\cdots\) from that point forward, and update \(V(s) \leftarrow V(s) + \alpha[G_t - V(s)]\) — using the true, fully realized future return instead of a bootstrapped one-step estimate. Monte Carlo's target is unbiased (it's the real thing, not an estimate of it) but has to wait for an episode to end and carries the full variance of an entire trajectory; TD's target is available after a single step and has much lower variance, but is biased whenever the current \(V(s')\) estimate is still wrong (which, early in learning, it always is).

Q-learning — the same idea applied to action values. State values alone don't tell you which action to take without also knowing the transition model; action values \(Q(s,a)\) do. Q-learning applies the identical TD logic to the Bellman optimality equation for \(Q^*\) derived in the previous lesson:

\[ Q(s,a) \leftarrow Q(s,a) + \alpha\Big[\,r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s',a') - Q(s,a)\,\Big] \]
Q(s,a)current estimated value of taking action a in state s
max_a' Q(s',a')the value of the best action available in the next state, under the current estimates
r+γmax Q(s',a')−Q(s,a)the TD error for Q-learning specifically
r
Real, observed reward
The only piece of this update that isn't an estimate
max_a' Q(s',a')
The bootstrap
Best-case estimate of the next state, using current (possibly still wrong) Q-values
Q(s,a)
The result
Nudged a small step (α) toward the TD target above

Why this is "off-policy." Notice precisely what appears in the update: \(\max_{a'} Q(s',a')\) — the value of the best available action in the next state, full stop. It doesn't matter what action the agent actually takes next in real life. In practice, an agent has to explore to discover good actions at all, so it typically behaves according to an ε-greedy policy — take the current best-known action most of the time, but with small probability ε take a random action instead, purely to keep gathering information about alternatives. Q-learning's update completely ignores which of those two cases just happened; it always bootstraps off the greedy choice. This means Q-learning is learning about (converging toward the values of) the greedy policy, while behaving according to a different, exploring policy — learning about one policy while following another is the textbook definition of off-policy learning.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Think of a mouse in a maze that sometimes wanders down a random tunnel just to see what's there, instead of always taking the path it currently thinks is fastest. Q-learning is like the mouse keeping a private notebook that always assumes "if I were being smart, I'd take the best-known path from here" — even on the runs where it actually wandered off to explore. It learns the value of the smart, no-detours path while its real feet keep occasionally wandering.

SARSA — the on-policy sibling, briefly. Change exactly one thing in the update — replace the \(\max\) over next actions with the value of whichever action the agent's actual behavior policy genuinely took next, \(a'\):

\[ Q(s,a) \leftarrow Q(s,a) + \alpha\Big[\,r + \gamma\, Q(s',a') - Q(s,a)\,\Big] \]
Q(s',a')the value of the action ACTUALLY taken next, by the same exploring behavior policy — not the best available one

Because SARSA's bootstrap term reflects the actual (exploring, sometimes random) behavior, it's on-policy: it learns the value of the policy it's genuinely following, exploration and all — including the occasional cost of a random exploratory action gone wrong. Q-learning, by always bootstrapping off the best action regardless of what was actually taken, learns the value of the purely greedy policy even while the agent is still exploring around it. This single substitution is the entire difference between the two algorithms, and it has a real behavioral consequence: Q-learning can end up preferring riskier paths that are optimal if you never explore off them again, while SARSA naturally accounts for the ongoing cost of the exploration it's actually doing.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

SARSA is the same mouse, but its notebook is more honest: it only ever writes down the value of whatever tunnel it genuinely just ran down next, wandering included. So if the mouse's exploring habit occasionally sends it down a dangerous tunnel, SARSA's numbers reflect that real risk, while Q-learning's numbers pretend the mouse always takes the best tunnel from here on — even though, in real life, it doesn't.

Worked example

Explicit Q-table updates on the flat/long/short MDP

Reuse the exact 3-state MDP from the previous lesson (Flat / Long / Short, \(\gamma=0.9\)), where value iteration solved for \(Q^*(\text{Long},\text{Hold})=10\) by hand. Now forget that we know the model at all — initialize every \(Q(s,a)=0\) and learn purely from sampled experience, with a deliberately large learning rate \(\alpha=0.5\) so the arithmetic stays readable.

1

Sample: Flat, take Open, land in Long, reward −0.1

TD target \(= -0.1 + 0.9\max(Q(\text{Long,Hold}),Q(\text{Long,Close})) = -0.1+0.9(0)=-0.1\). TD error \(=-0.1-0=-0.1\). Update: \(Q(\text{Flat,Open}) \leftarrow 0 + 0.5(-0.1) = -0.05\).

Notice this dips negative — a single unlucky-looking sample of the transition cost, before any of Long's real upside has been discovered yet, temporarily makes Open look worse than Stay (\(Q(\text{Flat,Stay})=0\)). This is expected and completely normal early in learning: individual samples are noisy, and only enough of them, combined with exploration that keeps trying Open anyway, will straighten the ranking out.

2

Sample: Long, take Hold, stay in Long, reward +1

TD target \(= 1+0.9\max(Q(\text{Long,Hold}),Q(\text{Long,Close})) = 1+0.9(0)=1\). TD error \(=1-0=1\). Update: \(Q(\text{Long,Hold}) \leftarrow 0+0.5(1)=0.5\).

3

Sample: Long, take Hold again, stay in Long, reward +1

TD target \(=1+0.9\max(0.5,0)=1+0.45=1.45\). TD error \(=1.45-0.5=0.95\). Update: \(Q(\text{Long,Hold}) \leftarrow 0.5+0.5(0.95)=0.975\).

Repeating this exact sampled transition (Long, Hold, +1, back to Long) follows a clean recursion, \(Q_{k+1} = 0.95\,Q_k+0.5\), whose fixed point is exactly \(Q^*(\text{Long},\text{Hold})=10\) — the same number solved for algebraically in the previous lesson, now being approached purely from repeated sampled experience instead of a known model.

Q(Long, Hold) climbing toward Q* = 10 under repeated sampling
Each repeated sample of the same (Long, Hold, +1, Long) transition closes roughly 5% of the remaining gap to \(Q^*=10\) (since \(\alpha(1-\gamma)=0.05\) here) — fast early progress, then a long, slow approach that never exactly touches the asymptote in finite time with a constant learning rate.
🎯
Real-world trading scenario

Why off-policy matters for a system that must keep exploring

A systematic strategy that's still refining its own rules can't only ever take the action it currently believes is best — it needs to keep testing alternatives to avoid getting stuck on a locally-good-looking but globally suboptimal habit (the same exploration/exploitation tension that shows up in any adaptive sizing or regime-detection system). Q-learning's off-policy property means the system can keep exploring — trying the occasional non-greedy action to gather information — while its learned \(Q\)-values still converge toward what the fully greedy, no-longer-exploring policy would be worth. SARSA would instead learn the value of "acting greedily, except for the exploration noise I'm actually generating" — arguably more honest about what's really happening, but conflating the policy's true worth with the cost of the exploration used to learn it.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

Q-learning in its tabular form (an explicit \(Q(s,a)\) entry for every state-action pair, as in the worked example) only works when the state space is small enough to enumerate. Deep Q-Networks (DQN, Mnih et al. 2015) replaced the table with a neural network approximating \(Q(s,a;\theta)\), which is what made Q-learning practical for high-dimensional inputs like raw pixels in Atari games — the same TD update rule, just with the table entry replaced by a network's output and the update turned into a gradient step on \(\theta\) instead of a direct table overwrite. TD methods more broadly (TD(0) here is the simplest case of a whole family, including multi-step and eligibility-trace variants like TD(λ)) remain the standard tool anywhere an agent needs to learn values from an ongoing stream of experience rather than from a known model or from waiting out full episodes.

Outside of games and robotics, the same off-policy-versus-on-policy distinction shows up any time a system logs data under one behavior (an existing rule, or exploration noise) but wants to evaluate a different candidate policy from those same logs — off-policy methods are exactly the tool built for reusing already-collected experience to answer "what would a different rule have been worth," without having to actually run that different rule live first.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where TD learning and Q-learning quietly go wrong

A constant learning rate never exactly converges. As the chart above shows, the sequence keeps closing roughly 5% of the remaining gap every step but never reaches it in finite time. Formal convergence guarantees for TD and Q-learning require the step size to decay over time under the Robbins-Monro conditions (\(\sum_k \alpha_k = \infty\) and \(\sum_k \alpha_k^2 < \infty\) — enough total movement to reach the target, but shrinking fast enough for the noise to average out). A constant \(\alpha\), used here purely so the worked arithmetic stays readable, converges only to a neighborhood of \(Q^*\) and keeps a permanent residual variance from every subsequent sample.

Insufficient exploration means some \((s,a)\) pairs never get learned correctly. Both TD and Q-learning's convergence guarantees require every state-action pair to be visited infinitely often (in the limit). A purely greedy behavior policy can get stuck always taking the same action from a state and never discover that a different action there was actually better — this is exactly why ε-greedy (or another explicit exploration mechanism) isn't optional machinery, it's load-bearing for the math to hold.

Q-learning's known overestimation bias. Because the update always bootstraps off a \(\max\) over noisy estimates, and the max of several noisy numbers is itself upward-biased relative to the true best value, tabular and especially function-approximated Q-learning systematically overestimates action values, particularly early in learning or under heavy noise. Double Q-learning (van Hasselt, 2010) is the standard fix, using two separately updated estimators to decouple the action-selection max from the action-value estimate being used.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

If you ask five noisy weather apps for tomorrow's temperature and then trust whichever one gave the highest number, you'll tend to end up too optimistic — not because any one app is lying, but because picking the max of several guesses systematically favors whichever one happened to overshoot. Q-learning does exactly that every update, picking the max over its own still-noisy value estimates, which is why its scores tend to run a little too high, especially early on.

Section 04

Key Takeaways

✓TD learning bootstraps off the next step's own estimate instead of waiting for the full episode's realized return — lower variance per update, but biased whenever the current estimate is still wrong.
✓The TD error \(r+\gamma V(s')-V(s)\) is the update signal underneath every method in this lesson — including, as the next lesson shows, actor-critic's advantage estimate.
✓Q-learning is off-policy: it bootstraps off \(\max_{a'}Q(s',a')\) regardless of the action actually taken next. It learns the value of the greedy policy while the agent's real behavior is still exploring.
✓SARSA changes exactly one term — the max becomes the actually-taken next action — making it on-policy instead. It learns the value of the policy actually being followed, exploration and all.
⚠A constant learning rate converges to a neighborhood of \(Q^*\), not exactly onto it. Formal convergence needs a decaying step size satisfying the Robbins-Monro conditions.
⚠Exploration isn't optional — it's part of the convergence guarantee. Every state-action pair needs to be visited enough for its Q-value to be learned correctly; a purely greedy behavior policy can permanently miss better alternatives.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. What does "bootstrapping" mean in the context of TD learning, and what does it trade away relative to a Monte Carlo update?
Bootstrapping means building a target for updating one value estimate out of another value estimate that is itself still being learned — TD's target \(r+\gamma V(s')\) uses the current (possibly still wrong) estimate of \(V(s')\) rather than the actual realized return from that point forward. This trades a small amount of bias (the target is only as good as the current, imperfect estimate of \(V(s')\)) for a large reduction in variance and the ability to update after every single step rather than waiting for an entire episode to finish, which is what a Monte Carlo update requires.
Q2. Why is Q-learning called "off-policy," specifically — what two things is it learning about versus following?
Q-learning's update always uses \(\max_{a'}Q(s',a')\) — the value of whichever action looks best in the next state under current estimates — regardless of what action the agent's actual behavior policy (often ε-greedy, which sometimes acts randomly to explore) took next. So the values being learned converge toward the value of the purely greedy policy, while the data used to learn them is generated by a different, exploring behavior policy. Learning about one policy's values while generating experience under a different one is exactly what "off-policy" means.
Q3. In the worked example, why did Q(Flat, Open) become negative after just one sampled transition, even though Open is actually the better action at Flat?
Because that one sample only captured the small negative transition cost (r=−0.1) of opening a position, before any subsequent sample had yet revealed the large positive value of holding a resulting Long position. Q-learning updates from whatever single sample it just saw; early on, with all downstream Q-values still at their initial zero, the bootstrap term contributes nothing, so the estimate temporarily reflects only the immediate cost. Repeated sampling of the full chain of transitions — including the profitable Hold-in-Long samples — is what eventually corrects the ranking, which is exactly why sufficient exploration and enough samples are part of the convergence guarantee, not an optional nicety.
Q4. What single substitution turns Q-learning's update rule into SARSA's, and what real behavioral difference does it create?
Replace \(\max_{a'}Q(s',a')\) with \(Q(s',a')\) for whichever action \(a'\) the behavior policy actually took next. The behavioral consequence: Q-learning evaluates the purely greedy policy regardless of exploration, so it can favor paths that are optimal only if exploration stops entirely; SARSA evaluates the policy actually being followed, exploration noise included, so it naturally accounts for the real, ongoing risk of the exploratory actions the agent keeps taking.
Q5. Why does a constant learning rate α in the worked example's recursion never let Q(Long, Hold) exactly reach 10, no matter how many updates run?
The recursion \(Q_{k+1}=0.95\,Q_k+0.5\) always closes exactly the same fraction (5%, i.e. \(\alpha(1-\gamma)\)) of whatever gap remains to the fixed point 10 — a geometric approach that gets arbitrarily close but only reaches the limit as the number of updates goes to infinity, never at any finite step. Formal convergence proofs for TD and Q-learning instead require a learning rate that decays over time (the Robbins-Monro conditions), which both allows enough total movement to reach the target and shrinks the residual noise from each sample to zero in the limit — something a fixed α cannot do.
