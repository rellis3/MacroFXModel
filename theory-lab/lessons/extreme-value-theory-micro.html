Lesson 14 · Risk & Sizing

Extreme Value Theory, or How Bad It Gets, Given That It's Already Bad

Markets crash harder — and more often — than a normal distribution predicts. Your worst historical day is not your worst possible day, and the plain historical way of estimating VaR and CVaR quietly runs out of road exactly where it matters most: the 1-in-500-day event, the 1-in-2,000-day event, the day a leveraged strategy actually blows up. Extreme Value Theory doesn't try to model the whole distribution of returns — it asks a narrower question: once you're already looking at an extreme observation, what shape does the tail beyond it take, and can that shape say something honest about outcomes you haven't seen yet?

Section 1

Why 500 days of data can't answer the question that matters most

A sample of ordinary trading days is great at describing an ordinary trading day — and almost useless at describing the extraordinary one.

📊

The middle is easy

500 daily returns give you hundreds of observations clustered around the typical case — plenty to average over, plenty of statistical stability.

🕳

The tail is starved of data

A 1-in-2,000-day event needs roughly eight years of daily data just to expect to see one — and one occurrence tells you almost nothing about the shape of what's beyond it.

💥

But the tail is what kills you

The flash crash, the surprise policy shock, the liquidity air-pocket — the event that decides whether a leveraged strategy survives is exactly the one your sample barely contains.

Seismologists face the identical problem with catastrophic earthquakes: the events that matter most for planning are rare enough that no single region's recorded history contains many — sometimes none at all. The fix isn't modeling every tremor ever recorded. It's throwing away everything below a high threshold and studying only what happens once things clear it.
Section 2

VaR draws a line. CVaR asks what happens past it.

Fix a confidence level p, like 0.99. Both of the next two numbers describe the loss tail — but they answer different questions.

VaR — Value at Risk

The loss level you expect to exceed only (1−p) of the time — the 99th percentile of the loss distribution, when p = 0.99. VaR marks where the bad-outcome cutoff sits. It says nothing about how bad things get once you're past it.

CVaR — Conditional VaR / Expected Shortfall

The average loss, given that you're already beyond VaR. Two distributions can share an identical VaR while one barely crosses it on bad days and the other runs ten times past it — only CVaR tells them apart.

This isn't a stylistic preference. VaR can fail subadditivity — combining two individually-fine positions can, in constructed examples, make VaR look worse, which is the opposite of what diversification should do. CVaR, being an average over the whole tail rather than one point on it, doesn't have this failure mode. It's part of why post-2008 banking regulation shifted its standard tail-risk measure toward Expected Shortfall.

Section 4

Peaks-over-threshold: don't model everything — model the overshoot

Fix a high threshold u. Look only at the observations that clear it, and how far past it they land.

📏

Pick a high bar u

Something like "the 90th percentile of historical losses" — high enough to isolate genuinely tail behavior.

✂️

Throw the rest away

Everything below u is discarded. Only the exceedances — how far past u an observation lands — get modeled.

🔺

The overshoot settles into one shape

Almost regardless of what the original data looked like, the pattern of "how much do things overshoot, given that they clear the bar at all" converges to one distribution family.

This is the tail-behavior analogue of the Central Limit Theorem. The CLT says averages of almost anything converge to a bell curve, regardless of the underlying distribution. The Balkema-de Haan-Pickands theorem says exceedances beyond a high threshold converge to one family of shapes — the Generalized Pareto Distribution — almost regardless of the underlying distribution too. That's the theorem that licenses everything on the next slide.
Section 5

One number does the heavy lifting: the shape parameter ξ

For exceedance Y = X − u beyond threshold u, the Generalized Pareto Distribution says: P(Y > y | X > u) = (1 + ξ·y/β)^(−1/ξ). β is the scale — like a standard deviation for the tail. ξ ("xi") is the payload.

📉

ξ > 0 — heavy, Pareto-type tail

Exceedance probability decays only polynomially, not exponentially — the fat-tail regime of real financial returns. No finite variance once ξ ≥ 0.5, no finite mean once ξ ≥ 1.

➖

ξ = 0 — exponential (boundary)

The formula reduces to a plain exponential decay — a "medium" tail with no special heaviness. This is the boundary between the two other regimes.

🧱

ξ < 0 — bounded tail

A hard finite ceiling on how large an exceedance can ever get, no matter how much data you collect. The least common case for financial losses.

Section 7

From a fitted tail to a number — at any confidence you like

Once ξ and β are fitted, there's a closed-form way to get VaR at any p — including levels beyond what your raw sample could ever support directly. That's the entire point.

Try it — change any input, live
Exceedance probability q = (n / n_u) × (1 − p) = 0.0500
VaR_p = u + (β/ξ) × [q^(−ξ) − 1] = 5.89% loss
CVaR_p = VaR_p /(1−ξ) + (β − ξu)/(1−ξ) = 8.29% loss
This works at any p, even beyond your raw sample — that's the extrapolation this lesson keeps returning to.
Section 8

Worked example: a 1-in-2,000-day loss from two years of data

n = 500 daily P&L observations. You want the 99.5% VaR — and you're curious what a 1-in-2,000-day loss (p = 0.9995) might look like, ten times rarer still.

Plain historical method
  • 99.5% VaR sits at the 500 × 0.005 = 2.5th-worst day — interpolated between your second-worst and third-worst observation
  • Re-run on a slightly different two-year window and this number can swing enormously
  • The 1-in-2,000-day quantile is entirely out of reach — you'd need roughly 4 separate two-year samples just to expect to see one such event
  • Not really "estimating" anything at that depth — it's naming your worst day and calling it a statistic
EVT / peaks-over-threshold method
  • Set u at the 90th percentile of losses — with n=500 that's ~50 exceedances, a far more workable sample than "2.5 observations"
  • Fit a GPD to those 50 exceedances, obtaining ξ and β (checked against a mean-excess plot and threshold sensitivity, not accepted blindly from one fit)
  • Plug the fit into the VaR formula at p=0.995 or even p=0.9995 — it produces an answer either way, extrapolating a smooth curve rather than reading a specific data point
  • Smoother and more stable than the single-worst-observation number
The honesty check

The EVT estimate is smoother — but it is not more "true." It assumes the GPD shape fitted from the 90th–99th percentile region continues to describe the distribution all the way to a 1-in-2,000-day level you've never actually observed. If reality has a structural break out there, the fitted tail has no way of knowing. Both numbers are worth having, reported together — the gap between them is itself information about how much you're relying on the model.

Section 9

How practitioners actually use this

🏦

Regulatory tail-capital

Insurers and banks use POT/GPD fits to set reserves against extreme losses, because regulators require an answer at confidence levels (99.5% for Solvency II insurers, 99.9% for bank credit capital) raw historical data can't support directly.

⚖️

Threshold choice is a live judgment call

Too low and the theorem's premise is violated; too high and there's not enough data to fit reliably. Practitioners look for a "plateau" where ξ and β stay stable across a range of thresholds.

🧪

Complements — never replaces — history

EVT-fitted tails generate "1-in-N-year" scenarios that are triangulated against actual historical stress events (2008, 2020), not swapped in for them.

🎯

Never a single point estimate

Serious use reports confidence intervals around ξ and the resulting VaR/CVaR, and checks sensitivity to threshold and estimation window — not one clean-looking number.

Where this needs qualifying

The classical fit assumes exceedances behave like independent draws. Financial returns violate this routinely — volatility clusters, so extreme days tend to arrive in clumps around the same macro event rather than scattering independently through time. A naive fit on raw daily data quietly assumes away exactly that clustering.

The GPD layer exists; it's just built on a different unit of data than "trade count" implies.

Section 11 · It actually happened

19 October 1987: the day a normal curve forbids

It actually happened

The S&P 500 fell about 20% in one session; the Dow fell 22.6%, nearly double the worst days of the 1929 crash (about 12–13%). With daily moves then around 1%, that is on the order of twenty standard deviations: impossible under a normal curve. And it came in a cluster: Dow about −4.6% the Friday before, about +10% two sessions after.

Where the textbook version breaks

The normal tail is far too thin. EVT does better but leans on two assumptions 1987 strained: exceedances are independent (they clustered), and the shape near the threshold continues further out (a crisis-only mechanism switched on).

Section 13

Quick check

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Why can't you reliably read a 1-in-2,000-day VaR straight off 500 days of historical data?

A 1-in-2,000-day event happens on average once every ~8 years. A 500-day sample probably doesn't contain one, so the empirical quantile can do no better than name your single worst day — a number driven by one data point, not a stable pattern.
Question 2

What does a Generalized Pareto shape parameter of ξ > 0 tell you, compared to ξ < 0?

ξ > 0 means exceedance probability decays only polynomially — genuinely fat-tailed, no finite variance once ξ ≥ 0.5. ξ < 0 means the opposite: a bounded tail with a hard upper limit, regardless of how much data you collect.
Question 3

Why is CVaR considered "coherent" in a way plain VaR is not?

A coherent risk measure must satisfy subadditivity — diversification should never look like it makes things worse. VaR, caring only about a single quantile cutoff, can fail this in constructed examples. CVaR is an average over the entire tail, so it doesn't have this failure mode.
Question 4

What does peaks-over-threshold actually do, conceptually?

Peaks-over-threshold picks a high bar u, throws away everything below it, and models only the exceedances — how far past u the remaining observations land. The Balkema-de Haan-Pickands theorem is what justifies fitting a single GPD family to that overshoot.
Question 5

In the worked example, why is the EVT estimate "smoother but not more true" than the empirical one?

The EVT number is smoother because it's built from a pattern fitted across many tail exceedances rather than one or two extreme points. But it assumes that pattern keeps holding all the way out to a quantile you've never observed — an assumption you cannot verify at exactly the level you're extrapolating to.
Section 14

What to carry forward

01

Historical VaR/CVaR are honest but run out of road far in the tail — beyond roughly the 99.5th percentile they're really just naming your worst day or two.

02

Peaks-over-threshold keeps only what clears a high bar u — the Balkema-de Haan-Pickands theorem says those overshoots converge to a Generalized Pareto Distribution almost regardless of the underlying data.

03

The shape parameter ξ is the entire payload: heavy/Pareto (ξ>0), exponential (ξ=0), or hard-ceiling bounded (ξ<0).

04

A fitted GPD gives you VaR/CVaR at any confidence level, even past your sample — but that's an extrapolation assumption, not a measurement, and it's unverifiable at the exact quantile you're extrapolating to.

05

Report the empirical and EVT-extrapolated numbers together — the gap between them is itself information about how much you're relying on the model.

06

1987: a roughly 20-sigma day, impossible under a normal and rare under a heavy tail, arriving in a cluster. GPD calibration out of sample is untested here; "something follows a rare move" is null (mv-extreme-forward).

What this now lets you do

Read a VaR or CVaR figure and immediately ask whether it's empirical or extrapolated, and how many tail observations sit behind it. Set up a peaks-over-threshold fit yourself — pick a threshold, count exceedances, read what ξ says about the tail's shape. Recognise when a "worst case" number is quietly just one bad historical day dressed up as a statistic.
