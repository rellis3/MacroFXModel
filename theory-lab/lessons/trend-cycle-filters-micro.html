Trend & Cycle Filters: Splitting the Wiggle from the Trend

Every "is the economy overheating" judgment rests on one deceptively simple move: splitting an observed series into a slow-moving trend and a faster cycle riding on top of it. The Hodrick-Prescott filter has been the default tool for that split for decades — and it carries a well-documented, load-bearing flaw that hits hardest exactly where policymakers and traders need it least. Two frequency-domain alternatives, Butterworth and Baxter-King, exist specifically because of it.

The whole idea
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

Central banks keep talking about the output gap. Where does that number actually come from?

From a filter. Think of the sea at a pier. There's the tide, a slow rise and fall, and the waves on top. The gap is the waves. But you only ever see the total, so someone has to decide where the tide line is.

And the usual way to decide?

The Hodrick-Prescott filter. It draws a trend that stays close to the data but is punished for bending. One dial sets how hard bending is punished. Turn it to zero and the trend is just the data. Turn it to infinity and you get a straight line.

What do people set the dial to?

Sixteen hundred, for quarterly data. That's the convention. The classic mistake is reusing sixteen hundred on monthly data. The right value scales with the fourth power of the frequency ratio. Three months to a quarter, three to the fourth is eighty-one, so monthly needs about a hundred and thirty thousand.

That's a huge difference.

It is. But the bigger problem is the end of the sample. The filter looks both backwards and forwards in time. At the newest quarter there is no forwards. So the newest trend reading, the one policy actually uses, is the least reliable and gets revised as new data arrive.

Has that ever mattered?

The 1970s. Trend growth slowed: productivity faded from the late sixties and the 1973 oil shock hit supply. Trend lines fitted to the strong fifties and sixties sat too high. So the measured gap showed lots of spare capacity, and policy stayed loose. Inflation rose through the decade, into double digits by its end.

How do we know the slack wasn't there?

Athanasios Orphanides rebuilt the numbers policymakers had at the time, in 2001. The real-time gaps were far more negative than today's estimates for the same quarters. Then, with Simon van Norden, he showed the revisions came mainly from the end of the sample, not from revised data.

So should I stop using the filter?

James Hamilton's 2018 paper is literally titled "Why You Should Never Use the Hodrick-Prescott Filter". Besides the end problem, it can invent cycles in a pure random walk. The alternatives are band-pass filters, which keep only cycles of six to thirty-two quarters, but they lose data at both ends instead.

One line?

A trend is a modelling choice, not a measurement, and its newest point is the one most likely to be wrong.

y_t is the observed series (GDP, industrial production…). τ_t is the unobserved trend — slow-moving, "potential." c_t is the unobserved cycle — the faster gap riding on top. ε_t is irreducible noise belonging to neither.
🌊

The tide and the waves

Watch sea level at a pier: a slow twelve-hour tide with choppy waves riding on top. Squint and mentally draw a smooth line through the chop — that's a trend/cycle filter, done by eye.

⊘

No ground truth to check against

Nobody observes "just the tide" or "just the waves" to grade an answer. Every filter here is a modeling choice about how to split one number back into two — never a measurement of a true split.

Section 2

The Hodrick-Prescott objective, piece by piece

HP defines the trend as whatever series minimizes this penalized objective. Step through what each piece does.

Try it
Σ(y_t−τ_t)²
+
λ · Σ[2nd diff of τ]²
Minimize this over every possible τ_t path. Two competing goals in one expression: track the data closely, but keep the trend smooth.
Σ(y_t − τ_t)²
An ordinary least-squares fidelity term. On its own it's minimized by τ_t = y_t exactly — the "trend" would just be the raw, wiggly data.
(τ_t+1 − τ_t) − (τ_t − τ_t−1)
The trend's second difference — how much its own slope changes step to step. Zero exactly on a straight line; grows whenever the trend bends.
λ→0
λ=1600
λ→∞
λ→0: no smoothing, τ_t=y_t. λ=1600: the standard quarterly convention. λ→∞: any curvature becomes infinitely costly, forcing τ_t toward a single straight line.

λ=1600 is calibrated for quarterly data specifically. Ravn & Uhlig (2002) showed it should be rescaled by the fourth power of the frequency ratio at any other spacing — reusing 1600 unscaled on monthly data under-smooths by roughly 81×, the single most common HP-filter mistake.

Section 4

The flaw: unreliable exactly where it matters most

Hamilton (2018), "Why You Should Never Use the Hodrick-Prescott Filter" — a real, load-bearing critique, not a footnote.

End-of-sample (end-point) bias

HP is two-sided: the trend estimate at time t depends on data both before and after t. Near the very end of a real, finite sample there is no "after" data yet, so the filter's effective behavior there becomes far more one-sided than mid-sample — producing a trend estimate that can swing noticeably once new data arrives and gets appended.

A central bank estimates the 2019 Q4 output gap from quarterly GDP through 2019 Q4, concluding the economy is near potential — a judgment that feeds that quarter's policy discussion. Two years later, with 2020's collapse and 2021's rebound added to the same dataset, the desk re-runs the identical filter and finds the trend estimate at 2019 Q4 itself has shifted meaningfully — not because the 2019 data changed, but because the two-sided filter's 2019 Q4 estimate was, at the time, computed without any of the "future" data it would eventually have. The reading a decision-maker actually had in hand was the least reliable part of the whole series.

Section 5

The second flaw: cycles that were never there

🎲

Spurious cyclical dynamics

Hamilton (2018) shows the HP filter can manufacture cycle-looking dynamics in the extracted c_t even when applied to data that is, by construction, a pure random walk with no genuine cyclical structure at all.

📄

Not a new finding

Cogley & Nason (1995) documented a related spurious-cyclicality result more than two decades earlier — Hamilton's 2018 paper made it impossible to keep ignoring.

⚠

The mechanism, not the economy

Some of what gets read as "the business cycle" in an HP-filtered series can be a mechanical artifact of the smoothing procedure itself — not a feature of the underlying economy.

The load-bearing caveat

Treat HP output — and especially its most recent few observations — as a debatable modeling choice with two known, documented flaws, never as ground truth about "the" output gap.

Section 6

A different idea: state the cutoff explicitly

Instead of an implicit smoothness tradeoff, a band-pass filter names exactly which cycle lengths count as "the cycle."

Read it
Frequencies corresponding to cycles longer than 32 quarters (trend) and shorter than 6 quarters (noise) are damped toward zero; the classic "business cycle" band in between passes through close to unchanged. Schematic and conceptual — not a fitted filter design.
Section 7

The Butterworth filter — borrowed from electrical engineering

🔌

Origin: analog circuit design

Originally designed for analog electrical filters, later adopted in macro and finance to implement a band-pass idea computationally.

📶

Order

Controls how sharply the filter transitions from passing to blocking frequencies. A higher order gives a steeper roll-off at the cost of more complexity.

✂

Cutoff frequency

The frequency (or pair, for a band-pass version) marking where that pass-to-block transition happens — stated explicitly, unlike HP's implicit smoothness dial.

📏

Maximally flat passband

Its defining engineering property: it doesn't ripple or distort frequencies it's supposed to let through, while still rolling off cleanly outside the band.

Choosing a Butterworth filter still means choosing an order and a cutoff up front — a real modeling decision, just a different one than HP's λ.

Section 8

The Baxter-King filter — an explicit, finite band-pass

Baxter & King (1999) — a finite moving average using K leads and K lags, approximating an ideal 6-to-32-quarter band-pass.

The tradeoff
lost: K obs
|
band-pass output (6–32Q cycle)
|
lost: K obs
Because it's a truncated moving average, it necessarily has no filtered value for the K most recent and K oldest observations in the sample — a straightforward, fully anticipated data-loss tradeoff, not a bias.
🎯

An explicit band, not an implicit one

States the target cycle-length range up front, classically 6–32 quarters — "the business cycle" band in Baxter & King's and much subsequent literature.

📅

The 6–32Q band is a convention

Chosen from prior research on typical U.S. cycle lengths — a reasonable default, not a universal constant true of every economy or series.

Section 11

Which one, when

Hodrick-Prescott
  • Simple, no cycle-length assumption required
  • Still the default for output-gap and potential-GDP work at many central banks
  • Documented end-point bias and spurious-cycle risk — treat recent output as provisional
Butterworth / Baxter-King
  • State the target cycle-length band explicitly instead of an implicit smoothness tradeoff
  • Baxter-King trades HP's bias for a stated, cleaner data-loss cost (K obs at each end)
  • The 6–32 quarter "business cycle" band is a convention to justify, not a law to assume
In practice, many researchers report HP results alongside a band-pass robustness check rather than relying on HP alone — the two families are complements, not rivals, for exactly this reason.
Section 12 · It actually happened

The 1970s: an output gap that wasn't there

It actually happened

In the 1970s US policymakers believed the economy had lots of spare capacity: a large negative output gap. But trend growth had slowed, with productivity fading from the late 1960s and the 1973 oil shock hitting supply. A trend fitted to the fast-growth years and carried to the newest quarter sat too high, so the gap looked deeply negative and policy stayed loose while inflation rose. Orphanides (AER, 2001) rebuilt the real-time numbers: far more negative than today's estimates for the same quarters. With van Norden (REStat, 2002) he showed that, across methods including HP, gap revisions were about as large as the gap itself, and came mainly from the end of the sample, not from revised GDP.

Section 13

Where the textbook breaks, and what this desk can say

Not tested here "The output gap tells you where rates or currencies go next" is not tested here: the desk has no real-time gap series. A fair test would rebuild the gap each quarter from the data vintages available then (one-sided filter or Hamilton's regression), score its sign against what followed in 2-year yields and the currency versus a no-change benchmark, and report how often that sign was later revised away.
Where the textbook version breaks

The HP objective treats every τ_t alike, but the newest one has data on one side only, and it is the one decisions use. The textbook also assumes a smooth trend separable from the cycle. A trend slowdown (the 1970s) or a collapse (2020) gets split between the two in a way only later data can fix, so the real-time gap mixes a real cycle with a trend error of unknown size.

Section 14

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

In y_t = τ_t + c_t + ε_t, what is actually observed?

None of τ_t, c_t, or ε_t is directly observed — only their sum, y_t. Every filter is a rule for estimating the split, not a measurement of a true one.
Question 2

What happens to the HP trend as λ → ∞?

Any nonzero curvature becomes infinitely costly, so the optimal trend is forced toward the one shape with zero curvature everywhere: a straight line.
Question 3

Why does Hamilton's end-of-sample bias hit hardest at the most recent data?

The trend estimate at time t normally uses data on both sides of t. Near the end of a real sample there's no "after" data, so the estimate there is far more one-sided — and far more likely to revise.
Question 4

What does the Baxter-King filter's K leads/lags cost, specifically?

As a truncated moving average using K leads and K lags, Baxter-King simply has no answer for the K points nearest each edge of the sample — a stated data loss, not a hidden bias.
Question 5

Rescaling λ=1600 from quarterly to monthly data (ratio = 3), Ravn-Uhlig's rule multiplies λ by:

The fourth-power rule gives λ_monthly = 1600 × 3⁴ = 1600 × 81 = 129,600. Using 1600 directly under-smooths monthly data by roughly that same 81× factor.
Your call
Scenario drill · the mid-1970s

The gap says there is plenty of slack

You advise a US policymaker in the mid-1970s. Your potential-output trend is fitted to past output, mostly the strong growth of the 1950s and 1960s, and carried forward to the newest quarter. It shows a large negative output gap: lots of spare capacity. But productivity growth has been fading since the late 1960s, the 1973 oil shock has hit supply, and inflation is rising. What do you tell the policymaker?

  1. The gap is large and negative, so there is room to keep policy loose.
    This trusts the single least reliable number in the series: the trend at the newest quarter, estimated with no later data and anchored to growth rates that may no longer hold. If trend growth has slowed, the trend sits too high and the slack is partly an artefact.
  2. The newest gap reading is the least reliable point, and trend growth may have slowed. Treat the slack as uncertain and give real weight to rising inflation.
    This is the lesson's point in practice. The end of a trend estimate revises the most, and a slowdown in trend growth shows up first as a gap that is too negative. Rising inflation is direct evidence that the slack may not be there.
  3. Switch to a band-pass filter that keeps only 6 to 32 quarter cycles.
    A fair instinct, since it states the cycle band openly, but a finite band-pass filter such as Baxter-King loses its last K observations, so it gives no reading at all for the newest quarters. It does not answer the question you were asked.
  4. Raise the smoothing so the trend is less influenced by the latest wobbles.
    More smoothing pushes the trend closer to a straight line drawn through the fast-growth past, so it sits even higher at the newest quarter and the gap looks even more negative. It makes this particular error worse.

What happened. Policy that believed in large slack stayed looser than an accurate gap would have called for, and inflation rose through the decade, into double digits by its end. Athanasios Orphanides (American Economic Review, 2001) rebuilt the real-time data and found the gaps policymakers had were far more negative than today's estimates for the same quarters: much of the slack had never existed. With Simon van Norden (2002) he showed that across many detrending methods, the HP filter included, revisions were about as large as the gap itself, driven mainly by the end of the sample, not by revised GDP.

Section 15

What to carry forward

01

y_t = τ_t + c_t + ε_t — the trend/cycle decomposition. None of the three pieces is directly observed, only their sum; every filter is a modeling choice, not a measurement.

02

The HP filter minimizes fit-vs-curvature, dialed by λ. λ=1600 is the quarterly convention; rescale by the fourth power of the frequency ratio elsewhere (Ravn-Uhlig).

03

Hamilton (2018): HP has two real, documented flaws — end-of-sample bias hitting the newest, most decision-relevant data hardest, and spurious cyclicality manufactured even from a pure random walk.

04

Butterworth borrows an explicit order + frequency cutoff from electrical engineering, trading HP's implicit smoothness dial for a stated frequency boundary.

05

Baxter-King is a finite band-pass moving average targeting the classic 6–32 quarter cycle band, at the cost of losing K observations at each end of the sample.

06

None of these three filters is built or tested anywhere in this repo's trading code today — verified while building this lesson; all three remain concept-only tools explained here as modeling background, not a claim of edge.

07

The 1970s: real-time output gaps showed slack that revised estimates later erased, and most of the revision came from the end of the sample, not from revised GDP (Orphanides & van Norden, 2002). Whether a gap reading predicts rates or FX is untested here.

What this now lets you do

Read an HP-filtered output-gap chart correctly, including distrusting its last few observations on sight. Recognize instantly when a paper or dashboard reuses λ=1600 at the wrong data frequency. And know when Butterworth's explicit cutoff, or Baxter-King's stated band-pass window, is the more honest tool for the question actually being asked — rather than reaching for HP by default because it's the one everyone already uses.
