Four Markets, Four Questions

Stocks, Treasuries, options and currencies aren't four opinions on the same question — each answers a different one. Ask each market its own question, read the answers side by side, and the interesting days are the ones where they disagree.

CONCEPT — explained here; desk verdicts quoted where tested ~18 min read Prereqs: lessons 1–4 of this sequence
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • Equities answer "how do investors feel about growth and earnings?" — mood. Treasuries answer "how much do people want safety, and what rate path is expected?" Options (the VIX) answer "how uncertain is everyone?" — size, not sign. FX answers "whose money do people want?" — relative rates and flows.
  • A yield rise is ambiguous until you split it: nominal = real + breakeven inflation. Which leg moved tells you whether it's a growth/policy story or an inflation story.
  • On most days the four answers agree. The days worth studying are the disagreements — stocks and bonds falling together, or the "safe" Treasury being sold in a crash.
  • Read them in the same moment, not in sequence. This desk tested the "one market leads the other" claims and they came back null: rates-pivot-lead, front-end-shock, curve-inversion.
  • What did survive testing is range, read inside a market: vix-inversion and breadth-all-down are validated, and neither says which way.
Section 01

Why this matters

This is the capstone of How Markets Talk to Each Other. Lesson 1 followed one shock as it dominoes across the board; lesson 2 looked at mood and forced selling in equities; lesson 3 showed that the VIX measures uncertainty, not fear; lesson 4 read the yield curve as a growth forecast. Each lesson took one market and asked what it was really saying. This one puts them on one board.

The trap on a busy screen is to treat every number as a vote on the same question — "is today good or bad?" — and then get confused when the votes don't line up. They aren't supposed to line up, because they aren't answering the same question. A stock index prices future earnings; a Treasury yield prices the path of interest rates and the demand for a safe asset; an option prices the size of future moves; an exchange rate prices one country's money against another's. Once you know which question each one answers, disagreement stops being noise and becomes information.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Four witnesses, each an expert on one thing

Picture four witnesses to the same event. One is a mood reader, one a banker who knows where interest rates are heading, one an insurer who prices risk, and one a money-changer who sees which currencies people are queuing for. Ask the insurer "is it going up or down?" and you'll get a useless answer — that isn't their expertise. Ask them "how big a move should I insure against?" and they're the best witness you have.

Most of the time the four tell compatible stories. When one contradicts the rest — the banker selling the safest asset in the room while the mood reader panics — that's the witness to listen to most closely, because something unusual is forcing their hand.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Four friends watch the same football match. One tells you how excited the crowd is, one tells you how much people want to stay safe inside, one tells you how wild the game might get, and one tells you which team's scarves everyone's buying. They're all watching the same match — but each one only answers their own question. Listen to each for what they know.

Two more witnesses are worth knowing. Gold answers more than one question at once, which is exactly why it's hard to read — this desk's own breakdown found gold is driven by real rates in some periods, the dollar in others, and by neither almost half the time (see Section 06). Credit spreads — what companies pay over Treasuries — answer "how worried are lenders about getting paid back?"; this desk has a first, marginal reading on them but nothing to act on yet. And there's a fifth kind of market people sometimes add: prediction markets, which quote explicit probabilities. The idea is appealing, but the macro contracts there are thin — a "probability" from a handful of trades isn't a consensus — so they're not on this board.

Section 03

The math, step by step

Very little arithmetic is needed to read the four answers properly, but three pieces do real work.

1. Splitting a yield move: real vs inflation

The classic ambiguity: "yields are up — is that because growth is strong, or because inflation is coming?" You can't tell from the nominal yield alone. But the US also issues inflation-protected Treasuries (TIPS), whose yield is a real yield. The gap between the two is the market's breakeven inflation rate:

\[ y^{\text{nom}}_{10} \;\approx\; y^{\text{real}}_{10} \;+\; \text{BEI}_{10} \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \Delta y^{\text{nom}} \approx \Delta y^{\text{real}} + \Delta\text{BEI} \]
yⁿᵒᵐ₁₀the ordinary 10-year Treasury yield
yʳᵉᵃˡ₁₀the 10-year TIPS yield — compensation after inflation; moves with growth and the expected policy path
BEI₁₀breakeven inflation — the inflation rate at which the two bonds would pay the same

The split turns a guess into arithmetic: whichever leg moved more is the driver. If the real yield did most of the moving, it's a growth or policy-path story; if breakevens did, it's an inflation-expectations story. (Strictly, the breakeven also carries an inflation risk premium and a liquidity difference between the two bonds — it's market-implied expectations plus noise, not a survey.)

2. The curve: what the front end and the long end each answer

\[ \text{10s2s} = y_{10} - y_{2} \]
y₂the 2-year yield — mostly the expected policy rate over the next two years
y₁₀the 10-year yield — the longer path plus a term premium
10s2s < 0an inverted curve — the market expects rates to be lower later than now

The Treasury market's two questions live at different ends. The front end answers "what will the central bank do?"; the long end mixes that with growth, inflation and the demand for safety. Lesson 4 covers the curve in depth — including the desk's verdict that the inversion's famous equity signal doesn't survive a control.

3. Options: turning uncertainty into a size

\[ \sigma_{\text{1m}} \approx \frac{\text{VIX}}{\sqrt{12}} \qquad \sigma_{\text{1d}} \approx \frac{\text{VIX}}{16} \]
σ₁ₘthe one-month, one-standard-deviation S&P move in %
÷16√252 trading days — the "rule of 16" for a one-day move

The options witness answers in percent, not in up or down. See lesson 3 for the full derivation and a calculator.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

When the price of borrowing money for ten years goes up, it could be because people think things will cost more later (inflation), or because the economy is zooming and the bank will keep rates high (growth). There's a special bond that strips out the "things cost more" part. Compare the two, and you can see which one moved — like checking whether a cake got bigger because of more flour or more icing.

🧮
Worked example

Reading one (hypothetical) day, question by question

An illustrative day, made up for practice: S&P 500 −1.8%; 10-year yield +12bp, of which the real yield +10bp and breakeven +2bp; 2-year +14bp; VIX 16 → 21 with VIX3M at 22; the dollar up against most currencies; the yen flat.

1

Equities — mood?

Down 1.8%: mood soured. On its own that's all it tells you.

2

Treasuries — safety and the rate path?

Yields up on a down day for stocks: no flight to safety. Split it: |Δreal| = 10 ≥ |ΔBEI| = 2 → real-rate driven. The 2-year rose most (+14bp) → the market priced a more hawkish policy path. This is a rates repricing, not an inflation scare.

3

Options — how uncertain?

VIX 21 → one-month 1σ ≈ 21/√12 ≈ 6.1%. But 21 < 22: still contango, so the validated inversion flag hasn't fired. More uncertainty, not a "something is happening now" signal.

4

FX — whose money?

The dollar up broadly while the yen is flat: consistent with relative rates (US yields rising) rather than a haven scramble, where the yen would usually be bid too.

5

Put it together — and stop there

Four answers, one story: a hawkish rates shock, not a panic. What you should not do is forecast from it: this desk found a 2-year shock is not followed by a volatile FX week (if anything, calmer), and yield moves don't predict the next move in FX or indices.

Section 05

When the markets disagree

The most informative disagreement is the Treasury market refusing to act as a haven. In an ordinary risk-off move, investors sell stocks and buy Treasuries, so yields fall. When stocks and Treasuries are sold together, someone is being forced to raise cash from everything they own — and the safest, most liquid asset is the easiest to sell.

ShockA sudden, global need for cash

It actually happened

March 2020: the bond market told the truth

As the pandemic hit, stocks fell hard and the VIX closed at a record 82.69 on 16 March 2020. The options market's answer was clear: extreme uncertainty. The equity market's answer was clear: terrible mood. But for several days in mid-March the Treasury market gave the "wrong" answer — the 10-year yield rose while stocks fell, and the dollar surged against almost every currency.

That disagreement was the most important reading on the board. It said this wasn't an ordinary flight to safety; it was a scramble for cash and for dollars, so severe that even Treasuries were being sold to raise it. The policy response targeted exactly that: on 15 March the Federal Reserve cut rates to near zero and announced large purchases of Treasuries and mortgage securities, and enhanced its dollar swap lines with other central banks; within days the swap lines were extended to more central banks, and on 23 March the purchases were made open-ended. The S&P 500's closing low of that crash came on 23 March.

The lesson isn't that bonds predicted the bottom. It's that the Treasury market answered its own question — "how much do people want safety, and can they get it?" — and its odd answer correctly identified a liquidity problem, which needed a liquidity fix.

It actually happened

August 2019: bonds and stocks answered different questions

In mid-August 2019 the 10-year yield dipped below the 2-year for the first time since 2007, and US stocks fell sharply that day. The bond market was answering its question — "where is the policy rate heading?" — with "lower". It was right that a recession followed: the NBER later dated a peak to February 2020, though the cause was a pandemic no yield curve could have forecast. The equity market was answering a different question — "what are future earnings worth?" — and on this desk's own count the Nasdaq rose 48.5% over the following year. Both markets were right about their own question. Anyone who treated the curve's answer as an answer to the stock market's question was badly wrong.

Section 06

What this desk found when it tested the links

Five verdicts used across this sequence bear directly on how the four markets relate. Read them together and a pattern jumps out.

Null Treasuries → equities: the inverted curve (curve-inversion). 12,581 daily observations 1976–2026 collapse to TEN inversion episodes. Recession within 24 months followed 6 of 10 — 60%, 95% CI [31%, 83%], an interval that contains a coin flip; within 12 months only 3 of 10. Forward Nasdaq against a month-matched control: +12m +10.5% vs +13.0% (p=0.33). The mirror killed it: steepening episodes returned −1.1% at +12m on n=6, worse than inversions. 2019-08: right about the recession, and +48.5% on the Nasdaq over the following year.
Null Treasuries → equities, intraday: "confirm the rates pivot, then pre-position" (rates-pivot-lead). 2,430 confirmed 2-year pivots, 2,010 de-clustered events at 1h: signed Nasdaq return −0.005% vs control [−0.021, +0.012]. Null at 1h, 2h and 4h, in both directions and both halves, and well powered. A confirmed rates low is followed by an up-move 54.1% of the time — and the base rate is 54.1%. Rates and equities move in the same bar, not in sequence.
Null Treasuries → FX: "a front-end shock means a volatile FX week" (front-end-shock). Null on EUR/USD, USD/JPY and GBP/USD (~211 shocks each). After a 2Y UP shock EUR/USD and GBP/USD ran calmer (−0.34 and −0.28 ATR, CIs clear of zero); after a 2Y DOWN shock USD/JPY was marginally wider (+0.33).
Validated Options, read inside its own market: VIX above VIX3M → a wider week (vix-inversion). First day of inversion → next-5-session range vs matched controls: SPX500 +0.76 ATR, NAS100 +0.82, USD/JPY +0.44, XAU/USD +0.43 (86 entries since 2008, all CIs clear of zero). Range, not direction.
Validated Equities, read inside its own market: every index down → a wider week (breadth-all-down). All six indices down on one session (n=1,002 since 2008): next-5 range SPX500 +0.31 ATR [+0.17, +0.45], NAS100 +0.26 [+0.15, +0.38]. Two in a row (n=243): +0.55 and +0.35. Direction five sessions later 58–61% up, the ordinary drift. A range fact, not a bounce call.

Two more entries round out the picture: stocks and bonds falling together is recorded only as context (stock-bond-flip: 71 episodes since 2008, median 5 sessions, only 36 clean flips — too few to test what follows), and gold is context too (which-gold: across 689 rolling windows, rates gold 19%, dollar gold 18%, both 16%, neither 45%). "Fear buys gold" was tested directly and is null (fear-gold, see lesson 3).

What the desk learned: validated

Range, read inside one market.

  • vix-inversion — VIX above VIX3M → a wider week (SPX500 +0.76 ATR, 86 entries)
  • mv-vixterm-range — reproduced over a month (+0.52 [0.38, 0.88] SPX500)
  • iv-over-rv-wider — implied far above realised → wider, not calmer
  • breadth-all-down — every index down → a wider week (+0.31 [+0.17, +0.45])

What the desk learned: null

One market forecasting another, or forecasting direction.

  • curve-inversion — no forward equity edge; 6 of 10, CI [31%, 83%]
  • rates-pivot-lead — 54.1% hit rate, 54.1% base rate
  • front-end-shock — FX calmer, not wilder, after a hawkish 2Y shock
  • yields-to-fx-direction — forward coupling null; real only in the same bar
  • fear-gold — no fear bid in gold on average
The capstone finding: markets talk to each other at the same moment, not in a queue. Read all four answers together to understand today. Don't use one market's answer to forecast another's next move — every time this desk tested that, it came back null. What does carry forward is range, read inside a market, and it never says which way.
Section 07

How practitioners actually use this

🗂️

Label the question, not just the number

Next to each market on a dashboard, write the question it answers. It stops "VIX up" being read as "stocks down", and "yields up" being read as "good news" or "bad news" without the split.

🔍

Hunt for the disagreement

Agreement is the boring case. The first question each morning: which market is telling a different story from the others, and what would force it to?

✂️

Always split the yield

A 10-year move without its real/breakeven split is half a reading. Growth story and inflation story call for very different conclusions about everything else.

📏

Use range, not direction

The tested, carry-forward signals here are range signals — a first-day VIX inversion, a breadth wipe-out. Size positions for a wider week; don't turn them into a call on which way.

⚠️
Common pitfalls
  • Asking a market the wrong question. The VIX can't tell you direction; the curve can't tell you what stocks will do next year.
  • Reading a yield move without the split. "Yields up" is a growth story or an inflation story, and they mean different things for every other market.
  • Treating one market as a leading indicator of another. Tested here three ways for rates → equities and rates → FX; null each time. They move together in the same bar.
  • Assuming Treasuries always rally in a crash. In a scramble for cash, the safest asset is sold too — March 2020.
  • Quoting a thin market's "probability". A price from a handful of trades isn't a consensus.

Key Takeaways

✓Each market answers its own question. Equities: mood. Treasuries: safety demand and the rate path. Options: uncertainty (size). FX: whose money people want.
✓Split the yield: nominal ≈ real + breakeven. Whichever leg moved more is the driver.
✓Disagreement is the signal. Treasuries sold in a crash means a scramble for cash, not ordinary risk-off.
✓Range read inside a market is validated. First-day VIX inversion: SPX500 +0.76 ATR next week. Every index down: +0.31 [+0.17, +0.45]. Neither says which way.
✗The inverted curve's equity signal is null. 6 of 10 inversions followed by recession within 24 months, CI [31%, 83%]; forward Nasdaq no different from a matched control.
✗Rates don't lead equities or FX. rates-pivot-lead: 54.1% hit rate against a 54.1% base rate. front-end-shock: FX was calmer, not wilder, after a hawkish 2Y shock.
Section 09

Self-test

Q1. Match each market to its question: equities, Treasuries, options, FX.
Equities: how investors feel about growth and earnings (mood). Treasuries: how much safety is wanted and what rate path is expected. Options/VIX: how uncertain everyone is — the size of coming moves. FX: whose money people want — relative rates and flows.
Q2. The 10-year yield rose 15bp. The real yield rose 3bp. What drove it?
Breakevens rose about 12bp (15 − 3), so |ΔBEI| > |Δreal|: an inflation-expectations story, not a growth or policy-path story.
Q3. Stocks are down 4% and the 10-year yield is up. Why is that unusual, and what might it mean?
In ordinary risk-off, Treasuries are bought as a haven and yields fall. Yields rising in a crash means Treasuries are being sold too — either an inflation/rates scare (check the split) or, in an extreme, a scramble for cash where even the safest asset is sold, as in March 2020.
Q4. A trader says: "The 2-year just put in a confirmed low — Nasdaq will rally next, get in now." What did this desk find?
Null. Across 2,010 de-clustered events the signed Nasdaq return at 1h was −0.005% vs control [−0.021, +0.012]; a confirmed rates low was followed by an up-move 54.1% of the time, exactly the 54.1% base rate. Rates and equities move in the same bar, not in sequence.
Q5. In August 2019 the curve inverted and the Nasdaq then rose 48.5% over the year. Was the bond market "wrong"?
No — it answered a different question. The curve priced a lower future policy rate, and a recession did follow (driven by the pandemic). It never answered "what will stocks do?". Over fifty years the desk found inversions' forward equity returns don't separate from a matched control.
Q6. Which of this desk's cross-market findings survived testing, and what do they have in common?
The validated ones — vix-inversion, mv-vixterm-range, iv-over-rv-wider, breadth-all-down — are all range findings read inside a market, and none says which way. The nulls — curve-inversion, rates-pivot-lead, front-end-shock, yields-to-fx-direction, fear-gold — are all one market forecasting another's direction or behaviour.
