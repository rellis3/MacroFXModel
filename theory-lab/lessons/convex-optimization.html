Convex Optimization

The one shape-property of a problem that turns "found a good answer" into "found the best possible answer, guaranteed" — and exactly what breaks the guarantee the moment a real-world constraint gets in the way.

CONCEPT — not built or tested in this repo ~28 min read Prereqs: Newton-Raphson & Gradient Descent
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • A function is convex if the line segment ("chord") joining any two points on its graph never dips below the graph itself: \(f(\theta x + (1-\theta)y) \le \theta f(x) + (1-\theta) f(y)\). Informally: bowl-shaped, never saddle-shaped or wavy.
  • The single most important practical consequence: on a convex function, any local minimum is automatically the global minimum — there's no hidden better answer lurking somewhere else in the search space.
  • A twice-differentiable function is convex exactly when its Hessian (second-derivative matrix) is positive semi-definite everywhere — never curving downward in any direction.
  • Markowitz portfolio variance, \(w^\top \Sigma w\), is a convex quadratic function because a valid covariance matrix \(\Sigma\) is always positive semi-definite — which is exactly why mean-variance optimization is a well-posed, reliably solvable problem in the first place.
  • The Lagrangian converts a constrained problem into an unconstrained one; the KKT conditions (stationarity, primal feasibility, dual feasibility, complementary slackness) are the necessary conditions a convex problem's true optimum must satisfy.
  • Adding a realistic-sounding constraint — "hold at most 10 names" (a cardinality constraint) — can destroy convexity entirely, turning a cleanly solvable problem into a combinatorial one that needs heuristics rather than a guaranteed-optimal solve.
  • How it reached trading: Markowitz (1952–56) posed and solved portfolio choice as a constrained convex problem, and the KKT conditions certify the answer; every long-only or capped optimiser on a desk is checking them.
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

Every optimizer I run says it converged. Does that mean it found the best portfolio?

Only if the problem is convex. Converged means the slope is zero where it stopped. Whether that spot is the best one depends on the shape of the problem.

What shape do I want?

A bowl. Drop a marble anywhere in a bowl and it ends up at the one bottom. Now picture an egg carton. The marble settles in whichever dip it lands in, and a deeper dip might be right next door.

And the solver is the marble.

Exactly. It only feels the slope under its feet. From inside one dip, it cannot tell a bowl from an egg carton.

So how do I know I've got a bowl?

Pick any two points on the curve and join them with a straight line. In a bowl, the curve never pokes above that line. Take the lesson's simple bowl and two points on it, at one and at eight. Halfway between them the curve sits at about three. The straight line sits at about four point nine. The curve is below the line, and that holds wherever you look.

And if that holds, any bottom I find is the bottom?

Yes, and that's the payoff. If a lower point existed somewhere else, the straight line towards it would dip below your spot, right next to you. Then your spot was never a bottom at all. So any local minimum of a convex function is the global one.

Is portfolio risk a bowl?

Always. Portfolio variance is built from a covariance matrix, and a real covariance matrix can never give a negative variance for any mix of assets. That makes risk a bowl, which is why Markowitz minimum variance is reliably solvable.

And my rules? No shorting, position caps?

Those are straight-line rules, so the allowed region stays convex too. The solver checks a short list called the KKT conditions. One of them says each rule is either pressing on the answer or doing nothing at all.

Then my portfolio manager says, just add one more rule. Hold at most ten names.

That one breaks everything. It is not a limit on how big a weight is. It is a choice of which ten names to use. From sixty names there are about seventy-five billion possible choices. The allowed region shatters into separate pieces, and the bowl becomes an egg carton.

So what do I do?

Keep the convex solver as the engine and put a heuristic on top. Pick the names greedily, then re-solve the convex problem on those ten. Or use a mixed-integer solver and accept that it is slower, with weaker guarantees. And say so before promising a quick turnaround.

One line?

A bowl has one bottom. Keep your problem a bowl, and know which rules turn it into an egg carton.

Section 01

Why this matters

The previous lesson in this category showed gradient descent and Newton's method reliably finding a point where the gradient is zero, and then flagged the same open question at the end: is that point actually the best answer, or just a locally comfortable one with a better answer sitting elsewhere in the search space? Convexity is the property that answers that question with a flat, provable "yes, it's the best" — and its absence is the property that turns optimization from "solve it" into "search for it, with no guarantee you found the best." This single shape-property is the dividing line between the reliably solvable optimization problems finance leans on constantly (Markowitz portfolio construction, the quadratic-programming lesson that follows this one) and the much harder combinatorial problems that show up the moment a realistic trading constraint gets added.

💡
Plain-English intuition

A bowl has exactly one bottom; an egg carton has many

Picture a mixing bowl sitting on a table. However you look at it, it curves upward in every direction from its single lowest point — there's no second dip anywhere in the bowl that could trick a marble rolling around inside it into settling somewhere other than the true bottom. Now picture an egg carton instead: a landscape with many separate dips, each one a local low point, but only one of them is the deepest. A marble dropped into any one dip will happily settle there and stop — even though a much lower dip might be sitting right next to it. If you can only feel the local slope under the marble (exactly what gradient descent and Newton's method do), you have no way to tell, from inside one dip, whether you're in the bowl or in one cell of the egg carton. Convexity is the mathematical statement "this landscape is a bowl, not an egg carton" — and it's the only thing that turns "the marble stopped rolling" into "the marble found the lowest point there is."

The same shape idea applies to the set of allowed answers, not just the function being minimized. A convex set is one where, if two points are both allowed, every point on the straight line between them is allowed too — a solid disk, a solid rectangle, a solid triangle. A set with a hole in the middle, or two separate blobs, is not convex — there exist pairs of allowed points whose connecting line passes through forbidden territory. Both halves matter for a full guarantee: a convex function minimized over a convex set of allowed points is the textbook definition of a convex optimization problem, and it's precisely this combination that makes "local optimum = global optimum" hold.

How it reached trading

The best portfolio that still obeys the rules

Constrained optimisation · Markowitz, 1952–56; KKT conditions, 1951

An unconstrained mean-variance optimiser has a closed form, and it is unusable: it shorts heavily, levers up and swings wildly with small changes in its inputs. Real mandates add no-short rules, position caps, sector limits and turnover budgets, and once inequality constraints appear there is no formula.

Markowitz's 1952 paper posed portfolio choice as exactly this kind of constrained convex problem, and in 1956 he published the critical-line algorithm to solve it with inequality constraints. The Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions, worked out in operations research around 1950, are what certify that a solution is optimal. Jagannathan and Ma (2003) showed the constraints also help statistically: a long-only cap acts like shrinkage on a noisy covariance matrix.

On a desk, convex solvers run portfolio construction, risk-budgeted and risk-parity allocation, and cost-aware rebalancing. Convexity is what guarantees the optimiser's answer is the global best, not a local accident.

Defining a convex function

Formally, a function \(f\) is convex on some set if, for any two points \(x\) and \(y\) in that set and any weight \(\theta \in [0,1]\):

\[ f(\theta x + (1-\theta) y) \;\le\; \theta f(x) + (1-\theta) f(y) \]
θx + (1−θ)ya point on the straight-line segment between x and y
f(θx+(1−θ)y)the curve's actual value there — the left-hand side
θf(x)+(1−θ)f(y)the value of the straight chord connecting (x,f(x)) and (y,f(y)) at that same point
≤the curve never lies above its own chord — this is the entire definition

Read the two sides directly against a picture: the right-hand side, \(\theta f(x) + (1-\theta) f(y)\), is exactly the height of the straight line ("chord") connecting the two points \((x, f(x))\) and \((y, f(y))\) on the graph, evaluated at the same horizontal position as the left-hand side. The inequality says the actual curve, at every point between \(x\) and \(y\), sits at or below that chord — the curve never bulges upward above the straight line joining any two of its own points. That is the entire, complete definition of convexity; the interactive chart below lets you verify this inequality on real numbers by sliding \(\theta\) between 0 and 1 for a specific function. (If the inequality is strict, \(<\), for all \(\theta\) in \((0,1)\) and \(x\ne y\), the function is strictly convex — it has no flat stretches, and its minimizer, if one exists, is unique.)

A convex function's actual curve never pokes up above that straight line anywhere in between — it always stays at or below it. The Hessian test — checking convexity with calculus

Checking the chord inequality directly for every pair of points is impractical. For a twice-differentiable function, there's a much more usable equivalent test using the second derivative — in one dimension:

\[ f \text{ is convex} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad f''(x) \ge 0 \ \text{ for every } x \]

In words: the function's curvature never bends downward anywhere — it's either curving upward (like a genuine bowl) or momentarily flat, never curving the "wrong" way. In many dimensions, the second derivative becomes the Hessian matrix \(H\) (every second partial derivative, arranged in a grid), and the equivalent condition is that \(H\) is positive semi-definite everywhere:

\[ f \text{ is convex} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad v^\top H v \ge 0 \ \text{ for every vector } v, \text{ at every point} \]
Hthe Hessian — the many-dimensional generalization of f''(x)
van arbitrary direction vector — "no matter which way you look"
v^T H v ≥ 0curvature is never negative in any direction — positive semi-definite (PSD)

"Positive semi-definite" is exactly the same property this curriculum's Linear Algebra & Matrices lesson uses to characterize a valid covariance matrix — no coincidence, since a Hessian and a covariance matrix are both symmetric matrices being asked the same structural question: "is there any direction in which this matrix produces a negative number?" A practical shortcut for checking PSD-ness on a small matrix by hand (used in the worked example below) is Sylvester's criterion: a symmetric matrix is positive semi-definite if every one of its leading principal minors (the determinant of the top-left \(1\times1\), then \(2\times2\), then \(3\times3\)… sub-block) is non-negative — for a strictly positive-definite check (the stronger, more common case in practice), each of those minors should be strictly positive.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Checking whether a curve bulges above every possible chord you could draw would take forever. The Hessian test is a shortcut: just check whether the function is curving upward (or staying flat) in every direction, at every point. If it never curves the wrong way anywhere, it's guaranteed to be a bowl, not an egg carton.

Why convexity guarantees local optimum = global optimum

Here is the single most important practical fact in this lesson, and the reasoning behind it in full. Suppose \(x^\*\) is a local minimum of a convex function \(f\) — meaning \(f(x^\*) \le f(x)\) for every \(x\) in some small neighborhood around \(x^\*\), but not necessarily compared to points far away. Suppose, for contradiction, some other point \(y\) elsewhere gives a strictly smaller value, \(f(y) < f(x^\*)\). Convexity says that for any \(\theta\) close to 1, the point \(\theta x^\* + (1-\theta) y\) — which sits just slightly toward \(y\) from \(x^\*\), and so lies inside \(x^\*\)'s small neighborhood for \(\theta\) close enough to 1 — must satisfy:

\[ f(\theta x^\* + (1-\theta)y) \le \theta f(x^\*) + (1-\theta) f(y) < \theta f(x^\*) + (1-\theta) f(x^\*) = f(x^\*) \]

(the middle strict inequality holds because \(f(y) < f(x^\*)\) and \((1-\theta) > 0\)). But that point is inside \(x^\*\)'s neighborhood, and it has a strictly smaller value than \(f(x^\*)\) — directly contradicting the assumption that \(x^\*\) was a local minimum in the first place. The contradiction means the assumption was false: no such \(y\) with a strictly smaller value can exist anywhere. Every local minimum of a convex function is therefore automatically a global minimum — not because you searched everywhere and confirmed it by exhaustion, but because the shape of the function itself makes a hidden better answer logically impossible.

The payoff: for a convex problem, gradient descent or Newton's method finding a point with zero gradient is enough to conclude it found the best point, full stop — no separate search over the rest of the space is needed. This is exactly the guarantee that was missing at the end of the previous lesson.
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

In a real bowl, there's only ever one bottom — so the moment a marble stops rolling, you know it's found the true lowest point, no need to go check anywhere else. That's exactly what convexity guarantees mathematically: find any spot where the slope is zero, and you've automatically found the best possible answer, not just a locally comfortable one.

Markowitz mean-variance is a convex quadratic program

Portfolio variance, from the Markowitz framework covered elsewhere in this curriculum, is \(w^\top \Sigma w\) — a quadratic form in the weight vector \(w\). Its Hessian (the matrix of second derivatives with respect to the weights) is a constant, \(2\Sigma\), independent of \(w\) itself. A valid covariance matrix \(\Sigma\) is always positive semi-definite by construction (it's built from actual variances and covariances of real random variables, and a variance can never be negative) — so the Hessian \(2\Sigma\) is PSD everywhere, and portfolio variance is a convex function of the weights, full stop, for any real covariance matrix. Combined with the fact that the constraints (weights summing to 1, hitting a target expected return, and — as the worked example below adds — no-shorting box constraints \(w_i \ge 0\)) are all linear, and a linear constraint always describes a convex set, the whole Markowitz minimum-variance problem is a convex quadratic program: a convex quadratic objective minimized over a convex (in fact, polyhedral) feasible set. That is precisely why mean-variance optimization is reliably, provably solvable — any solver that finds a point satisfying the optimality conditions below has found the genuine global minimum-variance portfolio, not a merely locally comfortable one.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

A portfolio's risk (variance) is always shaped like a bowl, never an egg carton, because variance itself can never be negative in any combination of assets — a basic fact of how risk is measured, not an assumption someone had to add. That's why finding the lowest-risk portfolio is a reliable, always-solvable calculation, not a hopeful search.

The Lagrangian and the KKT conditions

A constrained optimization problem — minimize \(f(x)\) subject to inequality constraints \(g_i(x) \le 0\) and equality constraints \(h_j(x) = 0\) — is converted into a single unconstrained expression by the Lagrangian, which folds every constraint into the objective using one multiplier per constraint:

\[ \mathcal{L}(x, \lambda, \nu) = f(x) + \sum_i \lambda_i\, g_i(x) + \sum_j \nu_j\, h_j(x) \]
f(x)the original objective being minimized
λᵢ, gᵢ(x)a Lagrange multiplier and its matching inequality constraint
νⱼ, hⱼ(x)a Lagrange multiplier and its matching equality constraint

For a convex problem (convex \(f\) and \(g_i\), affine — linear plus a constant — \(h_j\)), the following four conditions, together known as the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions, are both necessary and sufficient for a point \(x^\*\) (with matching multipliers \(\lambda^\*, \nu^\*\)) to be the true global optimum — this sufficiency, not just necessity, is itself a special benefit of convexity that does not hold in general for non-convex problems:

Stationarity
The Lagrangian's gradient with respect to x vanishes at the optimum
gᵢ(x)≤0, hⱼ(x)=0
Primal feasibility
The candidate point actually satisfies every original constraint
λᵢ ≥ 0
Dual feasibility
Every inequality-constraint multiplier is non-negative
λᵢgᵢ(x)=0
Complementary slackness
Each inequality constraint is either exactly binding, or its multiplier is zero

Stationarity, \(\nabla_x \mathcal{L} = 0\), is the direct generalization of "the gradient is zero" from the unconstrained case in the previous lesson — except now it's the gradient of the whole Lagrangian, which already accounts for the constraints' pull on the solution. Primal feasibility just restates that the candidate answer must actually satisfy the original problem's constraints — a mathematically convenient \(x\) that violates a constraint is not a valid answer. Dual feasibility, \(\lambda_i \ge 0\), only applies to inequality constraints (not equality constraints, whose multipliers \(\nu_j\) can be any sign) — a negative multiplier would mean the constraint is somehow being rewarded for being violated, which contradicts it being an upper-bound-type constraint in the first place. Complementary slackness, \(\lambda_i g_i(x) = 0\), is the subtlest and most useful condition in practice: for each inequality constraint, either the constraint is exactly binding (\(g_i(x)=0\), sitting right on its boundary) with a possibly nonzero multiplier, or it has slack (\(g_i(x) < 0\), strictly inside the feasible region) and its multiplier must be exactly zero — a constraint that isn't actually limiting the solution contributes nothing to the optimum. This is exactly how a solver (and a human checking its output by hand) identifies which constraints are the ones actually shaping the answer.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

The KKT conditions are just a checklist for confirming a candidate answer is really the best one: does it actually satisfy every rule (feasibility)? Is nothing being rewarded for breaking a rule (dual feasibility)? And for each rule, is it either being pushed right up against its limit, or not mattering at all (complementary slackness)? If every box on the checklist ticks, for a convex problem, you've found the true best answer — guaranteed.

Where convexity breaks — the cardinality constraint

Real portfolio construction very often wants a rule like "hold at most 10 names" — a cardinality constraint, limiting the number of nonzero weights rather than their size. This sounds like a small, natural addition to the clean convex problem above. It is not: cardinality is a constraint on how many weights are nonzero, which is fundamentally a discrete, combinatorial condition (choose which 10 of \(N\) assets), not a smooth geometric one. The set of vectors satisfying "at most 10 nonzero entries" is not convex — it's a union of many separate flat pieces (one for each possible choice of which 10 assets), and the straight line between a point in one piece and a point in a different piece typically passes through vectors with more than 10 nonzero entries, which are outside the allowed set entirely. The moment that constraint is added, the clean guarantees above are gone: there is no longer a guarantee that a local optimum is global, and the problem becomes combinatorially hard (technically NP-hard) — checking every one of the \(\binom{N}{10}\) possible subsets of assets exactly is computationally infeasible for any realistic \(N\).

The practical fallout: a cardinality-constrained portfolio problem is not solved with the same convex-QP solver used for Markowitz — practitioners instead use heuristics (greedy forward/backward selection), relaxations (replacing the hard cardinality constraint with an \(\ell_1\)-penalty that encourages, but doesn't force, sparsity — the same idea behind LASSO regression), or mixed-integer programming solvers that can handle the discreteness directly but scale far worse and offer weaker optimality guarantees than a convex QP solver ever needs.
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Telling a portfolio "hold at most 10 names" sounds like a small, harmless rule, but it secretly breaks the nice bowl shape the whole problem relied on. Now the allowed answers live in a bunch of separate little patches (one patch per possible group of 10 names) rather than one smooth connected shape, so there's no longer a guarantee that the best-looking local answer is actually the best one overall.

Convex — a bowl

Any local minimum found here — by gradient descent, Newton's method, or a KKT-satisfying solver — is guaranteed to be the global minimum. There is nowhere else lower to look.

Non-convex — an egg carton

A solver that starts near the left dip can converge cleanly and confidently to the shallower local minimum — with zero gradient, every appearance of success — and never discover the deeper, true minimum sitting elsewhere on the curve.

Worked example

Verifying a 2-asset covariance matrix is PSD, then writing the full KKT conditions

Part 1 — is this Σ actually PSD? Take a concrete 2-asset covariance matrix, with asset 1's volatility 20% and asset 2's volatility 30%, correlated at \(\rho = 0.2\) (so the covariance is \(0.2 \times 0.20 \times 0.30 = 0.012\)):

\[ \Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 0.0400 & 0.0120 \\ 0.0120 & 0.0900 \end{pmatrix} \]

Apply Sylvester's criterion — check the leading principal minors:

1

1×1 leading minor

\[ \Sigma_{11} = 0.0400 > 0 \ \checkmark \]
2

2×2 leading minor (the full determinant)

\[ \det(\Sigma) = (0.0400)(0.0900) - (0.0120)^2 = 0.003600 - 0.000144 = 0.003456 > 0 \ \checkmark \]
3

Conclusion

Both leading minors are strictly positive, so \(\Sigma\) is positive definite (a stronger condition than merely semi-definite) — confirmed by its eigenvalues, which work out to \(\lambda_1 \approx 0.0927\) and \(\lambda_2 \approx 0.0373\), both positive. Portfolio variance \(w^\top\Sigma w\) built from this matrix is therefore strictly convex, and any point satisfying the KKT conditions below is the unique global minimum-variance solution.

Part 2 — the full KKT conditions for the 2-asset minimum-variance problem, with no shorting allowed. Minimize portfolio variance, subject to hitting a target return \(R^\*\), the weights summing to 1, and a no-shorting box constraint on each weight:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{minimize} \quad & w^\top \Sigma w \\ \text{subject to} \quad & w_1 + w_2 = 1 \\ & w_1 \mu_1 + w_2 \mu_2 = R^\* \\ & -w_1 \le 0, \quad -w_2 \le 0 \end{aligned} \]

Build the Lagrangian with \(\nu_1\) on the budget constraint, \(\nu_2\) on the return target, and \(\lambda_1,\lambda_2 \ge 0\) on the two no-shorting constraints (written as \(-w_i \le 0\)):

\[ \mathcal{L} = w^\top\Sigma w + \nu_1(1 - w_1 - w_2) + \nu_2(R^\* - w_1\mu_1 - w_2\mu_2) - \lambda_1 w_1 - \lambda_2 w_2 \]

The full KKT system for this problem, written out in every piece:

\[ \begin{aligned} \textbf{Stationarity:} \quad & 2(\Sigma_{11}w_1 + \Sigma_{12}w_2) - \nu_1 - \nu_2\mu_1 - \lambda_1 = 0 \\ & 2(\Sigma_{21}w_1 + \Sigma_{22}w_2) - \nu_1 - \nu_2\mu_2 - \lambda_2 = 0 \\[4pt] \textbf{Primal feasibility:} \quad & w_1+w_2=1, \quad w_1\mu_1+w_2\mu_2=R^\*, \quad w_1\ge0, \quad w_2\ge0 \\[4pt] \textbf{Dual feasibility:} \quad & \lambda_1 \ge 0, \quad \lambda_2 \ge 0 \\[4pt] \textbf{Complementary slackness:} \quad & \lambda_1 w_1 = 0, \quad \lambda_2 w_2 = 0 \end{aligned} \]
λ₁w₁=0, λ₂w₂=0the practically useful line: if the optimum wants to short (would need wᵢ<0), λᵢ becomes positive and pins wᵢ to exactly 0 instead

Read the complementary-slackness line as the whole point of adding the constraint in the first place: if the unconstrained minimum-variance solution would want a negative weight on asset \(i\), the no-shorting constraint becomes binding (\(w_i=0\) exactly) and its multiplier \(\lambda_i\) turns positive, absorbing exactly the pull that would otherwise have pushed \(w_i\) negative. If the unconstrained solution already has \(w_i>0\), the constraint was never actually in the way, and \(\lambda_i=0\) — the constraint contributes nothing, exactly as complementary slackness says it must.

🎯
Real-world trading scenario

"Just add a max-10-names rule" turns a five-second solve into an overnight heuristic run

A quant researcher has a clean, working minimum-variance optimizer across a 60-name universe — a convex QP, solved to guaranteed optimality in well under a second every time it's re-run. A portfolio manager asks for one seemingly small addition: "the fund's mandate says we can only hold 10 positions at once — can you just add that as a constraint?"

Adding \(w_i \ge 0\) or \(w_i \le 0.1\) (position caps) to the existing solver is trivial — both are linear, and the problem stays a convex QP, solved just as fast as before. "At most 10 nonzero weights," though, is not a linear or convex constraint at all — it's a statement about which subset of the 60 names get used, a combinatorial choice among \(\binom{60}{10}\) (many billions) of possible subsets. The existing convex solver has no way to express that constraint directly; it either needs to be reformulated as a much harder mixed-integer quadratic program (solvable, but potentially minutes or hours instead of milliseconds, with weaker guarantees of finding the true optimum within a practical time budget), or approximated with a heuristic — for instance, solve the unconstrained problem, keep the 10 largest-magnitude weights, rescale, and accept that the result is a good candidate, not a provably optimal one. The manager's request looked like "add one more line to the constraint list"; underneath, it's a switch from a polynomial-time, guaranteed-optimal problem to an NP-hard one — a fact worth stating plainly before promising a quick turnaround.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

Quant researchers lean on convexity constantly, often implicitly: choosing to formulate a calibration or portfolio problem so that it stays convex is, in practice, one of the most valuable design decisions a researcher makes, precisely because it converts "search heuristically and hope" into "hand the problem to an off-the-shelf solver and trust the answer." Modern convex solvers (interior-point methods, covered at a conceptual level in the next lesson in this category) routinely solve problems with thousands of variables and constraints to high precision in a fraction of a second — a speed and reliability gap from non-convex, heuristic-driven optimization that is hard to overstate.

When a real business requirement (cardinality, minimum-lot-size trading constraints, all-or-nothing position rules) genuinely breaks convexity, the standard practitioner move is not to abandon the convex core — it's to keep the convex problem as the workhorse and layer a heuristic or relaxation on top: greedy asset selection followed by a convex re-solve on the selected subset, an \(\ell_1\)-penalty relaxation that nudges toward sparsity without forcing it exactly (the same convexity-preserving trick LASSO regression uses), or accepting a mixed-integer solver's slower, weaker-guarantee answer specifically because the discreteness is unavoidable for that particular rule. Knowing precisely which constraints in a given mandate are "free" (linear, convexity-preserving) versus "expensive" (combinatorial, convexity-breaking) is itself a genuinely valuable, non-obvious skill — many realistic-sounding portfolio rules turn out to be the free kind, and it's worth checking before assuming a heuristic is required.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where this goes wrong in practice

Assuming "the solver converged" means "found the global optimum," without checking convexity first. This is only guaranteed for a genuinely convex problem. Running a general-purpose optimizer on a non-convex objective (a common situation once a model has enough nonlinear structure) and reporting the result as "the optimal solution" is a routine, easy-to-miss overstatement — the honest claim is "a solution the solver found, from this starting point," full stop.

Forgetting that convexity of the objective isn't enough on its own — the feasible set matters too. A perfectly convex objective minimized over a non-convex feasible set (exactly what a cardinality constraint produces) does not carry the local-equals-global guarantee. Both halves — convex function, convex set — are required.

Treating a small nonlinear tweak as harmless. Adding a seemingly minor nonlinear term to an otherwise convex objective (a small transaction-cost penalty with a kink, say, or a threshold-based rule) can break convexity entirely, even though the added term looks numerically small next to the rest of the objective. Convexity is a global, structural property — it doesn't degrade gracefully with a "slightly non-convex" version; either the guarantee holds everywhere or it doesn't hold at all.

Misreading a non-binding constraint's multiplier. By complementary slackness, a constraint with slack (not exactly on its boundary) must have a multiplier of exactly zero at the true optimum. A solver reporting a small but nonzero multiplier on a supposedly non-binding constraint is a signal of numerical imprecision or an incomplete solve, not a meaningful economic result — the multiplier itself is only interpretable once you've confirmed the corresponding constraint is actually binding.

Scenario drill · an illustrative Monday

"Just add a max-10-names rule"

You run a minimum-variance optimizer across a 60-name universe. It is a convex quadratic program with a no-short rule, and it solves to a guaranteed optimum in under a second. Your portfolio manager says the mandate now allows only 10 positions at once, and asks you to "just add that as a constraint" before the afternoon rebalance. What do you tell them?

  1. "Easy. I'll add it as one more line and the solver will hand back the optimum as usual."
    "At most 10 nonzero weights" is not a linear rule. It is a choice among about 75 billion subsets of 60 names, and the allowed set is no longer convex. The convex solver cannot even express it, and nothing guarantees that a local answer is the best one.
  2. "I'll cap every weight at 10%. That does the same job."
    A 10% cap is linear and keeps the problem convex, but it does the opposite job. With weights summing to 100%, a 10% cap forces you to hold at least 10 names, never at most 10.
  3. "That rule breaks convexity. I'll pick 10 names with a greedy selection, then re-solve the convex problem on those 10. It's a strong candidate, not a proven optimum. If we need a better guarantee, a mixed-integer solver can run overnight."
    This keeps the convex solver as the engine, delivers on time, and is honest about what the answer is. It is the standard practitioner pattern from the lesson: heuristic selection on top, convex re-solve underneath.
  4. "I'll add an \(\ell_1\) penalty to push the portfolio towards fewer names."
    The \(\ell_1\) relaxation keeps the problem convex and does encourage sparsity, so it is a sensible tool. But it does not force exactly 10 or fewer names; you would still have to tune it and check the count.

What happens. This is an illustrative situation, built on the lesson's own scenario, not a specific historical event. The point it makes is general: a rule that limits how big weights are (caps, no shorting, budgets) keeps the problem a bowl; a rule that limits how many names you hold turns it into an egg carton. Spot which kind a request is before promising a turnaround.

Section 04

Key Takeaways

✓Convexity means the curve never bulges above its own chord. \(f(\theta x+(1-\theta)y)\le\theta f(x)+(1-\theta)f(y)\) — bowl-shaped, not egg-carton-shaped.
✓On a convex problem, local optimum = global optimum, provably. The single most practically important fact in this lesson — no hidden better answer can exist elsewhere.
✓The Hessian test makes convexity checkable with calculus. Positive semi-definite everywhere (\(v^\top Hv\ge0\) for every direction \(v\)) is equivalent to convexity for a twice-differentiable function.
✓Markowitz portfolio variance is always convex. A valid covariance matrix is always PSD, so \(w^\top\Sigma w\) is a convex quadratic — the reason mean-variance optimization is reliably solvable.
✓The KKT conditions (stationarity, primal/dual feasibility, complementary slackness) are necessary and sufficient for a convex problem's true optimum. Complementary slackness in particular identifies exactly which constraints are binding.
⚠A realistic-sounding constraint can destroy convexity entirely. Cardinality constraints ("at most 10 names") turn a clean, fast convex QP into a much harder combinatorial problem needing heuristics or mixed-integer solvers, with weaker guarantees.
ℹConstraints are where portfolio optimisation becomes convex optimisation. Markowitz's 1956 critical-line algorithm handled no-short and cap constraints, KKT certifies the optimum, and Jagannathan & Ma (2003) showed the constraints also tame estimation noise.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. In plain English, what does the convexity inequality f(θx+(1−θ)y) ≤ θf(x)+(1−θ)f(y) actually say about the graph of f?
It says the graph of the function, evaluated at any point between x and y, never sits above the straight line ("chord") connecting the two points (x,f(x)) and (y,f(y)) on the graph. Equivalently, the curve always lies at or below its own chords — the visual definition of "bowl-shaped": it never bulges upward between any two of its own points, which rules out wavy or saddle-shaped functions with multiple separate dips.
Q2. Why does a positive semi-definite Hessian everywhere guarantee a function is convex?
The Hessian describes the function's curvature in every direction at every point. A positive semi-definite Hessian means that in whichever direction you look, from wherever you're standing, the function is curving upward or is momentarily flat — never curving downward. A function that never curves the "wrong way" anywhere can never develop a second dip or a saddle shape, which is exactly the informal "bowl, not egg carton" picture of convexity made precise with calculus — the Hessian test and the chord-inequality definition are two equivalent ways of stating the same underlying shape property.
Q3. Why is portfolio variance, w^TΣw, guaranteed to be convex for any valid covariance matrix Σ, with no extra assumptions needed?
Portfolio variance's Hessian (its matrix of second derivatives with respect to the weights) works out to a constant, 2Σ, regardless of the weights themselves. A valid covariance matrix is always positive semi-definite by construction — it is literally built from variances and covariances of real random variables, and a variance can never be negative in any direction of combination. Since the Hessian 2Σ is PSD everywhere, portfolio variance is convex everywhere, for any real covariance matrix, with no additional assumptions required — this is exactly why Markowitz mean-variance optimization is a reliably solvable problem rather than a heuristic search.
Q4. In the KKT conditions, what does complementary slackness (λᵢgᵢ(x)=0) tell you about a constraint that turns out NOT to be binding at the optimum?
If a constraint is not binding — meaning it has slack, gᵢ(x) < 0, the candidate point sits strictly inside that constraint's boundary — then complementary slackness forces its multiplier λᵢ to be exactly zero. In other words, a constraint that isn't actually limiting the solution contributes nothing to the stationarity condition or to the final answer; only constraints that are exactly binding (sitting right on their boundary, gᵢ(x)=0) can carry a nonzero multiplier. This is precisely how complementary slackness identifies which constraints in a larger problem are the ones actually shaping the optimum.
Q5. Why does adding a cardinality constraint ("hold at most 10 names") break the guarantees that hold for the ordinary Markowitz problem?
The ordinary Markowitz problem's guarantees rest on both the objective (portfolio variance, always convex) and the feasible set (defined by linear equality/inequality constraints, always convex) being convex. A cardinality constraint restricts which subset of assets can be nonzero — a discrete, combinatorial condition, not a smooth geometric one. The set of weight vectors satisfying "at most 10 nonzero entries" is a union of many separate flat pieces (one per possible subset of 10 assets), and the straight line between a point in one piece and a point in another typically passes through vectors with more than 10 nonzero entries — outside the allowed set. Because the feasible set is no longer convex, the local-optimum-equals-global-optimum guarantee no longer applies, and the problem becomes combinatorially hard rather than a smoothly solvable convex QP.
