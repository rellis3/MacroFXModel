The VIX Is an Uncertainty Index, Not a Fear Gauge

The VIX is a price for the size of the next month's moves — built from puts and calls. It usually jumps when stocks fall, so people call it the fear gauge. But on its own it says nothing about which way the market goes next. Learn to read it as a size and turn it into points.

The whole idea
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

Every headline calls the VIX the fear gauge. When it jumps, should I be selling?

Not on that alone. The nickname comes from an old academic paper, and it stuck because the VIX often jumps on days stocks fall. But the number itself is a price for movement. It is not a forecast of direction.

A price for what, exactly?

For the size of the next thirty days of moves in the S and P 500. It is built from a whole strip of out of the money options. Puts below the market, calls above it. Both sides go into the sum.

So a rush into calls can lift it too?

Yes. A frenzy in upside calls, or real two-sided worry before a big event, lifts it just as surely as a rush into puts. If stocks and the VIX both rise on the same day, that is your tell.

Then why does it nearly always spike on down days?

Because people pay far more to insure against a crash than against a melt-up. Puts are usually priced richer than calls the same distance away. So downside demand drives it, even though it measures both sides.

Fine. How do I turn the number into something I can use?

Divide by the square root of twelve for a month. A VIX of twenty means about five point eight percent, one standard deviation, either way. With the index at five thousand, that is roughly two hundred and ninety points.

And for one day?

Divide by sixteen. Twenty becomes about one and a quarter percent a day.

What if it doubles to forty?

Every number doubles. About eleven and a half percent a month, two and a half percent a day. And the middle of the bell has not moved at all. The market is paying for twice the size of move. It has said nothing new about which way.

Give me a day when that mattered.

Monday the fifth of February 2018. The S and P fell about four percent, and the VIX closed above thirty-seven, up from about seventeen on the Friday. Products that had sold volatility were forced to buy it back into the close, and the biggest inverse VIX note lost more than ninety percent overnight.

And did the VIX call the direction after that?

No. The next few weeks were wilder, just as it said. Where the index would be a few months later, it never said a word.

So the one-line version?

The VIX tells you how hard the wind will blow, not which way. Use it to size your positions and your stops, never to pick a side.

Section 1

What the VIX actually measures

The nickname

"Fear gauge": a high VIX means the market is scared and about to fall.

What it is

30-day implied volatility of the S&P 500 — the market's price for how big moves will be, in either direction.

Think of it as a forecast of wind speed, not wind direction. A storm warning tells you to tie things down; it doesn't tell you which way the gusts will blow.
Section 2

How it's built: a strip of puts and calls

Not one option — a whole strip of out-of-the-money S&P options at the two expiries around 30 days.

OTM puts below the forward
+
OTM calls above it
→
expected variance σ²
→
VIX = 100 × √σ²

Each option's price is weighted by ΔK/K² and summed — the same replication that prices a variance swap. Variance squares every move, so up and down count the same. Then √ turns it back into a volatility and ×100 quotes it in points: 0.20 → "VIX 20".

The 1/K² weighting gives low-strike puts more say per dollar, and puts are usually pricier than calls (skew). That's why downside demand usually drives the VIX even though it measures both sides.
Section 3

Turn the VIX into an expected move — try it

σ for a horizon = VIX/100 × √h. Month: ÷√12. Week: ÷√52. Day: ÷√252 ≈ ÷16.

Section 4

Worked example: VIX 20, index at 5,000

Month: 20 ÷ √12 = 5.77% → about ±289 points (2σ: ±577)
Week: 20 ÷ √52 = 2.77% → about ±139 points
Day: 20 ÷ √252 = 1.26% → about ±63 points (rule of 16: 1.25%)

Double the VIX to 40 and every number doubles — ±11.5% a month, ±2.5% a day. The middle of the bell has not moved.

A guide, not a promise

About one month in three should land outside ±1σ even on a bell curve, real returns have fatter tails, and the VIX includes an insurance premium. Use it for order of magnitude.

Section 5

It can rise in a rally

Calls are in the strip. A rush into upside calls — a squeeze, a speculative frenzy, two-sided uncertainty before a big event — lifts implied variance just as a rush into puts does.

Usual day

Stocks down, VIX up. Institutions buy puts routinely; demand for crash insurance dominates.

The tell

Stocks up and VIX up. The uncertainty being priced isn't only about a fall.

The "fear" lives in the skew — how much more puts cost than calls — not in the VIX level itself.
Section 6

The term structure: VIX vs VIX3M

Contango (normal)

VIX below VIX3M. Near-term calm, longer-term uncertainty. Most days.

Backwardation (inversion)

VIX above VIX3M. The next 30 days are priced as wilder than the next 90 — something is happening now.

Desk test: held up vix-inversion: first day of inversion → next-5-session range vs matched controls: SPX500 +0.76 ATR, NAS100 +0.82, USD/JPY +0.44, XAU/USD +0.43 (86 entries since 2008, all CIs clear of zero). Inversions are brief (median 1 session). Range, not direction.
Desk test: held up mv-vixterm-range, an independent reproduction: next 20 sessions +0.52 [0.38, 0.88] on SPX500 and +0.44 [0.26, 0.78] on NQ, 14 de-clustered firings, both halves positive. Direction is null: 57% up against an 80%+ control.
Section 7

Implied vs realised: "over-insured" doesn't mean calm

Implied = what the market pays for (the VIX). Realised = what the index actually did. Implied usually sits above realised — the insurance premium. The folk claim: when implied is far above realised, everyone's over-hedged and things will calm down.

Desk test: held up iv-over-rv-wider — the opposite. VIX ÷ 20-session realised SPX vol in the top decile (88 first days, 2010→): NAS100 next-5 range +0.38 ATR [+0.11, +0.62], next-20 +0.56; SPX500 next-20 +0.67 [+0.13, +1.21] (next-5 +0.17, null). Realised catches up to implied.

Section 9 · It actually happened

5 Feb 2018 "Volmageddon" and 5 Aug 2024

Two real spikes

Short-vol products had grown through a calm 2017. They rebalanced daily, so a late rise in VIX futures forced them to buy VIX futures into the close.

On 5 Feb 2018 the S&P fell about 4% and the VIX closed at 37.32 against 17.31 the previous Friday — more than doubling in one session. Overnight the largest inverse-VIX note lost more than 90% of its indicative value; its issuer announced it would terminate the note.

During the yen carry-trade unwind, Japan's Nikkei fell more than 12% on 5 Aug 2024.

The VIX printed above 65 intraday around the US open, then closed near 39 the same day. Part of the spike came from thin, wide option quotes before liquidity returned — an intraday print is a stressed price, not a settled reading.
Section 10

The one "fear" test — and it failed

If the VIX were a fear signal with a direction, the classic fear trade — buy gold — should pay after a spike.

Desk test: nothing found fear-gold: 58 VIX spikes (+5 points in five sessions) 2010-2026. Gold five sessions on: excess +0.07% [-0.42, +0.30]; higher at five sessions 53% [41-66]. Twenty sessions on: excess -1.19% [-2.30, -0.48]. No fear bid in gold on average, and a month later gold had lagged its ordinary drift.
Three validated VIX findings, all about range. One directional "fear" claim, null. Vol tells you about size, never sign.
Section 11

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

The VIX is 20. Roughly what one-month move is one standard deviation?

20/√12 ≈ 5.77%. ±20% is the annualised figure; ±1.25% is the one-day move (20/16).
Question 2

The VIX just doubled. What has the market told you?

The VIX is priced from puts and calls and squares the moves; doubling it doubles the expected size and leaves the centre of the bell at zero. "Fear buys gold" was tested here and came back null.

Your call
Scenario drill · 5 February 2018

The VIX has doubled in a day. Which way do you lean?

It is the close on Monday 5 February 2018. The S&P 500 fell about 4% today, and the VIX closed at 37.32, up from 17.31 on Friday. You hold a long S&P 500 position sized for the calm market of last week. A colleague says the fear gauge is screaming and you should flip short. What do you do?

  1. Flip short. A VIX that doubles in a day is signalling more downside.
    The VIX is built from puts and calls, and its bell stays centred on zero. It says the next month's moves will be bigger, not which way they will go. Flipping on it reads a direction into a number that has none.
  2. Keep the direction call separate and resize for the new range. 37.32 ÷ 16 is about 2.3% a day, against about 1.1% at 17.31, so cut the position by roughly half and widen the stop to match.
    This is what the VIX is for. The expected daily move more than doubled, so the same position now carries more than twice the risk. Cutting size keeps your risk steady, and your view on direction stays your own call.
  3. Close everything and wait for the VIX to calm down.
    Safe, and better than flipping. But the VIX gave you a size message, not a reason to abandon your view. Resizing does the same risk job without throwing the position away.
  4. Sell volatility. Option prices have doubled, so collect the rich premium.
    Short-vol products were being forced to buy volatility back into the close, and that forced buying was making the spike bigger. Selling into it, sized for last week's calm, puts you on the side being squeezed.

What happened. Overnight, the largest inverse-VIX exchange-traded note lost more than 90% of its indicative value, and its issuer announced it would terminate the note. The forced buying from short-vol products had made the spike bigger. The S&P 500 stayed volatile into the following week, so the VIX was right about size. It gave no signal about where the index would be a few months later.

Sources: Cboe VIX daily closes, FRED (17.31 on 2 February, 37.32 on 5 February 2018); S&P 500 daily closes, FRED (2,762.13 to 2,648.94, −4.1%); Credit Suisse media release on the XIV ETN acceleration event, 6 February 2018.
