From a View to a Trade Plan

"EUR/USD looks cheap" is a view. A trade needs five numbers you write down before you click: entry, invalidation, stop, target and size. This lesson shows where each number comes from, and why the most important one, invalidation, comes from your analysis and not from how much loss you can stand.

CONCEPT — explained here; desk verdicts quoted where tested ~20 min read Prereqs: none; Research Pipeline helpful
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide. It covers the same ground in short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • A view is not a trade. A trade plan is five numbers written before entry: entry, invalidation, stop, target, size. A time limit makes a sixth.
  • Invalidation comes from the analysis. It is the price at which the reason for the trade is wrong. "I don't want to lose more than 20 pips" is not a reason. It is a pain limit, and the market does not know about it.
  • The stop sits just beyond invalidation, with a buffer scaled to ordinary noise (a fraction of ATR). If the stop is inside a normal day's wiggle, it will be hit by noise.
  • Size comes last: lots = money you will risk ÷ (stop distance × value per pip). You choose how much to lose; the stop distance decides how big the position is.
  • Reward-to-risk sets the bar. With target 1.74× the stop distance, you break even (before costs) if you win 36.4% of the time.
  • The desk's verdicts: a zone strategy with an 8-pip stop against ~46-pip structure tripled its stop-outs (and had no edge anyway); a validated range study says aim for the 75th-percentile band rather than the median; "writing the plan first improves results" is not tested here.
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

EUR/USD looks cheap. The rate spread says it should recover. I'm buying.

That's a view. Where do you get in, where are you wrong, where do you take profit, and how big is it?

In at ten eight fifty. I'll risk twenty pips. That's what I'm comfortable losing.

Twenty pips is a pain limit, not a reason. Does anything about your thesis change at ten eight thirty?

Not really. The spread model is just as right there.

Then the stop is in the wrong place. A normal day moves about sixty-five pips from high to low. Twenty pips is about a third of that. A quiet morning can stop you out, and then the trade does exactly what you said it would.

So where should it go?

Start from the reason. What price proves you wrong?

The March low at ten seven eighty held even when the spread was at its widest. A close below it means the market sees something my model doesn't.

That's your invalidation. Put the stop a little beyond it, a quarter of a day's range, so sixteen pips. Stop at ten seven sixty-four. That's eighty-six pips away.

Eighty-six pips? That's four times what I wanted to lose.

No. That's where the fear belongs: in the size, not the stop. Decide the money first. Half a percent of a hundred thousand is five hundred dollars. Divide by eighty-six pips at ten dollars a pip. That's point five eight lots. Wrong costs you five hundred dollars either way.

And the target?

From the analysis too. Eleven hundred is a hundred and fifty pips away. That's one point seven four times your risk, so you break even if you win about thirty-six percent of the time, before costs.

And if the stop just works, I'm safe.

A stop is an order, not a guarantee. In January twenty fifteen the Swiss central bank dropped its floor under the franc without warning. Stops just under one twenty filled far lower. Some traders lost many times what they planned, and a broker went under. Around pegs, events and weekends, size for the gap.

The one-line version?

Find where the reason breaks, put the stop just past it, and let that distance set your size.

Section 01

Why this matters

Most of this curriculum teaches you to analyse: to forecast volatility with GARCH, test a spread for cointegration, track a hedge ratio with a Kalman filter, and backtest honestly. At the end of all that you have a view: "the 2-year spread says EUR/USD is too low", or "gold looks stretched". A view has a direction and maybe a reason. It doesn't have a price where you get in, a price where you admit you were wrong, a price where you take the money, or a size.

Those missing numbers aren't details. They decide what the trade actually does to your account. Two traders with the same correct view can get opposite results: one has a stop inside the noise and is shaken out before the move, the other has a stop placed where the thesis breaks and holds on. This is the first lesson in the From Analysis to Action series. Each lesson takes a finished analysis and turns it into something you can act on. Here, the output is a filled-in trade plan card.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Decide what "wrong" looks like while you are still calm

Before a long drive you check the route, decide where you'll stop for fuel, and decide what would make you turn back, such as a road closure or a storm warning. You don't decide those things at 90 mph in the rain. A trade plan is the same idea. Every number in it is easy to choose before the trade and hard to choose once money is moving.

The key number is the "turn back" point. It shouldn't be "when I feel scared". It should be "when the road I planned on is actually closed". In trading terms: the price at which the reason for the trade has stopped being true.

How it reached trading

From floor-trader rules of thumb to written rules

Trading practice · the Turtle experiment (1983–84); R-multiples (Van Tharp, 1990s); triple-barrier labels (López de Prado, 2018)

Stops, targets and position size are as old as speculation. They became a written, complete plan through trading practice rather than theory. In 1983 Richard Dennis and William Eckhardt trained a group of novices, the "Turtles", on a fully specified rule set: where to enter, where the stop goes (a multiple of a volatility measure they called N, an average true range), and how many contracts to trade so each position risked a fixed slice of the account. The point of the experiment was that the rules could be written down and followed.

In the 1990s Van K. Tharp popularised measuring every trade in R, multiples of the amount risked. That makes "what was my stop?" the unit of every result. More recently, Marcos López de Prado's triple-barrier method labels research data the same way a plan exits a trade: an upper barrier (target), a lower barrier (stop) and a vertical barrier (time limit). See Triple-Barrier Labeling.

The five numbers, step by step

Watch the rule build

1. Invalidation: where the reason stops being true

Start with the reason, not the price. Write your thesis in one sentence and ask: what price action would prove this sentence wrong? That price is your invalidation level, \(P_{\text{inv}}\). Some examples:

Invalidation from the analysis

"The spread model says EUR/USD is cheap. The March low at 1.0780 held even when the spread was at its widest. A daily close below it means the market is pricing something my model can't see."

"Gold is range-bound between two levels my volatility model calls 2σ. A break outside the range means the regime changed."

"Invalidation" from pain

"I'll risk 20 pips because that's what I'm comfortable losing."

"I'll put the stop at 1% of the account away from entry." This mixes up how much to lose, which is a sizing question, with where the idea is wrong, which is a price question.

Pain is a legitimate input, but it belongs in size (step 5), not in the stop. If the honest invalidation is far away and that scares you, trade smaller. Don't move the stop closer. A stop closer than the invalidation level gets you out while your thesis is still true.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

You bet a friend it will be sunny at the picnic. You lose the bet if it rains. You don't lose it because a cloud floats past. Invalidation is "it rained". A stop set by pain is giving up because of one cloud.

2. The stop: invalidation plus a noise buffer

Markets wiggle. Price often pokes a little beyond an obvious level before turning, and many other traders' stops sit exactly at the obvious level. So the stop goes beyond invalidation by a buffer scaled to ordinary noise. A common yardstick is the average true range (ATR), the average daily high-to-low distance, including gaps:

\[ P_{\text{stop}} = P_{\text{inv}} - s\cdot b\cdot \text{ATR}, \qquad D = \lvert P_{\text{entry}} - P_{\text{stop}} \rvert \]
P_invinvalidation level: where the reason fails
s+1 for a long (stop below), −1 for a short (stop above)
bbuffer as a fraction of ATR, often 0.1–0.5
Dstop distance from entry, in price or pips

Then run one sanity check: stop distance in ATRs, \(D/\text{ATR}\). An ordinary day travels about one ATR from high to low. A stop at 0.3 ATR from entry can be hit by a quiet morning, so its stop-outs are mostly noise. If the stop distance comes out well under half an ATR, your entry is too far from the invalidation level, or your timeframe is wrong. The answer is a better entry (wait for price to come closer to invalidation), not a tighter stop.

3. The target: what the analysis says is reachable

A target should also come from the analysis. Examples: where the model says fair value is, a distance the volatility forecast says is reachable within your holding period, or a measured range level. It's "where my reason says price is going", not "how much would make me happy". Many plans use two targets: take part of the position off at the first, then let the rest run with the stop moved to entry.

4. Reward-to-risk and the break-even hit rate

With entry, stop and target fixed, the trade's shape is set. Measure the reward in units of the risk (Tharp's R-multiple):

\[ R = \frac{\lvert P_{\text{target}} - P_{\text{entry}} \rvert}{D}, \qquad p^{*} = \frac{1}{1+R} \]
Rreward-to-risk: how many stop-distances the target is worth
p*break-even win rate, before costs, if every trade ends at stop or target

Where \(p^{*}\) comes from: if a win pays \(R\) and a loss costs 1, the expected result is \(pR - (1-p)\). Set that to zero and \(p = 1/(1+R)\). A 2R target breaks even at 33.3%. A 1R target needs 50%, and a 0.5R target needs 66.7%. Whether your analysis wins often enough is the subject of the next lesson. Costs move \(p^{*}\) up, and lesson 4 shows by how much.

5. Size: the last number, set by the stop

Only now do you decide how big the position is. Pick the money you are willing to lose if the stop is hit: a fraction \(r\) of equity \(E\), typically 0.25–1%. Then:

\[ \text{lots} = \frac{r \cdot E}{D_{\text{pips}} \times v_{\text{pip}}} \]

This is the line that keeps pain out of the stop. A wide, honest stop just means fewer lots. The loss if you're wrong stays the same dollar amount. How much to risk per trade (\(r\)) is its own question, covered by Kelly and risk of ruin. How a volatility forecast becomes the stop distance is lesson 5.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

You have 10 sweets you're allowed to lose. If the game is risky and you might lose a long way, you bet with fewer sweets each step. If it's a short, safe step, you can bet more each step. Either way, the most you can lose is the same 10.

Worked example

A EUR/USD long, from view to plan card

1

The view

Your spread analysis says EUR/USD is too low relative to the 2-year yield gap and should recover toward 1.1000 over about three weeks. Price is 1.0850. Daily ATR is 65 pips. Account $100,000. You risk 0.5% per trade.

2

Invalidation, from the analysis

The March low at 1.0780 held even when the spread was at its most extreme. If EUR/USD closes below it, the market is pricing something the spread model doesn't see. \(P_{\text{inv}} = 1.0780\).

3

Stop

Buffer \(0.25 \times 65 = 16.25\), rounded to 16 pips. Stop \(= 1.0780 - 0.0016 = 1.0764\). Stop distance \(D = 1.0850 - 1.0764 = 86\) pips, which is \(86/65 = 1.32\) ATR. That's outside an ordinary day's noise.

4

Target and R

Target 1.1000 is 150 pips away. \(R = 150/86 = 1.74\). Break-even win rate \(p^{*} = 1/(1+1.744) = 36.4\%\) before costs.

5

Size

Money at risk \(0.005 \times 100{,}000 = \$500\). Lots \(= 500/(86 \times 10) = 0.581\), rounded down to 0.58 lots. Actual risk \(0.58 \times 86 \times 10 = \$498.80\). At target: \(0.58 \times 150 \times 10 = +\$870\).

6

Time stop and the gap note

The thesis is about three weeks, so exit after 15 sessions if neither level has been hit. The gap check: if a weekend gap opened at 1.0700, the stop would fill there, not at 1.0764. The loss would be \(0.58 \times 150 \times 10 = \$870\), 1.74× the plan. If that number is unacceptable, cut size before the weekend.

A trader with the same view says "I only want to risk 20 pips" and puts the stop at 1.0830. That's 20/65 = 0.31 ATR from entry, well inside an ordinary day's range. Section 03

It actually happened: when the stop isn't the exit

It actually happened

15 January 2015: the Swiss franc floor goes

In September 2011 the Swiss National Bank set a minimum exchange rate of 1.20 francs per euro and promised to defend it with unlimited currency purchases. For more than three years EUR/CHF sat just above 1.20. Many traders treated the floor as an obvious invalidation level: long EUR/CHF near 1.20, stop a little below it. On their analysis the floor could not break, so the stop was cheap insurance.

On the morning of 15 January 2015 the SNB abandoned the floor without warning. The franc jumped in minutes and EUR/CHF briefly traded well below parity. There was almost no market in between, so stop orders placed just under 1.20 were filled far lower, wherever the next real price was. Losses were many times what the plans had allowed. Some retail clients ended with negative balances. The UK arm of the broker Alpari went into insolvency, and FXCM, then one of the largest US retail FX brokers, needed an emergency rescue loan.

The lesson for a trade plan isn't "never use stops". The invalidation level was right: below 1.20, the thesis was dead. What failed was the assumption that the stop would fill near it. A stop is an order, not a guarantee. When the thesis depends on a policy, a peg, a weekend or a scheduled event, the plan needs a gap line: the loss if price jumps past the stop, and a size small enough to survive it.

You put a fence at the edge of a cliff so you'll stop there. But one day the ground in front of the fence falls away all at once, and you land far below it. A fence only works if the ground holds. So for really risky cliffs you carry less with you, in case you fall further than the fence.

Section 04

What this desk found when it tested the claims

Not tested here "Writing the plan before the trade improves results." Not tested here. The desk has no sample of plan-first versus plan-later trades to compare. It does apply the same discipline to its research: every test is pre-registered, with the hypothesis, the measure and the pass bar written down before the data are run. That is the research version of invalidation written before entry: it stops the conclusion from being chosen after the result is known.
How to test it: from a fixed start date, alternate the desk's discretionary FX trades week by week between "plan card written and logged before entry" and "levels set after entry", and resolve every trade on daily closes and stops. After at least 100 trades in each arm, compare average R after costs and the share of losses bigger than 1.1R (stops moved or ignored). Fix the gate now: plan-first passes only if it improves average R by at least 0.1R with \(t > 2\), or halves the share of oversized losses. The plan-later arm is the baseline.
Desk test: nothing found Confluence zones as entries, and the stop that didn't fit the structure. The desk tested stacked zones (Fibonacci, support/resistance, pivots, volatility levels) as entries. "M30: negative on 83k trades, both halves. H4: a coin flip." The detail that matters for this lesson: "the 8-pip stop tripled stop-outs against a ~46-pip structure". A stop chosen without reference to the structure it was protecting was mostly hit by noise. The zones had no edge either way. The desk's conclusion was not to revive them with a different stop.
Desk test: held up Targets from a measured range, not from hope. Across eight instruments and ~2,500 London sessions each, once price had reached the median daily band by 10:30 UK, the odds of reaching the 75th-percentile band before the close were well above the ordinary odds for that hour: NAS100 63% vs 23%, SPX500 59% vs 22%, USD/JPY 44% vs 17%, EUR/USD 43% vs 18%, gold 50% vs 21% (2.3–2.7×; n = 260–450 per cell). Once reached, the median band was the day's extreme in only 4–10% of sessions. That is a target guide, never an entry: on a day that's already moving, "aim for the 75th, not the median".
🛠 On the desk: Position sizer does step 5 for every pair at once: the stop sits outside the forecast range's noise, and the size is set so a stop-out loses the same money on each trade.
The trap: a tight stop feels like discipline, and it makes the reward-to-risk ratio look spectacular. But a stop inside the noise changes the trade's win rate, not just its risk, so the impressive R is fiction. The discipline is putting the stop where the thesis breaks and then trading small enough to accept that distance.
Section 05

How practitioners actually use this

📝

Orders in before the trade

The stop (and often the target) goes in as a resting order at entry, or as a bracket order, so the plan executes without a fresh decision under stress.

📏

Everything in R

Results are journalled as R-multiples (+1.7R, −1R), so trades of different sizes compare directly, and a −2.4R loss flags a stop that wasn't honoured or a gap.

⏳

Time stops

A thesis about the next three weeks is no longer being tested after six. Many desks exit at the horizon whatever the P&L, and the triple-barrier method builds this into research labels.

📅

Event and gap lines

Before a central-bank meeting, a data release or a weekend, risk managers ask: "what is the loss if price opens beyond the stop?" They size to that number, not to the stop.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where trade plans go wrong

Stop from pain, size from hope. This is the order reversed: choosing lots first, then fitting a stop that keeps the loss "comfortable". The stop ends up in the noise.

Stop exactly at the obvious level. Round numbers and prior lows attract stops and brief overshoots. Put the stop beyond invalidation with a buffer, not on it.

Moving the stop away once in the trade. That gives you a new, unplanned trade with a bigger loss. You may move a stop only toward profit, and only by a rule written in the plan.

A target from wishful thinking. A 5R target the analysis can't justify gives a tiny break-even rate that will never actually be hit.

Ignoring costs in R. The spread is paid on every trade. On a 20-pip stop a 1.5-pip spread is 7.5% of R. On an 86-pip stop it's under 2%.

Treating the stop as a guaranteed exit. Gaps, pegs and illiquid moments fill stops far beyond their level. Size for the gap when one is possible.

Scenario drill · 14 January 2015

Long EUR/CHF just above the 1.20 floor

Since September 2011 the Swiss National Bank has held a minimum rate of 1.20 francs per euro, promising unlimited purchases to defend it. For more than three years EUR/CHF has sat just above 1.20. You are long EUR/CHF near the floor with a stop a little below it. Your analysis says the floor is the invalidation level: below 1.20, the thesis is dead. How do you size the trade?

  1. Size it to the short stop as usual. The stop is close, so a big position only risks a small amount.
    This assumes the stop fills near its level. A stop is an order, not a guarantee. If the floor goes, there may be no prices between 1.20 and wherever the market lands, and a big position turns a small planned loss into a huge one.
  2. Keep the floor as invalidation, but add a gap line: the loss if price jumps far past the stop. Size so that loss is one you can survive.
    The invalidation is right; the exit assumption is the weak point. When a thesis rests on a policy or a peg, the plan sizes for the gap, not for the stop. That usually means a much smaller position.
  3. Skip the trade. A peg can break at any moment.
    Safe, and sometimes right. But the thesis may be sound. The problem is size, and a small position with a written gap line lets you take the trade without betting the account on the stop.
  4. Remove the stop. The central bank has promised unlimited purchases, so it cannot break.
    This turns an analysis into a belief. A plan with no exit for being wrong has no maximum loss at all.

What happened. On the morning of 15 January 2015 the SNB abandoned the floor without warning. The franc jumped in minutes and EUR/CHF briefly traded well below parity. Stops placed just under 1.20 were filled far lower, wherever the next real price was. Losses were many times what plans had allowed, some retail clients ended with negative balances, the UK arm of the broker Alpari went into insolvency, and FXCM needed an emergency rescue loan.

Key Takeaways

✓A plan is five numbers, written first: entry, invalidation, stop, target, size. Add a time stop.
✓Invalidation comes from the reason for the trade, the price where the thesis is wrong. Pain belongs in the size, never in the stop.
✓Stop = invalidation + a noise buffer. Check the distance in ATRs; well under half an ATR is inside ordinary noise.
✓Size last: lots = (r × equity) ÷ (stop pips × pip value), rounded down. In the example: $500 ÷ (86 × $10) → 0.58 lots.
✓R sets the bar: break-even hit rate = 1/(1+R). 1.74R needs 36.4% before costs.
⚠A stop is an order, not a guarantee. Around pegs, events and weekends, size for the gap (SNB, January 2015).
✗Desk test, nothing found: confluence zones as entries. Its 8-pip stop against ~46-pip structure tripled stop-outs. A stop that ignores the structure measures noise.
Section 07

Self-test

Q1. What is the difference between an invalidation level and a pain limit?
Invalidation is a price at which the reason for the trade is shown to be wrong, so it comes from the analysis. A pain limit is an amount of money you are willing to lose. The first sets the stop; the second sets the size. Using the pain limit as the stop puts the stop wherever your comfort ends, which has nothing to do with whether the thesis is right.
Q2. Long gold at 2,400.0. Invalidation 2,370.0, ATR $28, buffer 0.25 ATR. Where is the stop, and how far is it in ATRs?
Buffer \(0.25 \times 28 = \$7\), so stop \(= 2{,}370 - 7 = 2{,}363.0\). Distance \(2{,}400 - 2{,}363 = \$37\) \(= 37/28 = 1.32\) ATR, outside ordinary noise.
Q3. Continue Q2: equity $100,000, risk 0.5%, one lot = 100 oz (a $1 move = $100 per lot). How many lots?
Money at risk $500. Per lot, a $37 stop costs \(37 \times 100 = \$3{,}700\). Lots \(= 500/3{,}700 = 0.135\), rounded down to 0.13 lots (risk \(0.13 \times 3{,}700 = \$481\)). In "pips" of $0.10 worth $10 per lot, the same sum is \(500/(370 \times 10)\).
Q4. A plan has a stop 40 pips away and a target 60 pips away. What hit rate does it need to break even before costs? With a 2-pip round-trip cost?
\(R = 60/40 = 1.5\), \(p^{*} = 1/2.5 = 40\%\). With costs, a win nets 58 pips and a loss costs 42. Break-even \(p = 42/(58+42) = 42\%\). Costs raised the bar by two percentage points.
Q5. Your honest invalidation is 150 pips away and the loss scares you. What do you change?
The size, not the stop. Cut lots (or the risk fraction) so 150 pips costs an amount you accept. Or wait for an entry closer to the invalidation level, which shortens the distance honestly. Pulling the stop in to 50 pips just gets you out while the thesis is still alive.
Q6. Why did stops just below 1.20 on EUR/CHF fail in January 2015, even though the invalidation level was correct?
When the SNB removed the floor, price jumped far below 1.20 with almost no trading in between. A stop order fills at the next available price, not at its level. The invalidation was right but the exit assumption wasn't. Plans around pegs, events and weekends need a gap line and a size small enough to survive it.
