MDPs, Value Functions & the Bellman Equation

The formal object underneath every reinforcement-learning method: a Markov Decision Process, and the recursive identity — value equals immediate reward plus discounted value of what comes next — that makes it solvable at all.

CONCEPT — explained here, not built or tested anywhere in this repo ~17 min read Prereqs: Kelly Criterion & Position Sizing
TL;DR
  • A Markov Decision Process (MDP) is the tuple \((S,A,P,R,\gamma)\) — states, actions, transition probabilities, rewards, and a discount factor — the standard formalism for a sequential decision problem where today's action changes tomorrow's situation.
  • The value function \(V^\pi(s)\) is the expected discounted sum of all future rewards, starting in state \(s\) and following policy \(\pi\) forever after.
  • The Bellman equation is a recursive identity, not a new assumption: \(V^\pi(s) = \sum_a \pi(a|s)\sum_{s'} P(s'|s,a)[R(s,a,s') + \gamma V^\pi(s')]\) — value now equals immediate reward plus the discounted value of wherever you land next.
  • The Bellman optimality equation replaces "average over the policy's actions" with "take the best one": \(V^*(s) = \max_a \sum_{s'} P(s'|s,a)[R(s,a,s') + \gamma V^*(s')]\), and its action-value twin \(Q^*(s,a)\) scores a specific action directly.
  • Worked example in this lesson: a 3-state flat/long/short position MDP solved by value iteration — the numbers climb steadily every sweep and settle at a fixed point that also happens to encode the sensible policy (stay long, don't stay short).
  • This is the shared foundation both follow-up lessons build on: Q-learning (next lesson) turns this into a learning rule when \(P\) and \(R\) aren't known in advance; policy gradients (lesson 3) skip the \(\max_a\) altogether for continuous actions.
Section 01

Why this matters

Every strategy this curriculum has covered so far picks a static rule — a z-score threshold, a Kelly fraction, a regime classifier — and applies it the same way regardless of what happens next. Reinforcement learning (RL) is the branch of decision theory built for the opposite case: problems where an action today changes the situation you're reacting to tomorrow, and the "right" action can only be judged by its effect on the entire future path, not just its immediate payoff. Deciding whether to close a losing position now or hold it, or how much size to add to a winner, are both sequential decisions in exactly this sense — the position you're in tomorrow depends on what you do today.

The Kelly criterion already showed one version of this idea: it picked a bet size not to maximize the next bet's expected profit, but to maximize long-run compounded growth across many repeated bets — a sequential-decision problem solved by looking past the immediate payoff. The Markov Decision Process is the general machinery behind that same instinct, formalized enough to solve exactly (in a small problem) or approximately (in a large one, which is what the next two lessons are about). Understanding the MDP and its Bellman equation first is what makes Q-learning's update rule and the policy gradient theorem look like natural consequences of one idea, rather than two unrelated algorithms to memorize.

💡
Plain-English intuition

What makes a decision problem "Markov," and why does that matter?

Imagine grading a position not by "how good does this feel right now" but by "starting from here, and playing well from here on, how much do I expect to make in total, discounted for the fact that a dollar next year is worth less than a dollar today?" That single number — the expected total future payoff from a state, under some way of acting — is a value. An MDP is just a precise way of writing down: here are the situations you can be in (states), here are the choices available in each one (actions), here is how likely each choice is to land you in each next situation (transition probabilities), and here is the immediate payoff or cost of each transition (rewards).

"Markov" is the assumption that makes this tractable: the current state captures everything relevant about the past. What happens next depends only on where you are and what you do now — not on the full history of how you got there. This is obviously a simplification for real markets (path dependence, regime memory, and momentum all violate it to some degree), but it's a deliberate one: it's the same kind of "clean but useful" idealization the Kelly derivation makes when it assumes you know your edge exactly, or a normal-distribution primer makes when it ignores fat tails. You build the exact math on the idealization first, then reason carefully about where the real world will diverge from it.

Section 02

The math, step by step

The five pieces of an MDP. Formally, a Markov Decision Process is the tuple \((S, A, P, R, \gamma)\):

\(S\)The set of states — every distinct situation the process can be in.
\(A\)The set of actions — the choices available (possibly different sets per state).
\(P(s'|s,a)\)Transition probability — chance of landing in state \(s'\) given you took action \(a\) in state \(s\).
\(R(s,a,s')\)The reward received for that specific state-action-next-state transition.
\(\gamma\)(gamma) The discount factor, \(0 \le \gamma < 1\) — how much a unit of reward one step in the future is worth today.
\(\pi(a|s)\)A policy — the probability of taking action \(a\) when in state \(s\) (a deterministic policy is the special case where one action gets probability 1).
\(t\)The time step index, \(t=0,1,2,\dots\)
\(V^\pi(s)\)The value function — expected discounted return from state \(s\), following policy \(\pi\).
\(V^*(s)\)The optimal value function — the best possible \(V^\pi(s)\), over every policy \(\pi\).
\(Q^*(s,a)\)The optimal action-value function — the best possible expected return from taking action \(a\) in state \(s\), then acting optimally after.

The discount factor \(\gamma\) does two jobs at once: economically, it encodes that a reward tomorrow is worth less than the same reward today (the same reasoning behind discounting a bond's future cash flows back to present value); mathematically, it's what keeps an infinite sum of rewards from being infinite. A value of \(\gamma\) close to \(1\) means the agent cares almost as much about the distant future as the present; \(\gamma\) close to \(0\) means it's almost entirely short-sighted, caring only about the very next reward.

The value function — defining "how good is this state." Fix a policy \(\pi\) (a rule for choosing actions, possibly randomized). The value of state \(s\) under that policy is the expected sum of all future rewards, each discounted by how far away it is in time:

\[ V^\pi(s) = E\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \gamma^t r_t \;\middle|\; s_0=s,\ \pi\right] \]
V^π(s)expected total discounted future reward, starting in state s
γ^t r_tthe reward received t steps from now, shrunk by γ raised to the t-th power
s₀=s, πconditioned on: starting state is s, and every action is chosen by policy π

This single number compresses an entire infinite future — every reward the agent will ever collect from this point on, under this way of behaving — into one comparable quantity. It's exactly the same compression a bond's price performs on a stream of future coupons, or that Kelly's log-growth objective performs on an entire sequence of compounding bets: reduce a whole future to one number you can actually maximize.

Deriving the Bellman equation — the recursive shortcut. Computing \(V^\pi(s)\) by directly summing an infinite series looks hopeless. The trick is to notice that the infinite sum has the same shape one step later as it does right now — split off just the first reward and re-index the rest of the sum:

\[ V^\pi(s) = E\big[r_0 + \gamma r_1 + \gamma^2 r_2 + \cdots \mid s_0=s,\pi\big] = E\big[r_0 + \gamma(r_1 + \gamma r_2 + \cdots) \mid s_0=s,\pi\big] \]
r₁+γr₂+⋯the exact same infinite-sum shape as V^π itself, just starting one step later — from whatever state s' comes next

The term in parentheses is literally the value function again, just evaluated at whatever state \(s'\) the process happens to land in after one step: \(r_1 + \gamma r_2 + \cdots = V^\pi(s')\). Substituting that back in, and expanding the expectation over which action \(\pi\) picks and which next state \(P\) produces, gives the Bellman equation:

\[ V^\pi(s) = \sum_{a} \pi(a|s) \sum_{s'} P(s'|s,a)\Big[R(s,a,s') + \gamma V^\pi(s')\Big] \]
Σ_a π(a|s)average over which action the policy takes in state s
Σ_s' P(s'|s,a)average over which next state that action actually lands in
R(s,a,s')+γV^π(s')the one-step reward plus the discounted value of wherever you end up
R(s,a,s')
Immediate reward
What you get right now for this specific transition
γV^π(s')
Discounted future value
Everything after this step, compressed into the next state's own value
V^π(s)
The result
Today's value — one step of reward plus tomorrow's value, averaged over the randomness

This is the whole idea in one sentence: value now equals immediate reward plus the discounted value of what comes next. It's a recursive definition, not a new physical assumption — it follows purely from splitting an infinite sum. But it's exactly the identity that makes an MDP solvable: instead of needing to sum an infinite, unbounded future directly, you only ever need to relate a state's value to its immediate neighbors' values. That's what turns an infinite-horizon problem into a finite system of equations (one per state) that can be solved by substitution, by iteration, or by a learning rule that only ever looks one step ahead — which is exactly the door this equation opens for the next lesson.

The Bellman optimality equation. Everything above assumed a fixed policy \(\pi\) and asked "how good is it?" The more useful question is usually "what's the best possible value achievable from this state, over every policy?" Define \(V^*(s) = \max_\pi V^\pi(s)\). Because the best policy, by definition, always takes whichever action leads to the highest expected value, the averaging-over-actions term in the Bellman equation is replaced by a maximization over actions:

\[ V^*(s) = \max_{a} \sum_{s'} P(s'|s,a)\Big[R(s,a,s') + \gamma V^*(s')\Big] \]
max_areplaces "average over the policy's actions" with "take whichever action is best"
V*(s)the best possible value achievable from state s, over every policy

This single change — average becomes maximum — is what separates "evaluating a given policy" from "finding the best one." It's also self-referential in exactly the same recursive way as before: \(V^*\) appears on both sides, one step apart.

The action-value function \(Q^*(s,a)\). It's often more useful to score a specific action directly, rather than only scoring states. Define \(Q^*(s,a)\) as the expected return of taking action \(a\) in state \(s\) right now, then acting optimally forever after:

\[ Q^*(s,a) = \sum_{s'} P(s'|s,a)\Big[R(s,a,s') + \gamma V^*(s')\Big] = \sum_{s'} P(s'|s,a)\Big[R(s,a,s') + \gamma \max_{a'} Q^*(s',a')\Big] \]
Q*(s,a)value of taking action a now, then playing optimally forever after
max_a' Q*(s',a')rewriting V*(s') in terms of Q* itself — the same quantity, one step later

Notice the relationship between the two: \(V^*(s) = \max_a Q^*(s,a)\) — the best value of a state is just the value of its best action. This particular rewriting, expressing \(Q^*\) recursively in terms of itself one step later rather than in terms of \(V^*\), is the exact identity Q-learning's update rule is built from directly — the subject of the next lesson.

A deliberately tiny MDP: three states, two actions per state, one genuinely stochastic transition (opening a position lands you Long or Short with equal probability, representing not knowing which way the market moves next). Everything below is solved from this exact diagram.
🧮
Worked example

Solving the 3-state MDP by hand with value iteration

Take \(\gamma=0.9\). Value iteration starts every state's value at zero and repeatedly applies the Bellman optimality equation as an update rule — plug in last sweep's values, get this sweep's values, using \(V_{k+1}(s) = \max_a \sum_{s'} P(s'|s,a)[R(s,a,s')+\gamma V_k(s')]\):

0

Initialize

\(V_0(\text{Flat})=0,\ V_0(\text{Long})=0,\ V_0(\text{Short})=0\).

1

Sweep 1

Flat: \(\max(0+0.9(0),\ 0.5(-0.1+0.9(0))+0.5(-0.1+0.9(0))) = \max(0,-0.1)=0\) — Stay still ties the untested Open branch. Long: \(\max(1+0.9(0),\ -0.1+0.9(0)) = \max(1,-0.1)=1\) — Hold. Short: \(\max(-0.5+0.9(0),\ -0.1+0.9(0))=\max(-0.5,-0.1)=-0.1\) — Close.

\(V_1\) = (Flat: 0, Long: 1, Short: −0.1)

2

Sweep 2

Flat's Open branch now sees Long and Short's improved values: \(0.5(-0.1+0.9(1))+0.5(-0.1+0.9(-0.1)) = 0.5(0.80)+0.5(-0.19)=0.305\), beating Stay's \(0+0.9(0)=0\) for the first time. Long: \(\max(1+0.9(1),-0.1+0.9(0))=1.9\). Short: \(\max(-0.5+0.9(-0.1),-0.1+0.9(0))=\max(-0.59,-0.1)=-0.1\).

\(V_2\) = (Flat: 0.305, Long: 1.9, Short: −0.1)

3

Sweeps 3–4, and the policy locks in

\(V_3\) = (Flat: 0.71, Long: 2.71, Short: 0.1745). \(V_4\) = (Flat: 1.198, Long: 3.439, Short: 0.539). From sweep 2 onward the arg max at every state stops changing — Open at Flat, Hold at Long, Close at Short. The values keep climbing every sweep, but the greedy policy is already settled.

4

Solve the settled policy exactly

Once the policy has stopped changing, its three Bellman equations become an ordinary linear system — solving it exactly is equivalent to running the sweep infinitely many times. Writing \(x=V^*(\text{Flat})\), \(y=V^*(\text{Short})\), and using \(V^*(\text{Long})=1+0.9\,V^*(\text{Long}) \Rightarrow V^*(\text{Long})=10\):

\[ y = -0.1+0.9x, \qquad x = -0.1+0.45(10+y) \]

Substituting and solving gives the converged fixed point:

\[ V^*(\text{Flat})\approx 7.319,\quad V^*(\text{Long})=10,\quad V^*(\text{Short})\approx 6.487 \]

Sanity-check every state against its own equation: at Long, Hold (\(10\)) beats Close (\(-0.1+0.9(7.319)\approx6.487\)) — correctly stay long. At Short, Close (\(6.487\)) beats Hold (\(-0.5+0.9(6.487)\approx5.339\)) — correctly cut a losing position rather than sit in it. At Flat, Open (\(7.319\)) beats Stay (\(0.9\times7.319\approx6.587\)) — correctly seek exposure, since the expected payoff of finding out which side wins is positive. The numbers alone reconstruct a policy that looks like ordinary trading sense: don't sit flat forever, hold winners, cut losers — derived here purely from the reward structure, not asserted.

🎯
Real-world trading scenario

Why the discount factor is doing real work here

Suppose \(\gamma\) were \(0\) instead of \(0.9\) — a fully myopic trader who only cares about the very next reward. At Long, Hold's value collapses to just \(1\) (no credit for repeating that reward forever), and Close's value is \(-0.1\); Hold still wins, but the two options are no longer separated by nearly the entire compounding horizon the way they are at \(\gamma=0.9\), where Hold's value of \(10\) versus Close's \(6.487\) reflects the fact that staying long captures many future \(+1\) rewards, discounted but still substantial. This is the formal version of an intuition every systematic trader already has: how patient your evaluation horizon is changes which action looks best, and a value function makes that patience an explicit, tunable number instead of a vague feeling.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

Value iteration and its close cousin policy iteration (alternating "evaluate the current policy exactly" with "improve it by acting greedily on those values") are the textbook methods for solving an MDP exactly — but both require knowing \(P(s'|s,a)\) and \(R(s,a,s')\) up front, i.e. a full model of the environment. That's the entire reason the field moved beyond this chapter: real environments (a market, a robot, a game against an opponent) rarely hand you an explicit transition-probability table. Richard Bellman himself developed this framework in the 1950s at RAND Corporation for engineering and operations-research problems — inventory control, scheduling — where the model genuinely was known; extending it to problems where the model has to be learned from experience is exactly the subject of the next lesson.

Even where the transition model isn't known, the value-function framing itself is what practitioners keep: dynamic programming, model-predictive control, and even a simple decision tree evaluated by backward induction are all doing some version of "recursively relate a state's value to its successors' values." The Bellman equation is less a specific algorithm and more the master identity that every method in this family — from hand-solved toy MDPs to deep Q-networks — is a different way of approximating.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where the MDP framing quietly goes wrong

Assuming a market is actually Markov. The whole derivation rests on the current state capturing everything relevant. Real price processes carry momentum, volatility clustering, and regime memory that a naively small state (just "current position," say) doesn't capture — which understates how much history actually matters and can make a fitted MDP badly misspecified. Enlarging the state to include recent history or a regime label is the usual fix, at the cost of a much larger state space.

Treating \(V^\pi\) as if it were achievable, not just expected. The value function is an expectation over an entire distribution of future paths. A single realized trajectory can and will look nothing like the average — exactly the same "expected value hides the whole distribution" trap the Kelly lesson raises about a bet's expected value versus its long-run compounded growth.

Picking \(\gamma\) without thinking about it. \(\gamma\) is not a free technical knob — it encodes how much future reward genuinely matters to the decision-maker, and the optimal policy can change qualitatively depending on it (a myopic \(\gamma\) near \(0\) versus a patient \(\gamma\) near \(1\) can favor entirely different actions, as the scenario above shows even in a 3-state toy).

Section 04

Key Takeaways

✓An MDP is \((S,A,P,R,\gamma)\). States, actions, transition probabilities, rewards, and a discount factor — the standard formalism for any sequential decision problem.
✓The Bellman equation is a recursive identity, not an extra assumption. It falls straight out of splitting an infinite discounted sum into "this step's reward" plus "the same sum, one step later."
✓Optimality replaces averaging with maximizing. \(V^*(s)=\max_a\sum_{s'}P(s'|s,a)[R+\gamma V^*(s')]\) — the best policy always takes the action with the highest expected continuation value.
✓\(Q^*(s,a)\) scores actions directly, and \(V^*(s)=\max_a Q^*(s,a)\). This exact rewriting — \(Q^*\) in terms of itself one step later — is what Q-learning's update rule is built from.
✓Value iteration converges to a fixed point that also encodes the optimal policy. In the worked example the greedy action at each state stabilizes long before the values themselves fully converge.
⚠This whole chapter assumes the model (\(P\) and \(R\)) is known. Real problems almost never hand you an explicit transition table — that gap is exactly what the next lesson's learning rule is for.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. Where does the Bellman equation actually come from — is it an additional modeling assumption on top of the value function's definition?
No — it's a direct algebraic consequence of the value function's definition as an infinite discounted sum. Splitting off the first reward term and re-indexing the rest of the sum shows the remaining part is exactly the value function again, evaluated one step later at whatever state the process transitions to. No new assumption is introduced; the recursion is just what an infinite geometric-style sum looks like when you peel off its first term.
Q2. What's the one change that turns the (policy-evaluating) Bellman equation into the Bellman optimality equation?
Replacing the average over the policy's action probabilities, \(\sum_a \pi(a|s)(\cdots)\), with a maximization over actions, \(\max_a(\cdots)\). Averaging describes how good a specific, already-fixed policy is; maximizing describes the best value achievable over every possible policy, since the optimal policy by definition always picks whichever action yields the highest expected continuation value.
Q3. In the worked 3-state MDP, why does the optimal policy Hold at Long but Close at Short, even though both states offer a "Hold" action?
Because the two Hold rewards have opposite signs and both compound under the discount factor: holding Long earns +1 every period it's repeated, which under γ=0.9 sums to a converged value of 10 — far above what closing to Flat and re-opening would earn. Holding Short earns −0.5 every period, which compounds into a much worse value (≈5.34) than cutting the loss and returning to Flat (≈6.49). The sign and size of the per-period reward, run through the same discounting math, is what separates "let it ride" from "cut it," not any special-casing of the action names.
Q4. Why does value iteration's greedy policy often stabilize before the values themselves have fully converged?
The values keep growing (or shrinking) toward their true fixed point on every sweep because each update only partially propagates the eventual total; but once one action's estimated value pulls decisively ahead of its alternatives at a given state, further sweeps just make all the estimates more accurate together — the ranking between actions was already decided. In the worked example the arg max settles (Open / Hold / Close) by sweep 2, while the actual numeric values keep visibly rising for several more sweeps after that.
Q5. What has to be known in advance to run value iteration exactly as shown in this lesson, and why is that a real limitation?
The full transition probabilities \(P(s'|s,a)\) and reward function \(R(s,a,s')\) for every state-action pair — a complete model of the environment. Real decision problems (trading included) essentially never hand you this model explicitly; it would have to be estimated from data, and any estimation error propagates directly into the computed value function. This is exactly the gap the next lesson's method (learning values from sampled experience, without ever needing an explicit \(P\) or \(R\)) is designed to close.
