The Ornstein-Uhlenbeck Process & Mean Reversion

A price that "wants" to go somewhere and a price that just wanders are different mathematical objects — the OU process is the equation that tells them apart, and gives mean reversion an actual speed.

CONCEPT — not yet fit/tested in this repo ~15 min read Prereqs: none required, EMH lesson helpful for framing
TL;DR
  • OU process: \(dX_t = \theta(\mu - X_t)\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t\) — a drift term that pulls back toward \(\mu\) at speed \(\theta\), plus random noise scaled by \(\sigma\); setting \(\theta = 0\) collapses it to a plain random walk.
  • Half-life of mean reversion is \(t_{1/2} = \ln(2)/\theta\), derived by solving the drift-only decay \(\mathbb{E}[X_t-\mu] = (X_0-\mu)e^{-\theta t}\) for the time the expected deviation shrinks by half.
  • To fit this to daily data you regress an AR(1), \(X_t = \mu(1-\phi) + \phi X_{t-1} + \epsilon_t\), then convert \(\phi \to \theta\) via \(\theta = -\ln(\phi)/\Delta t\); the worked example uses \(\phi = 0.90 \Rightarrow \theta \approx 0.105\)/day \(\Rightarrow\) half-life \(\approx 6.58\) days (and contrasts \(\phi=0.98 \Rightarrow \approx 34.3\) days).
  • The interactive slider defaults to \(\theta = 0.15\), showing a half-life of \(\approx 4.6\) days for the simulated mean-reverting path.
Section 01

Why this matters

Half of trading-strategy design boils down to one question asked over and over at different timescales: if price is away from "normal," does it tend to come back, and if so how fast? "Fade the move" and "follow the move" are opposite bets on the answer. Right now in this codebase that question is answered by a same-day heuristic — this project's own trend-day-ness score T = drift ÷ diffusion, blended from several rolling-window estimators (efficiency ratio, variance ratio, Hurst exponent, and friends). Those are all reasonable proxies, but none of them produce a number you can say out loud to a non-quant: "this pair tends to revert halfway back to its mean every N days."

The Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) process is the textbook model that does produce that number. It is not a trading strategy by itself — it is a precise mathematical description of "mean reversion" that lets you (a) test whether a series reverts at all, (b) measure how fast, in real time units, and (c) compute a half-life you can compare across instruments and horizons. It shows up constantly in the literature this repo already leans on for regime and reversion-vs-continuation logic, so it is worth having the actual equation in your head rather than just the word "mean-reverting" as a vibe.

💡
Plain-English intuition

A rubber band attached to a moving average

Picture a ball tied to a fixed post by a rubber band, sitting in a tub of water that's constantly being jostled by random currents. Two things act on the ball every instant: the rubber band, which pulls it back toward the post in proportion to how far away it currently is (the further away, the harder the pull), and the random jostling, which pushes it in an unpredictable direction by an unpredictable amount. The ball never sits still at the post — the jostling keeps knocking it away — but it also never wanders off to infinity, because the rubber band always drags it back. Its position over time traces out a wobbly path that hovers around the post forever.

That is an OU process. The "post" is the long-run mean \(\mu\) (mu). The "stiffness of the rubber band" is the mean-reversion speed \(\theta\) (theta) — a bigger \(\theta\) means a stiffer band, meaning the price snaps back faster. The "random jostling" is Brownian noise scaled by \(\sigma\) (sigma), the same volatility concept used everywhere else in this repo. A plain random walk (ordinary Brownian motion, the model behind "prices don't have memory") is the special case where you cut the rubber band entirely: \(\theta = 0\), nothing pulls the ball back, and it really can wander off forever.

Both paths start at the same point and wander with similar-looking noise bar to bar. The difference only shows up over time: the OU path (\(\theta > 0\)) keeps getting reeled back toward its long-run mean \(\mu\) every time it strays far, while the random walk (\(\theta = 0\)) has nothing pulling it back and drifts arbitrarily far away. That pull-back, not the noise, is the entire visual signature of \(\theta > 0\).
Section 02

The math, step by step

The OU process is defined by a stochastic differential equation (SDE) — an equation that describes how a random quantity changes over an infinitesimally small instant of time \(dt\). If SDE notation is new to you, read it exactly like a normal differential equation ("the rate of change of \(X\) equals...") except one of the terms is explicitly random noise instead of a deterministic force. Here it is:

\[ dX_t = \theta(\mu - X_t)\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t \]
θ(μ − Xₜ)
The drift
The predictable pull back toward μ, stronger the further away you are
σ dWₜ
The diffusion
The random, unpredictable jostle — same σ as everywhere else in this repo's vol math
dXₜ
The result
The next tiny move — predictable nudge plus random shock

Read this left to right: dX_t is the tiny change in the process's value \(X\) that happens over the next tiny instant of time. The right-hand side says that change is the sum of two pieces — a predictable "drift" piece and a random "diffusion" piece. Every symbol, spelled out:

\(X_t\)The value of the process at time \(t\) — think "today's price," or more precisely a price-like quantity such as a spread, a z-scored deviation from a moving average, or a log-price. OU is usually applied to something that plausibly has a stable center, not to a raw trending price level.
\(dX_t\)The infinitesimal change in \(X\) over the next instant — "how much \(X\) moves in the next tiny tick of time."
\(\mu\)(mu) The long-run mean — the level \(X\) is being pulled toward. This is the "post" the rubber band is tied to. Estimated in practice as a sample mean, a moving average, or a fair-value/equilibrium level from some other model.
\(\theta\)(theta) The mean-reversion speed, a positive number. Larger \(\theta\) = stiffer pull back to \(\mu\) = faster reversion. \(\theta = 0\) means no pull at all — see the random-walk comparison below. \(\theta\) has units of "1 / time" (e.g. "per day"), which is exactly why it converts cleanly into a half-life measured in days.
\((\mu - X_t)\)The current deviation from the mean, with its sign. If price is above \(\mu\), this term is negative, pulling \(X\) down; if below \(\mu\), it's positive, pulling \(X\) up. This is the "restoring force," structurally identical to Hooke's law for a spring — the further you are from equilibrium, the harder you're pulled back, and the pull is always toward equilibrium, never away.
\(\theta(\mu - X_t)\,dt\)The drift term: the deterministic, predictable part of the next instant's move — restoring speed \(\times\) current deviation \(\times\) the length of the instant.
\(\sigma\)(sigma) The volatility (diffusion) coefficient — how large the random shocks are. This is the same conceptual quantity as the \(\sigma\) used throughout this repo's vol math, just in continuous-time SDE units rather than daily-bar HV/GARCH units.
\(W_t\)A Wiener process (standard Brownian motion) — the formal name for "pure random-walk noise with independent, normally distributed increments and no memory." \(dW_t\) is its infinitesimal increment: mean zero, variance \(dt\), unpredictable direction.
\(\sigma\,dW_t\)The diffusion term: the random, unpredictable part of the next instant's move, scaled by how volatile the process is.

So the whole equation says, in one sentence: the next tiny move in \(X\) is a predictable nudge back toward \(\mu\), sized by how far away you currently are and how fast you revert, plus an unpredictable random shock sized by volatility. Contrast this directly with plain Brownian motion / a random walk, which is the special case \(\theta = 0\):

\[ dX_t = \sigma\,dW_t \quad (\text{random walk: no drift term, no memory of } \mu) \]
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Every tiny step, the price does two things at once: it gets a little shove back toward "normal" (bigger shove if it's currently far from normal), and it gets a random jolt in some unpredictable direction. Drift is the shove; diffusion is the jolt. A random walk is what's left if you remove the shove entirely — nothing but jolts, so there's nothing keeping it near any particular level.

OU process (θ > 0)

Has both a drift term pulling back toward \(\mu\) and a diffusion term. Bounded variance over time — settles into a stable, stationary distribution.

Random walk (θ = 0)

Diffusion only, no drift — nothing "knows" where \(\mu\) is. Variance grows without bound as \(t\to\infty\); the process can wander arbitrarily far from any starting point.

With no drift term, there is nothing in the equation that "knows" where \(\mu\) is or pulls back toward it. A random walk can wander arbitrarily far from any starting point — its variance grows without bound as \(t \to \infty\). This is precisely the mathematical content of the weak-form Efficient Market Hypothesis: prices, if efficient, should behave like this term alone, with no exploitable pull back to a mean. Whether real FX prices behave more like the full OU equation (some \(\theta > 0\)) or the pure random walk (\(\theta = 0\)) at a given horizon is exactly the empirical question mean-reversion strategies are trying to answer — and exactly what a Hurst exponent or variance-ratio test (the next lesson) is designed to detect.

The stationary distribution — where does \(X\) settle?

Because the drift term always pulls back toward \(\mu\), an OU process does not blow up over time the way a random walk does. Instead, given long enough, its distribution converges to a stable, unchanging (stationary) normal distribution centered at \(\mu\):

\[ X_\infty \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(\mu,\ \frac{\sigma^2}{2\theta}\right) \]
μthe settled-in distribution's center — same long-run mean as before
σ²/2θthe stationary variance — how wide the resting spread is

The stationary variance \(\sigma^2/(2\theta)\) tells you how far \(X\) typically wanders from \(\mu\) once it has "settled in": more noise (\(\sigma\)) widens the resting spread, but a stronger pull-back (\(\theta\)) tightens it — a stiffer rubber band keeps the ball closer to the post even with the same amount of jostling. This is the formal reason "high vol" and "fast mean reversion" are not opposites: a process can be both loud (large \(\sigma\)) and quick to revert (large \(\theta\)), and the two combine into how wide its resting range actually is.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Leave the ball on its rubber band jiggling long enough, and it settles into a stable "typical range" around the post — not a fixed point, since the jostling never stops, but a predictable cloud of likely positions. A stronger rubber band (\(\theta\)) squeezes that cloud tighter; rougher jostling (\(\sigma\)) spreads it wider. So "high volatility" and "fast mean reversion" can both be true at once — loud jostling plus a strong band still keeps the ball fairly close to home.

Deriving the half-life

The single most useful number that falls out of this equation is the half-life of mean reversion: how long it takes for the expected deviation from \(\mu\) to shrink to half its current size. Ignoring the random noise term for a moment (it averages to zero in expectation) and solving the drift-only ODE \( \frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}[X_t - \mu] = -\theta\,\mathbb{E}[X_t - \mu] \) gives an exponential decay of the expected deviation:

\[ \mathbb{E}[X_t - \mu] = (X_0 - \mu)\,e^{-\theta t} \]
X₀ − μthe deviation you start with, right now
e^(−θt)exponential decay factor — shrinks toward 0 as t grows

This says: whatever the initial deviation \((X_0 - \mu)\) is, its expected size shrinks exponentially over time, at rate \(\theta\). To find the half-life \(t_{1/2}\) — the time at which the expected deviation has shrunk to exactly half — set the decay factor equal to \( \tfrac{1}{2}\) and solve for \(t\):

\[ e^{-\theta\,t_{1/2}} = \frac{1}{2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad -\theta\,t_{1/2} = \ln\!\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) = -\ln(2) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln(2)}{\theta} \]
ln(2) ≈ 0.693a fixed constant — same one in radioactive-decay half-life formulas
t₁/₂ = ln(2)/θtime for the expected deviation to shrink by half

Walking through that: take the natural log of both sides of \(e^{-\theta t_{1/2}} = 1/2\). The left side becomes \(-\theta t_{1/2}\) (log undoes the exponential). The right side, \(\ln(1/2)\), equals \(-\ln(2)\) by the log rule \(\ln(1/x) = -\ln(x)\). The two negative signs cancel, leaving \(\theta t_{1/2} = \ln(2)\), and dividing both sides by \(\theta\) isolates the half-life. \(\ln(2) \approx 0.6931\) is just a constant that falls out of the algebra — it is the same constant that shows up in radioactive-decay half-life and drug-elimination half-life formulas, because they are the same exponential-decay math applied to a different physical quantity.

Intuitively: a half-life is not "the time until the deviation is fully gone" — under continuous exponential decay it's never exactly zero, only asymptotically closer. It's "the time until you'd expect the current gap to \(\mu\) to be cut in half." A short half-life (say, 1 day) describes a snappy, fast-reverting series where a deviation today is mostly gone within a couple of days. A long half-life (say, 60 days) describes a lazy reversion — technically present, but far too slow to trade on a multi-day horizon; by the time it pulls back, the world has moved on. As \(\theta \to 0\), \(t_{1/2} = \ln(2)/\theta \to \infty\): the half-life blows up to infinity, consistent with the random-walk limit having no reversion at all — there is no time by which a deviation is "expected" to shrink, because nothing is pulling it back.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Half-life answers one question: "how long until this gap to normal is cut in half?" It's the same idea as a radioactive substance's half-life, just applied to a price gap instead of atoms decaying. A short half-life means a snappy series that closes gaps fast; a long half-life means a lazy one where today's gap is still mostly there weeks from now.

The discrete-time analogue: AR(1)

You almost never fit the continuous-time SDE directly to daily bars — instead you fit its well-known discrete-time twin, the first-order autoregressive model AR(1), and convert. If you sample an OU process at regular intervals \(\Delta t\) (e.g. \(\Delta t = 1\) day), the exact solution to the SDE is:

\[ X_t = \mu(1-\phi) + \phi\,X_{t-1} + \epsilon_t, \qquad \phi = e^{-\theta \Delta t} \]
φthe AR(1) coefficient — an ordinary regression estimate
ε_tthe regression's noise term — discrete-time stand-in for σdWₜ

Here \(\phi\) (phi) is the AR(1) autoregressive coefficient — the ordinary "regress today's value on yesterday's value" coefficient you'd get from a linear regression of \(X_t\) on \(X_{t-1}\) — and \(\epsilon_t\) is the regression's noise term (the discrete-time stand-in for \(\sigma\,dW_t\)). This equation should look familiar: it's exactly the AR(1) form behind this project's own framing of drift versus diffusion, just written out with the mean term made explicit. The relationship \(\phi = e^{-\theta \Delta t}\) is the bridge between "a regression coefficient you can estimate from data" and "a mean-reversion speed with physical time units," and inverting it gives you \(\theta\) from a fitted \(\phi\):

\[ \theta = -\frac{\ln(\phi)}{\Delta t} \]
−ln(φ)negative log of the fitted persistence coefficient
Δtthe sampling interval — e.g. 1 day for daily bars

Reading the map between the two worlds: \(\phi\) close to 1 means today's value is almost entirely explained by yesterday's value with little pull back to \(\mu\) — slow reversion, small \(\theta\), long half-life. \(\phi\) close to 0 means almost no persistence — the series snaps back to \(\mu\) almost immediately, large \(\theta\), short half-life. \(\phi \geq 1\) is not mean-reverting at all (\(\phi = 1\) is exactly the random walk; \(\phi > 1\) is explosive) — the log in \(\theta = -\ln(\phi)/\Delta t\) is undefined or negative there, which is the algebra's way of telling you the OU model doesn't apply.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

You almost never fit the fancy continuous-time equation directly — instead you run the simplest regression there is, "predict today's value from yesterday's," and then translate the result into the rubber-band language. A coefficient close to 1 means yesterday almost fully explains today (weak pull-back, slow reversion); a coefficient close to 0 means yesterday barely matters (strong pull-back, fast reversion).

The trap: a fitted \(\phi \ge 1\) doesn't mean "very slow mean reversion" — it means the OU model doesn't apply at all. Forcing a half-life number out of an undefined or negative \(\theta\) produces a meaningless result, not a conservative one.
🧮
Worked example

From a fitted AR(1) coefficient to a half-life in days

Suppose you take a candidate mean-reverting series — say, a z-scored spread or a deviation-from-moving-average series on daily closes — and run the simplest possible regression: today's value against yesterday's value, \(X_t = c + \phi X_{t-1} + \epsilon_t\). Suppose the fit comes back with \(\phi = 0.90\) (a plausible, moderately-persistent daily value; a pure noise series would fit closer to \(\phi = 0\), a near-random-walk series closer to \(\phi = 1\)).

Step 1 — convert to the continuous-time reversion speed, with \(\Delta t = 1\) day since the data is daily:

\[ \theta = -\frac{\ln(0.90)}{1} \]

\(\ln(0.90) \approx -0.10536\) (the natural log of a number just under 1 is a small negative number — you can sanity-check this because \(\ln(1) = 0\) exactly, and \(\ln\) is a smooth, slowly-changing function near 1). Negating that gives:

\[ \theta \approx 0.10536 \ \text{per day} \]

Step 2 — convert \(\theta\) to a half-life using the formula derived above:

\[ t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln(2)}{\theta} \approx \frac{0.6931}{0.10536} \approx 6.58 \ \text{days} \]

So a daily AR(1) fit of \(\phi = 0.90\) implies a half-life of roughly 6.6 trading days: if this series is 40 pips away from its estimated long-run mean today, the OU model's expectation is that the deviation will be down to roughly 20 pips in about 6–7 trading days (assuming \(\mu\) itself doesn't move — a real fair-value or moving-average mean typically does drift, which is a limitation covered in the pitfalls below), roughly 10 pips after another 6–7 days, and so on. For comparison, if the same regression had come back with \(\phi = 0.98\) (much more persistent), the half-life would stretch to \(\ln(2)/(-\ln(0.98)) \approx 0.6931/0.0202 \approx 34.3\) days — a genuinely slow reversion, far too sluggish to be the basis of a multi-day fade trade, even though the series is still technically mean-reverting in the statistical sense.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

OU fitting shows up most visibly in statistical arbitrage / pairs trading: you construct a spread between two related instruments (e.g. a hedge ratio between two correlated FX crosses, or a cointegrated equity pair), then fit an OU process to that spread's history. The fitted \(\theta\) tells you whether the spread mean-reverts fast enough to trade, the half-life tells you a sane holding-period expectation, and the stationary standard deviation \(\sqrt{\sigma^2/2\theta}\) gives you natural entry/exit thresholds in the same way a Bollinger Band or z-score gives thresholds — e.g. enter when the spread is 2 stationary-standard-deviations from \(\mu\), exit near \(\mu\). Avellaneda & Lee's statistical arbitrage paper (cited below) is the standard reference for exactly this workflow applied to equity residuals.

More generally, quant desks use the half-life as a horizon filter before anything else: if a signal's estimated half-life is 2 days, a strategy that holds for 3 weeks is fighting the wrong timescale, and a strategy that exits in 10 minutes never lets the edge play out. The half-life is a principled way to pick "how long should I hold this trade" instead of guessing or hand-tuning it. It's also used diagnostically: if you fit OU to a series and get \(\phi \geq 1\) (or a negative/undefined \(\theta\)), that's evidence the series is behaving like a random walk rather than mean-reverting — a useful, humbling null result rather than something to explain away.

A standard caveat practitioners build in from day one: real \(\phi\) estimates from short, noisy financial samples are themselves noisy and biased toward 1 in finite samples (this is a known small-sample bias in AR(1)/unit-root estimation). A single point estimate of \(\theta\) or a half-life should come with a confidence interval or a bootstrap, not be treated as a fixed physical constant — and per this repo's validation discipline, any resulting trading rule needs the same in-sample/out-of-sample split and minimum trade count as everything else here, not just a good-looking in-sample fit.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where OU fitting goes wrong in practice

Treating \(\mu\) as fixed when it isn't. The whole derivation assumes a constant long-run mean. Real fair-value levels, moving averages, and cointegrating relationships drift over time. Fitting OU to a series whose "true" mean has quietly shifted will misestimate \(\theta\) — often making reversion look stronger (or weaker) than it really is, because some of what looks like "pulling back to the old mean" is actually "chasing a new mean." Re-estimate \(\mu\) on a rolling basis and check it isn't the dominant source of the fit.

Small-sample bias in \(\phi\). OLS estimates of AR(1) coefficients on short, noisy samples are biased toward 1 (toward "looks more like a random walk than it is"), which pushes the implied half-life artificially high. This gets worse the more autocorrelated and the shorter the sample. Don't trust a single-sample point estimate; check stability across sub-periods or use a bias-corrected estimator.

Confusing "the null can't be rejected" with "it's a random walk." Failing to statistically distinguish \(\phi\) from 1 in a small sample is not the same as proving \(\theta = 0\) — it may just mean you don't have enough data to tell a slow-but-real reversion from none at all. This is the same logic as failing to reject the EMH lesson's null: absence of evidence isn't evidence of absence.

Regime dependence. \(\theta\), \(\mu\), and \(\sigma\) are all assumed constant in the basic OU model, but real market reversion speed changes across regimes — a spread might revert briskly in calm markets and barely at all in a trending or crisis regime. A single full-sample \(\theta\) averages across regimes and can be a poor guide to what's happening right now, echoing this repo's general caution about pooled-sample nulls hiding subset behavior.

Half-life is an expectation about the mean path, not a promise about any one trade. \(t_{1/2}\) describes how the expected value of the deviation decays; any individual path is still buffeted by \(\sigma\,dW_t\) and can widen before it narrows, or never fully revert within your holding period. Sizing a stop or a max-hold purely off the half-life without accounting for the noise term's dispersion is a common way to get stopped out right before the "expected" reversion would have arrived.

Section 04

Key Takeaways

✓The OU equation is drift plus diffusion. \(dX_t = \theta(\mu-X_t)dt + \sigma dW_t\) — a predictable pull back toward \(\mu\), sized by \(\theta\), plus unpredictable noise sized by \(\sigma\).
✓A random walk is the special case θ = 0. Cut the "rubber band" entirely and OU collapses to plain Brownian motion — the weak-form EMH's baseline model.
✓Half-life turns θ into a number you can say out loud. \(t_{1/2}=\ln(2)/\theta\) — the time for an expected deviation to shrink by half, comparable across instruments and horizons.
✓Fit it via AR(1), not the raw SDE. Regress \(X_t\) on \(X_{t-1}\) to get \(\phi\), then convert with \(\theta=-\ln(\phi)/\Delta t\) — the worked example's \(\phi=0.90\) implies a ≈6.6-day half-life.
⚠φ ≥ 1 means the model doesn't apply, not "very slow reversion." The half-life formula breaks down (undefined or negative θ) — that's the algebra telling you to stop, not to force a number.
⚠θ, μ and σ are all assumed constant — real markets don't cooperate. A drifting "true" mean, small-sample bias toward φ≈1, and regime-dependent reversion speed can all distort a single full-sample fit.
⚠Nothing here is built or validated in this repo. This is a research candidate, not a described feature — it would need synthetic-data unit tests and a real OOS trade count before touching real prices.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. What happens to the OU process, and to its half-life, as \(\theta \to 0\)?
As \(\theta \to 0\), the drift term \(\theta(\mu - X_t)\,dt\) shrinks toward zero regardless of how far \(X\) is from \(\mu\) — the "rubber band" goes slack. The SDE collapses to \(dX_t = \sigma\,dW_t\), pure Brownian motion with no pull back to any mean: a random walk. Correspondingly, the half-life \(t_{1/2} = \ln(2)/\theta\) grows without bound (division by a number approaching zero) — there is no finite time by which the deviation is expected to halve, consistent with there being no reversion at all.
Q2. Why is a random walk considered a limiting (degenerate) case of the OU process rather than a completely different model?
Because it falls directly out of the same SDE by setting one parameter, \(\theta\), to zero — nothing else about the equation's structure changes. This means OU strictly generalizes the random walk: every random walk is an OU process with \(\theta = 0\), but not every OU process is a random walk. Practically, this is why fitting OU (or its AR(1) analogue) to data and finding \(\phi \approx 1\) (equivalently \(\theta \approx 0\)) is exactly the statistical signature of "this series doesn't detectably mean-revert" — you're not switching models, you're finding the fitted parameter sitting at the edge of the same model's parameter space.
Q3. A spread has an estimated half-life of 3 trading days. What does that actually tell a trader about holding period, and what does it NOT tell them?
It tells them that, on average, the current deviation from the estimated mean is expected to shrink by half roughly every 3 trading days — so a trade betting on reversion to \(\mu\) has a natural timescale of a few days to a couple of weeks, not minutes and not months; holding much longer than several half-lives captures rapidly diminishing expected benefit since the deviation is expected to already be mostly gone. It does NOT tell them that any individual trade will actually revert on that schedule — the diffusion term \(\sigma\,dW_t\) means any specific path can widen further before narrowing, revert faster, or fail to revert within a reasonable window at all. Nor does it account for the mean \(\mu\) itself potentially drifting during the trade.
Q4. If an AR(1) regression on daily data returns \(\phi = 1.02\), what does that mean for the OU half-life formula, and what should a careful analyst conclude?
\(\phi = 1.02 > 1\) implies \(\ln(\phi) > 0\), which makes \(\theta = -\ln(\phi)/\Delta t\) negative — a negative mean-reversion speed, which is nonsensical in the OU model (it would imply the "restoring force" pushes away from the mean, i.e. explosive, not mean-reverting). The half-life formula breaks down because the model no longer applies: a careful analyst should conclude the series shows no evidence of stationary mean reversion in this sample (it looks explosive or at best like a random walk with estimation noise pushing the point estimate slightly above 1), not attempt to force a half-life number out of it. This is also a strong cue to check for small-sample bias and re-estimate rather than trust a single point estimate near the \(\phi = 1\) boundary.
Q5. Why does the stationary variance formula \(\sigma^2/(2\theta)\) make intuitive sense — specifically, why does a bigger \(\theta\) mean a SMALLER resting spread?
\(\theta\) sits in the denominator, so increasing it decreases the stationary variance. Intuitively, \(\theta\) is how hard the process gets pulled back toward \(\mu\) whenever noise knocks it away. A stiffer restoring force (larger \(\theta\)) corrects deviations faster, so the process doesn't have time to wander as far from \(\mu\) before being pulled back in, even though the noise (\(\sigma\)) hitting it every instant is the same size. Conversely, for fixed \(\theta\), more noise (larger \(\sigma\)) widens the resting spread because there's simply more random energy pushing the ball away from the post for the same rubber band to fight against.
Q6. Why can't you just fit \(\theta\) once and treat it as a fixed, permanent property of an instrument or spread?
Because the constant-parameter assumption is itself a modeling simplification, not a fact about markets. Reversion speed plausibly changes across volatility regimes, changes if the underlying relationship driving the mean reversion (e.g. a hedge ratio, a cointegration link, a macro relationship) weakens or breaks, and is estimated with small-sample noise and bias in any given fit. A single \(\theta\) from one sample window is a snapshot, not a law — this repo's discipline around disaggregating pooled nulls and re-validating out-of-sample applies directly here: a fit that looked stable in one period needs to be checked again in fresh data, not assumed to persist.

  • Evan Gatev, William N. Goetzmann & K. Geert Rouwenhorst, "Pairs Trading: Performance of a Relative-Value Arbitrage Rule," Review of Financial Studies, 2006 — the widely-cited empirical study of distance-based pairs trading that the OU-based approach is often contrasted with.
  • Marco Avellaneda & Jeong-Hyun Lee, "Statistical Arbitrage in the U.S. Equities Market," Quantitative Finance, 2010 — explicitly fits an OU process to cointegration-residual spreads and uses the half-life as a trading-horizon filter; the direct template for the "fit OU, use the half-life" workflow described above.
