Market Impact Models & Almgren-Chriss

Trade a large order fast and you pay for the impact of demanding immediacy; trade it slow and you pay in exposure to whatever the market does while you wait — Almgren-Chriss turns that trade-off into a single dial and a precise, closed-form execution schedule.

CONCEPT — not built or tested in this repo ~18 min read Prereqs: Limit Order Books & Queue Dynamics
TL;DR
  • Temporary impact is the price concession needed to trade right now — it reverts once you stop trading. Permanent impact is the lasting price move your own trading causes, because it reveals information — it does not revert.
  • The empirically observed square-root law says impact scales roughly with \(\sqrt{Q/\text{ADV}}\), not linearly with size — a 4× larger order costs roughly 2× more to execute, not 4× more, which also means impact grows unexpectedly fast for even modest-sized orders.
  • Almgren-Chriss frames execution as minimizing expected cost (impact) plus a risk-aversion-weighted penalty on the variance of that cost (timing risk) — trading fast lowers timing risk but raises impact cost; trading slow does the reverse.
  • Solving that trade-off (via the Euler-Lagrange equation, derived below) gives a closed form: the optimal remaining-inventory trajectory is \(x(t) = X\,\sinh(\kappa(T-t))/\sinh(\kappa T)\), with \(\kappa=\sqrt{\lambda\sigma^2/\eta}\) folding risk aversion \(\lambda\), volatility \(\sigma\), and the temporary-impact coefficient \(\eta\) into one shape parameter.
  • At \(\kappa\to0\) (no risk aversion) the trajectory collapses to a straight line — constant-rate, TWAP-like trading. As \(\kappa\) grows, the trajectory becomes increasingly front-loaded: fast early, nearly flat later — a risk-averse trader accepts more impact cost to cut timing-risk exposure.
  • Worked example: with \(\sigma^2=0.0004\), \(\eta=0.0012\), a risk-neutral trader (\(\lambda=0\)) trades in a straight line; a moderately risk-averse trader (\(\lambda=27\), giving \(\kappa=3\)) has only about 21% of the order left at the halfway point, versus 50% for the straight-line trader.
Section 01

Why this matters

Limit Order Books & Queue Dynamics showed that a single market order walking the book pays a volume-weighted average price worse than the best quote it started at, and that the gap grows with order size. That lesson answered "what does one order cost, right now, given the book as it currently stands." This lesson answers the question a step up from that: if you have to execute a large order — far larger than the whole visible book — over some window of time, what is the best way to spread it out? Trade it all immediately and you pay the full walking-the-book cost from that lesson, all at once, concentrated into one instant. Trade it too slowly, spread thin over a long window, and you are instead exposed to ordinary price volatility for the entire time you're still holding the unexecuted remainder — the price might simply move against you for reasons that have nothing to do with your own trading. Almgren and Chriss's 2000 framework is the formal answer to that trade-off, and it is the standard reference point behind essentially every institutional execution algorithm's underlying logic.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Two ways to get soaked, and a dial that trades one off against the other

Imagine you need to sell a very large position — far more than anyone would absorb at the current price without it moving. You have two bad options at the extremes, and a whole spectrum in between.

Sell it all right now, in one shot: you walk deep through the order book (the previous lesson's mechanics), paying a large, immediate, and fairly predictable cost for demanding that much immediacy at once — this is impact cost. Alternatively, sell a tiny sliver every hour over the next month: each individual sale barely moves the price, so your impact cost per share is much lower — but you are now sitting on a large unsold position for a whole month, fully exposed to the ordinary up-and-down noise of the market the entire time, for reasons that have nothing to do with your own trading. If the price happens to drift against you during that month, that loss can dwarf anything you saved on impact. This is timing risk — the risk of exposure to volatility while you wait.

Neither extreme is obviously right; the correct answer depends on how much you personally dislike uncertainty relative to how much you dislike a large but predictable cost. Almgren-Chriss is exactly a formal version of that personal dial: turn it toward "I hate uncertainty" and the model tells you to front-load your selling, getting most of it done early even though that costs more in impact; turn it toward "I'm indifferent to uncertainty, just minimize expected cost" and the model tells you to spread evenly across the whole window instead.

Section 02

The math, step by step

1. Temporary impact vs. permanent impact

Every trade moves the observed price, but the reason it moves, and whether that move sticks around, splits into two genuinely different effects:

Temporary impact

The extra price concession needed to get your specific trade filled right now — walking the book, crossing the spread, demanding immediacy from whoever is willing to supply it. Once you stop trading, this component reverts: liquidity refills, the book recovers, and the price tends to drift back toward where it would have been without your trade.

Permanent impact

The lasting shift in the price that your own trading causes, which does not revert. The mechanism is exactly the adverse-selection logic from the Market Microstructure lesson: other participants (and the market maker) rationally update their belief about fundamental value partly because you traded — your order is itself informative, whether or not you personally possess any real information — so the new price genuinely reflects new (if only partially justified) beliefs, and stays there.

A useful way to see both at once: imagine you execute a large sell order over some window, then stop. The price will have fallen during that window from both effects combined. Immediately after you stop, part of that fall reverts back up — the temporary component unwinding as the book refills — while the price settles at a new level below where it started before you traded at all. That residual, permanent gap is the permanent impact; the part that bounced back is the temporary impact.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Press your thumb into a memory-foam pillow. Some of the dent springs back the moment you let go — that's temporary impact, the market's normal "give" recovering. But if pressing hard enough actually changed something about the foam itself, part of the dent would stay forever — that's permanent impact, because your trade told everyone something real about value, and nobody un-learns that.

2. The square-root law of impact

A large body of empirical market-impact research — across equities, futures, and FX, and across many independent studies — finds that realized impact does not scale linearly with order size the way Kyle's simple \(\Delta p=\lambda Q\) assumes at very large sizes. Instead, impact is well approximated by a square-root relationship between order size and the market's typical trading volume:

\[ I \approx Y\,\sigma\,\sqrt{\frac{Q}{\text{ADV}}} \]
Iimpact, typically expressed as a fraction of price (e.g. in basis points)
Yan empirical constant (order 1, varies by study/market) capturing everything else
σthe instrument's volatility over the relevant period
Q / ADVorder size as a fraction of average daily (or period) volume

The headline, non-obvious consequence: because \(\sqrt{x}\) is a concave function, impact grows fast for small orders and progressively more slowly for large ones, relative to any straight-line ("linear-in-size") extrapolation. Concretely — doubling order size does not double the cost; quadrupling order size roughly doubles the cost (since \(\sqrt{4Q}=2\sqrt{Q}\)). This has two practical readings that both matter and point in different directions: (1) very large orders are, per share, cheaper to execute than a naive linear model would suggest — there is real economy of scale in impact once you're already trading size, and (2) small-to-moderate orders already carry more impact per share than a linear model calibrated to a large reference size would predict, because the square-root curve sits above a straight chord connecting the origin to any larger reference point.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Think about pouring water into a glass versus a bathtub. Doubling how much water you pour into a small glass makes a big visible difference; doubling the same amount poured into a bathtub barely changes the water line. Big orders work the same way: as they get bigger, each extra chunk moves the price by proportionally less — but that also means a "small" order that looks harmless already costs more per share than a straight-line rule would guess, because the real relationship curves, it doesn't run in a straight line from zero.

Both curves are pinned to agree at 0 and at 0.5 ADV (141bps), by construction — that is what makes them comparable. In between, the concave square-root curve bows above the straight chord: at a quarter of ADV, the square-root law implies about 100bps of impact versus roughly 70bps for a naive linear model calibrated off the same reference size — a direct illustration of "smaller orders already cost more per share than a linear extrapolation suggests," which is the practically important reading of the square-root law.

3. The Almgren-Chriss trade-off: impact cost vs. timing risk

Set up the execution problem directly. You must liquidate \(X\) units (shares, contracts, whatever the tradable unit is) over a fixed horizon \([0,T]\). Let \(x(t)\) be the units still remaining unexecuted at time \(t\), so \(x(0)=X\) and the constraint \(x(T)=0\) says the whole order must be done by the end. The trading rate at any instant is \(-\dot x(t)\) (the speed at which remaining inventory shrinks).

Two costs compete. First, a temporary impact cost: trading faster (a larger \(|\dot x|\)) demands more immediacy and costs more, modeled as growing with the square of the trading rate (this quadratic form is the standard Almgren-Chriss simplification — it keeps the optimization tractable and penalizes very fast trading disproportionately, in the same spirit as variance penalizing large deviations in the earlier statistics primer):

\[ \text{Impact cost} = \int_0^T \tfrac{1}{2}\,\eta\,\dot x(t)^2\,dt \]
η(eta) the temporary-impact coefficient — cost per unit of trading-rate-squared
ẋ(t)the trading rate at time t — how fast remaining inventory is shrinking

Second, a timing-risk penalty: every unit still held is exposed to ordinary price volatility \(\sigma\) until it's sold, so the total exposure at any instant is proportional to how much inventory, \(x(t)\), is still sitting unexecuted, and the risk (variance) contributed scales with \(\sigma^2 x(t)^2\). A risk-aversion parameter \(\lambda\) controls how heavily that risk gets weighted against the impact cost above — this is the single "dial" from the intuition section:

\[ \text{Risk penalty} = \int_0^T \tfrac{1}{2}\,\lambda\,\sigma^2\,x(t)^2\,dt \]
λ(lambda) risk aversion — how much the trader penalizes variance relative to expected cost
σ² x(t)²variance contributed by still holding x(t) units, exposed to volatility σ

The trader's objective is to choose the whole trajectory \(x(t)\) to minimize the total of both costs together:

\[ \min_{x(t)}\ \int_0^T \left[\tfrac{1}{2}\eta\,\dot x(t)^2 + \tfrac{1}{2}\lambda\sigma^2\,x(t)^2\right] dt \qquad \text{s.t. } x(0)=X,\ x(T)=0 \]

Notice permanent impact does not appear in this optimization at all. That's a deliberate and well-known feature of the Almgren-Chriss result, not an omission: for a permanent-impact function that is linear in the trading rate, the total permanent-impact cost paid over the whole execution only depends on the total quantity traded (\(X\), fixed) and not on the shape of the trajectory used to trade it — it's a sunk cost every trajectory pays identically, so it drops out of the trajectory-choice problem entirely, even though it is a real and often large component of total execution cost.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

You have to sell a big pile of something. Sell it all in one dump and you get a bad price right now, but you're done fast. Sell it in tiny pieces over a month and each piece barely moves the price, but you're exposed the whole month to the price wandering around for reasons that have nothing to do with you. Almgren-Chriss is just the math for picking the right speed between those two extremes, based on how much you personally hate that month-long uncertainty.

4. Solving it: the Euler-Lagrange equation and the sinh/cosh trajectory

This is a standard calculus-of-variations problem — minimize an integral of a function of \(x(t)\) and \(\dot x(t)\) — solved with the Euler-Lagrange equation. Write the integrand as \(L = \tfrac12\eta\dot x^2 + \tfrac12\lambda\sigma^2 x^2\). The Euler-Lagrange condition, \(\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot x} - \frac{\partial L}{\partial x}=0\), applied term by term:

\[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot x} = \eta\dot x \ \Rightarrow\ \frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot x}\right) = \eta\ddot x \qquad\qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial x} = \lambda\sigma^2 x \]
\[ \eta\,\ddot x(t) - \lambda\sigma^2\,x(t) = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ddot x(t) = \kappa^2\, x(t), \qquad \kappa \equiv \sqrt{\frac{\lambda\sigma^2}{\eta}} \]
ẍ(t) = κ²x(t)a simple linear ODE — its solutions are exponentials, or equivalently sinh/cosh
κ(kappa) the risk-aversion-scaled time constant — the one number that sets the whole trajectory's shape

This differential equation's general solution is any combination of \(\sinh(\kappa t)\) and \(\cosh(\kappa t)\) (or equivalently \(e^{\kappa t}\) and \(e^{-\kappa t}\) — the same family used for the mean-reverting solutions in the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck lesson elsewhere in this series, just with the sign on the \(x\) term flipped, which turns exponential decay into this hyperbolic form instead). Apply the two boundary conditions — \(x(0)=X\) and \(x(T)=0\) — to pin down the constants, and the result simplifies to a single clean closed form:

\[ x(t) = X\,\frac{\sinh(\kappa(T-t))}{\sinh(\kappa T)} \]
x(t)
Remaining inventory
Units still left to trade at time t — starts at X, ends at 0
κ = √(λσ²/η)
The shape parameter
Folds risk aversion, volatility, and impact cost into one number
sinh(κ(T−t)) / sinh(κT)
The trajectory shape
A straight line at κ→0; increasingly front-loaded as κ grows

Two limits reveal the whole qualitative story. As \(\kappa\to0\) (risk-neutral, \(\lambda=0\)) — using the small-angle approximation \(\sinh(z)\approx z\) for small \(z\) — the trajectory collapses to \(x(t)\to X\frac{T-t}{T}\): a perfectly straight line, meaning a constant trading rate for the whole window, i.e. TWAP (time-weighted average price) execution. As \(\kappa\) grows large (increasingly risk-averse), \(\sinh\) grows exponentially, and the trajectory becomes increasingly front-loaded: a fast initial burst of trading that gets most of the order done early, followed by a long, nearly-flat tail where very little remains to be traded and therefore very little further timing risk remains either. This matches the intuition directly: a highly risk-averse trader is willing to accept a worse (higher) total expected impact cost specifically to get the position down faster and cut their exposure to timing risk sooner.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

κ is basically a dial for "how nervous are you." Turn it all the way down and you sell at a perfectly steady pace all day, like a metronome. Turn it up and you rush to sell most of it early, accepting a worse average price, just to stop worrying about what the market might do later in the day.

The corresponding trading rate, \(v(t) = -\dot x(t)\), follows directly by differentiating:

\[ v(t) = X\,\kappa\,\frac{\cosh(\kappa(T-t))}{\sinh(\kappa T)} \]
v(t)trading rate — units executed per unit time, at instant t
cosh(κ(T−t))at κ→0, cosh→1 everywhere: a flat, constant rate (TWAP); at high κ, this term peaks sharply at t=0

At \(\kappa=0\) this reduces to the constant rate \(X/T\) — TWAP again, from the rate side this time. At high \(\kappa\), the rate is highest at \(t=0\) and decays quickly, confirming the front-loaded shape from the inventory trajectory directly.

\(x(t)\)Remaining (unexecuted) inventory at time \(t\); \(x(0)=X\), \(x(T)=0\).
\(\dot x(t)\)The trading rate (rate of change of remaining inventory).

Worked example

Backing out κ, then comparing a straight-line trajectory to a front-loaded one

Take a desk that needs to execute an order over a normalized horizon \(T=1\) (one trading day). Assume a volatility of \(\sigma^2=0.0004\) (a 2% daily standard deviation, squared) and a temporary-impact coefficient of \(\eta=0.0012\), both taken as given inputs from prior calibration.

1

Risk-neutral trader: λ = 0

With no risk aversion at all, \(\kappa=\sqrt{0\cdot\sigma^2/\eta}=0\) exactly, and the trajectory is the straight line \(x(t)/X = 1-t\) — pure TWAP, a constant trading rate for the entire day.

2

Moderately risk-averse trader: λ = 27

Plug into \(\kappa=\sqrt{\lambda\sigma^2/\eta}\):

\[ \kappa = \sqrt{\frac{27\times 0.0004}{0.0012}} = \sqrt{\frac{0.0108}{0.0012}} = \sqrt{9} = 3 \]
λ=27the trader's chosen risk-aversion level
κ=3the resulting shape parameter, in units of 1/day
3

Compare the two trajectories at the same checkpoints

Using \(x(t)/X = \sinh(\kappa(1-t))/\sinh(\kappa)\) for \(\kappa=3\), against the straight line \(1-t\) for \(\kappa=0\):

At the very first quarter of the day, the risk-neutral trader has only sold 25% of the order, while the risk-averse trader (\(\kappa=3\)) has already sold more than half of it (100% − 46.8% ≈ 53.2%). Real-world trading scenario

A portfolio manager liquidating a concentrated position ahead of a known risk event

A portfolio manager needs to exit a large, concentrated position over the course of a trading day, and a scheduled macro data release is due partway through the session — an event they have no informational edge on, but one they know is likely to raise realized volatility sharply around the release itself. Two desks handling similar-sized liquidations might reasonably choose very different schedules given the identical position and identical horizon.

A desk running close to risk-neutral (low \(\lambda\), low \(\kappa\)) spreads the sale evenly across the full day, including through the data release itself — accepting the timing risk of the news event because minimizing total expected impact cost is the dominant objective, and evening out the trading rate achieves that. A more risk-averse desk (high \(\lambda\), high \(\kappa\)) instead front-loads aggressively, aiming to have the large majority of the position already sold before the data release even occurs — deliberately accepting a higher expected impact cost from trading faster than the impact-minimizing pace, specifically to avoid holding size through a period of foreseeably elevated volatility. Neither choice is "wrong" in the abstract: they reflect two different, equally legitimate values of \(\lambda\), and Almgren-Chriss is precisely the framework that makes that difference explicit and quantifiable rather than a matter of unstated gut feel.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

Almost no execution desk solves the Euler-Lagrange equation by hand for every order — but the framework's outputs shape real, everyday execution-algorithm design and decision-making:

📐

Calibrating η and σ from real trade data

Execution desks estimate the temporary-impact coefficient and volatility inputs empirically from historical executions and market data (often using the square-root law relationship as a starting functional form), rather than assuming textbook constants — the whole framework is only as good as these calibrated inputs.

🎚️

Choosing λ as an explicit risk policy, not a hidden default

A desk or a portfolio manager sets risk aversion deliberately based on genuine risk tolerance and situational context (e.g. a known upcoming event, as in the scenario above) — treating it as a stated policy choice rather than whatever a default execution-algorithm setting happens to produce.

🤖

Building the actual TWAP/VWAP/IS algorithm families around it

TWAP corresponds to the \(\kappa\to0\) limit; volume-weighted (VWAP) schedules adapt the same trade-off to the market's typical intraday volume curve rather than to raw clock time; "implementation shortfall" algorithms are built directly on minimizing exactly this impact-cost-versus-timing-risk objective, often with the front-loading Almgren-Chriss derives explicitly built in.

🔄

Adapting the schedule in real time

Live execution algorithms re-solve or adjust the trajectory as conditions change mid-execution — realized volatility spiking, liquidity thinning, or the order being partially filled faster or slower than planned — rather than committing blindly to a static schedule computed once at the start.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where this model is misread or over-applied

Treating the closed-form trajectory as literally optimal in live markets. The sinh/cosh solution comes from assuming constant volatility and a constant, linear temporary-impact coefficient for the whole horizon. Real markets have time-varying volatility and liquidity (including predictable intraday patterns), so a live execution schedule is typically adapted around this closed form, not applied mechanically as a fixed plan computed once at the start of the day.

Forgetting that permanent impact still costs money, even though it doesn't affect the optimal shape. Permanent impact drops out of the trajectory-optimization math because it's trajectory-independent for a linear permanent-impact model — but it is still a real cost paid on the full order size, often a substantial share of total execution cost. "Doesn't affect the optimal shape" is not the same claim as "doesn't matter."

Assuming higher risk aversion is always the safer or better choice. A high-\(\kappa\), heavily front-loaded schedule pays a higher expected impact cost by construction — it's not a free lunch. It's the right choice only when the trader's genuine risk tolerance, or a specific situational reason (a known upcoming volatility event, as in the scenario above), actually justifies paying that higher expected cost for reduced timing-risk variance.

Applying Kyle's linear impact and the square-root law interchangeably without noting the size regime each is meant for. Kyle's Lambda (\(\Delta p=\lambda Q\)) is a tractable linear approximation most defensible for smaller orders relative to depth; the square-root law is the better empirical fit specifically documented at larger sizes relative to average daily volume. Using the linear form far outside the size range it was calibrated for will misestimate cost, typically understating it for large orders.

Section 04

Key Takeaways

✓Temporary impact reverts; permanent impact doesn't. One is the price of demanding immediacy right now; the other is a lasting, information-driven repricing that your own trading itself causes.
✓Impact scales roughly with the square root of relative order size, not linearly. A 4× larger order costs about 2× more, not 4× more — and smaller orders already cost more per share than a naive linear model would predict.
✓Almgren-Chriss frames execution as one explicit trade-off: impact cost vs. timing risk. A single risk-aversion parameter \(\lambda\) sets how heavily variance (timing risk) is weighted against expected impact cost.
✓The optimal trajectory has a clean closed form. \(x(t)=X\sinh(\kappa(T-t))/\sinh(\kappa T)\), with \(\kappa=\sqrt{\lambda\sigma^2/\eta}\): a straight line (TWAP) at \(\kappa=0\), increasingly front-loaded as \(\kappa\) rises.
✓Permanent impact doesn't change the optimal shape, but it still costs real money. It's trajectory-independent (a sunk cost on the fixed total size), which is why it drops out of the optimization — not because it's negligible.
⚠Higher risk aversion is not free — it's a deliberate trade of more expected cost for less variance. A front-loaded schedule pays more impact on average; it's only the right choice when that trade genuinely matches the trader's risk tolerance or situation.
⚠The closed-form trajectory assumes constant volatility and a constant linear impact coefficient. Real desks adapt the schedule as conditions change; treating the formula as a fixed, one-time plan ignores real intraday variation in both inputs.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. What is the practical difference between temporary and permanent impact, and why does only one of them revert?
Temporary impact is the price concession needed to get a trade filled immediately — it comes from consuming resting liquidity in the book right now, and it reverts once trading stops because the book refills and the concession was purely about the momentary demand for immediacy, not new information. Permanent impact is a lasting repricing because the trade itself is informative — other participants (and the market maker) rationally update their belief about fundamental value partly because of the trade having happened at all, the same adverse-selection logic that explains the bid-ask spread in the Market Microstructure lesson. Since that updated belief doesn't have a reason to revert on its own, the permanent component persists.
Q2. Why does the square-root law imply that quadrupling an order's size only roughly doubles its impact cost, and what does that same math imply about small orders relative to a naive linear model?
Because impact is proportional to \(\sqrt{Q/\text{ADV}}\), and \(\sqrt{4Q}=2\sqrt{Q}\) — quadrupling the input to a square root only doubles the output. This is the "economy of scale" reading: very large orders cost less per share than a linear model would suggest. But the same concavity cuts the other way for smaller orders: since the square-root curve lies above any straight chord connecting the origin to a larger reference point, small and moderate-sized orders already carry more impact per share than a linear model calibrated off a bigger reference size would predict — both readings come from the same concave shape, just read at different ends of the size range.
Q3. In the Almgren-Chriss optimization, why does permanent impact drop out of the objective function entirely, and does that mean it doesn't matter?
For a permanent-impact function that is linear in the trading rate, the total permanent-impact cost paid over the whole execution depends only on the total quantity traded (fixed at \(X\)) — not on how that trading is spread out over time. Since every candidate trajectory pays the identical permanent-impact cost, it contributes nothing to choosing between trajectories, so it drops out of the trajectory-optimization math (the \(x(t)\) that minimizes impact cost plus risk penalty is unaffected by adding a constant to the objective). It absolutely still matters as a real cost — it's just a cost every schedule pays equally, so it's irrelevant to the shape decision specifically, not irrelevant to total execution cost.
Q4. Why does the optimal trajectory become a straight line exactly when κ → 0, and what does κ → 0 actually correspond to economically?
Mathematically, as \(\kappa\to0\), \(\sinh(z)\to z\) for small \(z\) (the small-angle/small-argument approximation), so \(x(t)/X=\sinh(\kappa(T-t))/\sinh(\kappa T)\to (T-t)/T\) — a straight line in \(t\). Economically, \(\kappa=\sqrt{\lambda\sigma^2/\eta}\), so \(\kappa=0\) corresponds to \(\lambda=0\): a completely risk-neutral trader who places zero weight on the variance/timing-risk penalty and cares only about minimizing expected impact cost. With no risk-aversion term left in the objective, the optimal choice is simply to spread the (convex, rate-squared) impact cost as evenly as possible over the whole horizon — which is exactly a constant trading rate, i.e. TWAP.
Q5. In the worked example, why did the κ=3 trajectory have only about 21% of the order remaining at the halfway point, versus 50% for the straight-line (κ=0) trajectory — what is actually driving that gap?
The gap is entirely the risk-aversion parameter \(\lambda=27\) (versus \(\lambda=0\) for the straight-line case), translated through \(\kappa=\sqrt{\lambda\sigma^2/\eta}\) into \(\kappa=3\), and then through the exponential growth built into \(\sinh\)/\(\cosh\) into a strongly front-loaded shape. A higher \(\lambda\) means the trader weights the variance (timing-risk) penalty more heavily relative to expected impact cost, and the model's response to that heavier weighting is to reduce the time spent holding inventory as fast as the impact-cost penalty (which grows with the square of trading rate) allows — resulting in most of the order being sold in the first quarter or half of the horizon rather than spread evenly, exactly the 46.8% remaining at t=0.25 and 21.3% remaining at t=0.5 computed in the worked example.

  • Robert Almgren & Neil Chriss, "Optimal Execution of Portfolio Transactions," Journal of Risk, 3(2), 2000/2001 — the original paper deriving the impact-cost-versus-timing-risk trade-off and the closed-form sinh/cosh optimal trajectory covered in this lesson.
  • Robert Almgren, Chee Thum, Emmanuel Hauptmann & Hong Li, "Direct Estimation of Equity Market Impact," Risk, 2005 — a widely cited empirical paper estimating and validating the square-root law of market impact across a large real dataset.
  • Jean-Philippe Bouchaud, Julius Bonart, Jonathan Donier & Martin Gould, Trades, Quotes and Prices: Financial Markets Under the Microscope, Cambridge University Press, 2018 — a modern, comprehensive treatment connecting the square-root law, temporary/permanent impact, and optimal execution theory in one place.
