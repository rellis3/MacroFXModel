Vega & the Volatility Surface

Every option carries a bet on volatility itself, not just on direction — and the market's refusal to price that bet with one flat number, strike after strike and expiry after expiry, is what the "volatility surface" actually is.

CONCEPT — not built or tested in this repo ~35 min read Prereqs: Black-Scholes-Merton Option Pricing, Gamma Exposure & Dealer Hedging Flows
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • Vega is \(\nu = \partial C/\partial\sigma = S\varphi(d_1)\sqrt{T}\) — the dollar change in an option's value per unit change in implied volatility \(\sigma\). Unlike delta, it is exactly the same formula for a call and a put at the same strike and expiry, a direct consequence of put-call parity.
  • Vega grows roughly with \(\sqrt{T}\) for a fixed near-the-money strike (longer-dated options carry more absolute vega risk), and for a fixed expiry it peaks at-the-money and decays toward zero for deep in- or out-of-the-money strikes, because \(\varphi(d_1)\) is a bell curve centered at \(d_1=0\).
  • Real markets don't quote one \(\sigma\); they quote a different implied \(\sigma\) for every (strike, expiry) pair — the volatility surface. Its two axes are the strike-wise skew/smile (an empirical, post-1987 market fact Black-Scholes itself cannot produce) and the expiry-wise term structure (near-dated vol spikes around event risk; longer-dated vol is smoother, an averaging effect).
  • Vega is fundamentally different from delta in one crucial way: the underlying itself has zero vega, so a vega-exposed book can only be hedged by trading other options, never by trading more or less of the underlying. Delta is an underlying-hedging problem; vega is an options-against-options problem.
  • The genuine new-value piece here: an analogue of dealer gamma exposure, dollar vega exposure at a strike, \(\text{VegaExposure}_k = \nu_k\times\text{OI}_k\times100\) (with \(\nu_k\) the per-vol-point vega) — heavy open interest concentrates a dealer's vega risk exactly the way it concentrates gamma risk, but the fix looks completely different (buy/sell other options, not the underlying).
  • Real-world episode: on 19 October 1987 "portfolio insurance", puts replicated by selling futures (a hedge with no vega), failed in a 20%+ one-day crash, and the index skew has existed ever since. In March 2020 the other axis bent: the VIX closed at a record 82.69 and the term structure inverted for weeks. Tested here: an inverted VIX curve is followed by wider ranges (vix-inversion, mv-vixterm-range, validated), range only, never direction.
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

The stock didn't move, but my option's price did. What happened?

The market changed its mind about how jumpy the stock will be. That's implied volatility, and how much your option cares about it is vega.

How much does it care?

Take a stock at a hundred, an at-the-money option, twenty percent vol, thirty days left. Each point of implied vol is worth about eleven cents on that option. Give it ninety days and it's about twenty cents. A year, about thirty-nine cents.

So longer options carry more vega.

It grows with the square root of time and never decays the way gamma and theta do. And for a fixed expiry it's biggest at the money, where the outcome is still a coin flip. Deep in or out of the money, the vol forecast barely matters.

Is it different for calls and puts?

No. At the same strike and expiry they have exactly the same vega. Direction doesn't come into it.

Can I hedge it by trading the stock?

Never. The stock itself has zero vega. The only thing that offsets an option's vega is another option.

And the volatility surface?

The market doesn't quote one volatility. It quotes one for every strike and every expiry. Across strikes, crash protection costs more: out-of-the-money puts trade at richer vol than the matching calls. Across expiries, vol jumps ahead of events. If a big announcement counts like three normal days in a five-day option, it carries about forty-three percent of that option's expected variance.

Why are the puts always richer?

October nineteen eighty-seven. Big investors had portfolio insurance: they copied a put by selling futures as the market fell. That copy had no vega and needed a smooth market. On the nineteenth the Dow fell twenty-two point six percent in a day, the copy fell behind, and the insurance failed. People with real puts gained on price and on vol. Crash protection has carried a premium ever since.

And the expiry axis?

March twenty twenty. The VIX closed at eighty-two point six nine, a record, and near-dated vol sat above longer-dated vol for weeks.

So the one-line version?

Options are a bet on how much, not just which way. Vega measures that bet, and only another option can hedge it.

Section 01

Why this matters

The Black-Scholes-Merton lesson derives delta and gamma in full and names vega only in passing — "sensitivity to \(\sigma\)" — because at that point in the curriculum \(\sigma\) is still treated as a single constant input to one formula. The Gamma Exposure & Dealer Hedging lesson picks up gamma and shows what happens when you sum it across an entire open-interest book: a real, mechanical source of dealer hedging flow. Neither lesson stops to ask the two questions this one is built around: what exactly is vega, as a formula and as a shape — and why does the market that trades these options never actually agree on a single \(\sigma\) in the first place?

That second question turns out to matter more than it sounds. Every option-pricing formula in the previous two lessons takes \(\sigma\) as a given input. In practice, \(\sigma\) is never given — it is the one number in the whole model that has to be inferred from the price the market is already charging, and the market charges a different implied \(\sigma\) for almost every strike and every expiry on the same underlying, on the same day. That entire grid of implied volatilities — the volatility surface — is the market's honest admission that the flat-\(\sigma\) assumption underneath Black-Scholes is false, priced directly into every quote. Understanding vega properly, and understanding why the surface looks the way it does, is the direct prerequisite for the two stochastic-volatility lessons already in this curriculum (The Heston Model, The SABR Model) — both of which exist specifically to generate a surface that a single constant \(\sigma\) cannot.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Options are also a bet on "how much," not just "which way"

Delta answers "if the underlying moves $1, how much does my option move?" Vega answers a completely different question: "if the market's collective opinion about how volatile this underlying will be changes — with the underlying's price staying exactly where it is right now — how much does my option move?" That's a strange thing to have a price sensitivity to at all. Volatility isn't a price; it's a forecast of how much a price will jump around. Yet options are worth more when that forecast rises and worth less when it falls, holding everything else fixed, because a wider range of plausible future prices makes the option's asymmetric payoff (capped loss, open-ended gain for a long call) more valuable. Vega is the size of that effect in dollars.

Now stretch that idea across every strike and every expiry a market trades at once, and a second, subtler fact emerges: the market doesn't apply the same volatility forecast everywhere. It charges more implied volatility for a strike that would only pay off in a crash than for the mirror-image strike that would pay off in an equally-sized rally. It charges more implied volatility for an option expiring right after a central bank meeting than for one expiring a week later, once the news is old. Both of those are real, tradable facts about how option prices behave that a single flat \(\sigma\), the same number everywhere, simply cannot produce. That gap between "one number" and "the market's actual grid of numbers" is the volatility surface, and it's the second half of this lesson.

Four pieces build on the Black-Scholes formula and its \(d_1, d_2\) (used here exactly as defined in that lesson, not redefined): the closed-form vega, why it's identical for calls and puts, its shape across time and moneyness, and the two-dimensional surface real markets actually quote.

Vega — the closed-form result

As with delta and gamma, vega is simply a partial derivative of the Black-Scholes call price with respect to one input — here, volatility \(\sigma\) — with everything else held fixed. Differentiating \(C = S_0N(d_1) - Ke^{-rT}N(d_2)\) with respect to \(\sigma\) (a calculus step not carried out in full here, since it requires differentiating through both \(d_1\) and \(d_2\) and a good deal of the algebra cancels — this is a standard result, not derived from scratch, the same way Black-Scholes' own \(d_1,d_2\) formulas were stated as results rather than derived term-by-term) collapses to a remarkably clean closed form:

\[ \nu \;=\; \frac{\partial C}{\partial \sigma} \;=\; S\,\varphi(d_1)\,\sqrt{T} \]

Why vega is identical for a call and a put

The Gamma Exposure lesson proved \(\Gamma_{\text{call}}=\Gamma_{\text{put}}\) by differentiating put-call parity twice with respect to \(S\). The same trick, differentiating once with respect to \(\sigma\) instead of \(S\), proves the same fact for vega — and the argument is arguably even cleaner, since \(\sigma\) doesn't appear on the right-hand side at all:

\[ C - P = S - Ke^{-rT} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{\partial C}{\partial\sigma} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial\sigma} = \frac{\partial}{\partial\sigma}\big(S - Ke^{-rT}\big) = 0 \]
S − Ke⁻ʳᵀno σ term anywhere in this expression
∂/∂σ of a constant = 0the right side vanishes completely
ν_call = ν_putexact, not approximate — holds at any strike/expiry

Put-call parity's right-hand side, \(S - Ke^{-rT}\), doesn't contain \(\sigma\) at all — \(S\), \(K\), and \(e^{-rT}\) are simply not functions of volatility — so differentiating it with respect to \(\sigma\) gives exactly zero, for any value of \(\sigma\), not just approximately near some particular value. That's a stronger statement than "a call and put usually have similar vega": vega is identical for the two, always, at the same strike and expiry. This is the flip side of the reason delta is not the same for calls and puts: delta is the parity identity's first derivative with respect to \(S\), and \(\partial(S-Ke^{-rT})/\partial S=1\), not zero — so \(\Delta_{\text{call}}-\Delta_{\text{put}}=1\), a genuine difference. Vega skips that difference entirely because \(\sigma\) simply never enters the no-arbitrage relationship that defines parity in the first place.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Vega is one of the few things a call and a put agree on completely, no exceptions. Delta differs between them because a call and a put bet on opposite directions, but "how much does this option's value react to the market getting more or less jumpy" doesn't care about direction at all — a call and a put on the same strike are equally exposed to that one.

Vega's shape — time and moneyness

Two separate questions live inside the closed form \(\nu=S\varphi(d_1) \sqrt{T}\): how does vega behave as expiry gets further away (for a fixed strike near the money), and how does it behave across strikes (for a fixed expiry)? They have different answers, and both matter for different practical reasons.

Across time, for a fixed near-the-money strike: take \(S=K\) (at-the-money) so \(\ln(S_0/K)=0\), leaving \(d_1=(r+\sigma^2/2)\sqrt{T}/\sigma\). For realistic \(r\) and \(\sigma\) and expiries out to a year or so, this stays close to zero, which means \(\varphi(d_1)\) stays close to its maximum, roughly \(\varphi(0)\approx0.3989\), across that whole range. With \(\varphi(d_1)\) nearly constant, the entire time-dependence of vega comes from the \(\sqrt{T}\) term alone: vega grows roughly proportionally to \(\sqrt{T}\) for an at-the-money option as expiry lengthens. It does not decay with more time to expiry over any realistic horizon, unlike gamma or theta — a longer-dated option always carries more absolute dollar vega than a shorter-dated one on the same strike, which is exactly why event-vol traders describe far-dated options as "carrying more vega risk" even though their gamma is comparatively tame.

Across strikes, for a fixed expiry: \(\varphi(\cdot)\) is the standard normal bell curve, maximized at zero and monotonically decreasing as its argument moves away from zero in either direction. So vega is largest exactly where \(d_1\approx0\) — economically, close to at-the-money — and shrinks toward zero the deeper an option gets, in either direction. A deep in-the-money option behaves almost exactly like owning the underlying outright (its value barely depends on the vol forecast at all, since the outcome is nearly certain either way); a deep out-of-the-money option is worth close to nothing regardless of the vol forecast (there's so little chance of finishing in the money that a change in that forecast barely moves the price). The at-the-money strike is the one point where the outcome is genuinely uncertain, which is exactly where the market's opinion about "how much" the underlying will move matters most.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Vega is biggest for options that could genuinely go either way, and smallest for options whose outcome already feels obvious. A coin flip that's still up in the air cares a lot about "how volatile is the situation" — a game that's already basically decided doesn't. That's why vega peaks right at the money and fades out as an option gets deep in or out of the money.

Section 02b

The volatility surface

Black-Scholes assumes one constant \(\sigma\) — flat across every strike, flat across every expiry. Real options markets do not quote one number. They quote a different implied volatility for essentially every (strike, expiry) pair on the same underlying, on the same day — a full two-dimensional grid, \(\sigma(K,T)\), universally called the volatility surface. It has two genuinely distinct axes, and they come from different economics.

Axis 1 — the skew / smile (across strikes, fixed expiry)

Plot implied volatility against strike at one fixed expiry and, for equity/index underlyings, it is not flat. Deep out-of-the-money puts trade at persistently higher implied vol than the mirror-image out-of-the-money calls — a downward-sloping curve often called the volatility smirk (a lopsided cousin of a symmetric "smile"). This is an empirical, observed market fact, not something Black-Scholes predicts or can even produce — the model's constant \(\sigma\) is, by construction, the same number at every strike. The smirk has to be taken as a real feature of how the market prices these contracts, layered on top of the model rather than derived from it.

Axis 2 — the term structure (across expiries, fixed moneyness)

Plot implied volatility against time-to-expiry at one fixed moneyness (say, at-the-money) instead, and it also moves — but for a different reason. Near-dated implied vol jumps around ahead of concentrated event risk (earnings, a central bank meeting, an election); longer-dated implied vol is comparatively smooth. This is largely a dilution/averaging effect: a single high-impact day contributes disproportionately to a short expiry's average variance and gets diluted away inside a long expiry's average variance, explained numerically below.

Take the skew first. Before October 1987, index options traded close to a flat smile — implied vol barely varied by strike. After the 1987 crash (a single-day move far larger than a lognormal, constant-\(\sigma\) model would ever assign meaningful probability to), the market's pricing permanently changed: out-of-the-money puts have traded at a persistent implied-vol premium over out-of-the-money calls ever since, on essentially every major equity index. Mark Rubinstein's 1994 study "Implied Binomial Trees" is the standard empirical documentation of this shift — worth citing honestly here as evidence of when and how much the market's pricing changed, not as a theoretical derivation of why it had to. Two economic stories are commonly offered as color for why the skew persists, and both are worth knowing without treating either as proven: the leverage effect (as an equity's price falls, its financial leverage ratio mechanically rises, which itself raises the equity's volatility — a real, negative price-volatility correlation that skew pricing reflects) and a "crash-o-phobia" story (the market permanently re-priced tail risk higher after 1987 taught it that far-left-tail moves are more real than a lognormal model implies, so protection against them stays structurally bid). Neither story is derived from Black-Scholes — both are read off what the market actually charges.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Insurance against a car crash costs more than insurance against a minor scratch, even if both are "just insurance." The skew is the same idea: options that pay off in a crash (deep out-of-the-money puts) get priced with extra insurance premium baked in, because the market learned the hard way in 1987 that crashes really do happen and are worse than a calm, textbook model would predict.

The term structure's event-risk spikes have a cleaner, more mechanical explanation. Treat variance as roughly additive across trading days — each day contributes some expected variance to the total, and an expiry's average annualized implied vol is, loosely, driven by the sum of those daily contributions divided by the number of days. A single event day (say, an FOMC decision) carries far more expected variance than an ordinary trading day. If only five trading days remain before a weekly expiry and that event day is expected to contribute variance equivalent to three ordinary days, that one day accounts for roughly 43% (3 of 7 day-equivalents) of the whole period's expected variance — a huge share, and the near-dated implied vol reflects it directly. The same event, priced into an expiry 60 trading days out, contributes only a small fraction of that period's total expected variance — the other 59-ish ordinary days swamp it, an averaging effect that leaves longer-dated implied vol comparatively smooth. That's why term-structure "humps" right around a known event date collapse quickly once the event passes and that particular expiry is no longer the near-dated one carrying it.

Neither axis is reproducible from a single constant \(\sigma\), which is the entire point: a one-parameter model has exactly one degree of freedom, and the surface needs (at minimum) one degree of freedom per strike and a separate one per expiry. This is precisely why stochastic-volatility models exist — models where \(\sigma\) itself follows its own random process rather than sitting fixed. The Heston Model lets variance mean-revert around a long-run level with its own volatility-of-volatility and a correlation with the underlying's return, which jointly generates both a skew (through that correlation) and a term structure (through the mean reversion) endogenously, rather than fitting one by hand. The SABR Model takes a related but distinct stochastic-volatility approach (with a CEV-style backbone set by its \(\beta\) parameter), popular specifically because it fits an observed smile/skew shape well and prices consistently across nearby strikes — both lessons cover the formal machinery in full; this page only needs the reader to know why that machinery is necessary in the first place.

Portfolio vega — an options-against-options hedging problem

A book's total vega is simply the sum of every position's vega, weighted by the size (contracts × multiplier, signed long/short) of each position — no different in principle from summing delta or gamma across a book. What is different, and worth stating plainly because it's frequently under-stated, is how that risk gets hedged.

The underlying itself has zero vega. A share of stock, a futures contract, a spot FX position — none of these have any time value or any dependence on an implied-volatility input, because none of them are options. Their price sensitivity to a change in "the market's vol forecast," holding their own price fixed, is exactly zero. That means no amount of buying or selling the underlying can ever offset a book's vega exposure — not partially, not approximately, not at all. Delta can always be hedged with the underlying; vega structurally cannot be. The only way to flatten vega risk is to trade other options — any option, at any strike or expiry, carries some vega, so an options book's vega risk can only ever be offset by another options position, never by the underlying.
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Owning a plain share of stock is like owning a plain photograph — it has no opinion about how shaky the camera was, only about where the picture actually ended up. An option is like a bet on how shaky the camera will be. No amount of buying or selling plain photographs can hedge a bet about shakiness; you can only offset that bet with another bet of the same kind. That's why vega can only be hedged with other options, never with the underlying itself.

This is the single clearest way vega management differs from delta management, and it's why an options desk that's badly vega-exposed reaches for a different toolkit than one that's badly delta-exposed. A delta problem is solved by trading the underlying — fast, liquid, cheap. A vega problem is solved by trading more options — which themselves carry their own new delta and gamma that then need managing, and which are generally less liquid and more expensive to transact in size than the underlying. Vega risk is, structurally, an options-against-options hedging problem in a way delta and gamma simply are not.

A single flat \(\sigma\) would be a horizontal line on this chart. Going from 30 to 90 days, \(\sqrt{T}\) rises from 0.287 to 0.497 (a 1.73× ratio) and vega rises from 11.42 to 19.71 (a 1.73× ratio) — an almost exact match, since \(\varphi(d_1)\) barely moved (0.3983 → 0.3970). The small shortfall from a perfect \(\sqrt{T}\) ratio at the longest expiry (3.42× actual vs. 3.49× predicted by \(\sqrt{T}\) alone) is exactly the modest drift in \(\varphi(d_1)\) as \(d_1\) itself grows away from zero at longer \(T\) — the "roughly proportional to \(\sqrt{T}\)" claim is an approximation, not an identity.

Real-world trading scenario

A dealer's short-vega book heading into an FOMC week

A dealer's book shows a large concentration of open interest at the 100-strike weekly options on an index, expiring two days after an upcoming FOMC rate decision — the same 90-day-out-turned-near-dated strike from the worked example, now sitting close to expiry with its gamma and vega both elevated. The dealer wrote most of that open interest to customers and is net short it, meaning short both gamma and vega at that strike (as with put OI under the Gamma Exposure lesson's standard convention).

Two separate questions land on the desk the same week. First: the term-structure discussion above predicts near-dated implied vol should richen ahead of the meeting — the desk has to judge whether the current implied-vol premium on that near-dated strike is a fair price for the event's actual expected variance contribution, or whether the market has overpaid for an event that (as often happens) turns out to be a non-event once it passes. That's a genuine trading judgment, not a formula output — the term-structure math above explains why near-dated vol moves, not whether today's specific level is cheap or rich.

Second, and separately: whatever the desk decides about the event itself, the concentrated short-vega position at that strike is a real risk that has to be managed regardless. Unlike the delta/gamma risk at the same strike — which the desk manages by trading the index future, per the Gamma Exposure lesson's hedging mechanics — the vega risk cannot be touched by trading the future at all. The desk instead has to buy back options (the same strike, a different strike, or a further-dated expiry — any option carries vega) to bring the book's net vega down to a size it's willing to carry through the event. The two risks at the identical strike are managed with two completely different instruments, precisely because the underlying has zero vega.

Section 03

Dollar vega exposure — the OI angle

The Gamma Exposure lesson built \(\text{GEX}_k = \Gamma_k\times \text{OI}_k\times100\times S^2\times0.01\) — dollar gamma exposure at a strike, rescaled from "per $1 move" to "per 1% move" of the underlying. Vega has a genuine analogue, and it's worth deriving rather than memorizing, because the rescaling needed turns out to be much simpler than gamma's — vega is already a dollar quantity.

Start from the raw per-share vega, \(\nu = S\varphi(d_1)\sqrt{T}\), a dollar figure per 1.00 change in \(\sigma\) (i.e. per 100 percentage points, since \(\sigma\) is expressed as a decimal). Implied vol moves are quoted in vol points (a 1-percentage-point move), so convert by dividing by 100: \(\nu_k = \nu/100\) is the dollar sensitivity per one vol point, for one unit (one share) of the underlying — the "per vol pt" column of the worked example. Multiply by the standard 100-share contract multiplier, which numerically undoes the division (\(\nu_k\times100=\nu\), dollars per vol point per contract), then aggregate over the open interest sitting at that strike:

\[ \text{VegaExposure}_k \;=\; \underbrace{\left(\frac{\nu}{100}\right)}_{\nu_k:\ \text{per vol pt, per share}} \times\; \underbrace{100}_{\text{contract multiplier}} \;\times\; \text{OI}_k \;=\; \nu_k \times \text{OI}_k \times 100 \]
ν/100 = νₖrescale from "per 1.00σ" to "per 1 vol point"
×100the contract multiplier numerically undoes the rescaling
×OIₖaggregate over every outstanding contract at the strike

The clean cancellation is worth noticing explicitly: because vega is already denominated in dollars (unlike gamma, which starts as a pure "delta per $1 move" number with no dollar units at all), the per-vol-point rescale and the per-contract multiplier undo each other numerically (per contract, per vol point, the exposure is just the raw per-1.00σ vega), leaving \(\text{VegaExposure}_k=\nu_k\times\text{OI}_k\times100\), with \(\nu_k\) the per-vol-point vega, as the working formula — the dollar P&L change, for the open interest sitting at strike \(k\), from a 1-vol-point move in implied volatility. As with dealer GEX, this number needs an assumed sign (\(s_k=-1\) where the dealer wrote it and is net short, e.g. put OI under the standard convention) to say whether a rising or falling vol environment helps or hurts the dealer at that strike — the same unverifiable-assumption caveat from the Gamma Exposure lesson applies here without modification.

Reusing the fixed-90-day-expiry vegas from the worked example above (0.1011, 0.1971, 0.1368 per vol point at strikes 90/100/110) with a toy combined call+put open-interest distribution: 

Strike   ν (per vol pt)   Combined OI   VegaExposure = ν·OI·100
90       0.1011           3,000         0.1011×3,000×100  ≈ $30,330
100      0.1971           8,500         0.1971×8,500×100  ≈ $167,535
110      0.1368           5,000         0.1368×5,000×100  ≈ $68,400
                                        Total ≈ $266,265 per 1 vol point

The 100-strike again dominates the total, exactly as the 100-strike dominated the toy dealer-GEX table in the Gamma Exposure lesson — and for the identical underlying reason: vega itself is roughly 1.4–2× what it is at the neighboring strikes, so the open-interest-weighted total concentrates there even though its OI (8,500) isn't dramatically larger than the other two strikes'. Heavy open interest at a strike concentrates a dealer's vega risk the same way it concentrates gamma risk — the difference, restated one more time because it's the point of this whole section, is that the dealer fixes a concentrated gamma problem by trading the underlying, and fixes a concentrated vega problem by trading other options at that strike, a neighboring strike, or a different expiry entirely.

Vega itself has second-order sensitivities, the same way price itself has delta and gamma. How vega changes as the underlying moves is a named cross-Greek, Vanna (\(\partial\nu/\partial S= \partial\Delta/\partial\sigma\) — the same mixed partial derivative either way it's taken); how delta itself decays purely with the passage of time is Charm. Both are part of a broader family of second-order "Greeks of Greeks" that this lesson doesn't derive — a further deep-dive, Second-Order Greeks: Vanna, Charm & the Cross-Greeks, covers them properly.

Section 04

It actually happened: October 1987, the day the surface got its shape

Section 02b says the skew is a post-1987 fact and leaves the crash itself offstage. Here is the crash, told through the one idea this lesson keeps coming back to: the underlying has zero vega.

It actually happened

19 October 1987: insurance that had no vega

In the mid-1980s a strategy called portfolio insurance was sold to pension funds and other large investors. Instead of buying put options, the insurer replicated a put by trading the underlying: selling stock-index futures as the market fell and buying them back as it rose, tracking the delta of the put it was copying. By October 1987 tens of billions of dollars of equity were covered this way.

On Monday 19 October 1987 the Dow fell 22.6% and the S&P 500 about 20% in a single session, still the largest one-day percentage falls in their history. Index futures traded at a deep discount to the stocks underneath them. The presidential task force that studied the crash (the Brady Commission) found that portfolio insurers' futures selling was a large part of the selling pressure that week; how much it caused the fall, rather than accompanied it, is still debated.

Here is the part this lesson explains. A replicated put is a delta hedge only. It has no vega, and it assumes the market moves smoothly enough to re-hedge along the way. When the market gapped, the replication fell behind, and the insurance failed exactly when it was needed. Holders of actual puts held vega as well as delta: their puts gained from the fall and again from the jump in implied volatility.

The lasting change was in the price of that protection. Before the crash, index options traded close to a flat smile. Afterwards, out-of-the-money puts have carried a persistent implied-vol premium (Rubinstein's 1994 study, cited in Section 02b). The surface's strike axis is, in effect, the market charging ever since for the vega that portfolio insurance did not have.

It actually happened

March 2020: the other axis turns upside down

The expiry axis had its own extreme in March 2020. As the pandemic shut economies down, the S&P 500 fell about 34% from its February high to its 23 March low, and on 16 March the VIX closed at 82.69, its highest close on record. Near-dated implied vol rose far above longer-dated implied vol, and the term structure stayed inverted for weeks. In Section 02b's averaging terms, the market priced the next few weeks as the "event" and treated the far months as the dilution.

Where the textbook version breaks

Black-Scholes assumes one constant σ for every strike and a continuous path. Those two assumptions are exactly what made a futures-replicated put look equivalent to a real one. October 1987 broke both: the path gapped, and σ turned out to depend on strike. It also breaks the habit of reading vega as one number per option. Vega measures a parallel shift in σ, but in a crash the put wing moves far more than the at-the-money point. A book that is vega-flat in total can still be badly exposed to the shape of the surface.

So the term-structure axis carries real information here, about size. It says nothing about which way. The strike axis, the skew that 1987 left behind, has not been tested as a forward signal on this desk: whether a steeper skew predicts anything is still an open question here, not a finding.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

In 1987 lots of big investors thought they had insurance, but really they had a plan: "if prices fall, sell quickly." When prices fell so fast there was no time to sell, the plan didn't work. People who had bought real insurance were fine, and ever since, crash insurance has cost extra. In 2020 the price of insurance for the next few weeks went far above the price for next year, and the desk found that when that happens, the coming weeks really are bumpier.

Section 05

How practitioners actually use this

Vega and the surface show up constantly in how options desks actually talk and trade, well beyond the single-Greek bookkeeping above:

🗣️

The surface as the quoting language

Traders quote and think in implied vol per strike/expiry, not dollar premiums — the surface itself, not any single option's price, is the object being traded and risk-managed.

⚖️

Vega spreads, not underlying trades

A desk expressing a view on volatility (or hedging an existing vega imbalance) trades calendar spreads, strangles, or straddles — combinations of options designed to isolate vega risk — precisely because the underlying can't do the job.

📅

Event-vol trading around the term structure

Whether a near-dated implied-vol premium ahead of a known event (earnings, a central bank decision) is over- or under-priced relative to the event's plausible realized-variance contribution is a standing, actively debated trade at every options desk.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where vega and the surface get misused

Trying to hedge vega with the underlying. Stated plainly because it bears repeating: the underlying has zero vega. No amount of buying or selling stock, futures, or spot FX reduces a book's vega exposure by even a cent. Only other options can do that.

Expecting a flat single-\(\sigma\) model to reproduce the smile. Plain Black-Scholes, by construction, prices every strike and expiry off the same \(\sigma\). It cannot generate a skew or a term structure on its own — don't be surprised when a constant-vol model's output doesn't match a market quote that has both; that's not a bug in the model, it's the model's known, documented limitation, which is exactly why the smile/skew has to be taken as market data rather than derived.

Confusing implied vol with realized vol. A rich near-dated implied vol ahead of an event is the market's forward-looking price of volatility, not a guarantee of how much the underlying will actually move. Implied volatility has historically tended to run above subsequently realized volatility on average (often discussed as a "variance risk premium") — being short vega structurally collects that premium on average, at the cost of real tail risk when realized volatility does spike beyond what was priced in.

Treating open interest's dealer sign as observed fact. As with dealer GEX, "the dealer is net short this strike" is a standard but unverifiable assumption layered on raw OI, not something read directly off the data — every dollar-vega-exposure figure inherits that same uncertainty.

Extrapolating the surface past where options actually trade. A model fit to the visible strikes/expiries can output a number for a strike or expiry with no real liquidity — that output is a mathematical extrapolation, not a tradable quote, and treating it as one is a common, costly mistake.

Scenario drill · 19 October 1987

Your fund's crash insurance is a trading plan

It is the weekend before Monday 19 October 1987, after a week of sharp falls in stocks. You oversee a pension fund whose equity holdings are covered by portfolio insurance: a program that replicates a put by selling stock-index futures as the market falls, tracking the put's delta. It holds no actual options. What do you do before Monday's open?

  1. Nothing. The program does exactly what a put would do, and it was built for a week like this.
    A replicated put is a delta hedge only. It has no vega and it needs a market smooth enough to keep re-hedging. If prices gap, the replication falls behind just when the protection matters, and every other insurer is trying to sell the same futures.
  2. Accept that you are only partly insured: cut the equity exposure directly, or buy listed index puts, even at a higher price.
    Real puts carry vega as well as delta, so they gain from the fall and from the jump in implied volatility, with no re-hedging needed. Cutting exposure removes the risk outright. Both cost something now; neither depends on a smooth path.
  3. Keep the program, but tell the traders to sell futures faster at the open.
    It addresses the lag, which is the right worry. But you would be racing every other insurer to sell the same futures into a falling market, which is how the futures came to trade at a deep discount.
  4. Switch the program off and buy more stocks: the fall is a bargain.
    That turns a partly hedged book into an unhedged, larger one on the eve of a crash. Whatever the long-run value, you have removed the protection the fund was paying for.

What happened. On 19 October 1987 the Dow fell 22.6% and the S&P 500 about 20% in a single session, still the largest one-day percentage falls in their history. Index futures traded at a deep discount to the stocks underneath them, and the Brady Commission found portfolio insurers' futures selling was a large part of the selling pressure that week (how much it caused the fall is still debated). The replication fell behind and the insurance failed when it was needed, while holders of actual puts gained from the fall and from the jump in implied volatility. Out-of-the-money index puts have carried an implied-vol premium ever since.

Sources: Federal Reserve History, "Stock Market Crash of 1987"; Report of the Presidential Task Force on Market Mechanisms (the Brady Commission), January 1988; Rubinstein, "Implied Binomial Trees", Journal of Finance, 1994.

Section 06

Key Takeaways

✓Vega is \(\nu=S\varphi(d_1)\sqrt{T}\) — identical for a call and put. Put-call parity's right side has no \(\sigma\) term, so \(\partial C/\partial\sigma-\partial P/\partial\sigma=0\) exactly, not approximately.
✓Vega grows with \(\sqrt{T}\) and peaks at-the-money. Both fall directly out of the closed form: \(\sqrt{T}\) never decays, and \(\varphi(d_1)\) is a bell curve centered at \(d_1=0\).
✓The volatility surface has two distinct axes with different causes. Strike-wise skew/smile is an empirical, post-1987 market fact; expiry-wise term structure is shaped mechanically by event risk being averaged in or diluted away (and by volatility mean-reverting toward a long-run level).
⚠Vega cannot be hedged with the underlying — ever. The underlying has exactly zero vega; only other options can offset a vega-exposed book, structurally unlike delta.
✓Dollar vega exposure at a strike, \(\nu_k\times\text{OI}_k\times100\), concentrates the same way GEX does. Heavy open interest near the money dominates a book's vega risk, but the fix is other options, not the underlying.
⚠Implied vol is a price, not a forecast guarantee — and the dealer's assumed sign is still an assumption. A rich event-vol premium can be over- or under-priced either way, and every GEX-style figure here inherits the same unverifiable long/short assumption flagged in the Gamma Exposure lesson.
✓An inverted term structure is a validated range signal here, never a direction (vix-inversion, mv-vixterm-range). It tells you the next week and month run wider, not which way. October 1987 shows why the strike axis exists at all: a put replicated with futures has no vega, and it failed on a gap.
Section 07

Self-test

Q1. Why is vega exactly the same value for a call and a put at the same strike and expiry, and how does that argument differ from the one showing gamma is the same for both?
Both arguments start from put-call parity, \(C-P=S-Ke^{-rT}\), and differentiate both sides. The gamma argument differentiates twice with respect to \(S\); since the right side is linear in \(S\), its second derivative is zero, giving \(\Gamma_{\text{call}}=\Gamma_{\text{put}}\). The vega argument differentiates once with respect to \(\sigma\) instead; since the right side (\(S-Ke^{-rT}\)) doesn't contain \(\sigma\) at all, that derivative is zero on the first pass, giving \(\nu_{\text{call}}=\nu_{\text{put}}\) even more directly than the gamma case, which needed a second derivative to kill off the linear term.
Q2. Why does vega grow roughly with \(\sqrt{T}\) for a fixed at-the-money strike, and why is that only an approximation rather than an exact relationship?
At the money, \(d_1=(r+\sigma^2/2)\sqrt{T}/\sigma\), which stays close to zero for realistic \(r,\sigma\) over moderate expiries, so \(\varphi(d_1)\) stays close to its maximum \(\varphi(0)\approx0.3989\) and is roughly constant across different \(T\). With \(\varphi(d_1)\) treated as fixed, \(\nu=S\varphi(d_1)\sqrt{T}\) scales with \(\sqrt{T}\) alone. It's only approximate because \(d_1\) does grow slowly away from zero as \(T\) grows (as the worked example shows: d₁ rose from 0.057 at 30 days to 0.20 at 365 days), which nudges \(\varphi(d_1)\) down slightly and makes the actual vega ratio between a long-dated and short-dated option a little less than the pure \(\sqrt{T}\) ratio would predict.
Q3. What are the two distinct axes of the volatility surface, and why does each one require a fundamentally different kind of explanation?
The strike axis (skew/smile, fixed expiry) is an empirical market fact — out-of-the-money puts trading at persistently higher implied vol than out-of-the-money calls on equity/index underlyings, a pattern that emerged specifically after the 1987 crash and has to be taken as observed market data, since Black-Scholes' constant \(\sigma\) cannot produce a strike-dependent quantity at all. The expiry axis (term structure, fixed moneyness) has a cleaner mechanical explanation: a concentrated event's expected variance contribution is a much larger share of a near-dated expiry's total expected variance than of a far-dated expiry's, so near-dated implied vol spikes around event risk while longer-dated implied vol, averaged over many more ordinary days, stays comparatively smooth.
Q4. Why can't a dealer with a large, unwanted vega exposure at one strike simply trade the underlying to flatten it, the way they would for an unwanted delta or gamma exposure?
Vega measures sensitivity to a change in implied volatility, holding the underlying's own price fixed. A position in the underlying itself (shares, futures, spot FX) has no time value and no dependence on any volatility input at all — its vega is exactly zero, not small. Trading more or less of the underlying therefore cannot change a book's total vega by any amount, no matter how much is traded. The only positions with nonzero vega are other options, so the only way to offset an unwanted vega exposure is to trade other options — at the same strike, a different strike, or a different expiry — which is structurally different from delta and gamma management, both of which reduce, one way or another, to trading the underlying.
Q5. In the toy three-strike dollar-vega-exposure table, why does the 100 strike dominate the total even though its open interest (8,500) isn't overwhelmingly larger than the other two strikes' (3,000 and 5,000)?
Because vega itself, not just open interest, varies sharply across strikes for a fixed expiry — the 100 strike's per-contract vega (0.1971 per vol point) is about 1.4× the 110 strike's (0.1368) and almost twice the 90 strike's (0.1011), since vega peaks at the money per the \(\varphi(d_1)\) bell-curve shape. The dollar exposure at each strike is the product of vega and OI, so a strike with both moderately larger OI and substantially larger vega dominates the sum by more than either factor would alone — exactly the same mathematical reason the at-the-money strike dominated the toy dealer-GEX table in the Gamma Exposure lesson.
Q6. A near-dated option's implied vol is trading rich just ahead of a central bank meeting. Is that, by itself, evidence the market has mispriced the option? Why or why not?
Not by itself. The term-structure math in this lesson explains why near-dated implied vol should rise ahead of a concentrated event — it's the honest, expected mechanical consequence of one high-variance day being a large share of a short expiry's total expected variance. Whether that specific richening is a fair price for the event's plausible realized-variance contribution, an overpay (the event turns out calmer than priced), or an underpay (the event turns out more volatile than priced) is a separate judgment the term-structure formula alone cannot answer — it explains the shape of the effect, not whether today's specific level is cheap or expensive.
