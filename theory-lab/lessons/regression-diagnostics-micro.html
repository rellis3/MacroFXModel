Regression Diagnostics, the Fine Print Behind a P-Value

A regression's p-value is not a fact about the world — it's a fact about a model of the world, and that model comes with fine print. This guide walks through the three checks that verify the fine print actually holds before you trust the headline number: is the error serially correlated (Durbin-Watson), does its variance change across the sample (Breusch-Pagan), and does it look enough like a bell curve for small-sample inference to be valid (Jarque-Bera).

How it reached trading

Return regressions break textbook assumptions: volatility clusters, and overlapping horizons (a 3-month return sampled monthly) make errors correlated. Default t-statistics overstate the evidence.

White (1980) fixed changing variance; Hansen and Hodrick (1980), testing forward FX rates, fixed overlap; Newey and West (1987) handled both. Their standard errors are now standard in finance.

Section 2

Two different things live inside one regression

It's easy to conflate them, and the whole lesson hinges on keeping them apart.

Point estimate — β̂

"For a one-unit move in X, Y tends to move this much." Derived purely by minimizing the sum of squared residuals — β̂ = Cov(X,Y)/Var(X), with no assumption about the residuals' distribution required anywhere in that derivation. It doesn't know or care whether its own residuals are well-behaved.

Inference claim — SE, t, p

"And here's how sure we should be that this isn't zero." Built on top of β̂ using a formula that assumes the residuals are homoskedastic, non-autocorrelated, and — in small samples — roughly normal. Break one of those assumptions and this number, not β̂, is what goes wrong.

When any of the three assumptions is badly violated, the standard error a stats package prints is simply the wrong number for the true sampling uncertainty in β̂ — most often too small, which makes a t-statistic look bigger and a p-value look smaller than the evidence actually supports.

The distinction that matters most

A diagnostic failure means the classical standard error is wrong — it does not mean β̂ itself is meaningless. The point estimate can still be a perfectly reasonable guess at the slope while the significance claim built on top of it is fiction.

Section 3

Three checks, one underlying question

A regression's residuals — eᵢ = yᵢ − ŷᵢ, the part of Y the fitted line doesn't explain — are supposed to look like structureless noise.

📈

Durbin-Watson

Does today's residual predict tomorrow's? Catches serial correlation — "sticky" errors that wave through time instead of bouncing around independently.

🔀

Breusch-Pagan

Does the size of the typical residual depend on where you are in the data? Catches a fanning variance — tight for some X, wide and scattered for others.

📊

Jarque-Bera

Does the residual distribution's overall shape look normal? A lopsided or fat-tailed shape matters most when the sample is small.

All three are really the same diagnostic act performed on three different axes of the same object — the residual series — because all three are checking the same underlying promise: that what's left over after fitting the line really is noise, and not a second signal the inference machinery is silently mistaking for confidence.
Section 4

Durbin-Watson: does today's residual predict tomorrow's?

Line the residuals up in time order. Flip between three regimes and watch the shape.

Try it
DW ≈ 2. Consecutive residuals share no systematic sign — the "no autocorrelation" benchmark the classical standard error assumes.
DW → 0. Positive residuals cluster together, then negative ones do — "sticky" errors. Whatever caused today's miss is still partly causing tomorrow's.
DW → 4. Residuals flip sign almost every period — the mirror-image failure, much rarer in practice than positive autocorrelation.
DW = Σ(eₜ−eₜ₋₁)² / Σeₜ²

If consecutive residuals are independent, the cross term in that numerator averages to roughly zero and DW ≈ 2. Positive autocorrelation shrinks the gap between consecutive residuals, pushing DW toward 0; negative autocorrelation inflates it, pushing DW toward 4. A cleaner approximation makes the whole scale readable at a glance: DW ≈ 2(1 − ρ̂), where ρ̂ is the estimated lag-1 autocorrelation of the residuals.

Section 5

Why a "sticky" residual inflates significance

The classical standard error assumes n observations means n independent pieces of evidence. Change DW and n below and watch the honest count shrink.

Try it — from DW to effective sample size
,
Estimated autocorrelation: ρ̂ = 1 − DW/2 = 0.70
Effective sample size: n_eff ≈ n · (1−ρ̂)/(1+ρ̂) = 88
The classical formula still divides by the raw n, calibrating the standard error to more independent information than the sample actually contains — that gap is exactly what a robust (HAC/Newey-West) standard error corrects for.

At DW = 0.6 and ρ̂ ≈ 0.7, roughly 500 daily observations behave like only about 88 genuinely independent ones. A standard error computed as though it had 500 independent rows behind it is too small for what the data actually supports — which is exactly why a "significant" p-value on sticky residuals deserves a second look before anyone trusts it.

Section 6

Breusch-Pagan: does the typical error size depend on X?

A deliberately simple trick: if the true residual variance depends on X, the squared residuals themselves should show it.

  1. Fit the original regression and keep the residuals eᵢ.
  2. Run a second, "auxiliary" regression: eᵢ² as the dependent variable, on the same predictor(s) X used originally.
  3. Form LM = n · R²_aux and compare it to a χ² distribution — large means heteroskedastic.
eᵢ² = γ₀ + γ₁Xᵢ + vᵢ
→
LM = n · R²_aux
Try it
Constant band. The spread of residuals stays roughly the same width across the whole range of fitted values — nothing for X to predict about eᵢ².
Fanning out. Residuals stay tight for small fitted values and scatter wildly for large ones — the classic pattern common in return series where volatility itself varies with the level of some driver.

A significant Breusch-Pagan result means the single "one standard error fits all observations" number is wrong in the same direction as autocorrelation — it understates the true uncertainty exactly where variance is largest, making the coefficient look more precisely estimated than it should.

Section 7

Jarque-Bera: does the shape look normal?

Not spread, not stickiness — the overall shape of the residual distribution, combined into one number.

JB = (n/6)·(S² + K²/4)
S

Skewness

Lopsidedness — a long tail stretching one direction while the bulk of the mass sits on the other. Zero for a perfectly symmetric distribution.

K

Excess kurtosis

How much fatter the tails are than a normal distribution's, with normal itself defined as 0. Positive means more extreme outliers than a bell curve predicts.

χ²

The verdict

JB is compared against a χ² distribution with 2 degrees of freedom — large S or K, or both, pushes JB up and rejects "these residuals look normal."

The classical t-test is exact when residuals are normal, and otherwise justified only asymptotically — by the Central Limit Theorem, which says the sampling distribution of β̂ approaches normality as sample size grows, regardless of the residuals' own shape. With enough data, non-normal residuals stop mattering much. With a small sample, that averaging hasn't had enough terms to do its smoothing work yet — precisely when a low p-value is most tempting to over-trust and least backed by the assumptions it needs.

Section 8

A p-value of 0.01 that isn't what it looks like

A researcher regresses next-day FX returns on a yield-spread signal. Two years of daily data. p = 0.01.

Before trusting it, the researcher checks residual diagnostics, as the house rule requires. Durbin-Watson comes back at DW = 0.6 — squarely in strongly-positive-autocorrelation territory, because a yield-spread signal changes slowly day to day, so a persistent pricing error against it is exactly the kind of slow-moving mis-specification this test is built to catch.

Using the calculator from Section 4: DW = 0.6 implies ρ̂ ≈ 0.7, and with roughly 500 trading days that shrinks the effective sample size to n_eff ≈ 88. The regression's classical standard error was computed as though it had 500 independent observations backing it; the honest number is closer to what 88 would support. Recomputing with Newey-West (HAC) standard errors — which correct for exactly this — the p-value is nowhere near 0.01 once corrected; it's plausibly back in "not distinguishable from noise" territory.

What did NOT happen here

The original 0.01 p-value wasn't a miscalculation — it's an honest answer to the wrong question. The classical formula answers "how significant would this be if the residuals were independent," and here they simply weren't. β̂ itself might still be a reasonable read on the relationship's direction and rough size; the confidence the raw p-value implied was manufactured by the autocorrelation, not earned by the data.

Section 9

Where these three get misread

Common misreadings
  • Treating a failure as proof the relationship is fake — it's the standard error that's wrong, not necessarily β̂
  • Assuming DW ≈ 2 means the model is fully "cleared" — it only rules out lag-1 autocorrelation, not longer lags, heteroskedasticity, or normality
  • Reading a fan-shaped Breusch-Pagan failure as pure noise when it's really a wrong functional form forced through a curve
  • Over-weighting a "significant" Jarque-Bera on 10,000 rows — JB scales with n, and non-normality matters least exactly where samples are large
The correct read
  • A failure means: recompute inference with a robust method, don't discard the coefficient
  • Each test checks a separate axis — passing one says nothing about the other two
  • Always look at the actual residuals-vs-fitted scatter, not just the test's p-value
  • Read the actual skewness/kurtosis numbers, weighted by how small the sample really is

A regression can pass all three diagnostics in-sample and still fail completely out-of-sample — the checks verify the inference procedure was honestly computed, not that the relationship is durable. Both checks matter, and they check different things.

Section 10

A pre-trust gate, not a post-mortem

Fit the regression, then before quoting the p-value to anyone — including yourself — run all three checks.

🩹

Autocorrelation → Newey-West / HAC

Corrects for both autocorrelation and heteroskedasticity without respecifying the model, or add lagged terms to soak up the serial structure directly.

🩹

Heteroskedasticity → White / robust SE

Or model the variance directly — a GARCH-type approach is the time-series analogue of exactly this problem.

🩹

Non-normality → more data or bootstrap

Collect more independent observations to let the CLT do its job, or use bootstrap standard errors, which resample the data instead of assuming a shape.

A screening step before, not a substitute for, out-of-sample validation

Passing all three diagnostics only means the in-sample significance claim is honestly computed — it says nothing about whether the relationship holds up on data the model hasn't seen. An in-sample number, however carefully diagnosed, is not evidence on its own.

Section 11

Where this stands in this repo, right now

The rule this lesson derives isn't hypothetical — it's already written down as house discipline.

"Add residual diagnostics to any regression-based signal: Durbin-Watson (autocorrelation), Jarque-Bera (residual normality), Breusch-Pagan (heteroskedasticity) before trusting its p-value/coefficient. A statistically 'significant' coefficient on a heteroskedastic fit has optimistic standard errors — check before leaning on it." — this project's own backtest-build-discipline notes

The two ingredient statistics Jarque-Bera needs are already built and in production for a different purpose: a Fisher-Pearson sample-skewness function and an excess-kurtosis function (normal = 0) already compute a strategy's raw return-distribution shape for its Probabilistic Sharpe Ratio. Jarque-Bera would repurpose those same building blocks on a regression's residuals instead of a strategy's P&L — the rule is written down and the ingredients exist, though Durbin-Watson and Breusch-Pagan themselves aren't yet wired up as a reusable checked-in function.

Checked against the codebase

Both claims above were verified directly against the repo rather than assumed: the discipline quote still appears verbatim in the project's build-rules notes, and the skewness/excess-kurtosis functions exist and are actively used elsewhere — the ingredients this lesson describes are real, present-tense facts about this codebase, not aspirational ones.

Section 12

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

A regression has DW ≈ 2. What does that tell you?

DW ≈ 2 only clears lag-1 autocorrelation. It says nothing about longer-lag structure, heteroskedasticity, normality, or out-of-sample durability — those are separate checks.
Question 2

Why does positive residual autocorrelation make a p-value look more significant than it should?

Consecutive autocorrelated residuals restate the same information instead of each adding something new, so the true independent-evidence count (n_eff) is smaller than n — but the classical SE formula still assumes n independent draws, understating uncertainty.
Question 3

What does the Breusch-Pagan test actually regress on what?

It fits an auxiliary regression of eᵢ² on X. If X has real power to predict the squared residuals, the residual variance isn't constant — heteroskedasticity.
Question 4

A regression fails Durbin-Watson but its point estimate looks economically sensible. What's the right response?

β̂ is derived from minimizing squared residuals and doesn't assume anything about serial structure — it can still be a reasonable estimate. What's broken is specifically the classical standard error, fixed with a robust method.
Question 5

Why does Jarque-Bera matter more in a small sample than a large one?

The classical t-test is exact under normal errors and otherwise justified asymptotically by the CLT. With enough data, that averaging smooths over non-normal shape regardless. With few observations, it hasn't — so a skewed or fat-tailed residual distribution distorts the t-statistic directly.
Section 13

What to carry forward

01

A point estimate and an inference claim are two different things — β̂ is robust to residual misbehavior, its standard error, t-statistic, and p-value are not.

02

Durbin-Watson checks autocorrelation: DW ≈ 2 means independent residuals, toward 0 means positive (p-values look too good), toward 4 means negative.

03

Breusch-Pagan checks heteroskedasticity by regressing squared residuals on X — if X predicts them, one "standard error fits all" is wrong.

04

Jarque-Bera checks the residuals' shape via skewness and kurtosis, and matters most exactly when the sample is small enough that the CLT hasn't kicked in.

05

A diagnostic failure means fix the inference (HAC/Newey-West, robust SE, bootstrap) — it doesn't mean the coefficient is fake.

06

Passing all three is not out-of-sample evidence — it only certifies the in-sample significance claim was honestly computed.

What this now lets you do

Read a regression's p-value with appropriate suspicion, run all three residual checks before trusting it, recognize which specific fix a given failure calls for, and tell a genuine variance problem apart from a misspecified functional form wearing the same fan-shaped disguise.
