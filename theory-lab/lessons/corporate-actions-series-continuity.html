Corporate Actions & Series Continuity

A stock split, a dividend, or a futures roll all put a fake, non-tradeable jump in a raw price series — and the fix that removes the jump can itself break other calculations, if you don't know which tradeoff you're accepting.

CONCEPT — explains real industry practice, not a claim about this repo's own data pipeline ~15 min read Prereqs: The Data Pipeline: Raw Feeds to Trading-Ready Data
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • A raw, unadjusted price series shows a fake, non-tradeable jump across a stock split, a dividend, or a futures contract roll — a naive backtest misreads that jump as a real return.
  • Splits/dividends: fixed by back-adjusting historical prices with a cumulative adjustment factor computed from the corporate-action ratio.
  • Futures rolls: two named, well-known methodologies — back-adjustment ("Panama method"), which splices contracts by a constant additive offset so the roll date shows zero jump, and ratio-adjustment, which splices by a multiplicative ratio instead.
  • The real, well-documented tradeoff: a back-adjusted series can go negative or lose its relationship to actual historically-traded price levels — which breaks any calculation based on percentage returns off that level, even though it correctly fixes the jump problem.
  • This is a classic, well-documented source of silent backtest corruption if missed — not a hypothetical edge case.
  • Real-world episode: on 20 April 2020 the May WTI contract settled at −$37.63 while June stayed near $20. Depending on its roll rule, a "continuous" oil series shows that day as −$37.63, about $20, or (ratio-adjusted) sign-flipped. Whether this desk's signals survive a change of roll method is untested.
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

My backtest shows a stock losing half its value in one day. Nobody remembers a crash.

Check for a split. Two for one: every hundred-dollar share becomes two fifty-dollar shares. The pizza is cut into more slices, not made smaller. A raw price chart shows a fifty percent drop that nobody actually lost.

So every price is right, but the series is wrong.

Exactly. Data cleaning would never catch it, because there is no bad tick. The jump between two accurate prices just isn't a tradeable move. The fix is to restate the old prices: before the split, divide them all by two.

And futures?

Same problem, different cause. A contract expires and you splice in the next one, which trades at a different price. Say the old crude contract closes at seventy-eight forty and the new one at seventy-eight ninety. Splice them raw and the series jumps fifty cents that no position ever made.

How do you remove it?

Two named ways. The Panama method adds fifty cents to every older price. Seventy-two dollars a week earlier becomes seventy-two fifty. Dollar moves stay exact. Ratio adjustment multiplies the old prices by the ratio of the two contracts instead, so percentage moves stay exact, but the old price levels drift.

Which one is right?

Whichever matches your calculation. Dollar profit and position sizing want Panama. Long-run percentage statistics want ratio. After many rolls, a Panama series can even drift below zero, and percentage returns off that level mean nothing.

A negative price as a warning sign. Makes sense.

Until the twentieth of April twenty twenty. Storage at the oil delivery point was filling, and the May contract settled at minus thirty-seven dollars sixty-three a barrel. June stayed around twenty.

So what was the oil price that day?

Depends on your roll rule. Roll at expiry and you show minus thirty-seven sixty-three. Roll a few days early, as many vendor series do, and you never see a negative print. Ratio adjust on that day and you divide by a negative number. Same market, three histories.

And my trend backtest that breaks out every quarter?

If the breakouts land on roll dates, you are measuring the splice, not the market.

One line to remember?

Every continuous series hides a choice about splits and rolls, so know which adjustment you are using and match it to the number you are computing.

Section 01

Why this matters

The Data Pipeline: Raw Feeds to Trading-Ready Data named normalization as Stage 4 — the step that turns a raw, cleaned series into one that's continuous and tradeable-return-consistent across corporate actions and contract rolls. This lesson is that stage in full. The failure mode here is unusually dangerous precisely because the raw data isn't "wrong" in any way Data Cleaning would catch — every individual price print can be perfectly accurate, correctly timestamped, and free of any bad tick, and the series can still be completely unusable for a backtest, because the jump between two accurate prices doesn't represent a real, tradeable price move.

💡
Plain-English intuition

The stock that "crashed" 50% overnight — except nobody lost a cent

A company announces a 2-for-1 stock split: every shareholder who owned one $100 share now owns two $50 shares. Nothing about the company's value changed — the pizza was cut into more slices, not made smaller. But a raw, unadjusted price chart shows the stock going from $100 to $50 overnight, which looks, to any naive return calculation, exactly like a 50% single-day loss. Nobody who held the stock actually lost anything; their two $50 shares are worth exactly what their one $100 share was worth the day before. A backtest that computes "yesterday's close to today's close" as a real return, without knowing a split happened, records a catastrophic loss on a day when the true, economic return was zero. A futures contract roll produces the same kind of fake jump for a different reason: the front month contract expiring and a new contract becoming the tradeable one can sit at meaningfully different prices (a normal, expected effect of time and cost-of-carry), and splicing the two contracts together without adjustment shows the same kind of overnight "jump" that never corresponded to an actual trade anyone could have made.

Section 02

Splits and dividends: back-adjustment by a cumulative factor

Watch the formula build
The back-adjusted price, one corporate action at a time
\[ P_t^{adj} = \color{#fbbf24}{P_t} \]

Start with the raw price on an old date \(t\). Accurate on the day, but not comparable with prices after a split or dividend.

\[ P_t^{adj} = P_t \times \color{#fbbf24}{f_1} \]

Apply the factor of the first corporate action after \(t\). A 2-for-1 split has \(f_1 = 0.5\), so an old $100 reads as $50 and the split day shows a return of roughly zero, not −50%.

\[ P_t^{adj} = P_t \times f_1 \times \color{#fbbf24}{f_2 \times \cdots} \]

Every later action adds its own factor, a split ratio or a dividend-reinvestment factor, and they compound. A price far in the past carries all of them.

\[ \color{#10b981}{P_t^{adj} = P_t \times \prod_{k:\, t_k > t} f_k} \]

Written compactly: multiply by the factor of every corporate action that happened after \(t\). Futures rolls use the same idea with a ratio (ratio-adjustment) or swap the multiply for an add (the Panama method).

For an equity split, the fix is conceptually simple: every price before the split date gets multiplied by the split ratio, so the whole pre-split history is restated in "post-split share" terms. A 2-for-1 split means every historical price before the split date is divided by 2 (equivalently, multiplied by an adjustment factor of 0.5), so what used to read as a jump from $100 to $50 instead reads as a smooth, continuous $50-ish series the whole way through, with the true day-of-split return correctly showing as roughly zero.

A cash dividend is handled the same way, conceptually: on the ex-dividend date, the stock's price typically drops by roughly the dividend amount (this is expected and correct — the company just paid cash out the door, so its remaining value is genuinely lower by that amount), but a shareholder who received the dividend didn't actually lose that value, they received it in cash instead. A total-return-adjusted series restates historical prices to add back the cumulative effect of dividends paid, so that a return computed from the adjusted series reflects what a shareholder who reinvested every dividend would have actually experienced, rather than a raw price-only return that treats the dividend as a pure loss.

\[ P_t^{adj} = P_t \times \prod_{k:\, t_k > t} f_k \]
P_t^{adj}the back-adjusted price at historical date \(t\)
P_tthe original, raw (unadjusted) price at date \(t\)
f_kthe adjustment factor for the \(k\)-th corporate action that occurred after \(t\) (a split ratio, or a dividend-reinvestment factor)

The product runs over every corporate action that happened after date \(t\) — each one contributes its own multiplicative factor, and they compound, so a price far enough in the past can carry the accumulated effect of many splits and dividend payments between then and now.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine a pizza that gets cut into more slices, but the pizza itself doesn't get any bigger or smaller. A stock split is exactly that — more shares, each worth less, but the total value someone owns doesn't change. Back-adjustment just relabels all the old prices in "today's slice size" so the history reads as one smooth line instead of a fake cliff-drop the day the pizza got recut.

Section 03

Futures rolls: back-adjustment ("Panama method") vs. ratio-adjustment

A futures contract has a finite life and expires; a continuous historical series has to be built by splicing together a sequence of individual contracts (the front month, then the next front month once the roll happens, and so on). At the roll date, the old and new contracts are almost never at exactly the same price — this price difference is a real, expected feature of futures pricing (time value, cost of carry, differing supply/demand at different points on the curve), not an error — but splicing the raw price series of contract A directly into contract B's raw price series at the roll date reproduces exactly the same fake-jump problem a stock split does. Two named, well-established methods exist to remove it.

Back-adjustment — the "Panama method"

At each roll date, compute the constant additive offset between the expiring contract's price and the new contract's price, and shift every historical price before that roll date by that same constant offset. The roll date itself shows exactly zero jump by construction, and every subsequent roll's offset compounds onto the already-shifted history. Because the shift is additive (a fixed number of price units, not a percentage), the shape of historical price changes is preserved exactly — a $2 move is still a $2 move, unaffected by the shift.

Ratio-adjustment

Instead of a constant additive offset, compute the multiplicative ratio between the two contracts at the roll date, and scale every historical price before that roll date by that ratio. This keeps every historical percentage return exactly correct across the roll (a 2% move is still a 2% move), at the cost of the actual historical price levels shifting by a compounding multiplicative factor with every roll further back in time — the opposite tradeoff from back-adjustment.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Picture two relay racers handing off a baton. If they hand it off at slightly different heights, you can either shift the whole first runner's path up or down so the handoff looks seamless (that's back-adjustment — the actual gaps between the first runner's own steps stay the same size), or you can stretch or shrink the first runner's whole path proportionally so the handoff still looks seamless a different way (that's ratio-adjustment). Either trick removes the awkward jump at the handoff — they just preserve a different thing about the runner's original path.

The trap: a back-adjusted ("Panama method") series can drift to unusual levels far enough in the past — for a long enough history of a contract that has rolled many times, the accumulated additive offsets can even push historical prices negative (when the new contract has repeatedly rolled in below the expiring one, so each offset shifts history down), a value that obviously never actually traded. This breaks any calculation that depends on the price level being a real, positive, tradeable number — most importantly, percentage-return calculations, since a percentage return computed off a negative or artificially shifted price level is meaningless, even though the back-adjusted series' point-to-point price differences (its absolute-dollar returns) remain perfectly correct the whole way through. This is exactly the tradeoff ratio-adjustment exists to avoid — but ratio-adjustment pays for it by distorting historical absolute price levels instead. Neither method is free; both are well-documented, standard practitioner tools, and the choice between them has to match what the downstream calculation actually needs (percentage returns, or consistent dollar/point-based P&L).
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

If you keep shifting old measurements by the same fixed amount every time you get a new, slightly different ruler, eventually — far enough back — those old measurements can end up reading as a negative number, even though nothing was ever actually "negative" in real life. That's the warning sign that the relabeling trick has been stretched too far back to still make sense as an actual price.

Raw (unadjusted) vs. back-adjusted futures series across a contract roll
The top series is technically accurate at every single point (each segment is the real, traded price of its contract) but shows a fake jump at the roll. The bottom series removes the jump by shifting the earlier segment — the price differences (returns) are preserved, but the absolute historical price level no longer matches what actually traded on those old dates.
🧮
Worked example

Splicing a crude oil roll with the Panama method

Predict first. The expiring crude contract closes at $78.40 and the new one at $78.90 on the roll day. A week earlier the old contract printed $72.00. In a Panama (back-adjusted) series, what does that $72.00 become? Lock in a guess, then check it against the working below.

$72.50. The offset is \(78.90 - 78.40 = 0.50\), added to every earlier price, so dollar moves are unchanged. A ratio-adjusted series would instead multiply by \(78.90/78.40\), giving about $72.46, which keeps percentage moves exact instead.

Suppose the expiring front-month crude contract closes its final trading day at $78.40, and the new front-month contract closes that same day at $78.90 — a $0.50 gap reflecting the futures curve, not an error. Splicing the raw series directly would show a $0.50 jump on the roll date that no position actually experienced (a trader holding a continuously-rolled position captured the roll at approximately the prevailing spread, not a discontinuous overnight gain or loss).

Applying the Panama method: the additive offset is \(78.90 - 78.40 = 0.50\). Every historical price before the roll date gets shifted up by exactly $0.50 — so a historical print of, say, $72.00 the week before the roll becomes $72.50 in the back-adjusted series. The point-to-point dollar change between any two historical days is completely unaffected by this shift (both days moved by the same constant), so a strategy measuring dollar P&L off this series gets exactly correct numbers. But the percentage return between those same two days is now computed off a price level ($72.50) that never actually traded on that historical date (the real traded price was $72.00) — a small distortion for one roll, but one that compounds with every subsequent roll further back in a multi-year history, which is exactly how a long-back-adjusted series can eventually reach unusual levels: shifted up when most rolls are upward like this one, or, when the new contract keeps rolling in below the expiring one, shifted down far enough to go negative.

🎯
A real-world trading scenario

The trend-following backtest that "breaks out" every quarter, right on the roll date

A researcher builds a momentum/trend-following signal on a commodity futures series stitched together naively — splicing each new front-month contract's raw prices directly onto the previous one with no adjustment at all. The backtest shows a suspiciously large, suspiciously reliable "breakout" return clustering right around each quarterly contract roll — exactly the calendar pattern a roll would produce, not a coincidence. The signal is not detecting a real momentum effect; it is detecting its own data pipeline's un-adjusted roll jump, four times a year, every year, and a trend-following rule is by construction exactly the kind of rule most likely to fire on a large single-day directional move, real or fake. Once the same backtest is rerun on a properly back-adjusted series, the quarterly "breakout" pattern disappears entirely — it was never a market phenomenon, it was an artifact of stitching two different contracts together without correcting for the price gap between them.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

It's like being impressed that a runner sprints super fast every quarter mile — except it turns out the racecourse itself has a downhill ramp built in at each quarter-mile mark, and you're measuring the ramp, not the runner. The "signal" only looked real because the data had an artificial jump baked in on a predictable schedule; fix the data, and the fake speed boost disappears.

Section 04

It actually happened: the day "the oil price" depended on the roll rule

The red box above warns that a back-adjusted futures series can drift negative, and treats a negative level as the tell-tale sign of an adjustment artifact. On 20 April 2020 a real, traded futures price went negative, and every continuous series built on that contract had to decide what to show.

It actually happened

20 April 2020: WTI settles at −$37.63

In the spring of 2020 lockdowns cut oil demand faster than producers could cut supply, and crude went into storage. The NYMEX WTI contract is settled by physical delivery at Cushing, Oklahoma, so as storage there filled, holding a contract into expiry meant needing somewhere to put a thousand barrels per contract. In early April the exchange told the market its systems could handle negative prices.

On Monday 20 April, the day before the May contract's last trading day, long holders who could not take delivery had to sell to almost nobody. The May contract settled at −$37.63 a barrel, the first negative settlement in the contract's history, after settling at roughly $18 the Friday before. The June contract, a month further out, stayed positive, at around $20. Products that held front-month contracts close to expiry were caught: a retail oil product sold by Bank of China left its customers with heavy losses and later drew regulatory penalties, while the largest US oil ETF had already been moving its holdings into later months.

For this lesson the striking part came afterwards, in the data. "The oil price on 20 April 2020" has no single answer. A front-month series that rolls at expiry shows −$37.63. A series that rolls a few days early, as many vendor continuous contracts do, was already on June and never shows a negative print. A ratio-adjusted series that rolls on that day has to divide by a negative number. Same market, same day, three histories.

The link marked ★ is this lesson's concept. Nothing in the raw data was wrong. Every series was a faithful splice of real settlements; the roll rule decided which settlements went in.

Where the textbook version breaks

The lesson's methods assume two things that held for the whole history of WTI futures until 2020: traded prices are positive, and the roll is a small gap between two nearby prices. Ratio-adjustment needs both, because it multiplies history by new-price over old-price; with the old price at −$37.63 that ratio is negative and flips the sign of everything before it. A percentage return on the front-month series, from about $18 to −$37.63, comes out near −300%, which a simple return on a long position in an asset can never reach, while the dollar change is a real loss per barrel. For futures the honest unit is dollar P&L on the contract held, which is the Panama series' strength. And a negative level stopped being proof of an artifact: it was the market.

Not tested here Not tested here: that a breakout or trend signal on a continuous futures series reflects the market rather than the roll. This desk has no ledger entry on roll handling. A fair test would rebuild the same signal on a back-adjusted series, a ratio-adjusted series and per-contract data with explicit rolls, check that the trades agree, and count how many signals fall within a few sessions of a roll date against the share of sessions that are that close to a roll.
Section 05

How practitioners actually use this

Serious futures research desks typically maintain both a back-adjusted and a ratio-adjusted (or unadjusted, per-contract) version of a continuous series side by side, and pick deliberately based on what the calculation needs: absolute dollar P&L and position-sizing math generally want the back-adjusted (Panama) series, while long-horizon percentage-return statistics, correlation studies, or anything comparing return magnitudes across very different historical eras generally want the ratio-adjusted series, or the raw individual-contract data with rolls handled explicitly trade by trade. Treating "which adjustment method" as a single, one-time infrastructure decision rather than a per-use-case choice is itself a common source of quietly wrong results.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where series continuity quietly breaks a backtest

  • Splicing raw contract prices with no adjustment at all. The single most damaging version of this mistake — every roll date manufactures a fake return large enough to distort any signal sensitive to big single-day moves (breakout rules, volatility estimators, drawdown statistics).
  • Computing percentage returns off a back-adjusted (Panama) series without checking the price level makes sense. A back-adjusted series' absolute price differences are correct; its price levels, especially far in the past, may not be — and a percentage return needs the level, not just the difference.
  • Forgetting a corporate action happened at all. A missed split or dividend produces the exact same fake-jump problem as a missed futures roll, just for equities instead of futures.
  • Using the wrong adjustment method for the calculation. Percentage-return-based statistics want a ratio-adjusted series; dollar/point-based P&L and position sizing want a back-adjusted (Panama) series — using the wrong one for the job silently distorts the result.
  • Assuming a vendor's "continuous contract" series is automatically the right adjustment. Vendors differ in default methodology; know which one is being used before trusting the numbers it produces.
Scenario drill · 20 April 2020

The front-month oil contract has just settled below zero

You maintain a continuous WTI crude series for a trend-following backtest. It is ratio-adjusted and rolls at expiry. Today the May contract settled at −$37.63, after about $18 on Friday, while June is around $20. Your series is due to roll. What do you do?

  1. Ratio-adjust across the roll as usual.
    The ratio is new price over old price, and the old price is negative. Every earlier price in the history gets multiplied by a negative number and flips sign.
  2. Keep the front-month series and compute today's percentage return.
    From about $18 to −$37.63 is a return of roughly −300%, which a simple long position in an asset can never reach. A percentage return through zero is meaningless.
  3. Use a back-adjusted (Panama) series for this backtest, measure P&L in dollars on the contract held, and record the roll rule alongside the results.
    For futures, dollar P&L on the contract actually held is the honest unit, and it is exactly what the Panama series preserves. Writing down the roll rule lets anyone see which version of the day the backtest used.
  4. Switch to a vendor series that rolls a few days early, so it never shows a negative price.
    Fine if your strategy really rolls early, and many funds did. But it hides an event that hit anyone who held the front month into expiry, so know which rule you are using and why.

What happened. Lockdowns had cut oil demand and storage at Cushing, Oklahoma, the delivery point, was filling. The May contract's −$37.63 was the first negative settlement in its history, while June stayed positive. A retail oil product sold by Bank of China left its customers with heavy losses; the largest US oil ETF had already moved into later months. Depending on its roll rule, a continuous series shows that day as −$37.63, about $20, or sign-flipped.

Section 06

Key Takeaways

✓A raw price series is not directly usable across a split, dividend, or futures roll. The jump it shows is real in the raw data but fake as a tradeable return.
✓Splits/dividends are fixed by a cumulative back-adjustment factor. Historical prices are restated so the true, economic return across the corporate action shows correctly.
✓Futures rolls have two named, standard fixes. Back-adjustment (the "Panama method," additive) preserves absolute price differences; ratio-adjustment (multiplicative) preserves percentage returns.
⚠Neither futures adjustment method is free. Back-adjusted series can drift to unusual or negative levels far in the past, breaking percentage-return math; ratio-adjusted series distort absolute historical price levels instead.
⚠Getting this wrong is a classic, well-documented source of silent backtest corruption. An un-adjusted roll or split can manufacture large fake single-day returns that a momentum or breakout signal will misread as genuine market behavior.
✓Choose the adjustment method to match the calculation. Dollar P&L and position sizing want back-adjustment; percentage-return statistics want ratio-adjustment.
⚠A negative print can be real, and the roll rule decides whether you see it. On 20 April 2020 WTI settled at −$37.63; ratio-adjustment across that roll divides by a negative price, and a percentage return through zero is meaningless. Know the roll rule before trusting a "continuous" price.
Section 07

Self-test

Q1. Why does a 2-for-1 stock split make a raw, unadjusted price series show a fake 50% loss, and what does back-adjustment do to fix it?
A 2-for-1 split doubles the share count while halving the per-share price, with no actual change in total shareholder value — the pizza is cut into more slices, not made smaller. A raw price series simply shows the per-share price dropping by half overnight, which a naive return calculation reads as a 50% loss even though nobody lost anything. Back-adjustment fixes this by multiplying every historical price before the split date by the split ratio (here, roughly halving all the pre-split prices), so the whole series is restated in consistent post-split terms and the split date itself shows approximately a zero true return.
Q2. What's the difference between the Panama method and ratio-adjustment for splicing futures contracts across a roll?
The Panama method (back-adjustment) shifts every historical price before a roll date by a constant additive offset — the dollar/point gap between the expiring and new contract at the roll — which preserves absolute price differences (dollar returns) exactly. Ratio-adjustment instead scales every historical price by a constant multiplicative ratio, which preserves percentage returns exactly instead. Both remove the fake jump at the roll date; they differ in which property of the historical series (absolute differences vs. percentage returns) stays exactly correct as a result.
Q3. Why can a long history of back-adjusted (Panama method) futures prices eventually go negative, and why does that matter?
Each roll applies its own additive offset to every price before that roll date, and these offsets compound going further back in history — a long enough series with many mostly-downward rolls (new contract below the expiring one) can accumulate a large enough total downward shift that sufficiently old historical prices are pushed below zero, a level that obviously never actually traded. This matters because any calculation that needs the actual price level to be a real, positive, tradeable number — most importantly a percentage return, which divides by that level — becomes meaningless once the level itself is an artifact of the adjustment rather than a real historical price, even though the series' point-to-point dollar differences remain completely correct throughout.
Q4. In the trend-following scenario, why did the quarterly "breakout" pattern disappear once the series was properly back-adjusted?
The naive, un-adjusted series spliced each new front-month contract directly onto the previous one without correcting for the price gap between them at each roll date, manufacturing a fake large single-day price move four times a year, exactly on the roll dates. A trend-following/breakout rule is specifically designed to react to large single-day directional moves, so it was firing on this artifact rather than on any genuine market pattern. Once the series was back-adjusted so the roll dates showed no fake jump, the rule had nothing artificial left to react to on those specific dates, and the quarterly pattern vanished — confirming it was a data-pipeline artifact, not a real momentum effect.
Q5. A researcher wants to compute a correlation of percentage returns between two commodity futures over 15 years, including several rolls each. Which adjustment method should the series use, and why?
Ratio-adjustment, because correlation of percentage returns depends on the returns being correctly computed as percentages across every roll in the history. A back-adjusted (Panama) series preserves absolute price differences but can distort the price levels used to compute a percentage return, especially far back in a 15-year, multi-roll history — exactly the wrong property to have wrong when the calculation in question is a percentage-return correlation.
