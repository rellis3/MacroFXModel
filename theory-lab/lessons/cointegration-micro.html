Lesson 9 · Cross-Asset & Structure

Cointegration: the Leash Between Two Prices

Two series can be 95% correlated for years and still have no stable relationship at all. Cointegration is the specific, testable condition that tells you whether a spread has a home to come back to — or is just two wandering price series that happened to lean the same way for a while. This is the statistical backbone underneath every pairs trade.

Section 1

Correlated in the changes, unanchored in the levels

"EUR/USD tracks the yield spread." "AUD/USD tracks copper." Most of that reasoning is checked with a correlation coefficient — which answers the wrong question.

Correlation measures whether two series' changes tend to move in the same direction. It says nothing about whether their levels are anchored to each other — whether, if the gap between them stretches too wide, some force actually pulls it back. Two series can be highly correlated in returns while their spread wanders off to new extremes forever, never returning. Trading that spread as if it were mean-reverting is not a subtle mistake — it's betting real money on a coin flip mislabeled as a law of physics.

A well-known teaching analogy: a drunk wandering randomly is a random walk — unpredictable, no destination, could end up arbitrarily far from where she started. Now give her a dog on a leash. The dog also wanders unpredictably on its own. But the distance between them is bounded by the leash — it can stretch and slacken, but it can't grow without limit, because something mechanical keeps pulling it back. Neither position alone is predictable. The gap is what cointegration is about.
The crucial distinction

Cointegration is a statement about the levels, not the returns. Two series can have highly correlated returns for years while their levels drift apart without limit — only a levels-based test tells you whether a spread has an equilibrium to actually revert to.

Section 2

Stationary: does this series have a home?

A series is (weakly) stationary if its mean, variance, and autocovariance don't depend on the calendar date — only on how far apart two observations are.

μ

One fixed long-run mean

E[Xt] = μ, the same at every t — a "normal level" the series fluctuates around.

σ²

One fixed variance

Var(Xt) = σ², the same at every t — how far it typically strays from μ never changes.

γ

Lag-only autocovariance

Cov(Xt, Xt+k) = γ(k) depends only on the lag k, never on which date t is.

A random walk, Xt = Xt−1 + εt, is the simplest non-stationary model: each step adds an independent shock, so Var(Xt) = t·σ² — it grows without bound. There's no fixed level to return to. Written as Xt = φXt−1 + εt, a series is stationary — I(0) — when |φ| < 1; it's a random walk with a unit root when φ = 1, and needs one round of differencing to become stationary — written I(1). Most raw FX levels are treated as I(1); their returns are typically I(0).

Section 3

Why two random walks can fool a regression

Granger & Newbold (1974): regress one unrelated random walk on another, in levels, and you often get a high R² and a "significant"-looking t-stat — despite zero real connection.

The distribution theory behind R² and t-statistics assumes stationary inputs. Feed it two random walks and the t-statistics don't converge to the familiar bell curve at all — they diverge as the sample grows, so a "significant" result gets more likely with more data, the opposite of real evidence. Any two series that each drift for stretches will tend to look related over a shared window purely by chance.

Engle-Granger, Step 1 — regress the levels

Robert Engle and Clive Granger (1987) turned "is this real?" into a concrete two-step test. Step 1 fits a hedge ratio; it does not, by itself, prove anything.

This is exactly the setup Granger & Newbold warned about — its R² alone is just as vulnerable to being spurious. Engle and Granger's real insight is narrower: if X and Y genuinely are cointegrated, this OLS regression is super-consistent — β̂ converges to the true hedge ratio unusually fast, even though its standard errors aren't reliable. Translation: good enough for the hedge ratio, not yet evidence a relationship exists.

Engle-Granger, Step 2 — test the residual itself

If the spread mean-reverts, being far above average should predict a negative next change — γ comes out reliably negative. If it's just a random walk with no memory of a "correct" level, γ is statistically indistinguishable from zero.
H₀ — unit root

γ = 0. The spread has no memory of a level — not cointegrated. Fail to reject this and you have no statistical basis for trading the spread as mean-reverting.

H₁ — stationary

γ < 0, negative enough to clear a critical value. The spread reverts — cointegrated. Now it behaves like the OU mean-reverting object from the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck lesson.

Why the critical value has to be stricter

ε̂t isn't independently observed — β̂ was already chosen by OLS to make this exact residual look as small and well-behaved as possible. Plain Dickey-Fuller critical values would reject H₀ too easily. Engle-Granger derived stricter critical values for this two-step situation (refined by MacKinnon), and correct implementations — including Python's statsmodels.tsa.stattools.coint — use those, not plain ADF values.

Section 7

Worked example — AUD/USD vs. NZD/USD

Two commodity-bloc currencies, routinely highly correlated (China demand, risk sentiment, commodity revenue, similar central-bank cycles). Regress AUD/USD on NZD/USD in levels over two years of daily closes.

Step 1 plausibly returns β̂ ≈ 1.05–1.15 and a striking R² north of 0.85–0.90. Per Granger & Newbold, that R² alone means nothing yet — it's exactly the number two correlated-but-maybe-not-cointegrated series would tend to produce. Step 2 — the ADF test on the residual — is what actually tells you which world you're in.

World A — not cointegrated

The residual drifts to a new high in year one, sits there, drifts to a new low in year two — never really returning. ADF's γ ≈ 0; fail to reject the unit-root null. The high R² was driven by both currencies trending against a common third factor (say, broad USD strength), not a link tying their ratio together.

World B — cointegrated

The residual oscillates in a visibly bounded band throughout both years, repeatedly reverting. ADF's γ is negative enough to clear the stricter critical value — a legitimate stationary spread. Now a z-score, bands, and an OU-style trade all make statistical sense.

You cannot tell which world you're in from Step 1's R² or a correlated-looking chart — that's the trap this lesson opened with. This example illustrates the procedure; it isn't a reported result.

Section 8

Beyond two series — Johansen's test

Engle-Granger has two structural limits once you move past a single pair. Søren Johansen (1988, 1991) built the proper generalization.

Engle-Granger's limits
  • Forces an arbitrary choice of which series is "Y" — regressing Y on X isn't the same as X on Y in a finite sample
  • Can only ever test for one cointegrating relationship at a time
Johansen's fix
  • Fits a Vector Autoregression (VAR) jointly across the whole basket — symmetric, no Y-vs-X choice
  • The rank of its long-run matrix Π (via "trace" and "max eigenvalue" tests) tells you how many independent equilibria exist at once

A basket of three or more series — commodity-bloc currencies, a term structure of yields — can hold more than one genuine equilibrium relationship simultaneously. You don't need the eigenvalue algebra to use the result: Johansen answers "how many real long-run relationships are hiding in this group, and what are they," across all series at once, instead of Engle-Granger's asymmetric, one-pair-at-a-time answer.

Section 9

How practitioners actually use this

🔀

Pairs trading

The flagship application. Find an economically linked pair, test for cointegration rather than assuming it from correlation, and — if it passes — trade the z-scored spread market-neutral: long the cheap leg, short the expensive one, sized by β̂.

📦

Index / ETF arbitrage

The cointegrating relationship is close to mechanically guaranteed (ETF price vs. basket NAV, held together by creation/redemption arbitrage) — the interesting question becomes execution speed and cost, not whether the link is real.

🌍

Macro / cross-asset relative value

Non-obvious pairs — a currency against a commodity, a corporate spread against equity vol — where the economic link is a hypothesis. The test does real work separating structure from coincidence.

🛠️

Error-correction models

Once cointegration is established, model each series' next change partly on how far last period's spread sat from equilibrium — a forecasting equation, not just a trading rule.

Gatev, Goetzmann & Rouwenhorst's widely cited 1998/2006 study documented pairs trading's historical performance in US equities; the cointegration-based version of pair selection (rather than their original simpler distance method) became standard on quant desks through the 2000s.

Section 10

Where cointegration testing quietly goes wrong

⏮

Re-estimating β with data you wouldn't have had

β̂ must come only from data available at trade time — fitting it on the full history is lookahead, and makes every historical spread look tighter than it would have looked live.

🧱

Structural breaks kill relationships silently

A currency peg removed, a company delisted, an export mix shifting — a pair can be genuinely cointegrated for years and then permanently stop being so.

🎣

Data-mining a large universe of pairs

Testing every pair in a big basket and keeping only the 5%-significant survivors is a textbook multiple-comparisons problem — roughly 1 in 20 unrelated pairs passes by chance alone.

↔

Engle-Granger's asymmetry

Regressing Y on X vs. X on Y isn't guaranteed to give the same β̂ or even the same conclusion in a finite sample — OLS only minimizes error in one direction.

⚠

Stationary ≠ tradeable

A genuinely cointegrated spread can revert so slowly (a long half-life) that costs and slippage eat the edge before reversion completes. Passing the test only clears the first bar.

Section 12 · It actually happened

LTCM, 1998: the leash held, the fund didn't

It actually happened

Long-Term Capital Management, founded in 1994 and with Nobel laureates Myron Scholes and Robert Merton among its partners, ran convergence trades: pairs tied together by economics, such as the newest 30-year Treasury against a nearly identical older one that was only cheaper because it was harder to trade. The spreads were tiny, so leverage was roughly 25 times capital. After Russia devalued and defaulted on 17 August 1998, investors fled into the most liquid bonds. The spreads stretched far past their history, and LTCM lost about $4.6 billion in less than four months. On 23 September a consortium of 14 banks and brokers, convened by the New York Fed, put in about $3.6 billion to take the book over and unwind it.

Section 13

What this desk found when it tested the claim

The tempting claim: fit a levels regression, and when the residual's z-score is stretched, fade it.

Desk test: nothing found residual-reversion-fx: fading price minus an OLS macro fair value, out of sample on 6 instruments. Only 1/6 (EURUSD) clears both a real icEdge and an above-coin-flip hit rate: chance-level, mean icEdge −0.030, mean hit rate 0.485. No economic force closes that gap.
Desk test: held up yield-spread-sleeve, the contrast: a spread with an anchor. The z-scored 2-year yield spread, out of sample 2015–2025: ~109 trades, 63% win, PF 2.2. Backtest plus paper trading since 2026-07, not a forward record.
Where the textbook breaks: the test assumes tomorrow's spread comes from the same distribution as the sample. LTCM's σ came from years with no liquidity panic, and the force that pulls a spread back, other arbitrageurs with spare capital, became a forced seller. Stationary over decades is not stationary over the time your lenders give you.
Section 14

Quick check

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Two FX pairs have 90% correlated daily returns for five years. Does that alone tell you the spread is a good pairs trade?

Correlation measures whether changes move together. Cointegration is a separate, stronger condition about whether the levels combine into a stationary spread — the two can and do disagree.
Question 2

In the Engle-Granger method, what is Step 2 actually testing?

Step 2 runs an ADF test on ε̂ₜ = Yₜ − α̂ − β̂Xₜ, checking whether γ in Δε̂ₜ = γ·ε̂ₜ₋₁ + ... is reliably negative — the residual's own stationarity, not Step 1's fit quality.
Question 3

Why does Step 2 use stricter Engle-Granger/MacKinnon critical values instead of plain Dickey-Fuller ones?

The residual isn't independently observed — Step 1's OLS fit already minimized it. Plain Dickey-Fuller critical values would reject the unit-root null too easily; Engle-Granger/MacKinnon values correct for that.
Question 4

In the AUD/USD-vs-NZD/USD example, what actually distinguishes World A (spurious) from World B (cointegrated)?

Both worlds can produce a near-identical, high Step-1 R². Only testing whether the residual itself reverts (Step 2) tells them apart.
Question 5

What extra capability does Johansen's test give over Engle-Granger for a basket of three or more series?

Johansen fits a VAR/VECM jointly across the whole basket, symmetric across all series, and its Π matrix's rank reveals how many independent cointegrating relationships exist at once.
Section 15

What to carry forward

01

Correlation measures whether changes move together; cointegration asks whether the levels have a stable, bounded spread — a stronger, rarer condition.

02

A random walk (unit root, φ=1) has variance growing with time and no fixed level to return to — regressing one against another in levels can still fake a high R² (Granger & Newbold).

03

Engle-Granger's two-step test is concrete: Step 1 regresses the levels for a hedge ratio β; Step 2 runs an ADF test — with stricter EG/MacKinnon critical values — on the residual spread.

04

Johansen generalizes to baskets of three or more series, avoiding the arbitrary Y-vs-X choice and finding how many equilibria exist at once.

05

Passing a cointegration test only clears the first bar — lookahead bias, structural breaks, data-mining across candidate pairs, and slow half-lives can all still sink a live pairs trade.

06

This repo already runs Engle-Granger-screened pairs trading (portfolioBacktest) and ADF/half-life diagnostics (forge/residual.py) — the code exists, though no result from it has been scored on the desk ledger yet.

07

LTCM 1998: genuinely leashed spreads still stretched far enough, at ~25:1 leverage, to end the fund. On this desk, fading an FX levels-regression residual was null (1/6 instruments, mean hit rate 0.485); the anchored 2Y yield spread is the validated contrast.

What this now lets you do

Tell apart a correlated-looking chart from a genuinely tradeable spread before risking capital on it. Run the Engle-Granger two-step yourself — or read the code already doing it in this repo — and know what should convince you an ADF statistic actually cleared the stricter bar. Recognise that a pairs trade needs more than a plausible story: it needs a leash, not just two things that happened to lean the same way.
