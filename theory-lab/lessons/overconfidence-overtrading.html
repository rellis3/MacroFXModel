Overconfidence and the Cost of Trading Too Much

Most people believe they are better-than-average drivers, and most traders believe their next trade is better than average too. The data on tens of thousands of real brokerage accounts shows what that belief costs, and the bill arrives as turnover.

CONCEPT — explained here; desk verdicts quoted where tested ~20 min read Prereqs: Returns & Compounding, Probability
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • Overconfidence is measurable. When people give 90% ranges for things they don't know, the truth lands inside far less than 90% of the time. US finance chiefs' 80% ranges for the stock market caught the actual return only 36% of the time.
  • It shows up as trading. Barber & Odean (2000) followed 66,465 households at a discount broker over 1991–96. The fifth that traded most earned 11.4% a year net, while the market returned 17.9%.
  • Men traded 45% more than women in the same kind of data, and trading cut their net returns by 2.65 points a year against 1.72 for women (Barber & Odean, 2001).
  • The arithmetic is plain: annual cost drag ≈ turnover × round-trip cost. It compounds. A 2.5-point drag on a 10% return gives up about 43% of the gain over 20 years.
  • Two quieter biases feed it: the illusion of control (acting makes you feel you've improved the odds) and hindsight bias (the past looks predictable, so the future feels predictable too).
  • Desk verdicts: trading costs are a hard wall (execution-gate, validated: above a spread of 0.15 of the daily range, zero of 30 strategy cells made money). And a confidence score is not an edge: one of the desk's own systems was scored and its conviction vote was anti-predictive (conviction-vote, null).
Section 01

Why this matters

The previous lesson was about holding on: selling winners too early and riding losers too long. This one is about the opposite muscle, the urge to do something. It matters because it is the one bias whose cost you can compute to the decimal point. You don't need to agree with any psychology to see the bill. Every round trip pays a spread and a commission. Trading more only pays if the extra trades are good enough to cover that. The best evidence we have says that for most people they aren't.

It also ties straight into the rest of the curriculum. Deflated Sharpe ratios exist because researchers fool themselves about the skill in a backtest. Execution algorithms exist because costs eat edge. And the Kelly criterion shows that betting as if your edge were bigger than it really is does more than lower returns. It can turn a winning edge into a losing one.

💡
Plain-English intuition

The trader who is always a little too sure

Picture two traders with exactly the same skill. One knows how much she doesn't know. She trades when she has a real reason, and otherwise she leaves the position alone. The other feels a bit more certain about every idea. His ranges are a bit narrower, his conviction a bit higher. So he trades more often, sizes a bit bigger, and switches ideas faster.

Neither one has more edge than the other. But he pays the spread and commission three or four times as often. Over a year that difference doesn't show up in any single trade. It shows up as a steady leak in the account. Overconfidence rarely shows up as one spectacular mistake. Mostly it is a habit that charges a small fee every time you act on it.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Every time you swap one toy for another at the toy swap, the swap stall keeps one sweet as a fee. If you're sure every new toy will be better, you swap all day and pay lots of sweets. Your toys aren't any better at the end. You just have fewer sweets.

Section 02

The math, step by step

Three pieces: how to measure overconfidence (calibration), how to price what it costs (cost drag), and how that cost compounds. None of it is hard algebra. What makes it powerful is that each piece can be measured.

1. Calibration: do your 90% ranges catch the truth 90% of the time?

A forecaster is calibrated if the things they call 90% likely happen about 90% of the time. The cleanest test asks for ranges. For each of \(N\) unknown quantities you give a low and a high value you are 90% sure contains the answer. If you are calibrated, each range is a coin that lands "hit" with probability \(c = 0.9\), so the number of hits \(H\) should follow a binomial distribution:

\[ H \sim \text{Binomial}(N,\,c), \qquad \Pr(H \le h) = \sum_{k=0}^{h} \binom{N}{k} c^{k}(1-c)^{N-k} \]
Hhow many of your ranges contained the true answer
Nhow many questions you answered
cthe confidence you claimed: 0.9 for a 90% range
Pr(H ≤ h)how likely a calibrated person is to do this badly or worse

The calibration gap is the claimed confidence minus the hit rate you actually achieved:

\[ \text{gap} = c - \frac{H}{N} \]
gap > 0overconfident: your ranges were too narrow
gap ≈ 0calibrated: your stated confidence was honest

With small \(N\) one miss means little. A calibrated person answering six questions misses at least one about 47% of the time. Missing three or more is a different matter: the chance of that is about 1.6%. Large studies remove the small-sample excuse. In study after study, people's "90%" ranges catch the truth well under 90% of the time, often closer to half. The most striking finance example comes from Ben-David, Graham & Harvey (2013). Over many years they asked US chief financial officers for 80% ranges on the next year's stock-market return. The actual return fell inside those ranges only 36% of the time.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

If you say "I'm 9-out-of-10 sure," you should be right about 9 times out of 10. Most grown-ups who say that are right more like 5 or 6 times out of 10. Their guesses aren't silly. They just draw the fence too tight.

2. Cost drag: what each unit of turnover costs

Define annual turnover \(T\) as the fraction of the portfolio sold and replaced in a year (100% means the whole book was swapped once). Each swap is a round trip: you sell one thing and buy another, and pay the spread, commission and price impact on both legs. If one round trip costs a fraction \(k\) of the amount traded, the yearly cost is approximately:

\[ D \approx T \times k \]
T
Turnover
How many times a year the book is replaced. The habit you control.
k
Round-trip cost
Spread + commission + impact, as a fraction of the trade. Set by the market and the broker.
D
Drag
Return lost per year before skill is counted at all.

The only way extra trading pays is if the trades you add earn more than \(k\) each, on average, after the fact. That is the break-even test. It is a high bar, because the person who sells to you is also trading, and often knows more. Odean (1999) took this test to the same brokerage data. The stocks investors bought went on to do worse than the stocks they sold, even before any costs. The trades didn't just fail to cover the fee. On average they lost money before the fee was charged.

3. Compounding turns a small leak into a large hole

A drag is subtracted every year, so it compounds. With gross annual return \(r\) and \(n\) years:

\[ W_{\text{gross}} = (1+r)^{n}, \qquad W_{\text{net}} = (1+r-D)^{n}, \qquad \text{share of gain lost} = 1 - \frac{W_{\text{net}}-1}{W_{\text{gross}}-1} \]
Wwealth per 1 invested at the end
rwhat the portfolio earns before trading costs
nyears
share lostthe fraction of the profit that went to costs

The share lost is always larger than \(D/r\), because the money paid in costs would itself have compounded. Drag the sliders in the calculator below to see it.

Worked example

Turnover of 250% at a 1% round-trip cost

1

The annual drag

\(D = T\times k = 2.5 \times 1\% = 2.5\%\) a year. The portfolio is replaced every four to five months.

2

Twenty years, before and after

At 10% gross: \(1.10^{20} = 6.727\). After costs, 7.5%: \(1.075^{20} = 4.248\).

3

The share of the gain lost

\[ 1 - \frac{4.248 - 1}{6.727 - 1} = 1 - \frac{3.248}{5.727} \approx 43\% \]

A drag that is a quarter of the annual return takes away 43% of the 20-year profit, because the money paid in costs never gets to compound.

🎯
Real-world trading scenario

The intraday version: cost measured against the move

For a short-term trader the useful yardstick isn't cost per year. It is cost compared with the size of the move you are trying to catch. Say a currency pair moves about 80 pips on a typical day (its ATR, average true range) and costs 1.2 pips to trade round trip. That is 1.5% of the daily range: small. Now trade the same pair on a 5-minute chart, where a typical move you target is 8 pips. The same 1.2 pips is now 15% of the move. Your edge has to be larger than that before you make anything.

This is how overconfidence and cost drag compound each other. The confident trader drops to faster timeframes ("more opportunities"). That raises the cost as a share of each move at exactly the point where their real edge, if they have one, is weakest. The desk tested this crossover directly. The result is below.

Section 03

Two quieter biases that feed it

Illusion of control

Ellen Langer (1975) ran a lottery in which some people chose their own ticket and others were handed one. When later asked to sell, the people who had chosen asked for substantially more money, even though every ticket had identical odds. The act of choosing made the outcome feel more controllable. In trading, clicking the button, adjusting the stop, or watching the screen all create the same feeling. Activity is easily mistaken for skill.

Hindsight bias

Baruch Fischhoff (1975) showed that once people learn how an event turned out, they rate that outcome as having been more predictable than they judged it beforehand. He called this "creeping determinism". Every chart looks obvious from the right-hand edge. If the past was that predictable, you reason, the future must be too. So your ranges narrow and you trade more.

A third mechanism, self-attribution, keeps the loop going: wins are credited to skill and losses to bad luck. A run of good results therefore raises confidence more than a run of bad results lowers it. That predicts something specific: people should trade more after doing well. The online-trading story below fits that prediction.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Rolling the dice yourself feels luckier than letting someone else roll, but the dice don't know who's rolling. And after the game, everyone says "I knew that would happen", even the people who didn't.

Section 04

It actually happened: the brokerage accounts that told on us

It actually happened

1991–96: 66,465 households, and the ones who traded most

Brad Barber and Terrance Odean got the account records of 66,465 households at a large US discount broker for 1991–1996. That gave them every trade, every holding and every commission. They sorted the households by how much they traded. The average household turned over about 75% of its stock portfolio a year and earned 16.4% net, a little below the market. The fifth that traded most earned 11.4% a year net, while the market returned 17.9%. Before costs the active traders' picks were roughly as good as everyone else's. The gap came from costs: spread and commission, paid over and over. The paper's title said it bluntly: Trading Is Hazardous to Your Wealth.

The follow-up, Boys Will Be Boys (2001), used the same broker's data, split by gender. Psychology research had found men tend to be more overconfident than women in areas like finance. If overconfidence drives trading, men should trade more and lose more to it. They did. Men traded 45% more than women, and trading reduced men's net returns by 2.65 percentage points a year, against 1.72 for women. The gap between single men and single women was wider still.

It actually happened

The 1990s move online: doing well, then trading more

In the mid-1990s online brokers made trading faster and cheaper per click, and the late-1990s boom made it exciting. Barber and Odean (2002) followed investors who switched from phone-based to online trading. The investors who made the switch had been beating the market before they went online. A good run is exactly what self-attribution turns into confidence. After switching, they traded more actively and more speculatively, and their performance fell behind the market. The authors titled the paper Online Investors: Do the Slow Die First?

Day trading is the extreme version. A later study of Taiwan's market, which records every trade by every investor, followed day traders over many years. Barber, Lee, Liu and Odean (2014) found that fewer than 1% could be shown to profit reliably, year after year, net of fees. Most people who tried it lost money and stopped. Overconfidence doesn't need a bubble to cost you. A boom just lets more people act on it at once.

What the desk found

The folk claim this lesson is about is "if I'm confident, acting on it more often makes money." The desk hasn't studied retail accounts. It has tested two pieces of that claim on its own systems: what costs do to short-horizon edges, and whether a confidence score predicts which trades win.

Desk test: held up execution-gate: when the spread is a large fraction of the day's range, no short-horizon edge survives it. With spread ÷ ATR above 0.15, zero of 30 strategy × timeframe cells were profitable. Cost outruns edge as the timeframe speeds up, and the crossover sits in the same place across strategies. In this lesson's terms: moving to faster charts to "find more trades" buys you more trades, and each one costs more as a share of the move.
Desk test: nothing found conviction-vote: a higher conviction score on an entry does not mean a better trade. One of the desk's own automated systems gave every entry a conviction vote. When it was scored, the vote was anti-predictive of outcome, and the system was a net loser (−£38k) on a 30% win rate, not because of a few deep losses. Confidence that has never been scored is a feeling, not a measurement. That applies to a model as much as to a person.
Not tested here Retail turnover and returns on this desk's markets. The household figures above are Barber and Odean's, from US equities in the 1990s. Nothing here re-tests them for FX or index CFDs, or for today's lower commissions. Lower commissions shrink \(k\) but not the spread. Cheaper trades also invite more of them, so \(T\) tends to rise.

The grown-ups who swapped their toys most ended up with the fewest sweets. The boys swapped more than the girls and lost more sweets. And when the desk let a robot say how sure it was about each swap, its "really sure" swaps turned out worse, not better.

Section 05

How practitioners actually use this

Nobody fixes overconfidence by deciding to be humble. What works is building checks into the process, so that the bias has to get past a rule before it reaches the order ticket:

1

Write the range, not the point

Before each trade, write the 80% range for where price will be at your exit time. Score those ranges every month the way the quiz above did. If fewer than 80% hit, widen your stops or cut your size until they do.

2

Compute cost ÷ move before the idea, not after

Divide the round-trip cost by the typical move on your chosen timeframe. If it is a big share, the idea is dead before it starts, however good the chart looks.

3

Budget your turnover

Set a maximum number of round trips per week or month, in advance. Then each trade has to beat the others for one of the limited slots. A budget makes you ask "is this better than my average idea?", which is the question overconfidence skips.

4

Score your confidence

If you tag trades "A", "B", "C", check after 50 trades whether the A trades actually did better. If they didn't, the tag is noise, and sizing up on it adds risk without adding edge. Lesson 6 builds the journal that makes this possible.

🎯
Self-audit · do this now

Five questions before your next trade

Answer honestly. A "yes" to two or more means the trade is coming from the feeling, not the evidence.

  • Is this my second or later trade in this market today?
  • Did my last two or three trades win, and do I feel "in the zone"?
  • Is my stop tighter than the market's typical noise, because I'm "sure" of the entry?
  • Have I switched to a faster chart than the one my plan was written for?
  • Would I struggle to write down the specific thing that would prove this trade wrong?

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where people get this wrong

  • "Commissions are zero now, so turnover is free." The spread is still there, and so is the price impact. "Zero commission" brokers are typically paid through order flow instead, which isn't free to you either.
  • Judging calibration from one miss. With six questions, a calibrated person misses at least one about 47% of the time. You need many forecasts before a gap means anything.
  • Mistaking a bull market for skill. In 1991–96 almost everyone made money. The question is whether you beat a do-nothing alternative after costs, not whether you made money at all.
  • Over-correcting into paralysis. The lesson is not "never trade." It is "trade when the expected edge clearly beats \(k\)." Doing nothing is also a position.
  • Counting the wins and forgetting the costs. Most trading platforms show P&L per trade before costs. Add spread and fees back in before you judge any streak.

Key Takeaways

✓Overconfidence is a measurable miscalibration. People's 90% ranges catch the truth far less than 90% of the time. CFOs' 80% stock-market ranges caught it 36% of the time.
✓It costs money through turnover. The most active fifth of 66,465 households earned 11.4% net against the market's 17.9% (Barber & Odean, 2000). Men traded 45% more than women and lost more to it (2001).
✓Drag ≈ turnover × round-trip cost, and it compounds. 250% turnover at 1% costs 2.5 points a year, about 43% of a 20-year gain at 10%.
✓Cost against the move is a hard wall (execution-gate, validated). Above a spread of 0.15 of ATR, zero of 30 strategy cells made money. Faster charts mean more trades, each costing more as a share of the move.
⚠Illusion of control and hindsight bias feed the loop. Acting feels like improving the odds, and the past always looks predictable from the right-hand edge of the chart.
✗Confidence is not edge (conviction-vote, null). One of the desk's own systems scored its conviction on every entry. The score was anti-predictive, and the system lost money on a 30% win rate. Score your A/B/C tags before you size on them.
Section 07

Self-test

Q1. Someone answers ten 90%-range questions and gets 9 right. Are they calibrated?
On this evidence, yes: 9 of 10 is exactly the expected hit rate. Ten questions is still a small sample, though, so a calibrated person could easily have scored 8, and an overconfident one could have got lucky. Calibration is judged over many forecasts.
Q2. Turnover is 400% a year and a round trip costs 0.5%. What is the annual drag, and what must the extra trades earn to justify it?
\(D = 4 \times 0.5\% = 2\%\) a year. To break even the trading has to add at least 2 points a year above what the portfolio would earn if you held it. That means each of the four round trips has to earn more than 0.5% above the alternative, on average, after the fact.
Q3. In Barber & Odean (2000), was the most active traders' underperformance mainly about bad stock picks or about costs?
Mainly costs. Before costs, the active households' returns were roughly comparable with everyone else's. The 11.4% vs 17.9% gap opened up after spread and commission, paid on very high turnover. Odean (1999) adds that the stocks traders bought did worse than the ones they sold, so the trades didn't add value even before costs.
Q4. Why does the "Boys Will Be Boys" result support overconfidence as the cause, rather than just "some people like trading"?
Psychology research had found men to be more overconfident than women in areas like finance. Overconfidence predicts that men will trade more and lose more from that trading. Both things happened: 45% more trading, and 2.65 vs 1.72 points of annual return lost. That makes the gender split a test of the prediction, not just a description of who trades.
Q5. A trader moves from the daily chart to the 5-minute chart "to get more opportunities". What happens to their costs, using the execution-gate result?
The cost of one round trip stays about the same in pips, but the move each trade aims for shrinks a lot. Cost as a share of the move (spread ÷ ATR on that timeframe) rises sharply. The desk found that above 0.15, zero of 30 strategy × timeframe cells were profitable. More opportunities, each with a worse cost-to-move ratio.
Q6. You tag your trades A, B or C by conviction. What would tell you the tags are worth sizing on?
A scored record: after a decent sample (dozens of trades per tag, not five), A trades should do clearly better than C trades after costs. If they don't, the tag measures how you feel, not what happens next, and sizing on it adds risk without adding return. The desk's conviction-vote null is exactly this check, and it failed.
