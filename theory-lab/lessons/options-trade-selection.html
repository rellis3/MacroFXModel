From View to Trade: Choosing the Expiry and Strike

Being right about direction is the easy half. An option only pays if you are right about how far, by when, and by more than the market has already charged you for.

CONCEPT — a way to choose between contracts, not a signal and not advice ~17 min read Prereqs: The Four Option Positions, Black-Scholes-Merton
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
Risk: this is education, not advice and not a recommendation. Every number is illustrative. A bought option can lose its whole premium; a sold option can lose far more than the premium. Options are leveraged, wide-spread and easy to get wrong, so size every trade as if the worst case will happen.
TL;DR
  • A view has four parts: direction, size of the move, time window, and how confident you are. An option price already contains the market's own guess at the size and the timing, so direction alone is not an edge.
  • The market's guess is readable. One standard deviation over the life of the option is \(M_1 = S\sigma\sqrt{\tau}\), and the at-the-money straddle costs about \(0.8\,M_1\). On a $100 stock at 22% implied volatility, 28 days gives \(M_1\) = $6.09 and a straddle of $4.86.
  • The test for any strike: the breakeven distance divided by \(M_1\), call it \(R\). An at-the-money option sits near \(R \approx 0.4\) at every expiry. Out-of-the-money strikes need a larger \(R\), so "cheap" options ask for bigger moves: a 5% out-of-the-money 56-day call needs \(R\) = 0.77.
  • Choose the expiry to outlast your window with room to spare, not to match it. A 28-day call loses 1.84% of its value a day to theta; the 56-day call loses 0.93%. If your move arrives late, the shorter option may already be dead.
  • Check the volatility you are paying against what you expect to be realised, and check costs. A 56-day at-the-money call bought at 22% costs 21% more than the same call at your 18% forecast, and a $0.05 spread is 22.6% of a 22-cent option.
  • Real-world episode: Netflix, 20 April 2022. Its first fall in subscribers in over a decade sent the shares down about 35% in a day, about three and a half times the move that market commentary said the options were pricing. Implied moves are averages, not ceilings. Nothing in this lesson has been tested as a trading rule on this desk.

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

I think this stock rises about four percent over the next four weeks. So I buy a call and wait. Done?

Not yet. A call is not a bet on direction alone. It is a bet on direction, size and timing, and the market has already priced its own guess at all three. What does the market think the stock will do in four weeks?

Go up or down, I suppose.

Wrong question. The right one is how far, in either direction. Take a hundred-dollar stock with twenty-two percent implied volatility. Over twenty-eight days a typical move, one standard deviation, is about six dollars. And the at-the-money straddle, a call plus a put, costs about four dollars eighty-six. That is the market's price for the size of the move.

So my four percent is smaller than the market's typical move?

Yes. The market already expects more wobble than your target. So direction alone is not an edge. Your edge is that you think the centre of the distribution sits higher than the market does, and that gap has to pay for the premium.

And the call itself?

About two fifty at the money, twenty-eight days. Break even is a hundred and two fifty-one at expiry. A handy rule: an at-the-money option costs roughly four tenths of a typical move, whatever the expiry.

Which expiry, then? My view is four weeks.

Not four weeks. If you buy exactly four weeks and the move turns up on day thirty, the option is dead. Let the expiry outlast your window with room to spare. Eight weeks costs about three fifty-nine. It loses a bit under one percent of its value a day to time decay, against nearly two percent a day for the four-week option.

So longer is just better?

Longer is more robust. It costs more cash and has more sensitivity to volatility. If everything happens on schedule the two do about the same. The longer one wins when you are late.

And the strike?

Further out of the money is cheaper but needs a bigger move. Five percent out of the money at eight weeks costs about a dollar sixty-six and needs the stock to rise nearly seven percent by expiry just to break even. Cheap is not the same as good value.

What about volatility itself?

You are paying twenty-two percent. If you think the stock will really move at eighteen, you are paying about a fifth more than the call is worth to you. And watch events. Before earnings, implied volatility is high, then it collapses. In our example a call gains only fourteen percent on a five percent rally, and loses forty percent if the stock just sits there.

Anything else?

Costs. Five cents of spread on a three-fifty option is nothing. Five cents on a twenty-two cent option is nearly a quarter of what you pay. And remember that averages are not ceilings. In April twenty twenty-two Netflix reported its first fall in subscribers in over a decade. Commentary at the time put the options-implied move near ten percent. The shares fell about thirty-five percent in a day.

So the one-line version?

Before you buy an option, write down direction, size, timing and what you are paying for each. Trade it only if your numbers beat the market's by more than the costs.

Section 01

Why this matters

Most beginners meet options as a cheaper way to be bullish or bearish. They pick a direction, glance at a few prices, and buy whichever contract looks affordable. Then the stock goes the right way and the position loses money. Nothing was broken: the trade was never a direction bet. It was a bet on direction, size and timing, all at once, bought at a price that already assumed an answer to the last two.

The earlier lessons in this group gave you the parts: the four positions, how a price is built, why time decay accelerates and why volatility matters. This lesson is the step in between: given a view, how do you choose which contract, and how do you know whether you are being paid for taking it? Later in this series the same machinery is used for spreads, for selling premium and for hedging.

A warning that experienced traders repeat for good reason: options punish loose thinking. A stock trade with a sloppy view is wrong by the size of the miss. An option trade with a sloppy view can be wrong when the stock does exactly what you said, because the contract had the wrong expiry, the wrong strike, or was bought when volatility was at its highest. The aim here is to name each of those traps and put a number on it before you trade.

💡
Plain-English intuition

The premium is a bookmaker's quote, so ask what it assumes

Imagine a bookmaker offers odds on "the stock ends the month above 104". The odds were not chosen at random. They encode how far the bookmaker thinks the price will move and how likely each outcome is. You do not win by agreeing with the bookmaker. You win only if your own estimate is better than theirs by more than their margin.

An option works the same way. Its premium is the market's quote for a range of outcomes, scaled by implied volatility and time. Choosing a trade is therefore two steps. First, read what the quote assumes: the typical move, the breakeven, the daily cost of waiting. Second, compare that with your own numbers for direction, size and timing. If your numbers are not different enough to cover the margin, the right trade is no trade.

Section 02

The math, step by step: from a view to four numbers

Watch the formula build
How to read a strike against the market's own expected move, one term at a time
\[ M_1 = S\,\color{#fbbf24}{\sigma_{imp}}\sqrt{\tau} \]

Start with the market's typical move. Implied volatility is quoted per year, so scale it by the square root of the time left, \(\tau\) in years, and by the spot price \(S\) to get dollars. This is one standard deviation, \(M_1\).

\[ \mathbb{E}\,|S_\tau - S| \approx \color{#fbbf24}{\sqrt{2/\pi}}\,M_1 \approx 0.8\,M_1 \]

The average size of a move, ignoring its sign, is about 80% of one standard deviation. The at-the-money straddle costs about this much, which is why the straddle is the market's quote for "how far, either way".

\[ B = K + \color{#fbbf24}{C} \]

Now your trade. A long call only breaks even at expiry once spot has risen by the whole premium \(C\) beyond the strike \(K\). The breakeven is \(B\). (For a put it is \(B = K - P\).)

\[ m = \color{#fbbf24}{B - S} \]

The move you need is the breakeven minus spot. It has two parts, how far the strike is from spot and how much you paid for the right.

\[ R = \frac{B - S}{S\,\sigma_{imp}\sqrt{\tau}} = \color{#10b981}{\frac{m}{M_1}} \]

Divide the move you need by the market's typical move. \(R\) is the breakeven measured in implied moves. About 0.4 means "a normal wobble gets me there". Above 1 means "I need the market to be surprised".

1. The market's expected move. Options are priced from a volatility number and a time. For a stock with spot \(S\), the standard deviation of the price at expiry, if volatility is \(\sigma_{imp}\) per year and \(\tau\) years remain, is approximately:

\[ M_1 = S\,\sigma_{imp}\sqrt{\tau} \]
M₁one standard deviation of the price move at expiry, in price units
σ_impimplied volatility, per year, as a decimal (0.22 = 22%)
√τsquare root of the time left in years; four times the time doubles the move

With \(S = 100\), \(\sigma_{imp} = 22\%\) and 28 days (\(\tau = 28/365\)), \(M_1 = 100 \times 0.22 \times 0.277 =\) $6.09, which is 6.1% of spot. Roughly two thirds of outcomes are expected inside that band, and one third outside it. That is the funnel in the picture above.

2. The straddle is the quote for "how far". A call plus a put at the same strike pays the absolute size of the move. For a normally distributed move the average absolute value is \(\sqrt{2/\pi} \approx 0.80\) standard deviations, so:

\[ \text{ATM straddle} \approx \sqrt{\tfrac{2}{\pi}}\,S\,\sigma_{imp}\sqrt{\tau} \approx 0.8\,M_1 \]
√(2/π)0.798, the average of |z| for a standard normal z
straddleat-the-money call plus put, same strike and expiry
≈very close when interest is zero and the move is small

Check it with the same inputs: \(0.798 \times 6.09 =\) $4.86, and Black-Scholes with a 2% rate gives $4.86. The match is the reason traders quote "the implied move" straight off the straddle. It is also why half the straddle, about \(0.4\,M_1\), is the price of an at-the-money call or put.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

The straddle price is what the market charges for "the stock will move, I do not know which way". If it costs $4.86, the market is saying "we expect it to end up around $4.86 away from where it is now, on average". If your idea is that it only goes up $4, the market has already priced a bigger jump than your idea.

3. Breakeven and the selection ratio \(R\). For a call, premium \(C\) paid, the position is profitable at expiry once \(S_\tau > K + C\). The move needed from today's spot, relative to the market's typical move, is:

\[ R = \frac{(K + C) - S}{S\,\sigma_{imp}\sqrt{\tau}} \]

At the money, \(C \approx 0.4\,M_1\) and the strike equals spot, so \(R \approx 0.4\) whatever the expiry. The 56-day call has \(R =\) 0.42 and the 28-day call \(R =\) 0.41. Moving the strike out of the money adds the strike distance to the numerator while the premium shrinks only slowly, so \(R\) rises with distance. That is the whole reason cheap options are not good value by default: you pay less because the market thinks you are less likely to get there.

4. The daily cost of waiting. Theta, the daily decay, is the rent from the Theta Decay lesson. What matters for choosing an expiry is not theta in dollars but theta as a share of what you paid, and what you would lose if you held the position flat while waiting:

\[ d = \frac{|\Theta|}{C} \qquad\qquad \text{cost of waiting } h \text{ days} = C(S,\tau) - C(S,\tau - h/365) \]
dfraction of the premium lost per day to time decay, spot unchanged
Θtheta per day from Black-Scholes, in dollars
hdays you expect to wait before your view plays out

For the at-the-money 28-day call, \(d =\) 1.84% per day. For the 56-day call, \(d =\) 0.93%. If the stock goes nowhere for your 28-day window, the 56-day call still has $2.51 of its original $3.59 premium to sell; you lose $1.08. The 28-day call is worth zero if spot ends at the strike.

5. Your own expected value. The only reason to buy an option is that your numbers differ from the market's. Suppose you think the stock drifts at an annual rate \(\mu\) and will actually move with volatility \(\sigma_r\). Then for an option held to expiry the expected payoff of a call under your view, with \(\ln S_\tau\) normally distributed, is:

\[ \mathbb{E}\big[(S_\tau - K)^+\big] = S\,e^{\mu\tau}\,N(d_1^{*}) - K\,N(d_2^{*}),\qquad d_{1}^{*} = \frac{\ln(S/K) + (\mu + \tfrac12\sigma_r^2)\tau}{\sigma_r\sqrt{\tau}},\; d_2^{*} = d_1^{*} - \sigma_r\sqrt{\tau} \]
μyour forecast of the stock's annual drift (set by your target price)
σ_ryour forecast of realised volatility over the window
N(·)the standard normal cumulative distribution
d₁*, d₂*the Black-Scholes d-terms, but built from your \(\mu\) and \(\sigma_r\) rather than the market's \(r\) and \(\sigma_{imp}\)

This is Black-Scholes with the market's inputs swapped for yours and no discounting. Your edge is that expected payoff minus the premium and the spread. Set your inputs equal to the market's (drift equal to the interest rate, volatility equal to \(\sigma_{imp}\)) and the edge falls to roughly minus the spread: the 56-day at-the-money call has an expected P&L of about minus $0.04 per share. Every dollar of expected profit has to come from a difference between your numbers and the market's, and the calculator below lets you see how large that difference has to be.

The calculator uses a slightly fuller version that you can exit early: it values the option on your exit day with Black-Scholes at the same implied volatility, averaging over the same lognormal view. It is a model of a belief, not a forecast.

Section 03

Choosing the expiry, the strike and the price you pay

The five numbers below, taken in order, turn a view into a contract. None is a trading signal. Each is a way to check that the contract fits the view and that you are not overpaying for the privilege.

The expiry: outlast your window, do not match it

Your view has a window: "within about four weeks". Dates slip. The catalyst arrives late, or the move starts and then stalls. If the option expires on the day the window ends, any delay turns a correct view into a total loss. An expiry beyond the window, say twice as far out, gives timing error somewhere to go. That is a rule of thumb rather than a law. The right cushion depends on how sure you are of the timing and what the extra time costs.

Extra time costs money, but less than it first looks. A longer option costs more in total, yet it loses a smaller share of itself each day, and you only pay for the days you actually hold it. In our example (spot 100, 22% implied volatility, at the money) the 28-day call is $2.51 and loses 1.84% a day. The 56-day call is $3.59 and loses 0.93% a day. If you sell on day 28 the longer option has kept most of its time value.

A strike is a trade between price and distance. A lower strike (for a call) costs more and breaks even sooner. A higher strike costs less and needs more. The volatility you are paying for

Implied volatility is the one input you do not control, and it is not constant. Compare it with what has recently been realised and with what you expect over the window. An option is a bet that realised movement will be large enough to cover the implied level. If you buy at 22% and the stock then moves at 15%, you have paid for movement that did not arrive. For a rough size of the overpayment, use vega:

\[ C(\sigma_{imp}) - C(\sigma_{r}) \;\approx\; \nu\,(\sigma_{imp} - \sigma_{r}) \]
νvega: change in the option price per one unit of volatility (per point, divide by 100)
σ_imp − σ_rthe gap between what you pay and what you expect to be delivered

The 56-day at-the-money call has a vega of $0.156 per volatility point. A four-point gap, 22% against your 18%, is about $0.62 by that rule, and the full repricing gives $3.59 against $2.96: the call costs 21% more than at your volatility. Against the 15% the stock has recently realised, the gap is 44%. On average, implied volatility in index options has tended to sit above the volatility realised afterwards, which is a headwind for buyers. The variance risk premium lesson later in this series covers the evidence and its limits. It is an average, not a prediction for any one trade.

Worked example (illustrative numbers)

Right direction, volatility crushed: an event call

A stock is at $100 the day before it reports earnings. The 30-day at-the-money call costs $5.79 with implied volatility at 50%, because the date of the report is inside the option's life. The company reports, the stock rises 5%, and the uncertainty is resolved, so implied volatility drops to 30%. One day has passed (29 days left).

The call is now worth $6.63, a gain of only $0.84 or 14%. Had implied volatility stayed at 50% the same 5% rise would have left it worth $8.70, a gain of $2.90. The $2.07 difference is the volatility crush. And if the stock had simply stayed at $100, the same fall in volatility would have taken the call to $3.45, a loss of −40% even though nothing bad happened to the stock. The earnings move was priced in advance, so you only profit from the amount by which it exceeds what the market already charged.

Two lessons. When an event sits inside the option's life, you are buying the event's volatility and will lose it the moment the event passes. And the right comparison for an event is the straddle price against your own estimate of the move.

Liquidity and costs

You pay half the bid-ask spread to get in and half to get out. In dollars that is the same on a cheap and a dear option, so as a share of the premium it is brutal on cheap contracts: $0.05 is 1.4% of a $3.59 option and 22.6% of a 22-cent one. Check the quoted spread, the open interest and the day's volume before you look at anything else. As a working rule for this lesson, not a market standard, treat a spread above about 5% of the premium as a cost that needs a reason. Wide spreads also mean the mid-price is a guess, so work a limit order and know your price before you click.

Worked example (illustrative numbers)

From "I think it goes up about 4%" to one contract

An ETF, call it XYZ, trades at $100. You expect a re-rating on a known event and think it will be near $104 in about four weeks (28 days), with a realised volatility around 18% (the last 20 days realised about 15%, you add a little for the event). The market's options show 22% implied for every expiry. Interest 2%, no dividends, an option is quoted per share, the spread is $0.05, and your risk budget is $500.

1

Write the view in four parts

Direction up. Size: +4% to $104. Window: 28 days. Confidence: moderate, so assume the timing can slip. No view on anything else.

2

Read the market's quote

\(M_1 = 100 \times 0.22 \times \sqrt{28/365} =\) $6.09, and the straddle is $4.86. Your +4% is smaller than the market's typical move of 6.1% and about the size of its average move (4.9%). So your edge is that the centre sits higher, not that the stock will move a lot.

3

Choose the expiry

A 28-day option ends on the day your window ends, so a late move kills it. Choose 56 days. The at-the-money call is $3.59, with delta 0.53, theta $−0.033 a day (0.93% of premium) and vega $0.156 per point.

4

Choose the strike

The breakeven at expiry is $103.59, 3.6% above spot, \(R =\) 0.42. The 105 strike is cheaper ($1.66) but breaks even at $106.66, above your target. The 95 strike is $6.64 and protects more, but ties up much more money. The at-the-money 100 strike balances them. On your exit day (28) its breakeven spot is only 101.86.

5

Check what happens if you are right, and if you are flat

If the stock is at 104 on day 28, the call is worth $5.09 and the trade makes $1.50 a share. If nothing happens, you lose $1.08 of the premium by day 28.

6

Check volatility and costs

At your 18% volatility the same call is worth $2.96, so you pay 21% for the gap. The $0.05 spread is 1.4% of the premium, acceptable. Under your view the chance of being in profit on day 28 is 65.5% and the expected P&L is $2.06 a share.

7

Size it by the worst case

Maximum loss is the whole premium: $3.59 a share, $358.68 for one contract of 100 shares. Against a $500 budget that is one contract, with $141.32 of budget left over.

Notice what the numbers also say. If you had thought the stock would reach only $101, the same trade has an expected P&L of $0.13 a share and a 42.6% chance of profit. A one-point edge barely covers the costs, and you would not do it.

\(S = K = 80\), implied volatility 25%, 30 days, and the call costs \(C = 2.35\). Then \(\tau = 30/365 = 0.0822\) and \(\sqrt{\tau} = 0.2867\), so \(M_1 = 80 \times 0.25 \times 0.2867 = 5.73\). The average move is \(0.8 \times 5.73 = 4.58\) and the breakeven is \(B = 80 + 2.35 = 82.35\). The selection ratio is \(R = 2.35 / 5.73 =\) .

Real-world trading scenario (illustrative numbers)

A currency view: EUR/USD down 100 pips in three weeks

EUR/USD is at 1.0800. You expect it to fall about 100 pips, to 1.0700, over roughly three weeks, on a central bank repricing. One-month at-the-money implied volatility is 7.5%. To keep the arithmetic clean this example uses zero interest on both currencies. FX options conventions (the New York 10:00 cut, premium in pips or per cent) come in the first lesson of this series.

The market's typical one-month move is \(M_1 = 1.0800 \times 0.075 \times \sqrt{30/365} = \) 232 pips, and the at-the-money straddle is about 185 pips. Your 100-pip view is well inside that, the same shape as the share example. An at-the-money 30-day put costs about 93 pips and breaks even at 1.0707, so it needs the 93-pip fall you expect, with \(R \approx\) 0.40.

The tempting cheaper trade is a put struck at your target, 1.0700. It costs about 51 pips, breaks even at 1.0649, and needs a 151-pip fall, \(R \approx\) 0.65. If the pair goes exactly to your target at expiry, it pays nothing and you lose the whole 51 pips. A strike at the target is the classic error: you can be exactly right and still lose. Put the breakeven inside the move you expect, not the strike.

Section 05

It actually happened: Netflix, 20 April 2022

The implied move is the market's average guess for how far a stock will travel. This episode is a reminder that it is a guide to the typical move, not a ceiling on the possible one.

It actually happened

A report that moved the stock several implied moves in one day

Netflix reported its first-quarter 2022 results after the US close on 19 April 2022. It had guided to adding about 2.5 million paying subscribers. It reported a loss of about 200,000, its first fall in subscribers in more than a decade.

Pre-earnings market commentary put the move the options market was pricing at roughly 10% either way. That is a single figure from commentary, not an exchange record, so treat it as approximate. On 20 April the shares closed down about 35%, at $226.19, taking roughly $54 billion off the company's market value, according to Bloomberg's report that day, which called it the biggest one-day fall since October 2004. That was about three and a half times the quoted implied move.

Think about what that does to the strike choices from this lesson. A buyer of puts near the pre-report price was paid for a move that was much larger than the average the premium assumed. A trader who sold a put 15% below the pre-report price, outside the roughly 10% "expected move", found it deep in the money: the pre-report price was about $349, so that strike was near $296, against a close of $226. Being outside the expected move did not make the strike safe. Gaps like this are why the later lessons on short premium and sizing exist.

⚠️
Where the textbook version breaks

One volatility, a normal curve and no gaps

Everything above prices every strike at one implied volatility and assumes a smooth, roughly normal move. Real markets quote a different implied volatility for each strike (see The IV Smile & Skew), so out-of-the-money options are priced differently from the flat-volatility numbers here. Real moves have fatter tails and gaps, as Netflix showed. Volatility itself moves after you buy, which is why the charts hold it constant only for clarity. And equity options can be exercised early and pay dividends, which this lesson ignores. Treat the numbers as the shape of the trade-offs, not as prices to trade at.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

The "expected move" is like a weather forecast that says "about 10 degrees warmer or colder than today". Most days it is right. But some days the weather does something far bigger, and if your plan only worked inside the forecast range, that day ruins it.

Section 06

How practitioners actually use this

Professional option traders rarely say "I'm bullish, I'll buy a call". They say what they think the stock does, how far and by when, and then ask what the market is charging for each piece. They think about exposures in the Greeks, how many dollars per point of volatility or per day of decay, rather than in contracts. They look at implied volatility against its own history and against realised volatility, they check the calendar for events inside the option's life, and they write down the maximum loss before they enter. Where the premium looks too dear for the view, they often change the structure (a spread, covered in the next lesson group) rather than pay full price. None of that makes the trade right, only deliberate.

✅
Pre-trade options checklist

Eight questions to answer in writing before you click

1

What is the view, in four parts?

Direction, size of the move, window, confidence. Also what would prove you wrong and by when.

2

What is the market's quote?

Compute \(M_1\) and the straddle for the expiry you are considering. Is your move bigger, or do you think the centre sits higher? Which?

3

Does the expiry outlast the window?

Leave room for a late move. Note theta as a share of premium per day, and what holding flat for your window would cost.

4

Is the breakeven inside the move you expect?

Check \(R\) and the breakeven against your target. Never set the strike at the target. Remember delta is an exposure, not a probability.

5

What volatility are you paying?

Implied against recent realised and against your forecast. Is there an event inside the option's life that will take volatility away when it passes?

6

What does it cost to get in and out?

Spread as a share of premium, open interest, volume. Use a limit order, and ask whether you could leave if you needed to.

7

How big, by worst case?

Size so that losing the whole premium (or the full defined risk) is an amount you chose in advance. Never size by margin or by contract count.

8

What is the exit plan?

A profit target, an exit if the thesis breaks, and a time stop: "if nothing has happened by day X, I close it". Write them before you trade.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where choosing a contract quietly goes wrong

Strike at the target. If the stock goes exactly where you said, the option pays nothing at expiry, and you lose the premium. Your breakeven must sit inside your expected move.

Buying the cheap far-out strike because it is cheap. The price is low because the chance is low. Check \(R\) and the spread: a 15% out-of-the-money call needs almost two typical moves and gives nearly a quarter of its premium to a $0.05 spread in the example.

Matching the expiry to the window. A late move turns a correct view into a total loss. Time decay accelerates near expiry, so the short option is the most expensive per dollar to hold.

Paying peak volatility into an event. The option includes the event, and the event's volatility vanishes when it passes. A right call on an earnings move can still lose money.

Treating delta as a probability. A 0.30 delta call is not a 30% chance of profit. Breakeven, not the strike, sets that, and the model's own chance is a different number again.

Sizing by contracts. One contract of a cheap option and one of an expensive one carry very different risk. Size by the maximum loss you have chosen to accept.

Scenario drill · an illustrative day before earnings

You expect good results and the weekly straddle costs $8

This is a made-up situation with invented numbers, to practise the arithmetic. A stock is at $100 and reports after tomorrow's close. You expect good results and a rise of about 4%. The seven-day at-the-money straddle costs $8.00, so the market is pricing an average move of about 8% either way (about 72% annualised volatility). The seven-day call costs $3.99. Your past studies of this stock's earnings moves suggest they average 4 to 5%. What do you do?

  1. Buy the seven-day at-the-money call. Direction is all that matters.
    Breakeven is about $104, exactly where you expect the stock to go, so there is no edge before costs. And if the stock does rise 4% while volatility falls to 30% after the report, the call is worth only about $4.34, a gain of $0.35 on $3.99 before the spread. You can be right on direction and make almost nothing.
  2. Compare your expected move with the straddle first. The market is charging $8 for a move you expect to be about half that, so pass, or use a smaller defined-risk structure.
    The straddle is the average absolute move the market is pricing. You expect roughly half of it. Unless you can show the move will exceed the implied one, or that the direction is unusually predictable, you are overpaying for the volatility. "No trade" is a position.
  3. Buy a three-month call instead, to avoid the volatility crush.
    Longer options carry less event premium, so there is less to crush, and a late reaction is survivable. But you still pay for the same direction view, with more vega and more cash at risk, and a one-day event is a poor reason to own three months of time.
  4. Sell the straddle: your history says the stock moves 4 to 5% and the market prices 8%.
    Averages favour the seller here, but the loss when it goes wrong is a multiple of the premium collected and on the call side has no limit. One gap of 20 or 30% costs many quiet events' profits. The short-premium lesson later in this series covers exactly this trade, and sizing for it.

What this shows. This is an illustrative scenario with invented numbers, not a real episode, so there is no outcome to report and no source to cite. The arithmetic is real: a $100 stock with a $8.00 seven-day straddle implies about 72% annualised volatility. After a report, with volatility at 30% and six days left, the same call would be worth about $4.34 after a 4% rise and about $8.07 after an 8% rise (the size the straddle was pricing). Only a move at least as big as the quoted one pays well, which is why the comparison comes before the trade.

Section 07

Key Takeaways

✓Write the view in four parts: direction, size, window, confidence. An option price contains the market's own answer to size and timing, so only the gap between your answers and its answers can be an edge.
✓Read the quote. The expected move is \(M_1 = S\sigma\sqrt{\tau}\) and the at-the-money straddle costs about \(0.8\,M_1\). Compare your target with both before looking at a single contract.
✓Score strikes by \(R\). The breakeven distance divided by \(M_1\) is about 0.4 at the money and climbs as you move out. A cheap strike is cheap because it needs a bigger move.
✓Let the expiry outlast the window. Longer options cost more cash but lose a smaller share a day and survive a late move. You pay for the days you hold, not the days you buy.
⚠Check the volatility and the costs. Buying 22% when you expect 18% costs about 21% more. Events carry volatility that crushes afterwards. A spread that is 20% of the premium is the first thing to fix.
⚠Your numbers are estimates. The expected value calculator turns beliefs into dollars, but a 4% target, an 18% forecast and a timing guess are all uncertain. A one-point edge barely covers the costs.
✗Never put the strike at your target, and never treat the expected move as a ceiling. At the target the option pays nothing, and Netflix moved about 3.5 times the quoted implied move in a day. Size by the worst case. Nothing in this lesson has been tested as a trading rule on this desk.
Section 08

Self-test

Q1. You are right about direction and the option still loses money. Give three ways that can happen.
The move was smaller than the breakeven: the strike or premium was too far from the stock, for example a strike at the target. The move arrived after the option expired or after time decay had eaten the premium. Or volatility fell after you bought (a crush after an event), which can cancel the gain from a move that was priced in. Direction is only one of the three things the premium charges you for.
Q2. A $50 stock has 30% implied volatility and 36 days to expiry. Estimate the one-standard-deviation move and the at-the-money straddle.
\(\tau = 36/365 = 0.0986\), \(\sqrt{\tau} = 0.314\). So \(M_1 = 50 \times 0.30 \times 0.314 \approx\) $4.71, and the straddle is about \(0.8 \times 4.71 \approx\) $3.77. The at-the-money call or put is about half of that, roughly $1.93.
Q3. Why is buying a call struck exactly at your price target usually a mistake?
At expiry the call is worth the amount spot is above the strike. If the stock ends exactly at your target the payoff is zero, and you have paid the premium, so you lose all of it while being exactly right. The breakeven is the strike plus the premium, so the strike must be low enough that the breakeven sits inside the move you expect.
Q4. Your view has a 28-day window. Why might a 56-day option be a better choice than a 28-day one, and what does it cost?
A 28-day option expires on the day your window ends, so any delay can leave it worthless. The 56-day option survives a late move. It costs more cash ($3.59 against $2.51) and has more vega, but it loses a smaller share of itself per day (0.93% against 1.84%). If you sell on day 28 it keeps time value you can recover. If your timing is exactly right the two do about the same, so the extra cost is insurance against timing error.
Q5. A stock rises 5% after earnings and your call gains only 14%. What happened?
Implied volatility fell once the event was over. In the worked example the call bought at 50% implied volatility is repriced at 30% the next day, so most of the gain from the 5% rise is cancelled. Before the event you paid for its volatility. Afterwards that volatility disappears, and what is left is the effect of the move alone.
Q6. Why is a far out-of-the-money call usually not "cheap" even though its price is small?
Its low price reflects a low chance of finishing beyond the breakeven. In the example the 15% out-of-the-money 56-day call has \(R\) = 1.77, nearly two typical moves, and a model chance of about 4.9%. The bid-ask spread is also a large share of a tiny premium (22.6% for a $0.05 spread). Per dollar spent, the odds are poor, and costs take a bigger bite.
