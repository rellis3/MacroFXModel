Bootstrapping the Yield Curve, One Rate at a Time

A single bond's yield to maturity looks like "the" interest rate for that bond's maturity — but it's actually a blend across every one of its cash flows, and it's the wrong rate to use on anyone else's cash flow. Bootstrapping is the sequential technique that untangles a handful of bond prices into a genuine per-maturity zero curve: the true, single-cash-flow rate that every serious discounting, hedging, and swap-pricing operation is actually built on.

The whole idea
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

If I know a bond's yield, why can't I use it to discount any cash flow at that maturity?

Because a yield is a blend. A five-year bond's yield mixes the rates for its year-one coupon, its year-two coupon and so on, into one number. A single payment due in five years needs the rate for year five alone. That is the zero rate.

And the market doesn't quote zero rates directly.

Mostly it quotes bundles. Bootstrapping unpicks them, starting from the short end. Take three bonds, all priced at a hundred: a one-year paying three percent, a two-year paying three and a half, a three-year paying four.

The one-year is easy.

One payment, a hundred and three in a year, for a hundred today. The one-year zero rate is three percent. No blend to unpick.

Then the two-year.

It pays three fifty in year one. You already know the one-year rate, so that coupon is worth about three forty today. Take it off the price and ninety-six sixty is left for the final payment. Solve, and the two-year zero rate is about three point five one percent.

Just above the bond's own yield.

Yes. Close, but not the same number. Do it once more for the three-year and you get about four point oh three. Short to long, one unknown at a time.

Where do forward rates come in?

Two ways to invest for two years: straight through at the two-year rate, or one year at three percent and then a one-year rate locked in today. With no free money, both must pay the same. That locks the one-year rate starting next year at about four point oh two percent.

So the market expects rates to rise to four?

Careful. That is an arbitrage identity, not a forecast. It tells you what is priced, not what will happen.

What breaks it?

Inputs that disagree. In March twenty twenty, everyone wanted cash and sold Treasuries at once. Dealers ran out of balance sheet, and older bonds cheapened against new ones with almost the same cash flows. There was no longer one curve to find.

How did it end?

The Fed cut to near zero, then bought Treasuries without limit from the twenty-third of March, and eased the banks' leverage rule on the first of April. Within weeks the bonds lined up on one curve again.

So the one-line version?

Discount each payment at its own maturity's rate, solve those rates from the short end outward, and check your inputs, because one bad short price spoils every rate after it.

Section 2

Same maturity, different coupon, different YTM

Here's the concrete proof that a bond's YTM can't be "the" 5-year rate: two 5-year bonds, same underlying zero curve, different YTMs.

Bond A — 6% coupon

Pays back more of its value early, via large coupons. Its YTM is weighted more toward short-maturity zero rates — the very same "duration" idea from the bond-math lessons before this one.

Bond B — 2% coupon

Pays back most of its value only at maturity, via the face amount. Its YTM leans more heavily on the long-maturity zero rate — pulling its blended number away from Bond A's.

Both bonds mature in exactly 5 years. Both are priced off the same true zero curve. And yet they will not, in general, share the same YTM — because YTM is a cash-flow-weighted blend, and the two bonds weight their cash flows differently. Neither number is "the" 5-year rate. Only a zero rate can claim that.
The practical mistake this causes

Using one bond's YTM to discount an unrelated cash flow of the same maturity — say, a liability payment due in 5 years — silently mismatches what the number represents with what it's being used for. The error grows with how different the discounting bond's coupon is from the cash flow actually being valued.

Section 3

Three rates, constantly confused with each other

⬡

Par rate

The coupon that makes a bond of a given maturity price exactly at face value — equivalently, that bond's YTM. A real, quotable rate, but a blend across every cash flow it pays.

●

Spot (zero) rate

The discount rate for one single cash flow at one single maturity — the rate on a hypothetical zero-coupon bond of that exact date. This is the only "true" per-maturity rate.

→

Forward rate

The rate, locked in today, for lending or borrowing between two future dates. Derived entirely from today's zero curve via no-arbitrage — it doesn't require waiting for the future date to know it.

Only the spot rate answers "what is the correct discount rate for a cash flow landing at time t" — and it's exactly the quantity bootstrapping exists to recover, one maturity at a time, from the par rates the market actually quotes.

Section 4

The bootstrapping algorithm, in three moves

Recovering z₁, z₂, z₃, … from a set of observed bond prices — strictly shortest maturity to longest.

1

Start with the shortest instrument

A 1-year bond has exactly one cash flow left. One unknown, one equation — solve z₁ directly, the same way ordinary YTM works with n = 1.

2

Move to the next-shortest

A 2-year bond's year-1 coupon gets discounted at the now-known z₁, leaving exactly one new unknown — z₂ — in its price equation. Solve it.

3

Repeat outward, one maturity at a time

Every earlier cash flow always uses an already-solved shorter zero rate. The only new unknown at each step is that maturity's own zero rate — never more than one.

One equation, one unknown, at every stage — that's what keeps each individual solve as simple as a single bond's YTM problem, even though the curve being built up is genuinely multi-point.

Section 7

Why it's called "bootstrapping"

The name comes from the old idiom "pull yourself up by your own bootstraps" — achieving something using only resources you already have, with no outside help. Each step of this algorithm does exactly that: it solves a new zero rate using nothing but that maturity's own market price and the zero rates the algorithm already solved for itself at earlier steps.
⛓

Every step leans on every earlier step

z₃'s solve needs z₁ and z₂. z₂'s solve needs z₁. Nothing after step 1 can be computed in isolation — the curve builds itself, one rate lifting the next.

↛

The order can't be shuffled

Try solving z₃ before z₂ and there are two unknowns in one equation — unsolvable. Shortest-to-longest isn't a convention here, it's a mathematical requirement.

⚠

Errors travel the same chain

A stale or illiquid price at the short end doesn't just distort the short end — it corrupts every zero rate solved after it, since each later step trusts every earlier one.

Section 8

Forward rates fall straight out of the zero curve

Once z₁ and z₂ are both known, a "1-year rate starting in 1 year" is already implied — no new market data required.

The no-arbitrage identity
(1+z₂)²
=
(1+z₁)(1+f₁,₂)
Two riskless routes to the same dollar, two years from now: invest directly at the 2-year zero rate, or invest one year at z₁ then roll into a forward rate f₁,₂ locked in today. Both routes must pay the same, or there's a free, riskless arbitrage.
1

(1+z₂)² — direct 2-year growth

Observable today from the bootstrapped 2-year zero rate alone.

2

(1+z₁) — the known 1-year leg

Already solved in step 1 of the bootstrap.

3

f₁,₂ — the forward rate

The only unknown — solved directly: f₁,₂ = (1+z₂)²/(1+z₁) − 1 ≈ 4.02%, using z₁=3.00%, z₂≈3.51%.

Because this curve slopes upward, the implied forward (≈4.02%) sits above both z₁ and z₂ — the zero curve is embedding an expectation that short rates run higher down the road.

Section 10

How a rates desk actually uses this

💱

Pricing a swap's floating leg

Fair value of a future floating payment is set to the forward rate for that period — read straight off a bootstrapped curve, never guessed at.

🎯

Key-rate duration

The finer-grained successor to single-number duration: sensitivity to a bump in one zero rate on the curve, holding the rest fixed — only definable once the curve is genuinely per-maturity.

📉

Curve shape as a macro signal

A steep curve is widely read as pricing in future hikes; an inversion (short above long) is a closely watched recession signal — neither reading is possible from a single blended YTM.

🏦

In practice: deposits, futures, swaps

Real desks bootstrap from short-dated deposit/OIS rates, futures, and swap rates rather than toy coupon bonds — but the sequential logic (shortest first, work outward) is identical.

Section 11

Where this goes wrong in practice

Common pitfalls

Using a bond's own YTM to discount an unrelated cash flow — the exact mistake bootstrapping exists to prevent. Treating a forward rate as a forecast — it's a no-arbitrage identity, not a probability-weighted prediction of the future spot rate. Mixing compounding conventions across input instruments silently corrupts every zero rate solved downstream. Trusting a thin, illiquid short end — since the algorithm is sequential, a bad short-maturity input degrades every longer maturity too, not just the short end.

This lesson uses simple annual compounding throughout for clarity, exactly like the bond-math lessons before it — real curve construction has to be scrupulous about day-count and compounding conventions across every input instrument, since a mismatch there corrupts everything solved after it, the same way a bad short-end price does.

Verified against this repo's current source (Sept 2026): a yield-curve slope engine exists in production (comparing published 2yr/10yr par yields as a macro signal) — but no bootstrapping routine or bootstrapped zero/discount curve exists anywhere in this codebase. Section 12 · It actually happened

March 2020: near-identical bonds stop agreeing

It actually happened

In the Covid dash for cash, foreign official holders, funds meeting redemptions and levered basis traders all sold Treasuries at once. For several days yields rose while stocks fell. Dealers ran out of balance sheet, bid-ask spreads widened, and off-the-run bonds cheapened against near-identical on-the-runs. The Fed bought without limit from 23 March and loosened the leverage ratio on 1 April; functioning returned within weeks.

Section 13 · Tested here

Where the textbook breaks, and the verdict

The tempting claim: an inverted curve warns of recession, so expect weaker risk assets.

Desk test: nothing found curve-inversion: ten inversion episodes since 1976. Recession within 24 months after 6 of 10 (60%, CI [31%, 83%]). Nasdaq +12m: +10.5% vs +13.0% control (p=0.33); steepenings did worse (−1.1%). 2019-08: right about the recession, and +48.5% on the Nasdaq-100.
The bootstrap assumes the law of one price: two bonds with the same cash flows are worth the same. Arbitrage enforces that, and arbitrage needs balance sheet. In March 2020 dealers had none to spare, so "the" zero curve stopped being unique.
Section 14

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Why can't a 5-year bond's YTM be used to discount an unrelated 5-year cash flow?

The correct discount rate for a cash flow due at year 5 is the bootstrapped 5-year zero rate, z₅ — which applies to that maturity alone, unlike any bond's blended YTM.
Question 2

Why must bootstrapping proceed from the shortest maturity to the longest?

Solve a longer maturity first and you'd have multiple unknown zero rates in one equation, with no way to isolate any of them.
Question 3

In the worked example, why is z₂ (≈3.51%) slightly above the 2-year bond's own 3.50% par yield?

Since z₁ (3.00%) sits below the 2yr par yield, more "discounting work" falls on the year-2 cash flow's own rate to make the price come out to par — pushing z₂ slightly above the blended par yield.
Question 4

What does (1+z₂)² = (1+z₁)(1+f₁,₂) actually guarantee?

It's a no-arbitrage identity, not a forecast — and forwards only sit above spot when the underlying curve is upward sloping, which isn't guaranteed.
Question 5

True or false: bootstrapping only works if every input bond happens to be priced exactly at par.

The algorithm only needs a price, a coupon, and a maturity for each instrument. Par pricing (P=F) is a convenient teaching simplification, not a requirement.
Your call
Scenario drill · 16 March 2020

Near-identical Treasuries no longer agree

You build the zero curve that a rates book is marked and hedged off. It is the dash for cash: foreign official holders, funds meeting redemptions and levered basis traders are all selling Treasuries, and for several days yields have risen while stocks fell. Dealers are out of balance sheet, bid-ask spreads are wide, and older off-the-run bonds have cheapened against on-the-run bonds of almost the same maturity. Yesterday the Fed cut rates to near zero and announced at least $500 billion of Treasury purchases. How do you build today's curve?

  1. Feed in whichever bond prints at each maturity, on- or off-the-run, as usual.
    Bootstrapping is sequential. If a cheap, illiquid bond sets one of the short rates, its error carries into every longer rate solved after it, and your hedges move for reasons that have nothing to do with rates.
  2. Build from the most liquid inputs you trust, check the short end hard, and treat the off-the-run gap as a liquidity cost, not a rate.
    The algorithm still works; its inputs disagree. Choosing clean inputs keeps the curve meaningful, and recording the scatter of other bonds around it tells you how much illiquidity you are carrying.
  3. Lever up: buy the cheap off-the-runs and short the on-the-runs. They must converge.
    They probably will, but convergence needs someone with balance sheet to force it, and that is exactly what has run out. Levered relative-value positions like this were being forced out at the worst prices.
  4. Fit one smooth curve through every bond and price everything off it.
    It gives you a single curve, which is useful for reading the shape. But it hides that some of those bonds can only be sold well below the fitted price right now.

What happened. On 23 March the Fed dropped its limit and said it would buy Treasuries in the amounts needed. On 1 April it temporarily excluded Treasuries and reserves from banks' supplementary leverage ratio, freeing dealer balance sheet. Market functioning recovered over the following weeks, and near-identical bonds lined up on one curve again. The cost: leveraged relative-value funds were forced out at the worst prices, and the Fed's balance sheet grew by more than a trillion dollars in about a month.

Sources: FOMC statement, 15 March 2020; Federal Reserve press release, 23 March 2020; Federal Reserve Board press release, 1 April 2020; Fed total assets, FRED (4.31 trillion dollars on 11 March, 5.81 trillion on 1 April 2020).

Section 15

What to carry forward

01

A bond's YTM is a blended average across all of its own cash flows' maturities — it is never the "true" rate for any single point in time.

02

The zero (spot) rate is the discount rate for exactly one cash flow at exactly one maturity — it's the only rate that's correct to use on a cash flow of that maturity.

03

Bootstrapping solves zero rates sequentially, shortest to longest — each step has exactly one unknown because every shorter cash flow already uses an already-solved zero rate.

04

It's called "bootstrapping" because every step lifts itself using only the market price at that maturity plus rates the algorithm already solved for itself.

05

Forward rates fall straight out of a no-arbitrage identity between two zero rates — they are not a probability-weighted forecast of the future.

06

A stale or illiquid price at the short end corrupts every zero rate solved after it — the whole curve, not just the short maturities.

07

Real-world episode: March 2020. Off-the-runs cheapened as dealer balance sheets filled, so the inputs stopped sitting on one curve. Desk verdict, NULL: an inversion is not a tradeable signal (6 of 10 followed by recession, CI 31–83%; 2019-08 then +48.5% on the Nasdaq-100).

What this now lets you do

Build a genuine per-maturity discount curve from a handful of observed bond or swap prices, instead of leaning on any single bond's blended YTM. Discount any cash flow of a given maturity at the correct zero rate for that maturity, not someone else's bond's average rate. Read forward rates straight off the curve — to price a swap's floating leg, or to compare your own rate view against what's already priced in.
