Delta Hedging & Gamma Scalping

Hedge away the direction and what is left is a bet on how much the stock moves, set against a rent you pay every day. One equation explains it, and the same equation shows what running it costs.

CONCEPT — how the hedge works and what it costs, shown with simulated numbers, not a track record ~18 min read Prereqs: The Four Option Positions, Black-Scholes-Merton, Theta Decay
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
Risk: this is education, not advice. A bought option can lose its whole premium, and a sold option can lose far more than the premium. A delta hedge does not make a position safe. It swaps one risk for others, and this lesson shows which. Every number here comes from a model with the simplifications stated on the page.
TL;DR
  • To delta-hedge a long call you short \(\Delta\) shares per share the option covers. A 21-trading-day at-the-money call on a $100 stock at 20% implied volatility costs $2.30 and has \(\Delta \approx 0.51\), so you short about 51 shares per 100.
  • Over one day the hedged position makes \(\tfrac{1}{2}\Gamma\,(dS^2 - \sigma_{imp}^2 S^2\,dt)\). Gamma pays you for every move, theta charges you rent. You come out ahead on a day only if the stock moves more than \(\sigma_{imp}\sqrt{dt}\): 1.26% at 20% implied.
  • Over the option's life that becomes roughly vega × (realised vol − implied vol). Here vega is $0.115 per vol point, so realised 25% against 20% implied is worth about +$0.58 on a $2.30 option, and 15% loses the same. In a simulation, one month's result still has a standard deviation of $0.42 to $0.57 around those averages.
  • Hedging more often shrinks the noise like \(1/\sqrt{n}\) and raises the trading cost like \(\sqrt{n}\). There is no free setting.
  • The identity leaves out the things that actually hurt: implied vol falling (a 3-point drop costs about $0.35, which is 60% of the whole $0.58 edge in the example), the option's own bid-ask spread, a delta that depends on the model, and gaps.
  • Real-world episode: on 19 October 1987 the Dow fell 22.6%. Portfolio insurers were copying a put option by selling index futures as the market fell, the same trades as a dealer hedging a put he had sold. Per the Brady Commission, as cited by a Federal Reserve staff paper, they made roughly 40% of the futures sales by non-market-makers that day.
🎧 Listen · the five-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

Everyone says dealers hedge their options. What does that actually mean?

Say you buy a call that costs two dollars thirty. If the stock rises you make money, about fifty-one cents for every dollar it moves, to start with. That fifty-one cents is the delta.

So I'm betting on direction.

Unless you cancel it. Short fifty-one shares for every hundred the option covers, and a small move in the stock barely changes your total. You have hedged the direction away.

Then what am I left with? Nothing?

Two things that pull against each other. First, the delta changes as the stock moves. Stock up, delta up, your hedge is now too small, so you sell more shares, higher. Stock down, delta down, you buy shares back, lower. Sell high, buy low, mechanically. That is gamma, and it pays you for movement.

And the catch?

Time. The option loses a little value every day. For this one it is about five and a half cents a day if the stock sits still. That is theta, and it is the rent you pay for owning the gamma.

So who wins?

Whoever is right about how far the stock really moves. The option price assumes twenty percent volatility a year. Divide by the square root of the number of trading days in a year, about sixteen, and you get one point two six percent a day. Move more than that on a typical day and the trading gains beat the rent. Move less and you lose.

Everything hangs on that one comparison?

Over a month it works out to roughly the option's vega, about eleven and a half cents per point of volatility, times the gap between realised and implied. Realised at twenty-five against twenty implied is worth about fifty-eight cents on a two-thirty option. At fifteen, you lose about the same.

That sounds attractive.

Here is the honest part. On a single month the spread of outcomes is about forty cents even if realised exactly matches implied. And implied volatility itself moves. If it drops three points while you hold, that costs about thirty-five cents, well over half of the edge you were hoping for.

What about hedging more often, to cut the noise?

The noise falls with the square root of the number of hedges. But every hedge crosses a spread, and the total traded grows with the same square root. There is no free setting.

Has this ever gone badly in public?

The nineteenth of October, 1987. Portfolio insurers copied a put option by selling index futures as the market fell. Those are the same trades as a dealer hedging a put he has sold. Sell when it falls, so they became part of the fall. The Dow dropped twenty-two point six percent, and the official inquiry put the insurers at roughly forty percent of non-market-maker futures sales that day.

So the one-line version?

Hedging removes direction, not risk. A gamma scalper is long volatility: paid for movement, charged rent every day, and hurt if implied volatility sinks. Know what your rent is before you buy it.

Section 01

Why this matters

Almost everyone meets delta hedging as a phrase: "dealers hedge their gamma", "the market maker is short gamma", "he is just scalping". The phrase hides a calculation, and the calculation is where options trading is won or lost. An option position carries several risks at once: direction, how much the stock moves, how fast time passes, and where implied volatility goes. A hedge is a way of choosing which of those you keep.

A delta hedge removes the first one, to a first approximation. What remains is a position on movement itself. If you bought the option, you are long gamma: you get paid when the stock moves a lot and charged when it does not. If you sold it, you are short gamma and everything flips. That is why a hedged long option is called gamma scalping, and why the question "is this a good trade?" always reduces to "will the stock move more than the option's price assumed?"

This lesson is the accounting behind that sentence. It is not a strategy to copy. The honest summary of a delta-hedged book is that it loses money on quiet days, on days when implied volatility falls, on every trade it pays a spread for, and in markets that gap past the price the model wanted. It wins when the stock really does move more than was priced. Whether that is worth doing is a question about edge and cost, and the arithmetic below lets you check it for yourself.

💡
Plain-English intuition

You sell the high and buy the low, and you pay rent for the right

You own a call on a $100 stock. At the start it behaves like owning about 51 shares, so you sell 51 shares against it. Now the stock rises to $101.30. Your option has become more sensitive, like owning 60 shares, so your hedge is too small and you sell nine more, at a higher price. If the stock then falls back to $100, you are over-hedged and buy those nine back, cheaper. You did nothing clever. The rule forced you to sell into strength and buy into weakness, and each round trip leaves a little profit.

The option, meanwhile, loses value as time passes. That is the price of having a position whose delta keeps adjusting in your favour. So the real question is a race: do the small profits from the stock's wiggles add up to more than the daily rent? The answer depends on one thing, how far the stock actually moves compared with how far the option's price said it would.

The math, step by step

Watch the formula build
Where the hedged P&L, ½Γ(dS² − σ²S²dt), comes from, one term at a time
\[ \Pi = C \color{#fbbf24}{- \Delta S} \]

The hedged position \(\Pi\): long one call worth \(C\), short \(\Delta\) shares of the stock at price \(S\). Delta is the call's sensitivity to the stock, so the short shares offset it.

\[ dC = \Delta\,dS \color{#fbbf24}{+ \tfrac{1}{2}\Gamma\,dS^2} + \Theta\,dt \]

How the call's value changes when the stock moves by \(dS\) and a short time \(dt\) passes. Delta is the straight-line part. Gamma is the curvature: the call gains more from a rise than it loses from an equal fall. Theta is the value lost to time.

\[ d\Pi = dC - \Delta\,dS = \Theta\,dt + \tfrac{1}{2}\Gamma\,dS^2 \]

Subtract the hedge's own gain or loss, \(\Delta\,dS\). The delta terms cancel exactly. What survives is time decay and curvature, and nothing that depends on which way the stock went.

\[ \Theta = \color{#fbbf24}{-\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma_{imp}^2 S^2 \Gamma} \]

From the Black-Scholes equation with zero interest rate, theta is fixed by gamma and by the implied volatility the option was priced with. This is the "theta pays for gamma" relation from the theta lesson: the rent is set by the volatility in the price.

\[ d\Pi = \tfrac{1}{2}\Gamma\left(dS^2 - \sigma_{imp}^2 S^2\,dt\right) \]

Put the two together. The hedged position earns half of gamma times the gap between the stock's squared move and the squared move the option priced in. Zero when they match, positive when the stock moves more, negative when it moves less.

\[ \begin{aligned} P\&L &\approx \color{#10b981}{\tfrac{1}{2}\int \Gamma S^2\left(\sigma_{real}^2 - \sigma_{imp}^2\right)dt} \\ &\approx \color{#10b981}{\nu\,(\sigma_{real} - \sigma_{imp})} \end{aligned} \]

Add up the days. You are paid on the squared gap between realised and implied volatility, weighted by how much gamma you held, and for an at-the-money option that is close to vega times the gap in volatility points.

The hedged position. Start with what you hold. You are long one call worth \(C\) and short \(\Delta\) shares at price \(S\). Call the value of the whole position \(\Pi\):

\[ \Pi = C - \Delta S \]

One small step in time. A short time \(dt\) passes and the stock moves by \(dS\). To second order, the call's price changes by:

\[ dC = \Delta\,dS + \tfrac{1}{2}\Gamma\,dS^2 + \Theta\,dt \]

This is a Taylor expansion, so it is an approximation: it drops terms of third order in the move and, for now, any change in implied volatility. Both come back in the pitfalls. The hedge's own gain over the step is \(-\Delta\,dS\), so the position's change is the call's change minus \(\Delta\,dS\), and the delta terms cancel:

\[ d\Pi = \Theta\,dt + \tfrac{1}{2}\Gamma\,dS^2 \]
dΠthe hedged position's profit or loss over the step
Θ dtthe rent: negative, and paid whatever the stock does
½ Γ dS²what the move pays: positive whichever way the stock went

The move enters only as \(dS^2\), a square, so up and down moves pay the same. That is what "direction-neutral" means here. Now use the Black-Scholes relation between theta and gamma. With a zero interest rate it reads \(\Theta = -\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma_{imp}^2 S^2 \Gamma\), where \(\sigma_{imp}\) is the implied volatility the option was priced with. Substituting gives the headline identity:

\[ d\Pi = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\Gamma\,\left(dS^2 - \sigma_{imp}^2\,S^2\,dt\right) \]

σ²S²dtthe squared move the option's price assumed: implied movement
½ Γhow much that gap is worth: the size of your convexity

The breakeven move. Write the move as a return, \(dS = S\,r\). The bracket is zero when \(r^2 = \sigma_{imp}^2\,dt\), so the hedged position breaks even on a day when the stock moves by \(\sigma_{imp}\sqrt{dt}\). With \(dt = 1/252\) and \(\sigma_{imp} = 20\%\):

\[ |r|_{breakeven} = \sigma_{imp}\sqrt{dt} = \frac{0.20}{\sqrt{252}} = 1.26\% \]
|r|the size of the day's move as a fraction of the price
σ_impimplied volatility, annualised: 0.20 means 20% a year
√dtone trading day as a fraction of a year, square-rooted: 1/√252 ≈ 0.063

In plain terms, 1.26% is the one-day move the option's price implies. If the stock typically moves more than that, a long gamma position gets paid. If it moves less, it pays. Note the average matters, not the typical day: one 5% day can pay for a lot of 0.5% days, because the gain grows with the square of the move.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine renting a swing set by the day. Every day you pay a fixed fee, even if the swing does not move. In return, every time it swings far you collect a prize, and the further it swings, the bigger the prize. If it swings less than the fee was set for, you lose. If it swings more, you win. The fee is theta, the prize is gamma, and "how far it was expected to swing" is the implied volatility.

Over the whole life. Add the daily results up. When you re-hedge continuously with the delta computed at the implied volatility, and implied volatility stays put, the total is:

\[ P\&L = \tfrac{1}{2}\int_0^T \Gamma_t\,S_t^2\,\left(\sigma_{real}^2 - \sigma_{imp}^2\right)dt \]
σ_realthe volatility the stock actually delivers over the period
ΓS²dollar gamma: how much convexity you hold at each moment, in dollars
∫ … dtthe sum over every instant until expiry

This is a published result (El Karoui, Jeanblanc-Picqué and Shreve, 1998). It says the profit comes from the gap between realised and implied variance, weighted by dollar gamma. Dollar gamma is not constant. It is largest when the stock sits near the strike with little time left, so the rent is not constant either. You will see this in the chart: the rent you pay depends on the path the stock takes.

A rule of thumb. In Black-Scholes, vega satisfies \(\nu = \sigma\,\tau\,S^2\,\Gamma\). If dollar gamma stayed roughly constant, the integral above would be \(\tfrac{1}{2}\Gamma S^2 T (\sigma_{real}^2 - \sigma_{imp}^2)\), and since \(\sigma_{real}^2 - \sigma_{imp}^2 \approx 2\sigma_{imp}(\sigma_{real} - \sigma_{imp})\) for small gaps, this is about:

\[ P\&L \approx \nu\,(\sigma_{real} - \sigma_{imp}) \]
νvega: the option's price change per 1.00 (100 points) of volatility
σ_real − σ_impthe gap, in volatility terms: 5 points is 0.05

For the option in this lesson, vega is $0.1151 per volatility point per share. So each point of realised volatility above 20% is worth about 11.5 cents per share, hedged daily, on average. The table in the worked example checks this against a simulation and finds it holds closely for an at-the-money option bought at the start. It gets rougher when the stock drifts far from the strike.

Worked example

One day, then one month: a long at-the-money call hedged daily

Stock \(S = \$100\), strike \(K = \$100\), implied volatility 20%, 21 trading days to expiry (\(T = 21/252 = 1/12\) year), zero rates and dividends. Black-Scholes gives:

\[ \begin{aligned} C &= \$2.303, \quad \Delta = 0.5115 \\ \Gamma &= 0.0691, \quad \Theta = -\$0.0548 \text{ per day} \\ \nu &= \$0.1151 \text{ per vol point} \end{aligned} \]
C=2.303the premium: $230.30 for a 100-share contract
Δ=0.5115so short about 51 shares per 100 covered
Θ=−0.0548equals −½σ²S²Γ/252: the daily rent

\[ \begin{aligned} d\Pi &= \tfrac{1}{2}(0.0691)(100^2)\left(0.02^2 - \tfrac{0.20^2}{252}\right) \\ &= 345.4 \times 0.0002413 = +\$0.0833 \end{aligned} \]

Repricing the option in full after the move gives +$0.0827, a hair lower, because the identity is second order. A flat day costs the rent, \(-\$0.0548\). A 1% day gives \(-\$0.0203\). The breakeven is 1.26%, and a 3% day gives +$0.256. That is the curve in the picture at the top.

Section 03

It actually happened: 19 October 1987, hedging an option you do not own

Everything so far is from the point of view of someone long gamma. The reverse case, trading the same hedge from the other side, is where the damage has been done in public. The clearest example is the crash of 1987.

It actually happened

Portfolio insurance: a put built from trades, and trades that moved the market

In the early 1980s Hayne Leland and Mark Rubinstein described how a stock portfolio could be given the payoff of a put option without buying one: hold less stock as prices fall and more as they rise (Rubinstein and Leland, 1981). It is delta hedging used to manufacture an option. The models told the investor to cut the weight in stocks in falling markets, and many insurers did it in index futures because it was cheaper than selling the stocks, according to the Brady Commission's 1988 report as summarised in a Federal Reserve staff paper (Carlson, 2007). Those trades are the same ones a dealer makes to hedge a put he has sold: sell as the market falls. A gamma scalper does the opposite, and buys dips.

On Monday 19 October 1987 the Dow Jones Industrial Average fell 508 points, 22.6%, and the S&P 500 fell about 20%. The same staff paper reports that insurers ran their models periodically and traded in batches, rather than continuously, and that on that day about 40% of the futures sales by non-market-makers came from portfolio insurers (citing the Brady report). Futures fell to a discount to the cash index, and the usual arbitrage that links the two was disrupted in places.

The role of portfolio insurance is still debated. The Chicago Mercantile Exchange's inquiry concluded: "Portfolio insurance did contribute significantly to selling in the futures markets. However, this strategy was only one of many sources of selling, and does not by itself explain the magnitude of the crash." The fair reading is that a hedging rule that sells into a falling market can add to the fall when many people run it at once, not that it caused the crash on its own.

The starred link is the sign of gamma. Whoever is long it trades against the move and steadies the price. Whoever is short it, or is copying someone who is, trades with the move.

Long gamma: trades against the move

You own the option and hedge it. Price falls, delta falls, you buy shares back. Price rises, you sell. You supply liquidity, you pay theta, and you profit when the stock moves more than priced.

Short gamma: trades with the move

You sold the option, or you are replicating one. Price falls, you sell more. Price rises, you buy more. You collect theta, and you lose when the stock moves more than priced, and your hedging adds to the move.

⚠️
Where the textbook version breaks

The identity assumes you can trade at the price on your screen

The derivation assumes continuous trading, no cost, constant volatility, and that your own hedging does not move the price. In 1987 none of that held at once. Trading was in batches, futures and cash prices disagreed, and many participants wanted to trade in the same direction at the same time. The dealer version of this is in Gamma Exposure & Dealer Hedging Flows. The general lesson: a hedge is a rule that trades when price moves, and a rule followed by enough people becomes part of the price.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Some people wanted a safety net for their stocks, so instead of buying one they promised themselves to sell a bit more every time prices dropped. That sounds careful. But when lots of people made the same promise, a drop made them all sell at once, which made prices drop more, which made them sell more. The safety plan turned into a slide.

Section 04

How practitioners actually use this

Option market makers hedge almost continuously, because their books are large and every unhedged delta is a directional bet they did not choose to make. They rarely hedge to exactly zero. A common approach is a band: leave a small net delta alone until it exceeds a threshold, then trade it back, which cuts the number of trades at the cost of some noise. The theory of the best band is the subject of the Leland and Whalley-Wilmott papers in the reading list. They hedge index options with index futures, and single-stock options with the stock, because those are the cheapest liquid instruments.

In FX the hedge is a trade in spot or a forward. The delta convention (spot or forward delta, with or without the premium) has to match the one on the ticket, and the interest-rate difference between the two currencies means theta includes a carry term, as covered in FX Options Quoting. The zero-rate numbers here are the clean case.

Volatility traders who go long gamma on purpose do so when they think realised volatility will exceed implied, often around scheduled events or when implied looks cheap against a forecast from something like a GARCH model. They size the position to the rent they can afford, and they treat the rent as a running cost to be recovered. For the average retail account the arithmetic is harsh: whole-share lots on small positions, spreads on the option and the hedge, and real-time delta monitoring all cut into an edge that is usually only a few volatility points.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where a hedged long-gamma book quietly loses

Implied volatility moves. The identity holds implied volatility fixed. Real implied volatility changes, and the option's vega does not go away because you hedged delta. At 20% implied, vega is $0.115 per point per share. A 3-point fall costs about $0.35, about the same as all the gamma gains ($0.37) in the five-day scenario above. Hedging delta takes the stock's direction out; it leaves the vega position fully in place. See Vega & the Volatility Surface.

Comparing the wrong two volatilities. What matters is the realised volatility over the path you actually hedge, at the frequency you hedge, against the implied volatility you paid. A forecast of the annual volatility is not the same thing. A month of 1% daily moves that all go one way has the same realised daily volatility as a month that zig-zags, but the hedged P&L is not the same, because the gamma you hold depends on where the stock is.

The delta is a model output. With a skew or smile the delta you hedge with depends on how you assume implied volatility moves as the stock moves. Two reasonable models give two different hedge ratios, and the difference shows up in your P&L. See The IV Smile & Skew.

Cost is mostly not the hedge. On a liquid stock the share-side cost in the table is small. The bigger cost is buying the option at the offer and, later, selling it at the bid. One volatility point of option spread on this contract is $0.115, about 5% of the premium.

Running a short-gamma version. Everything above mirrors for the seller, who collects the rent and loses on large moves. The seller's loss is not limited the way the buyer's is, which is the subject of the next lessons in this series, on short premium and sizing.

Gaps and pins. A hedge assumes you can trade between prices. When the stock jumps overnight, the long-gamma holder actually benefits, but a short-gamma book takes the full jump. Near expiry with the stock at the strike, gamma becomes very large and delta flips between 0 and 1 on a small move, so the hedge needs constant attention, and early exercise on American options adds another wrinkle.

Scenario drill · an illustrative week (not a historical episode)

Five quiet days, and the option is bleeding

This is a made-up situation with computed numbers. You bought the lesson's 21-day at-the-money call at 20% implied ($2.30, stock $100) because you expect the stock to move more than 20% a year implies. You hedge once a day. Over the last five days the stock has moved only 0.4% a day, up, down, up, down, up, and is now $100.40. Rent has exceeded gamma gains, you are down about $0.26 a share on the hedged position, and implied volatility has also slipped from 20% to 18%, which costs a further $0.20 or so. Sixteen trading days remain. What do you do?

  1. Double the position so a single big move would make the money back.
    Doubling doubles the rent as well as the gamma. The $0.26 already lost is gone whatever you do next, and chasing it turns a small bleed into a bigger bet on the same unproven view.
  2. Stop hedging until the stock moves, to save trading costs.
    With delta still near 0.5 you are no longer hedged. You are running a directional position you did not mean to hold, and you give up exactly the buy-low, sell-high trades that make the strategy work. The cost saved is a few cents.
  3. Keep hedging by your rule, and judge the trade on what you now expect over the remaining 16 days, against 18% implied.
    The five quiet days are sunk. What matters is whether you still expect realised volatility to exceed what the option now prices. If yes, hold: the rent per day is the same and the price of the view is lower. If your forecast has changed, close it. Either way the decision comes from the forecast, not from the bleed.
  4. Close it now to stop the bleed.
    Defensible if your forecast of volatility has come down. It is not defensible as a reaction to five quiet days alone: on a one-month trade, five quiet days are well within the normal spread of outcomes, as the simulation showed.

What this shows. This is an illustrative scenario, not a recorded trade. The numbers come from repricing the option each day with the stated moves: five days of ±0.4% cost about $0.26 per share, and the lost two vol points are worth roughly $0.10 a point at 16 days. The point is the discipline. A hedged long-gamma position has a known daily cost and an uncertain payoff, so the useful questions are about the forecast and the size, not about the days already gone.

Section 05

Key Takeaways

✓A delta hedge removes direction, not risk. What is left is gamma (convexity), theta (rent), and vega (volatility). Which of them hurts you depends on the position.
✓The daily identity is \(\tfrac{1}{2}\Gamma(dS^2 - \sigma_{imp}^2 S^2 dt)\). Realised movement against implied movement, scaled by gamma. Breakeven is \(\sigma_{imp}/\sqrt{252}\) a day: 1.26% at 20%.
✓Over a life, the profit is about vega times the volatility gap. $0.115 per point on the worked option. It is an average. Single months spread widely around it.
✓Hedging frequency trades noise for cost. Noise falls like \(1/\sqrt{n}\), shares traded rise like \(\sqrt{n}\). Practitioners use bands, not "as often as possible".
⚠The identity assumes implied volatility stays put. A 3-point drop costs about $0.35 on the worked option, about as much as the whole five-day scenario's gamma gains. Hedging delta does nothing about vega.
⚠Costs and model risk are real. The option's spread is usually a bigger drag than the hedge, and the delta you hedge with is a model output that depends on the smile.
✗Hedging with the move can feed the move. In 1987 portfolio insurers replicated a put by selling into a falling market, roughly 40% of non-market-maker futures sales on the day, in a 22.6% fall in the Dow. The hedge assumes you can trade at the price on your screen. A crowd following the rule makes that false.
Section 06

Self-test

Q1. A delta-hedged long call is "direction-neutral". Why does it still make money when the stock falls sharply?
The hedge cancels the first-order effect of the move, not the second-order one. The call's gain or loss from a move \(dS\) has a straight-line part (delta, cancelled by the short shares) and a curvature part, \(\tfrac{1}{2}\Gamma\,dS^2\), which depends on the square of the move and is positive for a long option whichever way the stock goes. A large fall earns the same gamma gain as an equal rise. The profit shows up as the shares being re-bought cheaper after the fall.
Q2. Implied volatility is 30%. Roughly how large a daily move does a delta-hedged long option need to break even that day?
\(0.30/\sqrt{252} \approx 1.89\%\). The breakeven move is \(\sigma_{imp}\sqrt{dt}\), where \(dt = 1/252\) for one trading day. It is the one-day move the option's price implies, and it does not depend on the strike, gamma or the days left (those scale the size of the profit and loss, not the breakeven).
Q3. You hold the worked option, hedged daily, and the stock's realised volatility over the month is 18% against 20% implied. Roughly what do you expect, and how sure are you?
About \(\nu(\sigma_{real} - \sigma_{imp}) = 0.115 \times (-2) \approx -\$0.23\) per share, around 10% of the premium. But the simulation shows a standard deviation of about $0.4 even when realised equals implied, so a single month at 18% could easily finish positive. The average is a guide to the expected value, not a forecast for one month. That is why the edge needs to be large and repeated before it can be relied on.
Q4. Why not hedge as often as possible?
Each re-hedge trades some shares and crosses a spread, so total cost grows with the number of trades, roughly as \(\sqrt{n}\) because each trade is smaller as \(n\) rises. The hedging noise falls only as \(1/\sqrt{n}\). At some point the extra cost outweighs the noise you are removing, and the best frequency depends on the spread, the position size and how much you dislike noise. Practitioners hedge to a delta band rather than on a fixed schedule.
Q5. Implied volatility falls 3 points the day after you buy and hedge the option. Does the delta hedge protect you?
No. The delta hedge neutralises small moves in the stock, not changes in implied volatility. A long option has positive vega, so a 3-point fall costs about \(3 \times \$0.115 \approx \$0.35\) per share on the worked option, whatever the stock did. This is the main reason the neat realised-versus-implied identity understates the risk of a real hedged position.
Q6. Why did portfolio insurance trade in the same direction as the market in 1987, and why does that matter?
Replicating a put means holding less stock as prices fall, so the insurer sells into weakness. That is the hedge of someone short gamma: it trades with the move rather than against it. When many insurers follow the same rule, their selling adds to the fall that triggered it. A long-gamma hedger does the opposite, buying dips, which steadies prices. The sign of gamma decides whether a hedger is a stabiliser or an amplifier.

Further reading
  • Fischer Black and Myron Scholes, "The Pricing of Options and Corporate Liabilities", Journal of Political Economy 81(3), 1973 — the original argument: a continuously rebalanced hedge of the option with the stock removes risk, which is where the pricing equation comes from.
  • Nicole El Karoui, Monique Jeanblanc-Picqué and Steven Shreve, "Robustness of the Black and Scholes Formula", Mathematical Finance 8(2), 1998 — the result that a hedger using the wrong volatility earns the integral of dollar gamma times the difference in variances.
  • Phelim Boyle and David Emanuel, "Discretely Adjusted Option Hedges", Journal of Financial Economics 8(3), 1980 — the error from hedging at discrete times, and how it shrinks as hedges become more frequent.
  • Hayne Leland, "Option Pricing and Replication with Transactions Costs", Journal of Finance 40(5), 1985 — hedging at fixed intervals when trading is costly; and A. Elizabeth Whalley and Paul Wilmott, "An Asymptotic Analysis of an Optimal Hedging Model for Option Pricing with Transaction Costs", Mathematical Finance 7(3), 1997 — the optimal no-trade band.
  • Dimitris Bertsimas, Leonid Kogan and Andrew Lo, "When Is Time Continuous?", Journal of Financial Economics 55(2), 2000 — how quickly the discrete-hedging error behaves as the number of rebalances rises.
  • Sheldon Natenberg, Option Volatility and Pricing, McGraw-Hill — the standard practitioner text on gamma, theta and volatility trading.
  • Nassim Nicholas Taleb, Dynamic Hedging: Managing Vanilla and Exotic Options, Wiley, 1997 — a practitioner's account of what hedging an options book involves in real markets.
  • Euan Sinclair, Volatility Trading, Wiley — on trading volatility, including hedging and sizing.
  • Mark Rubinstein and Hayne Leland, "Replicating Options with Positions in Stock and Cash", Financial Analysts Journal 37(4), 1981 — the origin of portfolio insurance.
  • Presidential Task Force on Market Mechanisms (the Brady Commission), Report, January 1988, and Mark Carlson, "A Brief History of the 1987 Stock Market Crash with a Discussion of the Federal Reserve Response", Federal Reserve Board FEDS Working Paper 2007-13 — the official account and a clear summary of it.
