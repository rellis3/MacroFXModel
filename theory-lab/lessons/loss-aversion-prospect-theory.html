Why a Loss Hurts Twice as Much

Prospect theory in plain English: why you play safe when you are winning, gamble when you are losing, and why "I'll just wait until it gets back to even" has ended careers and, once, a 233-year-old bank.

CONCEPT — explained here; desk verdicts quoted where tested ~20 min read Prereqs: Probability primer (expected value)
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • You do not judge outcomes by how much money you end up with. You judge them as gains or losses from a reference point, usually your entry price or your account high. Kahneman and Tversky built this into prospect theory (1979).
  • The value you feel is an S-shaped curve: concave for gains (you lock them in), convex for losses (you gamble to avoid them), and steeper for losses. Tversky and Kahneman's 1992 estimate of that steepness, the loss-aversion coefficient, was λ ≈ 2.25: a loss weighs roughly twice a gain of the same size.
  • People also bend probabilities: small chances feel bigger than they are (lottery tickets, far out-of-the-money options) and near-certainties feel less sure than they are.
  • On a trading screen this shows up as moving the stop, refusing to take a small loss, and adding size when behind to "get back to even". The maths says a losing position makes you risk-seeking at exactly the moment your judgement is worst.
  • It actually happened: Nick Leeson hid losses in Barings' error account 88888 and kept doubling down. After the Kobe earthquake in January 1995 the losses passed the bank's capital and Barings collapsed in February 1995.
  • Desk verdict: untested. No ledger entry measures loss aversion in trading results. The desk's guard is procedural: write the exit rule and the kill criterion before the trade or test, so the curve never gets a vote.
Section 01

Why this matters

Most of this curriculum is about markets: how prices move, how risk is measured, how a signal is tested. This six-lesson sequence is about the other half of every trade, the person at the screen. You can own a validated edge and still lose money with it if you exit winners early, sit in losers, and size up when you are behind. Those are not random mistakes. They follow a pattern that was mapped in detail in the 1970s, and the pattern has a shape you can draw.

That shape is prospect theory's value function. Once you can see it, a lot of trading folklore stops being folklore. "Cut your losses and let your winners run" is advice to do the opposite of what the curve pulls you toward. The next lesson, Selling Winners, Holding Losers, measures that pull in real brokerage accounts. Later lessons cover overconfidence, herding, drawdowns and the risk of ruin, and how to judge a decision separately from its outcome. They all build on the curve on this page.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Your brain keeps score from where you started, and losses count double

Finding $100 in a coat pocket feels good. Losing $100 out of your wallet feels worse than the find felt good. Most people, asked to bet on a coin flip that loses them $100 on tails, want to win about $200 on heads before they will play. That gap is loss aversion.

Two more things follow. Because the first $100 of a gain feels better than the next $100, you are happy to bank a sure gain rather than gamble for a bigger one. And because the first $100 of a loss hurts more than the next $100, you are willing to gamble to avoid a sure loss, even when the gamble is worse on average. Safe when winning, reckless when losing: exactly backwards for a trader.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

If someone gives you a sticker you feel a bit happy. If someone takes a sticker away you feel very sad, much sadder than the sticker made you happy. And if you've already lost one sticker, you'll do almost anything to win it back, even a silly bet that will probably lose you two more.

Section 02

The theory, step by step

1. Outcomes are gains and losses from a reference point

Classical expected-utility theory says you value final wealth: a portfolio worth $100,000 is worth $100,000 to you whether it was $90,000 or $110,000 yesterday. Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky's 1979 paper Prospect Theory: An Analysis of Decision under Risk showed that people do not behave that way. They code each outcome as a change from a reference point \(r\), so what matters is

\[ x = W_{\text{outcome}} - r \]
xthe gain (x > 0) or loss (x < 0) you actually feel
Wthe money you end up with
rthe reference point: entry price, yesterday's balance, the account high

For a trader the reference point is almost always the entry price. Your P&L column shows \(x\) directly, in red or green. That is convenient, and it is also how the bias gets in.

2. The value function: S-shaped and kinked at zero

Kahneman and Tversky proposed a value function \(v(x)\) with three properties. Tversky and Kahneman's 1992 follow-up, Advances in Prospect Theory: Cumulative Representation of Uncertainty, gave it the power form that is still the standard textbook version:

\[ v(x) = \begin{cases} x^{\alpha} & x \ge 0 \\[4pt] -\lambda\,(-x)^{\beta} & x < 0 \end{cases} \]
αcurvature for gains; below 1 means each extra dollar of gain feels smaller
βcurvature for losses; below 1 means each extra dollar of loss hurts a little less
λloss aversion: how many times more a loss weighs than an equal gain
xα, α < 1
Concave for gains
Risk-averse when ahead: a sure gain beats a gamble with the same average
−(−x)β, β < 1
Convex for losses
Risk-seeking when behind: a gamble beats a sure loss with the same average
λ > 1
Steeper for losses
The kink at zero: a 50/50 bet needs a much bigger win than loss before it feels fair

The 1992 paper's median estimates from its experiments were \(\alpha = \beta = 0.88\) and \(\lambda = 2.25\). That λ is where the slogan "losses hurt about twice as much" comes from. Treat the numbers as a typical person in a laboratory, not a law: later studies find λ anywhere from a little above 1 to well above 2 depending on the stakes, the people and how the question is framed.

Curvature alone decides the sure-loss question: any α below 1 makes the coin flip for −$1,000 feel less bad than a certain −$500, whatever λ is. Push α to 1 and the preference disappears. λ alone decides the mixed bet: the +$25-average coin flip is accepted only when \(150^{\alpha} > \lambda\cdot 100^{\alpha}\), which at the default curvature means λ below about 1.4.
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

The purple line is how you feel. On the right side (winning) it gets flatter, so the second cookie is nice but not as exciting as the first. On the left side (losing) it drops twice as fast, so losing a cookie feels like a big deal, but losing a second one doesn't feel much worse than losing one.

3. Probabilities get bent too

Prospect theory has a second ingredient. People do not weigh outcomes by their true probability \(p\) but by a decision weight \(w(p)\). The 1992 paper's one-parameter form is

\[ w(p) = \frac{p^{\gamma}}{\big(p^{\gamma} + (1-p)^{\gamma}\big)^{1/\gamma}} \]
pthe true probability of the outcome
w(p)the weight it gets in your head
γhow bent the curve is; 1 = no distortion, the 1992 estimates were 0.61 for gains, 0.69 for losses

With γ below 1, small probabilities are overweighted and large ones underweighted. A 1% chance gets a weight of about 5.5%, and a 99% chance only about 91%. That is the lottery ticket and the insurance policy in one curve, and on a screen it is the pull of the far out-of-the-money option that "could" go ten times.

w(0.01) = 0.055 · w(0.50) = 0.421 · w(0.99) = 0.912
The dashed diagonal is "probabilities as they are". The default γ = 0.61 is Tversky and Kahneman's 1992 estimate for gains.

4. Putting it together

A prospect with outcomes \(x_i\) at probabilities \(p_i\) is valued as weighted feelings, not as weighted dollars:

\[ V = \sum_i w(p_i)\, v(x_i) \qquad \text{versus} \qquad EV = \sum_i p_i\, x_i \]
Vhow good the prospect feels: what you actually choose by
EVexpected value in dollars: what a long series of such bets pays on average

(The 1992 "cumulative" version applies the weights to ranked outcomes rather than one by one. For the two-outcome bets on this page, with at most one gain and one loss, the two versions give the same answer.) Every trading mistake in this lesson lives in the gap between \(V\) and \(EV\).

\(r\)Reference point: the level you measure gains and losses from (usually your entry price).
\(x\)Gain or loss relative to \(r\), in dollars.
\(v(x)\)Value function: how good or bad \(x\) feels.
\(\alpha,\ \beta\)Curvature for gains and for losses (0.88 each in the 1992 estimates). Below 1 means diminishing sensitivity.
\(\lambda\)(lambda) Loss-aversion coefficient: 2.25 in the 1992 estimates.
\(p\)True probability of an outcome.
\(w(p)\)Decision weight: how big the probability feels.
\(\gamma\)(gamma) Distortion parameter in \(w(p)\): 0.61 for gains, 0.69 for losses (written δ in the paper).
\(V,\ EV\)Prospect value (how a bet feels) and expected value (what it pays on average).
\(W\)Final wealth after the outcome.

Section 04

Worked example and the trading version

🧮
Worked example

The same $500, framed two ways

Use the 1992 parameters: \(v(x)=x^{0.88}\) for gains, \(-2.25(-x)^{0.88}\) for losses, \(w_+(0.5)=0.421\), \(w_-(0.5)=0.454\).

1

Gain frame: sure +$500 or 50% of +$1,000

Both have EV = +$500. Sure: \(500^{0.88} = 237.2\). Gamble: \(0.421 \times 1000^{0.88} = 0.421 \times 436.5 = 183.6\). The sure thing feels better: risk-averse.

2

Loss frame: sure −$500 or 50% of −$1,000

Both have EV = −$500. Sure: \(-2.25\times 237.2 = -533.7\). Gamble: \(0.454 \times (-2.25 \times 436.5) = -445.9\). The gamble feels less bad: risk-seeking.

3

The flip is the lesson

Same sizes, same odds, same expected values. Only the sign changed, and the preferred choice reversed. Kahneman and Tversky called this the reflection effect, and their 1979 paper showed it in experiment after experiment: a large majority took the sure gain, and an even larger majority took the gamble once the same numbers were losses.

🎯
Real-world trading scenario

Down $1,000, and the stop is right there

You are long, the trade is down $1,000, and price is sitting just above your stop. Your options: let the stop take you out (a certain −$1,000), or pull the stop and hold through tomorrow's data, where you judge it 50/50 between getting back to breakeven and being taken out at −$2,200. The hold has EV = −$1,100, worse than the stop's −$1,000.

Measured from your entry price, prospect theory scores the stop at \(v(-1000) = -982\) and the hold at \(0.454 \times v(-2200) = -892\). The worse bet feels better, so you move the stop. Measured from today's price, with the −$1,000 already accepted, the hold is a fresh coin flip for +$1,000 or −$1,200, scored at \(-340\) against \(0\) for walking away. Now it is obviously a bad bet.

Same position, same odds: the only thing that changed is where you measure from. That is why the professional habit is to ask "would I open this trade, at this size, right now?" It moves the reference point to today, where the curve stops lying.

Section 05

It actually happened: "getting back to even"

Richard Thaler and Eric Johnson gave the pattern a name in 1990: the break-even effect. After a loss, people become unusually drawn to gambles that offer a chance to get back to where they started. Here is the most expensive example on record.

It actually happened

Barings, 1992–1995: account 88888

Nick Leeson ran Barings' futures operation in Singapore, trading on the Singapore exchange (SIMEX) and in Osaka. Unusually, he controlled both the trading and the back office that settled and recorded those trades. In 1992 an error account numbered 88888 was set up, and Leeson began using it to park losing positions, first small errors, then his own losing trades. London saw a profitable star trader, apparently running low-risk arbitrage between the two exchanges. The account held the truth.

Every point on the curve in this lesson is in the story. A small loss could have been booked and explained. Booking it meant a certain, visible loss; hiding it and trading bigger offered a chance of getting back to even. The hidden losses grew, and so did the size needed to recover them. Leeson sold large amounts of Nikkei options (straddles, which pay as long as the index stays in a range) and built a large long position in Nikkei 225 futures.

On 17 January 1995 the Kobe earthquake struck and the Nikkei fell sharply. The short options and the long futures lost money together. Leeson bought more futures, as if to hold the market up. Margin calls from the exchange were met with funds wired from Barings in London, which still believed the business was hedged.

In late February 1995 Leeson left Singapore. The losses, roughly £827 million, were more than the bank's capital. Barings, founded in 1762, was placed into administration on 26 February 1995 and sold to the Dutch group ING for £1. Leeson was arrested in Frankfurt, extradited, and sentenced in Singapore to six and a half years in prison. The Bank of England's inquiry that July put the failure on the absence of basic controls, starting with one person running both trading and settlement.

It actually happened

Boston condos in the 1990s: sellers who would not sell below what they paid

Loss aversion is not only a trading-floor story. David Genesove and Christopher Mayer studied the downtown Boston condominium market after prices fell in the early 1990s (published 2001). Owners whose flats were now worth less than they had paid set higher asking prices than comparable owners who were not facing a loss, and their flats took longer to sell. The purchase price, a number that had nothing to do with what the flat was now worth, acted as the reference point. Ordinary people, real money, the same kink.

Where the textbook version breaks

Prospect theory describes how people choose; it does not say every trader who holds a loser is biased. Sometimes holding is right: the thesis is intact and the stop was badly placed. The diagnosis is not "you held a loser" but "you changed the rule because you were losing". Leeson's case also needed a control failure; the bias supplied the motive, and the missing separation of duties supplied the opportunity.

Not tested here "Loss aversion costs traders money; fixed exits beat discretionary ones." Not tested here. The desk's ledger tests market claims, not the behaviour of the person trading them, and has no entry that measures moved stops or break-even chasing. What the desk does have is the guard: every study's rules, sample and kill criterion are written down before the data is seen, and a null is logged as a null. That is the research version of a stop you are not allowed to move. A fair test of the trading claim would compare the same signal with its pre-set exits against the exits actually taken, trade by trade.

A man lost some of his bank's money and was too scared to say so. He hid it and made bigger and bigger bets to win it back before anyone noticed. Then an earthquake made his bets go wrong all at once, and the whole bank, which was over 200 years old, ran out of money.

Section 06

How it shows up at your screen, and what professionals do about it

🛑

Moving the stop

The stop is a sure loss; moving it buys a gamble. The convex loss side of the curve makes that gamble feel cheaper than it is.

📉

Averaging down without a plan

Adding to a loser lowers the break-even price, which feels like progress. It is often just a bigger bet placed while on the steep side of the curve.

💸

Banking winners too early

Concave for gains: a small sure profit beats a larger uncertain one. This is the "selling winners" half of the next lesson.

🎰

Lottery-ticket trades

Overweighted small probabilities make far out-of-the-money options and "this could 10×" setups feel better value than they are.

The professional answer is not willpower; it is to decide before the curve is active. Exits and maximum loss are set when the trade is opened, flat, and sit with someone or something other than the person in the trade: a hard stop with the broker, a risk manager, a daily loss limit that closes the screen. Banks separate trading from settlement precisely because of cases like Barings. Shlomo Benartzi and Richard Thaler (1995) also showed that checking results more often makes loss aversion bite harder, because more frequent looks mean more small losses to feel. Looking at the P&L less often is a real, evidence-based tool.

⚠️
Common pitfalls
  • Treating λ = 2.25 as a constant of nature. It is a median from lab experiments with modest stakes. Your own number may be higher or lower, and it changes with mood, fatigue and recent results.
  • Thinking knowing about the bias removes it. It does not. Rules set in advance work; good intentions in the moment mostly do not.
  • Over-correcting into "never hold a loser". The goal is that exits follow the plan, not that every red trade is closed instantly.
  • Forgetting the house-money effect. Thaler and Johnson also found the mirror image: after a gain, people take more risk with "the house's money". A hot streak can loosen discipline as much as a loss can.
Section 07

Key Takeaways

✓You feel changes, not levels. Gains and losses are measured from a reference point, and for a trader that is usually the entry price.
✓The value curve is S-shaped and kinked. Concave for gains (bank them), convex for losses (gamble to avoid them), and steeper for losses: λ ≈ 2.25 in Tversky and Kahneman (1992).
✓Small probabilities feel bigger than they are. That is the pull of lottery-ticket trades.
✓Move the reference point to today. "Would I open this trade now?" turns a break-even gamble back into the bad bet it is.
✗Getting back to even is how small losses become fatal ones. Barings went from hidden errors to a ~£827m loss and collapse in February 1995 by repeating that one choice.
⚠Untested on this desk. No ledger entry measures loss aversion in trading results; the desk's guard is pre-registered rules, not a measured edge.
Section 08

Self-test

Q1. Why does the same person take a sure +$500 over a coin flip for +$1,000, but take a coin flip for −$1,000 over a sure −$500?
The value function is concave for gains, so half the feeling of $1,000 is less than the feeling of $500, and convex for losses, so half the pain of losing $1,000 is less than the pain of losing $500. Same expected values, opposite preferences: the reflection effect. Loss aversion λ is not needed for this; curvature does it.
Q2. What does λ = 2.25 mean in plain words, and where does the number come from?
A loss weighs about 2.25 times as much as a gain of the same size, so a 50/50 bet needs a win roughly twice the loss before it feels fair. It is the median estimate from Tversky and Kahneman's 1992 paper on cumulative prospect theory, from laboratory choices, not a universal constant.
Q3. A trade is down $1,000. Holding offers 50% back to even and 50% a total loss of $2,200. Why does holding feel right, and what single question fixes it?
From the entry price, holding is a gamble between 0 and −$2,200 versus a sure −$1,000. On the convex loss side, the gamble scores −892 against −982, so it feels better despite its worse expected value (−$1,100). Asking "would I open this trade now?" moves the reference point to today, where holding is a coin flip for +$1,000 or −$1,200, plainly a bad bet.
Q4. How does probability weighting explain the appeal of buying far out-of-the-money options?
With γ below 1, small probabilities get decision weights larger than their true size (a 1% chance feels like about 5.5% with γ = 0.61). A small chance of a large payoff therefore feels more valuable than its expected value, the same reason lottery tickets sell.
Q5. In the Barings collapse, what did loss aversion supply and what did it not?
It supplied the motive: booking a visible loss felt worse than hiding it and betting bigger to get back to even, and each larger loss pushed further down the convex side of the curve. It did not supply the opportunity: that came from one person controlling both trading and settlement, the control failure the Bank of England's inquiry highlighted.
Q6. Has this desk shown that loss aversion costs traders money?
No. The verdict is untested: there is no ledger entry on trader behaviour. The desk applies the remedy procedurally (rules and kill criteria fixed before results are seen), which is a guard, not evidence of a measured effect.
