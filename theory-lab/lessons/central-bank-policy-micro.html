Lesson 18 · Macro & FX Theory

Central Bank Policy, Read the Way a Desk Reads It

A dual-mandate central bank answers to two goals at once — jobs and prices — and hawkish, dovish, dot plot, forward guidance are all just shorthand for how a committee argues those two goals against each other. The single fact that actually moves a currency isn't which way the bank leaned; it's whether that lean was bigger or smaller than what the market had already priced in.

Section 1

Two goals, one committee, one lever

Every meeting is an argument about how these two goals weigh against each other right now.

🦅

Hawkish — the price-stability half talking

Favors tighter policy (higher rates), usually to fight inflation. A hawk accepts some cost to growth or jobs to bring prices back to target.

🕊️

Dovish — the employment half talking

Favors looser policy (lower rates), usually to protect growth or jobs. A dove tolerates some above-target inflation to keep demand alive.

Most of the time this is easy — an overheating economy argues for tightening on both counts, a weak one for easing on both counts, and the committee moves decisively because nobody is pulling a different direction. Almost every other term in this lesson is really just a specific instrument, forecast, or communication choice expressing one of these two leanings.

Where this needs qualifying

The Fed's dual mandate (jobs and prices, co-equal by law) isn't universal. The ECB's mandate is price stability first, with other goals explicitly subordinate — a structural difference, not just a stylistic one, in how each committee is legally allowed to weigh the two goals.

Section 2

The toolkit — four buckets of vocabulary

Every term you'll hit falls into one of these. Flip through them.

Try it

Hike / Cut — a single move at one meeting, sized in basis points (25bp = 0.25 pts). Hold / Pause — unchanged, which means "not ready to move again," not "the cycle is over." Neutral rate (r*) — the unobservable rate that neither stimulates nor restrains. Restrictive / Accommodative — policy sitting above or below r*, on purpose.

Dot plot — the Fed's chart of each policymaker's own rate guess; the median headlines, but can hide a split committee. Forward guidance — words used deliberately to shape expectations before the next meeting. Data-dependent — declining to commit to a path, often a tell for internal disagreement. Reaction function — the bank's implicit rule for how it responds to conditions.

Quantitative easing (QE) — creating money to buy bonds, pushing down long yields directly; reached for once rate cuts run out of room near zero. Quantitative tightening (QT) — shrinking the balance sheet, the reverse. Tapering — slowing the pace of QE without reversing it yet — still stimulus, just less of it.

Terminal rate — where the cycle is ultimately headed, not where rates sit today. Lift-off — the first hike after a long hold near zero. Policy lag — the quarters-long delay before a rate change fully hits the real economy. Jawboning — moving markets with words alone. Blackout period — the days before a meeting when officials go quiet.

Section 3

The dual mandate as a 2×2

Inflation on one axis, unemployment on the other. Three quadrants are clean. One is a trap.

The top-right box looks like it should mirror the comfortable bottom-left one — twice the problem, sure, but still one clean read. It isn't. Normal intuition expects inflation and unemployment to move in opposite directions; stagflation is both rising together, so the tool that fixes one (tighten) actively worsens the other. That's what makes it a trap, not just more work.

Section 4

When the mandate agrees, the bank moves fast

Same fictional bank (the FCB), same tools, two mirror-image "clean" cases.

Fighting inflation

Unemployment 3.6%, inflation 5%. Both halves say tighten. The committee delivers a 50bp hike, dots shift up, guidance is firm — and because the market had only priced 25bp, the extra 25bp is a hawkish surprise that rallies the currency.

Fighting unemployment

Unemployment jumps 4%→6%, inflation falls to 1.2%. Both halves say ease. The committee cuts, and — with rates already near zero — reaches past conventional cuts into QE. Guidance promises "low for an extended period"; lift-off gets pushed far out.

Same model, opposite direction: whenever inflation and unemployment point the same way, the committee has one clear lever and one clear reason to pull it. There's no internal argument to have.

Section 5

Stagflation — the case that matters most

Unemployment rising to 5.5% AND inflation rising to 6%, together.

What happens inside the room

The hawks look at 6% inflation and push their dots up. The doves look at 5.5% unemployment and hold theirs flat or push them down. Same staff forecast, opposite conclusions, because each side weights the two mandate halves differently. The committee still has to land on one number — a reluctant hold, or a small hike, built around the Chair and the median — but forward guidance turns deliberately vague and data-dependent, because the committee genuinely can't commit to a path it doesn't agree on internally.

The load-bearing, fragile median

The median dot is the number everyone quotes — but in a split committee it's just the midpoint between hawks sitting high and doves sitting low, not a sign of real agreement. One hawk or dove changing their mind can move it without the underlying picture changing at all. Dispersion across the dots — tight cluster versus wide spread — is often more informative than the median alone.

Section 6

It's the surprise, not the stance, that moves price

A hike can weaken a currency. A hold can rally one. Here's why.

Markets are forward-looking — by the time a decision lands, the market has usually already "studied" for an expected outcome and priced it in. A bank can hike and still see its currency fall, if the market had priced an even bigger hike; it can hold unchanged and see its currency rally, if the market had priced a cut and the hold reads as tighter by comparison. "Hawkish" and "dovish" describe a direction. "Surprise" describes the gap against what was already priced — and it's the gap that moves price.

This is exactly why desks spend as much energy tracking what's priced into rate futures and OIS curves ahead of a meeting as they spend on the meeting itself — the decision only matters relative to that baseline.

Section 7

Two ways dots can converge — only one is predictable

The subtlest distinction in this lesson: reacting to new data versus rewriting the rule itself.

Reacting within the rule

Inflation keeps accelerating, unemployment stabilizes — the doves lose footing on the data itself, and dots converge hawkish. Same reaction function, new inputs. Anticipatable once you see the data that caused it.

The rule itself shifting

The committee decides to prioritize killing inflation even at real cost to jobs — independent of what the next print says. The reaction function itself changed. A genuine regime change that can catch a market still trading the old rulebook off guard.

The same-looking outcome — dots moving hawkish — means something very different depending on which of these caused it. Conflating them means missing which repricing is the slow, anticipatable kind and which is the fast, violent kind.

Section 8

Where people misread this in practice

📌

Treating the median dot as a promise

The dot plot is revised almost every quarter and has a documented history of missing the eventual path. It's a snapshot of current opinion, not a binding forecast.

😐

Calling a hold "nothing happened"

A hold can be a major hawkish or dovish surprise depending on what was priced going in — exactly the "reluctant hold" case from Section 5, where the hold itself is the news.

🔀

Reading the stance instead of the surprise

"The bank hiked" says almost nothing about what the currency does next. "The bank surprised hawkish" is the actual tradable signal — and it requires knowing what was priced beforehand.

Section 9

Quick check

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Why is stagflation harder to handle than fighting inflation or unemployment alone?

In the clean cases both halves agree, so there's one lever and one clear direction. In stagflation, fighting inflation demands tightening while fighting unemployment demands easing — one rate can't do both.
Question 2

The market prices a 25bp cut. The bank cuts 25bp exactly as expected. What's the surprise?

Surprise = actual − priced = (−25) − (−25) = 0. An outcome that matches what was already priced carries little new information and should move price comparatively little.
Question 3

What makes a hold a "hawkish surprise" rather than a non-event?

It's the gap between the priced expectation and the actual outcome that defines the surprise — not whether the rate number moved at all.
Question 4

Dots converge hawkish because inflation kept accelerating while unemployment stabilized. Is that a reaction-function shift?

A reaction-function shift is the committee re-weighting which half of the mandate matters more, independent of the data. Reacting to new data with the same rule is the anticipatable, "normal" case.
Question 5

Why does the committee reach for QE in the fighting-unemployment scenario instead of just cutting rates further?

QE reaches the same stimulative goal — pushing down borrowing costs, here longer-term yields — through a different mechanism (buying bonds directly) once the ordinary rate-cut lever is out of room.
Section 10

What to carry forward

01

Every decision is an argument between two goals — jobs and prices — and hawkish/dovish are just those two goals speaking.

02

When the mandate halves agree, the committee acts decisively in either direction. When they conflict — stagflation — the committee splits, guidance goes vague, and the dot plot goes mixed.

03

The dot plot is a distribution of current opinion, not a promise — it's revised almost every quarter and has a documented history of missing the eventual path.

04

It's the surprise relative to what was priced — not the absolute hawkish/dovish stance — that moves a currency. Surprise = actual − priced.

05

A reaction-function shift (the rule itself changing) is a different, more violent event than reacting predictably within an unchanged rule.

What this now lets you do

Read a press conference or projections release for where the committee's internal disagreement actually sits, instead of just its headline number. Judge a hike or hold by what it means against what was already priced, not by its label alone. Recognise a stagflation-shaped split — mixed dots, vague guidance — for what it is: a genuine, unresolved tradeoff, not confusion.
