Transfer Entropy, Beyond the Linear Arrow

The previous lesson built mutual information as a nonlinear generalization of correlation. The ARIMA & VAR guide's Granger causality section then asked a directional question with that same linear toolkit: does X's past significantly improve a linear forecast of Y? Transfer entropy asks the identical question — model-free, with mutual information standing in for a regression's R² — and comes bundled with the same hard warning either way: a directional signal is not proof of causation.

The whole idea
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

I want to know which market moves first. I've been using Granger causality. Why would I need anything else?

Granger asks whether one series' past improves a straight-line forecast of another. If the link only shows up above a threshold, or only in big moves, a straight line can miss it completely.

So what's the alternative?

Transfer entropy. It asks the same question with information instead of a regression. Once I already know Y's own past, does X's past tell me anything new about Y's future?

Why hold Y's own past fixed?

Because most series predict themselves a bit. Think of mood. A good mood today makes a good mood tomorrow likely, whatever the weather. You only credit the weather with what it adds on top.

Give me a number.

Let X be fair coin flips, a fresh one every day. Let Y today copy yesterday's X, except that one bit in ten gets flipped by noise. Before you look at X, Y today is a full bit of uncertainty. Once you know X yesterday, only the noise is left, about point four seven of a bit. So X tells you about point five three bits about Y.

And the other way round?

Zero, exactly. X is a brand-new coin flip each day. Nothing in anyone's past helps predict it. So you get a big number one way and nothing the other way. That lopsidedness is the whole point.

So X causes Y.

Careful. Here it does, because we built it that way. In real data, a hidden driver can hit both series with different delays. Picture a risk-off shock that reaches one market a little sooner. You'd see the same one-way pattern with no direct link at all.

So what is it actually good for?

A nonlinear check on a lead-lag result. Within two years of the 2000 paper that introduced it, Marschinski and Kantz were using it to measure information flow between the Dow Jones and the DAX. And for normally distributed, linear data it gives the same answer as Granger, so it only earns its extra data cost when the link is nonlinear.

Anything that trips people up?

Estimating it. The coin example is exact. Real data needs binning choices, lots of history, and a test against shuffled series, or small samples will show you flows that aren't there.

One line?

Transfer entropy tells you whose past helps predict whose future, in any shape, but it never tells you who is pushing whom.

How it reached trading

Lead-lag between markets was measured with Granger's 1969 linear test, which misses links that live in the tails or in volatility; mutual information catches those but cannot say which way influence runs.

Schreiber's 2000 measure fixed both, and Marschinski & Kantz (2002) applied it to flows between the Dow Jones and DAX. It is now a standard nonlinear cross-check on Granger results and a way to draw directed spillover edges.

Section 2

Granger causality only ever looks for a straight line

Recall the test from the ARIMA & VAR guide: fit Y on its own lags, add X's lags, and F-test whether that addition helps.

Yₜ = a·Yₜ₋₁ + b·Xₜ₋₁ + εₜ → is b significant?
R²

The test is exactly as powerful as the model

Granger causality's F-test lives entirely inside a linear regression. It can only ever detect the kind of dependence a straight-line coefficient can express.

⊘

A real, usable relationship can hide from it

If X's past predicts Y's future through a threshold, an interaction, or any other nonlinear channel, the linear coefficient b can come back statistically indistinguishable from zero — even with genuine predictive information sitting right there in the data.

↔

The identical blind spot as correlation

This is the same structural gap the previous lesson's Y = X² example demonstrated for correlation — a real dependence a linear summary statistic simply cannot see, not because the relationship is weak, but because it isn't linear.

Roosters crowing right before sunrise "Granger-cause" the sunrise by the narrow statistical definition — a rooster's crow is a fine linear predictor of dawn. Whatever tool replaces the linear regression underneath, that warning never goes away; it only gets carried forward, unchanged, to whatever comes next.
Section 3

The model-free version: does X's past reduce your uncertainty about Y's future?

Not "does adding X's lags improve a linear fit" — but "does knowing X's past tell you anything about Y's future that Y's own past didn't already tell you."

TE(X→Y) = I( Yₜ ; Xₜ₋₁ | Yₜ₋₁ )
Yₜ

The target — Y's future value

What you're trying to predict.

Xₜ₋₁

The candidate source — X's past

Does it carry genuinely new information?

|Yₜ₋₁

Conditioned on Y's own past

Held fixed so only X's contribution is counted — not Y predicting itself.

This is a conditional mutual information — the mutual information from the previous lesson, computed within each fixed value of Yₜ₋₁ and averaged, the same way conditional entropy generalizes entropy. Because mutual information registers any form of statistical dependence, not just a linear one, transfer entropy catches exactly the nonlinear relationships Section 2's linear regression is structurally blind to — while asking the identical directional question.

Where this needs qualifying

Like ordinary mutual information, TE(X→Y) ≥ 0 always — it is built from an average of KL divergences, each individually nonnegative. Equality holds exactly when Xₜ₋₁ adds nothing to predicting Yₜ beyond what Yₜ₋₁ already provides. And nothing forces TE(X→Y) to equal TE(Y→X) — conditioning each direction on a different series' own past makes them two genuinely different computations.

Section 8

The message that must not get lost

What a nonzero, asymmetric TE(X→Y) is real evidence of
  • X's history carries genuine predictive content for Y that Y's own history doesn't already provide
  • A directional, model-free signal — the same kind of asymmetry Granger causality's directionality already gave you
  • A relationship worth investigating further, with real out-of-sample checks
What it is never, on its own, proof of
  • Causation in the formal, interventionist sense (Judea Pearl's do-calculus: "if I intervened on X, would Y change")
  • The absence of a third, unobserved common driver hitting both series at slightly different speeds
  • A mechanism — only ever "X's past predicts Y's future," never "X makes Y happen"
Imagine two friends who always sneeze within seconds of each other. It's tempting to say one sneeze causes the other — but maybe they're both just standing next to the same pile of pollen, reacting at slightly different speeds. Transfer entropy can spot the timing pattern perfectly; it has no way of telling "one triggered the other" apart from "something else set them both off."
Mandatory caveat, restated

This is the identical confounding failure mode already flagged for Granger causality in the ARIMA & VAR guide — restated for a nonlinear, model-free measure rather than solved by it. Neither tool distinguishes a real direct link from a shared unobserved driver on its own.

Section 9

Why this is much harder to compute from real data than Granger's regression

Granger causality's data requirement

Fit two linear regressions and run one F-test. The number of parameters is small and fixed — it grows only linearly as more lags are added.

Transfer entropy's data requirement

Estimate a full joint probability distribution over Yₜ, Xₜ₋₁, and Yₜ₋₁ — and every additional lag of history multiplies the number of distinct states that distribution has to cover, not just adds to it.

📊

A joint distribution, not a coefficient

A linear regression needs a handful of numbers. A conditional mutual information needs the entire shape of how three variables co-occur — vastly more information to pin down accurately.

📈

Longer history makes it worse fast

Extending the relevant lag length from one step to several doesn't add a little more data need — it multiplies the number of joint states, the same curse of dimensionality that makes any density estimation harder in more dimensions.

🎛

Every estimator choice adds bias

Binning, kernel bandwidth, or embedding-dimension choices for the joint density all introduce their own estimation bias — the identical concern already flagged for mutual information itself, now compounded by an extra conditioning variable.

This lesson's worked example on the earlier slide is exact by construction — the process's true distributions are known, not estimated. A real, finite, noisy sample never comes with known distributions, which is precisely why a naive transfer-entropy estimator can report a spuriously large number purely from overfitting a small sample's joint density.

Section 10

Where this sits in the literature — and in this repo

Thomas Schreiber introduced transfer entropy (2000) explicitly as a model-free alternative to Granger causality for nonlinear systems. Barnett, Barrett & Seth (2009) then proved the two are mathematically equivalent, up to a constant, for jointly Gaussian variables under a linear model — confirming transfer entropy is a strict generalization, adding real detection power only once the true relationship stops being linear. In neuroscience, it's a standard tool for directional flow between EEG or physiological channels, where nonlinear coupling is the norm rather than the exception.

Factual grounding — verified against this repo's current source

The full lesson tags itself "CONCEPT — not built or tested anywhere in this repo," and that check still holds: a repo-wide search finds no transfer-entropy code and no Granger-causality F-test or multi-series VAR fit anywhere in this codebase — confirming the same gap the ARIMA & VAR visual guide already flagged for Granger causality and VAR. One thing worth not confusing: js/goldMinerArbEngine.js and js/hedgeSignalV2Engine.js do implement Engle-Granger cointegration tests — a different test, named after the same economist, that checks whether two non-stationary series share a long-run equilibrium. It is not the lagged-forecast-improvement test this lesson and the ARIMA & VAR guide describe. Nothing here was stale; both claims check out as still accurate.

Section 11

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

What's the fundamental limitation of Granger causality that transfer entropy is built to fix?

Granger causality's F-test lives entirely inside a linear regression. Transfer entropy asks the same directional question using mutual information instead, catching nonlinear predictive relationships a linear coefficient can miss entirely.
Question 2

In TE(X→Y) = I(Yₜ; Xₜ₋₁ | Yₜ₋₁), what does conditioning on Yₜ₋₁ accomplish?

Holding Yₜ₋₁ fixed strips out the part of Yₜ's predictability that comes from Y predicting itself, so what's left and attributed to Xₜ₋₁ is only the new information X's past adds.
Question 3

A hidden variable Z drives both X and Y, with no direct link between X and Y at all. What can transfer entropy report here?

A shared, unobserved driver hitting both series with slightly different timing produces exactly this signature — a clean, asymmetric, nonzero transfer entropy with zero direct causal link, the same confounding failure mode already flagged for Granger causality.
Question 4

Why is estimating transfer entropy from real, finite data typically harder than a Granger-causality regression?

A linear regression estimates a handful of coefficients. Transfer entropy needs the joint distribution's full shape across every relevant lag — a much larger, harder estimation problem that grows fast as the lag length grows.
Your call
Scenario drill · an illustrative research review

Granger says nothing, transfer entropy says something

You tested whether the DE–US yield spread leads EUR/USD. The linear Granger test came back null in both directions. A colleague then estimated transfer entropy on the same daily data and found a clearly positive flow from the spread to EUR/USD, with almost nothing the other way. What do you do with the result?

  1. Write it up as "the spread causes EUR/USD" and size a trade on it.
    Transfer entropy measures predictive information flow, not cause. A shared driver, such as a change in risk sentiment that reaches the bond market slightly before FX, could produce exactly this one-way pattern. And nothing has been checked out of sample yet.
  2. Ignore it. The linear test was null, so the transfer entropy must be noise.
    A null linear test is exactly the case where transfer entropy can add something: a threshold or regime-dependent link that a straight line cannot represent. Throwing it out unchecked repeats the blind spot the tool exists to fix.
  3. Test it against shuffled series, recheck it out of sample and at other lags, and look for a common driver that could hit both.
    This treats the result as what it is: a candidate nonlinear lead-lag finding. The shuffle test guards against estimation bias, the out-of-sample check guards against overfitting, and the confound check keeps the claim at "predicts", not "causes".
  4. Accept it as "the spread's history predicts EUR/USD beyond its own history" and go straight to a backtest.
    The wording is honest, but you have skipped the significance test. Real-data transfer entropy estimates are biased upwards on finite samples, so the flow may not be there at all.

What the lesson says. This is an illustrative case built on the lesson's own spread and FX scenario, not a reported result. A clearly asymmetric transfer entropy where the linear test was null would be a more informative finding than the linear result alone, but it still needs a real out-of-sample check, a test against shuffled data, and the causal caveat stated plainly before it counts as anything more than predictive content a linear model missed.

Section 12

What to carry forward

01

Granger causality tests whether X's lagged values significantly improve a linear regression forecast of Y, beyond Y's own past — and is structurally blind to nonlinear predictive relationships.

02

Transfer entropy, TE(X→Y) = I(Yₜ; Xₜ₋₁ | Yₜ₋₁), generalizes it model-free — a conditional mutual information asking the identical directional question without assuming linearity.

03

The directional asymmetry is the whole point — TE(X→Y) ≠ TE(Y→X) in general, and that asymmetry is what gives it a causality-like signature at all.

04

It reduces exactly to Granger causality for jointly Gaussian, linear systems (Barnett, Barrett & Seth, 2009) — added detection power appears only once the relationship is genuinely nonlinear.

05

Neither tool proves causation — an unobserved common driver Z can produce a nonzero, asymmetric reading with zero real direct link, exactly as demonstrated on the confounder slider.

06

Estimating transfer entropy from real data needs a full joint probability distribution — far more data than Granger's linear regression, and much more as the relevant lag length grows.

07

Transfer entropy reached markets as a nonlinear lead-lag test (Marschinski & Kantz, 2002): Granger's question without the linearity, worth its data cost only when the link is nonlinear.

What this now lets you do

Recognize when a null linear Granger-causality result might be hiding a real nonlinear predictive relationship, and know transfer entropy is the model-free follow-up question to ask, not a different question entirely. Carry the exact same causation caveat forward regardless of which tool produced the directional number. And weigh the real cost — transfer entropy needs a joint distribution's worth of data, not a handful of regression coefficients — before reaching for it on a small sample.
