The Fed's Corridor: IORB, the SRF and the Discount Window

Every night trillions of dollars are lent against Treasuries. The Fed doesn't set that price directly. It puts a floor under it and a ceiling over it, and lets the market trade in between. This deck covers what each wall is, who leans on it, and what happened the two times it was tested.

Section 1

Overnight funding, and who is in the room

Overnight funding means borrowing cash today and repaying it tomorrow, mostly as repo against Treasuries. SOFR is its measured price, built from real trades and published each morning by the New York Fed.

🏦

Banks

Hold reserves at the Fed and earn IORB on them. They lend into repo only when it pays more. Only banks can use the discount window.

📑

Primary dealers

Hold big bond inventories and fund them overnight, so they are the main cash borrowers. They can use the SRF.

💵

Money market funds

The main cash lenders. They can't hold reserves or use the discount window, but they can lend to the Fed through the ON RRP.

🏛️

The Treasury's account

When taxes are paid or new debt settles, cash moves into the Treasury General Account at the Fed and leaves the market.

IORB sits inside the band, a little below the top. Each wall is an arbitrage:

The floor

No one lends below what the Fed pays them. A bank lends reserves into repo only if SOFR minus its balance-sheet cost beats IORB. A money fund won't take less than the ON RRP rate.

The ceiling

No one with access borrows above what the Fed charges. If market repo costs more than the SRF rate, a dealer borrows from the Fed instead.

The "≲" is deliberate. The ceiling is soft: not everyone has access to the SRF, and some would pay a little more than be seen using it.

Section 4

The floor has two parts

IORB: banks

The Fed pays banks interest on reserves. Since a bank can earn that risk-free, it won't lend overnight for much less. Raising IORB lifts every overnight rate the same afternoon.

ON RRP: non-banks

Money funds have no reserve account, so they can't earn IORB. The ON RRP lets them lend to the Fed against Treasuries at a rate at or near the bottom of the target range.

A big ON RRP balance means spare cash with nowhere better to go. When it drains toward zero, private repo is paying more and the system is moving from abundant cash toward something tighter.
Section 5

The ceiling has two doors

Both lend at the top of the target range. They mean very different things.

Standing Repo Facility

Permanent since July 2021. Repo against Treasuries and agency debt, for primary dealers and eligible banks. Built for routine use: drawing it means "the Fed was the cheapest cash tonight."

Discount window

Loans to banks only, against broad collateral. The emergency door. It carries a stigma: borrowers fear it will be read as "can't raise cash anywhere else."

Arbitrage, not collapse

A ceiling people are afraid to touch doesn't cap anything. That is why the SRF was built to be ordinary. Rising SRF usage on its own means the ceiling is being used as designed.

No failure anywhere. The cash is just briefly in the wrong place: in the Treasury's account at the Fed instead of in the repo market. Month-ends work the same way, driven by banks shrinking balance sheets for reporting dates.

Section 7 · It actually happened

September 2019: no ceiling in place

It actually happened

On September 16, 2019, quarterly corporate tax payments and a large Treasury settlement drained cash into the Treasury's account on the same day, after reserves had been shrinking for months. Overnight repo rates jumped: on September 17 SOFR printed 5.25% against a 2.00–2.25% target range. There was no standing facility to cap it. The New York Fed restarted overnight repo operations that day, for the first time in about a decade, and in October began buying Treasury bills to rebuild reserves.

Flip the toggle on the corridor slide to "No standing ceiling" to see that shape. The lesson the Fed drew: an improvised ceiling comes too late. The SRF, permanent since July 2021, is the standing version.

Section 8 · It actually happened

March 2023: SVB and the emergency door

It actually happened

Silicon Valley Bank failed on March 10, 2023, after a depositor run. It held bonds bought when rates were low, now worth well below face value. On March 12 the Fed launched the Bank Term Funding Program (BTFP): loans of up to a year against Treasuries and agency debt valued at par, so banks didn't have to sell at a loss. Discount-window borrowing hit a record of about $152.9 billion in the week of March 15, above the October 2008 peak of about $110.7 billion.

Section 9

What this desk found when it tested the claim

The folk claim: SOFR above the floor, or a drawn SRF, warns of rougher markets. 2,123 SOFR sessions, 2018-04 to 2026-09, next-5-session range vs a matched control.

Desk test: nothing found funding-stress, SOFR over the floor: month-end excluded, only 15 episodes (untestable). 12 of 15 fall on the 14th–18th of a month: the mid-month chain.
Desk test: nothing found funding-stress, SRF drawn: 21 episodes. SPX500 −0.561 ATR [−0.984, −0.181]: a calmer tape, not a wider one. EUR/USD and USD/JPY null. The SRF is drawn on 667 of 2,123 sessions (31%).
Desk data: base rate repo-stress-range: the 99th-percentile SOFR spike (26 episodes) is month-end plumbing. SPX500 +0.14 ATR [−0.54, +0.98]; it fires on a quarter of sessions since 2024.
The desk's written prior said a drawn SRF precedes wider ranges. It lost. "Arbitrage, not collapse" predicted what came back. But this is not proof the backstop works: SRF days are mostly ordinary days, and the sample has no crisis in it.
Section 10

The arbitrage in dollars, and the traps

Worked example (actual/360)
Dealer: market repo 4.62% vs SRF 4.50% on $1bn overnight → $1,000,000,000 × 0.0012 / 360 = $3,333.33 saved
Bank: SOFR 4.45% vs IORB 4.40% on $500m → $500,000,000 × 0.0005 / 360 = $694.44 extra, before balance-sheet cost
Common pitfalls

Reading SRF usage as an alarm: it's drawn on about a third of sessions. Forgetting the calendar: a jump on the 15th or at quarter-end is the null hypothesis. Treating the ceiling as hard: SOFR can print a few bp above it. Lumping the two doors together: same rate, very different signal. Over-reading the null: no crisis in the sample.

Section 11

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Why does the Fed need the ON RRP as well as IORB?

Without the ON RRP, money funds might lend below IORB and leak the floor. The ON RRP pays them a fixed rate at or near the bottom of the range.
Question 2

A dealer draws the SRF on a day market repo costs more than the SRF rate. What is the best reading?

The desk tested it: after SRF draws, SPX500 ranges were narrower (−0.561 ATR), and the SRF is drawn on 31% of sessions. That is routine plumbing, not an alarm.
Question 3

Why does SOFR tend to lift around the 15th of the month?

12 of 15 non-month-end SOFR episodes on this desk's data fell on the 14th–18th. It's the calendar, not stress.
Question 4

What made September 2019 different from a tight day today?

SOFR printed 5.25% against a 2.00–2.25% range. The SRF, permanent since July 2021, now caps that kind of move.
Section 12

What to carry forward

01

The Fed steers overnight rates with standing offers: a floor (IORB for banks, ON RRP for money funds) and a ceiling (the SRF, permanent since July 2021).

02

Each wall is an arbitrage. No one lends below what the Fed pays; no one with access borrows above what it charges. The ceiling is soft.

03

Mid-month chain: tax date → money fund withdrawals → funds pull cash from repo → cash scarcer → SOFR lifts.

04

Two doors: the SRF is the routine price cap; the discount window is the stigmatised emergency door, used in size in March 2023 alongside the BTFP.

05

Desk verdict, NULL: SOFR above the floor is the calendar, and a drawn SRF was followed by a calmer tape (−0.561 ATR). The desk's own prior lost. Not proof the backstop works.

What this now lets you do

Read a SOFR print against the walls instead of on its own. Tell a calendar squeeze from real distress. And know that SRF usage is a price cap working, while large discount-window borrowing is the thing to look at twice.
