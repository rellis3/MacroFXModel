Merton's Continuous-Time Portfolio Problem

Where the Kelly Criterion's \(f^*=\mu/\sigma^2\) actually comes from: the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation, expected-utility maximization, and the single general formula that Kelly turns out to be one exact special case of.

CONCEPT — the continuous-time generalization of Kelly sizing ~18 min read Prereqs: Kelly Criterion & Position Sizing, Itô's Lemma
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • Merton's problem asks: choose consumption and a risky-asset allocation fraction continuously through time to maximize expected lifetime utility — solved via the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (HJB) equation, which finds the value function \(J(W,t)\) (best-achievable utility from wealth \(W\) at time \(t\)) rather than a single number, because today's optimal choice depends on the value of every possible future wealth level.
  • For an investor with constant relative risk aversion \(\gamma\), the HJB equation solves out to a clean, wealth- and horizon-independent constant allocation: \(f^*=\mu/(\gamma\sigma^2)\) — the "Merton fraction."
  • Setting \(\gamma=1\) (log utility) reduces this exactly, term for term, to the Kelly Criterion's \(f^*=\mu/\sigma^2\) — not an approximation, but a literal algebraic identity, because maximizing expected log-wealth is identical, as an optimization problem, to expected-utility maximization under \(U(c)=\ln c\).
  • Fractional Kelly (e.g. half-Kelly for \(\gamma=2\)) is reframed as the exact utility-optimum for a more risk-averse investor, not a safety discount off one "true" answer.
  • This is the final lesson of the numbered Theory Lab curriculum, closing the loop by deriving the very first sizing formula the series introduced — and it still ends on the same discipline that governs everything in this repo: a piece of math is a tool, not a result, until it's proven on out-of-sample data with real costs.
  • Real-world episode: LTCM, where Robert Merton was a partner, ran about 25-to-1 in 1998. At that leverage a constant fraction means a 1% fall in the positions forces selling about a quarter of the book, and after Russia's default the market knew the positions, so the rebalancing the model assumes free could not be done. Sizing by the Merton fraction is untested on this desk.

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

Kelly told me to bet my edge over my variance. Where does Merton come in?

Kelly answers one narrow question: what bet grows my money fastest in the long run. Merton asks the bigger one. How much should I hold in a risky asset, at every moment, given how much I personally dislike risk?

And he solved that?

In two papers, in 1969 and 1971. The investor re-decides at every instant, so he needs a tool for choices made through time. It is called the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation.

That sounds heavy.

The idea is a sat nav. For every place and time it knows the best remaining journey from there. Merton's version knows the best you can do from every level of wealth. Once you know that, the best move right now is a simple comparison.

So what comes out at the end?

One clean rule. The share to hold is your edge, divided by your risk aversion times the variance. And it is a constant. It does not change with how rich you are or how long you have left.

Give me a number.

Say the asset beats cash by six percent a year, with twenty-five percent volatility. Square the volatility and you get a variance of six and a quarter percent. Six divided by six and a quarter is ninety-six percent. That is the answer for a risk aversion of one.

And a risk aversion of one is Kelly?

Exactly Kelly, not roughly. Risk aversion of one is log utility, and maximising expected log wealth is what Kelly does. Same problem, same answer.

What if I'm more nervous than that?

Set it to two and the answer halves, to forty-eight percent. That is half-Kelly. Not a safety discount on the true answer. It is the true answer for someone with your stomach.

Where does it go wrong?

It assumes you can rebalance for free, all the time. Take Long-Term Capital Management, where Merton was a partner. By early 1998 it ran about twenty-five to one. At that leverage, a one percent fall in the positions cuts wealth by about a quarter, so holding the fraction steady means selling about a quarter of the book.

And after Russia defaulted, everyone knew what they held.

So every sale moved the price against them. About four point six billion dollars was lost, and on the twenty-third of September fourteen banks and brokers put in about three point six billion to take the portfolio over.

So the one-line version?

Hold your edge divided by your risk aversion times variance, be honest about your risk aversion, and never size so big that the rebalancing itself becomes the risk.

Section 01

Why this matters

The Kelly Criterion lesson derived a clean, practical formula — \(f^*=\mu/\sigma^2\) — by asking a narrow question: what fraction of bankroll maximizes the long-run compounded growth rate of a repeated bet? That derivation worked by taking a Taylor expansion of expected log-wealth and setting a derivative to zero. It is correct, and it is also, on its own, a slightly unsatisfying place to stop, because it never asks the question a real investor actually faces: how much of my current wealth should I hold in a risky asset, at every instant, given how much I personally dislike risk — not assuming I am, specifically, a long-run-growth maximizer, but letting that be one possible answer among many.

That is exactly the question Robert Merton posed and solved in two papers, in 1969 and 1971: choose, continuously through time, both a consumption rate and a portfolio allocation fraction to maximize expected lifetime utility, where wealth evolves as a continuous stochastic process and the allocation fraction is a control variable chosen at every instant, not just once. Solving a problem shaped like that — optimize a running choice through time, against uncertainty, where today's decision affects tomorrow's state — requires a different mathematical tool than the plain calculus used in the Kelly lesson: the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (HJB) equation, the continuous-time face of dynamic programming. This lesson is the payoff for that machinery: Merton's classic result for an investor with constant relative risk aversion \(\gamma\) is \(f^*=\mu/(\gamma\sigma^2)\) — and setting \(\gamma=1\) recovers the Kelly formula from the previous lesson exactly, term for term. Kelly's growth-optimal criterion is not a separate, competing idea from utility-based portfolio theory — it is the specific point on Merton's dial where an investor's risk aversion happens to equal exactly one. This is also the closing lesson of the full Theory Lab curriculum, and it closes the loop deliberately: the very last piece of math in the series explains the very first sizing formula the series introduced.

Plain-English intuition

What a "value function" is, and why a GPS is the right analogy

Imagine planning a road trip with real-time traffic and weather that keep changing as you drive. Deciding the entire route in advance, all at once, is a nightmare — you'd have to enumerate every possible sequence of turns against every possible future traffic jam. A GPS doesn't do that. Instead, for every location and every point in time, it effectively knows one number: the best possible remaining travel time from here, if you drive optimally from this point onward. Call that number the "value" of being at this location, at this time. Given that number at every nearby location one step ahead, picking the single best next turn from where you are right now becomes a trivial one-step comparison — no need to re-plan the whole trip.

A value function in Merton's problem is exactly that GPS number, applied to wealth instead of location: \(J(W,t)\) is the best-achievable expected (lifetime) utility an investor can get, starting from wealth \(W\) at time \(t\), assuming they make every future consumption and allocation decision optimally from here on. This is Richard Bellman's "principle of optimality" — an optimal policy has the property that, whatever the earlier decisions were, the remaining decisions must still be optimal given the state that resulted — applied to a continuous-time, continuously-uncertain wealth process. The HJB equation is simply the mathematical statement of "today's best choice equals today's payoff plus the value of wherever that choice leaves you tomorrow," written as a differential equation instead of a turn-by-turn comparison, because in continuous time there is no discrete "next turn" — only an infinitesimal instant, and Itô's Lemma is the tool that lets you correctly write down what happens to \(J\) over that infinitesimal instant when wealth itself is jittering around randomly.

Setting up the control problem. An investor holds wealth \(W_t\), split between a riskless asset earning rate \(r\) and a risky asset with expected excess return \(\mu\) (over \(r\)) and variance \(\sigma^2\). At every instant they choose two things: a consumption rate \(C_t\) (money withdrawn and spent, permanently leaving the portfolio) and an allocation fraction \(f_t\) (the share of remaining wealth held in the risky asset). Wealth then evolves as a stochastic differential equation — the continuous-time, control-variable generalization of the same geometric-Brownian-motion-style wealth process used in the Kelly lesson's continuous form:

\[ dW_t = \big[(r + f_t\mu)W_t - C_t\big]\,dt + f_t\sigma W_t\,dZ_t \]
dWₜthe instantaneous change in wealth
(r+fₜμ)Wₜ−Cₜthe deterministic drift: riskless plus risky return, minus consumption
fₜσWₜ dZₜthe random shock from holding the risky asset
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Unlike Kelly's lesson, where you pick one bet size and stick with it, Merton's investor gets to make two decisions fresh at every single instant forever: how much money to spend right now, and how much of what's left to risk. It's the difference between deciding your grocery budget once for the whole year versus re-deciding it, continuously, every moment, as your bank balance and the market both keep moving under you.

The investor's objective is to choose the entire future path of \(\{C_t, f_t\}\) — not just their values right now, but the whole strategy for every future instant — to maximize expected discounted lifetime utility of consumption (plus, in the finite-horizon version, a utility of whatever wealth is left at the terminal date \(T\)):

\[ \max_{\{C_s,f_s\}} \; E_t\!\left[\int_t^T e^{-\rho(s-t)}U(C_s)\,ds \;+\; e^{-\rho(T-t)}B(W_T)\right] \]
E_t[…]expectation over future randomness, as of today
e^{-ρ(s-t)}U(C_s)discounted utility of consumption at each future instant
e^{-ρ(T-t)}B(W_T)discounted utility of whatever wealth remains at the horizon

The value function. Define \(J(W,t)\) as the value of that maximized objective, starting from wealth \(W\) at time \(t\) — the "best-achievable expected utility from here onward" described in the intuition box above, now written precisely:

\[ J(W,t) \;=\; \max_{\{C_s,f_s\}_{s\ge t}} \; E_t\!\left[\int_t^T e^{-\rho(s-t)}U(C_s)\,ds \;+\; e^{-\rho(T-t)}B(W_T)\;\middle|\; W_t=W\right] \]

This single function \(J\) packages the entire remaining decision problem — every possible future consumption path, every possible future allocation path, every possible future random wealth outcome — into one number per \((W,t)\) pair. That compression is the entire point of dynamic programming: instead of solving one enormous problem over the whole time horizon at once, Bellman's principle lets you solve a much simpler problem — what to do right now — provided you already know \(J\) an instant into the future.

The HJB equation, and what it's actually asking. Applying Itô's Lemma to \(J(W,t)\) (necessary because \(W\) itself follows a stochastic process — an ordinary Taylor expansion isn't valid once Brownian noise is in the picture) and taking the dynamic-programming recursion to its continuous-time limit produces the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation for this problem:

\[ 0 = \max_{C,f}\left\{\, U(C) - \rho J + J_t + J_W\big[(r+f\mu)W - C\big] + \tfrac{1}{2}J_{WW}f^2\sigma^2 W^2 \,\right\} \]
J, J_t, J_W, J_WWthe value function and its time/wealth derivatives
U(C) − ρJflow utility of consuming now, minus discounting the future
max_{C,f}{…}choose consumption and allocation to make this zero-sum balance hold optimally

This is a nonlinear partial differential equation in the unknown function \(J(W,t)\), and it is exactly the calculus-textbook idea "set the derivative to zero to find the max" — the same move the Kelly lesson used on \(g(f)=p\ln(1+f)+q\ln(1-f)\) — except the thing being maximized inside the braces at every instant is itself a function of \(J\) and its derivatives, which is what makes this a genuinely harder problem than the single-variable calculus in the Kelly derivation, and why it needs its own named equation and its own solution technique (rather than being solvable by direct substitution). You do not need to carry the full derivation further than this to understand what's coming next; the important thing to take away is why a problem like this needs an equation for a whole function \(J\), rather than a single number: because the optimal choice today depends on how valuable every possible future wealth level will be, and that "value of every possible future wealth level" is precisely what \(J\) encodes.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

The HJB equation is a formal way of writing down one sentence: "the best move right now equals what you get from that move immediately, plus how good your situation becomes right after." It's the GPS logic from the intuition box above, turned into an equation — instead of comparing a handful of next turns, it balances an instant's worth of decision against the value of wherever that instant leaves you, over and over, continuously.

Taking the first-order condition on \(f\) inside the braces (holding \(C\) at its own optimum) gives a clean expression for the optimal allocation in terms of \(J\)'s derivatives:

\[ f^* \;=\; -\,\frac{J_W}{W\,J_{WW}}\;\cdot\;\frac{\mu}{\sigma^2} \]
−J_W/(W J_WW)the investor's relative risk tolerance, implied by J
μ/σ²the same Sharpe-ratio-like edge term from the Kelly lesson

The term \(-J_W/(W J_{WW})\) is the investor's relative risk tolerance implied by their value function — the reciprocal of the Arrow-Pratt coefficient of relative risk aversion, \(\gamma = -c\,U''(c)/U'(c)\), evaluated along the optimal path. This is the point where a specific choice of utility function is needed to go from "an expression involving \(J\)'s derivatives" to "an actual number" — and this is exactly where Merton's 1969/1971 results plug in a specific, tractable utility family.

Solving for CRRA utility. Merton's classic case uses constant relative risk aversion (CRRA) utility, \(U(c)=\dfrac{c^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma}\) for a risk-aversion parameter \(\gamma > 0,\; \gamma \ne 1\) (with \(U(c)=\ln c\) as the exact limiting case as \(\gamma \to 1\) — a standard, well-known limit of the CRRA family, not a separate assumption bolted on). Guessing that \(J(W,t)\) itself takes a power form matching \(U\) — the standard "guess and verify" technique for solving HJB equations with CRRA utility, not something this lesson derives in full — and substituting back into the HJB equation shows that this guess does solve the PDE, and that for this utility family the risk-tolerance term collapses to a constant: \(-J_W/(W J_{WW}) = 1/\gamma\), exactly. That turns the general expression above into Merton's headline result:

\[ f^* \;=\; \frac{\mu}{\gamma\,\sigma^2} \]

This is a genuinely notable result on its own, independent of anything to do with Kelly: for CRRA utility, the optimal fraction of wealth held in the risky asset is a constant — it does not depend on the investor's current wealth level or on how much time remains until the horizon \(T\). A CRRA investor holds the same risky-asset weight whether they are wealthy or poor, young or old (a strong, somewhat surprising implication that depends specifically on the CRRA assumption — other utility functions, e.g. non-constant relative risk aversion, generally produce allocation rules that do vary with wealth and horizon). In the literature this constant is often called the Merton fraction or Merton ratio.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

You might expect a cautious investor to bet a smaller fraction of a big pile of money than a small one, or to pull back as retirement nears — but for this specific kind of investor (CRRA), that's not what happens: the right fraction to risk stays exactly the same dial setting no matter how rich you get or how much time is left. It's less like adjusting your grip as the rope gets longer, and more like a thermostat set to one temperature that doesn't care how big the house is.

The reduction to Kelly, made exact. Set \(\gamma=1\) — the log-utility case, \(U(c)=\ln c\) — directly into Merton's formula:

\[ f^*\Big|_{\gamma=1} \;=\; \frac{\mu}{1\cdot\sigma^2} \;=\; \frac{\mu}{\sigma^2} \]

This is exactly the continuous-time Kelly formula derived in the previous lesson — not an approximation, not a coincidence of similar notation, but a literal algebraic identity once \(\gamma\) is set to 1. And there is a clean reason it has to come out this way, not just a numerical accident: maximizing expected log-wealth (Kelly's entire objective, \(E[\ln W_T]\), the quantity Kelly (1956) proved maximizes long-run compounded growth) is identical, as an optimization problem, to expected-utility maximization under the specific utility function \(U(\cdot)=\ln(\cdot)\). Kelly's growth-rate criterion and Merton's expected-utility framework are not two different theories that happen to agree at one point — they are the same theory, and \(\gamma=1\) is simply the name, inside the more general utility framework, for the specific investor Kelly's original derivation was describing all along.

The key insight: Kelly and Merton were never two different ideas that happen to line up — they are the same expected-utility optimization, and "Kelly sizing" is simply Merton's framework evaluated at the single risk-aversion value \(\gamma=1\).

What \(\gamma \ne 1\) means — fractional Kelly, justified rather than assumed. The Kelly lesson introduced fractional Kelly (betting some fraction \(c\) of \(f^*\), commonly \(c=0.5\)) as a practical response to estimation error and drawdown pain — a sound argument, but one that leaves \(f^*\) itself as "the growth-optimal answer" and treats sizing below it as a deliberate compromise. Merton's framework reframes that completely. Rewrite the CRRA result as a fraction of the Kelly fraction:

\[ f^*(\gamma) \;=\; \frac{1}{\gamma}\cdot\frac{\mu}{\sigma^2} \;=\; \frac{1}{\gamma}\cdot f^*_{Kelly} \]

An investor with \(\gamma=2\) has a mathematically optimal allocation of exactly \(\tfrac{1}{2}f^*_{Kelly}\) — half-Kelly, precisely, not approximately. An investor with \(\gamma=4\) has an optimal allocation of exactly \(\tfrac{1}{4}f^*_{Kelly}\) — quarter-Kelly. Fractional Kelly is not, under this lens, a safety margin layered on top of "the" right answer — it is the right answer, for any investor whose honestly self-assessed relative risk aversion genuinely exceeds 1. Since \(\gamma=1\) (log utility) is a specific, fairly aggressive risk preference — not the typical one — and most empirical estimates of investors' revealed risk aversion sit meaningfully above 1, Merton's framework says most real investors' correct, personal, utility-maximizing bet size was never full Kelly to begin with. Betting less than full Kelly is not a hedge against being wrong about \(\mu\) and \(\sigma^2\) (though it also happens to help with that, as the Kelly lesson covered) — for a genuinely more risk-averse investor, it is what "correct" means.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Betting less than "full Kelly" used to sound like flinching — playing it safe out of fear that your numbers might be wrong. Merton's math says it can instead just be a different, equally correct answer to a different, equally honest question: not "how fast can this grow," but "how fast can this grow given how much stomach-churn I'm actually willing to sign up for." Neither investor is playing scared — they're solving for different people.

Real-world trading scenario

Two traders, identical edge, different risk aversion — and two different, both-correct bet sizes

Two traders run the exact same systematic FX strategy. It has been honestly validated out-of-sample (per this repo's own OOS-Sharpe discipline), and both traders agree on its \(\mu\) (expected excess return) and \(\sigma^2\) (variance) — there is no disagreement about the edge itself, only about how much of their own capital they are willing to put behind it.

Trader A has, after honest self-assessment, a relative risk aversion of \(\gamma=1\) — genuinely comfortable riding out the depth and frequency of drawdown that full, log-utility-optimal growth requires. Merton's formula says their mathematically optimal allocation is:

\[ f^*_A = \frac{\mu}{1\cdot\sigma^2} = \frac{\mu}{\sigma^2} \]

Trader B runs the identical strategy but, honestly assessed, has \(\gamma=2\) — meaningfully more risk-averse, not comfortable with the same drawdown path Trader A accepts. Merton's exact formula for Trader B is:

\[ f^*_B = \frac{\mu}{2\sigma^2} = \frac{1}{2}f^*_A \]

Trader B should rationally bet exactly half of what Trader A bets — not as an arbitrary "half-Kelly for safety" rule of thumb applied on top of a single "correct" answer, but as the precise, mathematically optimal answer for Trader B's own, honestly-stated risk aversion. Both traders are behaving optimally; they are simply optimizing different utility functions, because they are, honestly, different people. This reframes the perennial "why not just bet full Kelly" question from a vague caution about drawdown pain into a precise, personal, computable number — once an investor is honest with themselves about \(\gamma\), Merton's formula tells them exactly what to do with it, and the answer isn't the same for everyone by design.

Section 03

It actually happened: LTCM, 1998, and the rebalancing nobody could do

The pitfalls box below says the constant-fraction result assumes continuous, frictionless rebalancing. That sounds like a technicality. It is not, once \(f\) is large. The clearest case involves the author of this lesson's model himself.

It actually happened

Holding a constant fraction at 25 to 1

Robert Merton was a partner in Long-Term Capital Management, founded in 1994 by John Meriwether, alongside Myron Scholes; the two shared the 1997 Nobel prize. The fund's trades were small, highly levered convergence bets, and the full story, told through leverage and through spreads, is in Kelly Criterion & Position Sizing and Cointegration & Statistical Arbitrage. Nothing public says LTCM sized its book with the formula in this lesson, so this is not a claim that Merton's model failed. It is about one assumption this lesson's result shares with every levered book: that you can always rebalance back to your target fraction at the current price.

In early 1998 LTCM held well over $100 billion of balance-sheet assets on under $5 billion of capital, roughly 25 to 1. Run the constant-fraction rule at that leverage. If the positions fall 1% in value, wealth falls by about 25%, and to keep the same fraction \(f\) the position must shrink to match: the fund must sell about a quarter of everything it owns. With \(f<1\) the same rule says "buy after a fall"; with \(f>1\) it says "sell after a fall", and the larger \(f\), the larger the sale relative to the move.

After Russia devalued and defaulted on 17 August 1998, investors fled to the most liquid bonds and LTCM's spreads widened together. Its lenders wanted more collateral, which is the de-levering the rule demands, enforced from outside. But much of the market knew roughly what LTCM held, so selling meant moving prices against itself, and every sale deepened the next mark. By late September it had lost about $4.6 billion. On 23 September the New York Fed brought together fourteen banks and brokers, who put in about $3.6 billion to take over the portfolio and wind it down. Many of the trades did converge later, after the fund could no longer hold them.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Merton's rule is like always carrying the same share of your weight in a backpack. If you're carrying less than your own weight, that's easy to keep right. If you borrowed so much you're carrying 25 times your weight, every little stumble means you must drop a quarter of the load right now, and if everyone is watching you drop it, they won't pay you much for what you drop.

Where the textbook version breaks

The constant-fraction result assumes continuous rebalancing, no transaction costs, no price impact from your own trades and a return process with continuous paths and constant \(\sigma\). 1998 broke all four together: the spreads gapped, volatility jumped, the trades needed to restore \(f\) were large enough to move the market, and lenders forced the timing. The lesson's own pitfall about a "no-trade region" under costs is the mild version of this; price impact at high leverage is the severe one.

Not tested here Not tested here: "sizing a validated sleeve at its Merton fraction, or a fraction \(1/\gamma\) of it, gives the best growth for the drawdown on this desk's data." No sizing rule has been scored against alternatives here. A fair test would estimate \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) walk-forward with no look-ahead, size at \(\gamma = 1, 2, 4\) and at a fixed-risk baseline, charge realistic costs including the rebalancing trades, and compare realised growth and worst drawdown. With the few dozen to hundred trades a slow macro sleeve produces, the \(\mu\) estimate will be noisy, which is itself an argument for \(\gamma\) well above 1.
Section 04

How practitioners actually use this

Merton's 1969 and 1971 papers are foundational texts in continuous-time finance — part of the body of work that, together with the Black-Scholes-Merton option-pricing result built on the same stochastic-calculus toolkit, earned Merton the 1997 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences (shared with Myron Scholes). This is the same Merton behind the Merton Jump-Diffusion Model covered earlier in this curriculum — one researcher, two of the field's foundational continuous-time results, a decade apart.

In practice, the pure single-risky-asset CRRA solution is rarely used as a literal, direct sizing formula, for reasons that echo the honesty discipline already applied to Kelly: \(\gamma\) is not directly observable any more than a strategy's true \(\mu\) and \(\sigma^2\) are. It is typically elicited indirectly — through questionnaires, revealed choices under real risk, or calibration to observed market risk premia (the "equity premium puzzle" literature is largely a debate over what \(\gamma\) would have to be to rationalize historically observed equity returns under exactly this framework) — which layers a third estimation problem on top of the two the Kelly lesson already flagged. What survives into everyday practice is less the exact formula and more its structure: the "Merton fraction" or "Merton share" language is genuinely used at quantitative shops and in the academic asset-allocation literature as shorthand for "the utility-optimal constant weight in the risky asset," and target-date and lifecycle fund glide paths are conceptually descended from this framework even though real-world glide paths are built from ad hoc age-based rules rather than a literal solved HJB equation with an honestly elicited \(\gamma\). The rigorous version matters less for its direct number and more because it is the reason the entire "how much should I bet" question has a real mathematical floor underneath it at all — one that the Kelly lesson's practical formula sits on top of, rather than beside.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where the Merton framework quietly goes wrong

Treating \(\gamma\) as knowable rather than elicited. The entire clean result \(f^*=\mu/(\gamma\sigma^2)\) depends on plugging in a specific number for \(\gamma\), but relative risk aversion is not something an investor can look up — it must be elicited, and different elicitation methods (survey questions, revealed behavior under real losses, calibration to market risk premia) routinely disagree with each other. An honestly-stated \(\gamma\) is a rough, uncertain input, the same way a backtested \(\mu\) is — plugging in an over-precise \(\gamma\) and treating the resulting \(f^*\) as exact repeats the Kelly lesson's core caution one level up the stack.

Assuming the constant-proportion result survives real market frictions. The clean, wealth-and-horizon-independent constant fraction is a direct consequence of assuming CRRA utility, continuous frictionless rebalancing, and constant \(\mu,\sigma\). Add transaction costs, and the optimal policy becomes a "no-trade region" around the target rather than a single constant weight (rebalancing only when allocation drifts far enough to be worth the cost) — a well-known result in the continuous-time finance literature, but a genuinely different, more complex policy than the clean formula above.

Assuming constant \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) through time. Merton's classic solution takes the risky asset's return distribution as fixed. Real markets have regime shifts, volatility clustering, and occasional discontinuous jumps — exactly the phenomena the Merton Jump-Diffusion Model lesson, by the same author, was built to handle, and exactly what a plain constant-\(\sigma\) CRRA solution does not account for. A regime change in \(\mu\) or \(\sigma\) changes \(f^*\) immediately in theory; in practice it means the "optimal constant fraction" is only as stable as the underlying \(\mu,\sigma\) actually are, which — as every other lesson in this curriculum involving estimated volatility has stressed — is never perfectly stable.

Choosing CRRA for its tractability rather than because it describes the investor. CRRA utility is used throughout this literature largely because it produces the clean, closed-form, constant-fraction answer — a real mathematical convenience, not necessarily an accurate description of how any specific person's risk preferences actually behave as their wealth changes. Other utility families (e.g. those exhibiting decreasing rather than constant relative risk aversion) are arguably more realistic for many investors and do not produce a constant \(f^*\) at all — a reminder that "the math is clean" and "the math describes you" are different claims.

Scenario drill · 17 August 1998

Russia has defaulted, and you are running 25 to 1

You run a book of small convergence trades at roughly 25 to 1: well over $100 billion of positions on under $5 billion of capital. You keep the leverage at a constant fraction of wealth. Today Russia devalued and defaulted, investors are rushing into the most liquid bonds, and your spreads are starting to widen together. Much of the market knows roughly what you hold. At this leverage, every 1% fall in the positions means selling about a quarter of the book to stay at your fraction. What do you do?

  1. Hold the book. The trades are right and the spreads must converge.
    The trades may well be right, but at 25 to 1 a 1% fall cuts wealth by about a quarter. You do not get to choose when to de-lever: your lenders will, by demanding collateral, at the worst prices.
  2. Add to the trades. Wider spreads mean a bigger edge.
    A bigger edge does not help if the variance has jumped too, and adding raises the fraction further above 1. Every extra unit of leverage makes the next forced sale bigger, in a market that already knows your positions.
  3. Bring the leverage down now, in sizes the market can absorb, and take the loss while you still choose the timing.
    Above a fraction of 1, the constant-fraction rule sells into losses, and the sale scales with the fraction minus 1. Cutting the fraction early shrinks every future forced sale. It costs money today, but it keeps you in charge of the trades instead of your lenders.
  4. Follow the rule to the letter: after each 1% fall, sell a quarter of the book at whatever bid is there.
    It is what the model prescribes, but the model assumes you trade instantly and for free at the quoted price. When the market knows what you hold, a sale that big moves prices against you and deepens the next mark.

What happened. Long-Term Capital Management, where Merton himself was a partner, could not rebalance at quoted prices. Its spreads widened together, lenders demanded more collateral, and because the market knew roughly what it held, every sale moved prices against it. By late September it had lost about $4.6 billion. On 23 September 1998 the New York Fed brought together fourteen banks and brokers, who put in about $3.6 billion to take over the portfolio and wind it down. Many of the trades converged later, after the fund could no longer hold them.

Section 05

Key Takeaways

✓Merton generalizes Kelly, it doesn't compete with it. Kelly's \(f^*=\mu/\sigma^2\) is the exact \(\gamma=1\) special case of Merton's \(f^*=\mu/(\gamma\sigma^2)\) — a literal algebraic identity, not an approximation.
✓The HJB equation solves for a whole function, not a number. \(J(W,t)\) is the best-achievable utility from wealth \(W\) at time \(t\) — the "GPS" that lets today's decision be made without re-solving the entire future.
✓For CRRA utility, the optimal allocation is a constant fraction. \(f^*=\mu/(\gamma\sigma^2)\) doesn't depend on current wealth or time remaining — a strong, CRRA-specific result, often called the Merton fraction.
✓Fractional Kelly is a mathematically exact optimum, not a discount. An investor with \(\gamma=2\) betting half-Kelly isn't being cautious relative to "the" right answer — half-Kelly is their own personal, exact optimum.
⚠γ is elicited, not observed. Just like \(\mu\) and \(\sigma^2\) are estimates, an investor's risk aversion must be inferred indirectly (surveys, revealed behavior, risk-premia calibration) — plugging in an over-precise γ overstates confidence in \(f^*\).
⚠The clean constant-fraction result assumes away real frictions. Transaction costs turn the optimal policy into a "no-trade region"; time-varying \(\mu,\sigma\) (regimes, jumps) mean the "optimal constant" is only as stable as the return distribution actually is.
⚠Nothing here is built or validated in this repo. This lesson closes a theoretical loop; a sizing layer only ever gets applied on top of a signal that has already proven itself on real OOS Sharpe with a non-trivial trade count.
✗Above \(f=1\), the constant fraction sells into losses, and the sale scales with \(f-1\) (LTCM, 1998). At roughly 25 to 1, a 1% fall in the positions demands selling about a quarter of the book; after Russia's default that rebalancing could not be done at quoted prices. Sizing by the Merton fraction is untested on this desk.
Section 06

Self-test

Q1. Why does Merton's formula \(f^*=\mu/(\gamma\sigma^2)\) reduce exactly to Kelly's \(f^*=\mu/\sigma^2\) when \(\gamma=1\), rather than just approximately?
Setting \(\gamma=1\) in \(f^*=\mu/(\gamma\sigma^2)\) is a direct algebraic substitution: \(\mu/(1\cdot\sigma^2)=\mu/\sigma^2\), the exact Kelly formula, term for term. This is not a coincidence of similar formulas — \(\gamma=1\) in the CRRA family corresponds exactly to log utility, \(U(c)=\ln c\), and maximizing expected log-wealth (Kelly's entire objective, proven by Kelly in 1956 to maximize long-run compounded growth) is identical, as an optimization problem, to maximizing expected utility under \(U(\cdot)=\ln(\cdot)\). Kelly's criterion and Merton's expected-utility framework are the same theory; \(\gamma=1\) is simply where they meet.
Q2. What does the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation actually solve for, conceptually, and why does this problem need it instead of ordinary calculus?
The HJB equation solves for the value function \(J(W,t)\) — the best-achievable expected (discounted) utility obtainable from wealth \(W\) at time \(t\), assuming every future decision is made optimally. Ordinary calculus (as used in the Kelly lesson) finds the maximum of a single fixed function of one variable. Here, the quantity being maximized at every instant depends on how valuable every possible future wealth level will turn out to be — which is exactly what \(J\) encodes — so the "maximize" step and the "figure out the value of the future" step have to be solved together, as one differential equation for an entire function, rather than as a single derivative-equals-zero calculation.
Q3. Why is \(f^*=\mu/(\gamma\sigma^2)\) a constant fraction that doesn't depend on wealth or time remaining, and is that a general property of all utility functions?
It is a specific consequence of choosing constant relative risk aversion (CRRA) utility — solving the HJB equation with a CRRA guess for \(J(W,t)\) causes the wealth- and time-dependent terms to cancel out of the risk-tolerance ratio \(-J_W/(WJ_{WW})\), leaving a pure constant, \(1/\gamma\). It is not a general property of expected-utility maximization — other utility functions (e.g. those with decreasing rather than constant relative risk aversion) produce optimal allocations that do vary with wealth and/or the time remaining to the horizon.
Q4. Two investors have the same edge (μ, σ) but genuinely different risk aversion, γ=1 and γ=2. Is one of them "wrong" to bet less?
No — under Merton's framework each is doing the mathematically optimal thing for their own, honestly-stated risk aversion. The γ=2 investor's optimal fraction is exactly half the γ=1 investor's (f* scales as 1/γ), not a discount applied to a single "correct" answer that only the γ=1 investor achieves. Full Kelly (γ=1) is optimal only for an investor whose true risk aversion genuinely is 1; for anyone more risk-averse than that, betting less than full Kelly is what "optimal" means, not a compromise away from it.
Q5. Name two assumptions behind Merton's classic constant-fraction result that don't hold in real markets, and what breaks when they're relaxed.
(1) Frictionless, continuous rebalancing at no cost — with real transaction costs, the optimal policy becomes a "no-trade region" around a target weight rather than a single constant fraction rebalanced continuously. (2) Constant μ and σ through time — real markets have volatility clustering, regime shifts, and occasional jumps (exactly what the Merton Jump-Diffusion lesson addresses), so the "optimal constant" is only as stable as the underlying return distribution actually is, which in practice it is not.
Q6. Why does this lesson call γ (risk aversion) an "estimation problem" in the same way the Kelly lesson called μ and σ² estimation problems?
Just as a backtested μ and σ² are estimates of a true, unobservable edge and variance — with real sampling error — an investor's γ is not directly observable either. It must be elicited through indirect means (questionnaires, revealed behavior under real losses, or calibration to market risk premia), and these methods routinely disagree. Plugging a falsely precise γ into f*=μ/(γσ²) produces an overconfident bet size in exactly the same way plugging an inflated backtested μ into the Kelly formula does — the formula is only as honest as its least honest input.

  • Robert C. Merton, "Optimum Consumption and Portfolio Rules in a Continuous-Time Model", Journal of Economic Theory, 1971 (erratum 1973) — the fuller, more rigorous treatment of the same problem, including the HJB derivation in detail and extensions beyond the single-risky-asset case.
