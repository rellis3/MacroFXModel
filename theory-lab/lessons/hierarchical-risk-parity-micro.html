Hierarchical Risk Parity, Without Ever Inverting the Matrix

Both Markowitz optimization and the plain Equal Risk Contribution approach from the previous lesson lean on the full covariance matrix, and in most implementations that means inverting it. Inversion is a numerically dangerous operation once assets get highly correlated — small estimation noise gets amplified into wild, unstable weights. Hierarchical Risk Parity's answer is almost mischievously simple: cluster the assets by similarity first, using the same correlation-distance idea the correlation-networks/MST lesson introduced, then allocate capital by splitting the risk budget down the resulting tree — never touching a full-matrix inversion at any step.

Section 1

A second, purely mechanical fragility

The Markowitz lesson flagged fragile expected-return inputs. There's a separate problem sitting underneath, in the matrix algebra itself.

Σ⁻¹

Both approaches need the inverse

Mean-variance optimization and traditional risk-parity/ERC solves both work with the full covariance matrix Σ, and most numerical implementations touch an inversion (or an equivalent operation) somewhere in the solve.

⚠

Inversion gets dangerous near-singular

As a matrix's rows become close to linear combinations of each other, inverting it amplifies small estimation errors into large, unstable results — a well-known numerical-analysis fact, not something specific to finance.

📈

High correlation pushes toward that regime

A basket of assets that mostly share one dominant factor — several FX pairs riding the same dollar or risk-on/risk-off move — produces near-duplicate rows in Σ. More assets, or more extreme correlations, both push the matrix closer to singular.

🗑

Garbage in, garbage amplified out

Covariance is always estimated from a finite, noisy sample. Feed that noisy estimate through a near-singular inversion and the noise doesn't stay small — it gets magnified into large, arbitrary-looking weight swings between re-estimation periods.

This is a second, independent source of Markowitz-style brittleness — one that shows up even if expected returns were somehow known perfectly. It comes from the matrix algebra itself, not from the forecasting problem.
Section 2

The company-budget intuition

Allocate by employee, all at once
  • Account for how every person's travel co-varies with every other person's, simultaneously
  • Gets more fragile the more employees share the same role — near-duplicate patterns look linearly dependent to the math
  • One giant, all-at-once, fragile inversion
Allocate by department, then team, then person
  • Split the budget across departments first, then within each department across teams, then individuals
  • Every step is a small, well-behaved decision — "more to this half or that half"
  • Never one large matrix inversion — a cascade of small two-way splits

HRP applies exactly this idea to a correlation matrix: cluster similar assets into a tree first, then allocate top-down, one well-conditioned split at a time. It never needs to solve for every asset's weight simultaneously against every other asset's — which is exactly the step that requires inverting the full matrix.

Connects to an earlier lesson

The clustering half of this idea isn't new here — the correlation-networks / minimum-spanning-tree lesson already showed how to turn a correlation matrix into a graph of "distances" and group assets by similarity. HRP borrows that same distance transform, but feeds it into a hierarchical clustering tree (a dendrogram) instead of a pruned network, then uses that tree to allocate weight rather than just to visualize structure.

Correlation runs the wrong direction for clustering — bigger means more alike, not less. The distance transform flips it: identical assets (ρ=1) land at distance 0, perfectly anti-correlated ones (ρ=−1) land at distance 1. Feed those distances into standard agglomerative clustering — repeatedly merge the two closest clusters — and the result is a tree (dendrogram) whose leaves are assets and whose branches are merges.

Reorder the covariance matrix's rows and columns to match the dendrogram's leaf order (the order the assets naturally line up along the bottom of the tree). No covariance value changes — this only re-seats the assets so similar ones sit adjacent. The effect: large correlations bunch into contiguous blocks near the diagonal instead of being scattered arbitrarily. That block structure is what makes the next stage tractable.

Starting at the tree's root, split each cluster's current weight between its two immediate sub-clusters in inverse proportion to each side's own variance: α₁ = 1 − V₁/(V₁+V₂). The lower-variance side always gets the bigger share. Recurse down both branches to individual assets — every split is a small, two-number calculation, never a full-matrix inversion.

None of the three stages, individually, ever requires inverting the full N×N covariance matrix — only small, well-conditioned sub-problems at every step.

Section 4

The dendrogram: two blocs, revealed by distance

Same 5-pair basket, single-linkage clustering on correlation-distance. Lower merge height = higher correlation.

Hand-built SVG — no chart library
Quasi-diagonalizing means reading the covariance matrix's rows/columns in exactly this leaf order — EURUSD, GBPUSD, AUDUSD, USDJPY, USDCHF — so the two blocs form visible, contiguous blocks. Recursive bisection then walks the tree from the root (purple, highest distance) down to the leaves, splitting weight at every branch.

EURUSD/GBPUSD merge first (most correlated), then USDJPY/USDCHF, then AUDUSD joins the first pair to form a "risk-on" bloc; the risk-on bloc and the "safe-haven" bloc only merge at the root — the point of lowest cross-bloc correlation. No one had to hand-label these groups; the clustering discovered them from correlation alone, the same way the MST lesson's network revealed structure without hand-labeling.

Section 7

Why this tends to be more robust

Mean-variance / plain ERC
  • Needs Σ⁻¹ (or an equivalent full-matrix solve) at some point
  • Degrades catastrophically as correlation rises toward the singular regime
  • Small covariance-estimation noise can produce large, unstable weight swings
  • Weights can concentrate heavily if the optimizer exploits near-collinear assets
Hierarchical Risk Parity
  • Every calculation is a small sub-cluster's inverse-variance weighting, or a one-number split between two already-computed variances
  • Degrades gracefully as correlation rises — clustering just groups similar assets more tightly, it doesn't blow up
  • No full-matrix inversion anywhere in the pipeline, at any of the three stages
  • Weights tend to be less extreme, spread more evenly across correlated clusters
Marcos López de Prado, HRP's originator, reports it as more robust out-of-sample than mean-variance optimization specifically because of this ill-conditioning avoidance — a real, peer-reviewed result, stated here as the source's own claim.
Where this needs qualifying

Avoiding matrix inversion instability does not avoid correlation estimation instability — the dendrogram, the leaf ordering, and every split still depend on a correlation matrix estimated from a finite, noisy sample, and can look different on the next re-estimation window. HRP also has no mechanism for expressing a view that one asset's expected return is better than another's — like plain risk parity, it allocates purely on variance and correlation structure, all the way down the tree. And the dendrogram itself is not unique: single, complete, average, and Ward linkage can each produce a meaningfully different tree — and therefore different final weights — from the identical correlation matrix.

Section 8

A 12-pair basket where this actually matters

Consider a G10 basket with commodity-bloc crosses (AUD, NZD, CAD) that move together on risk sentiment, and a separate safe-haven bloc (JPY, CHF) that tends to move the other way — plus European crosses somewhere in between.

⚠

Mean-variance's problem

Several commodity-bloc pairs carry almost the same signal — near-duplicate rows in the 12×12 covariance matrix, exactly the ill-conditioned structure that makes Σ⁻¹ numerically unreliable and prone to wild weight swings from tiny estimation noise.

✓

HRP's handling

The commodity-bloc and safe-haven clusters get discovered automatically from the correlation-distance dendrogram — no one hand-labels which pairs "belong" together.

✂

Then it's splits, all the way down

How much to the commodity bloc as a whole vs. the safe-haven bloc, then how much to each pair within its own bloc — every decision a small, two-way split, never one fragile 12×12 inversion.

This is the practical case HRP is built for: large, highly correlated universes — broad equity universes, credit baskets, or an FX universe with several currencies sharing common blocs — where a literal matrix inversion is either numerically unreliable or produces weights that swing dramatically between re-estimation periods.

Section 9

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Why does mean-variance optimization become more numerically fragile as assets become more correlated — even setting expected returns aside entirely?

Highly correlated assets produce near-duplicate rows in the covariance matrix, pushing it toward the ill-conditioned, near-singular regime where inversion turns small noise into large, unstable weight changes.
Question 2

What does quasi-diagonalization actually change?

It's a re-seating, not a re-calculation — no covariance value changes, but similar assets end up adjacent, pushing large correlations into contiguous diagonal blocks.
Question 3

In α₁ = 1 − V₁/(V₁+V₂), which cluster gets the larger share of the parent's weight?

If V₁ is small relative to V₂, the ratio V₁/(V₁+V₂) is small, so α₁ = 1 − (small number) is close to 1. The lower-variance side is always favored, regardless of how many members it has.
Question 4

Does HRP ever invert the full N×N covariance matrix?

All three stages — clustering, reordering, recursive bisection — avoid full-matrix inversion. That's the entire point of the algorithm.
Question 5

Is the dendrogram HRP produces from a given correlation matrix unique?

The choice of linkage method changes the tree, and therefore the final weights — "the" clustering is not unique, even from an identical correlation matrix.
Section 10

Not built here — worth being explicit about

Original lesson's claim, checked against current source

The full lesson states this repo has "no clustering-based allocator built or tested" — theory only. A repo-wide search for hierarchical-clustering-based portfolio allocation code (dendrograms, quasi-diagonalization, recursive bisection, scipy.cluster / AgglomerativeClustering usage tied to weight construction) turned up nothing outside this lesson and the correlation-networks/MST lesson's own text. That claim still checks out as accurate — nothing has been silently built or silently changed since the full lesson was written.

Every strategy actually running in this codebase is single-instrument. A clustering-based allocator like HRP would be genuinely new infrastructure — worth understanding conceptually now, since it addresses a real and well-documented weakness of both Markowitz and plain risk-parity approaches, but not something to assume is available today.

Section 11

What to carry forward

01

Inverting a large covariance matrix gets numerically fragile as assets grow more numerous or more correlated — a mechanical fragility separate from and additional to expected-return risk.

02

HRP's workaround is to never invert the full matrix at all — cluster by correlation-distance first, then allocate down the resulting tree.

03

Three stages: tree clustering (dᵢⱼ = √(½(1−ρᵢⱼ)) feeds agglomerative clustering), quasi-diagonalization (reorder by leaf order, no values change), recursive bisection (split weight in half at each branch, inversely by variance).

04

The lower-variance side of every split always gets the larger share — the same risk-parity intuition from the previous lesson, applied one binary split at a time.

05

The dendrogram borrows its distance metric directly from the correlation-networks/MST lesson, but uses it to build an allocation tree rather than a pruned network.

06

Not a free lunch: linkage choice changes the tree, correlation estimates are still noisy, and expected returns still play no role — and this is unbuilt, unvalidated theory in this repo today.

What this now lets you do

Recognize why a large, highly correlated basket makes traditional mean-variance or ERC weights unstable, and explain the mechanism — near-singular Σ, amplified estimation noise — precisely. Walk through HRP's three stages on paper for a small basket, and know why "cluster first, allocate down the tree" sidesteps the inversion problem entirely rather than just papering over it.
