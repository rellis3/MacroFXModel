Deeper Foundations 5

Limits, Integrals & the Annuity Factor

Every fixed-income formula in this curriculum — duration, DV01, the annuity factor, forward rates off a yield curve — is a limit, a derivative, or an integral wearing a trading-desk name. This deck formalizes the one idea underneath a derivative and an integral, then uses it to derive the annuity factor from scratch and to show, algebraically, why duration and DV01 aren't separate formulas to memorize but this exact same calculus, applied.

The whole idea
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

A lesson on limits and integrals. I trade rates. Why should I care?

Because the numbers on a rates risk screen are these ideas wearing trading names. Duration is a derivative. A perpetuity is a limit. Continuous discounting is an integral.

Start with the limit.

A limit is where something is heading as you push an input closer and closer to a target, even if it never arrives. Walk half the remaining distance to a door, again and again. You never touch it, but you get as close as anyone likes.

And the derivative?

Shrink the gap between two yields towards zero and watch the average price change per unit of yield settle down. Where it settles is the slope of the price curve. Flip the sign and divide by the price, and you have modified duration.

Give me a number.

Take one dollar a year for five years, at four percent. Add up the five discount factors and you get about four dollars forty-five. Its modified duration comes out at about two point eight one years. Bump the yield ten basis points each way, reprice, and you get the same answer to four decimal places.

So the formula and the bump agree.

They have to. The bump is the same limit with a small gap instead of an infinitely small one. That is how desks get duration for callable bonds that have no clean formula: bump the curve, reprice, divide.

Where does the annuity factor itself come from?

It is a geometric series. Each payment is worth the one before it, divided by one plus the yield. Multiply the whole sum by one plus the yield, subtract the original, and almost everything cancels. What is left is one minus the last discount factor, divided by the yield.

And the perpetuity?

Let the payments run forever. The last discount factor shrinks to nothing, so the value heads to one over the yield. At five percent, a dollar a year forever is worth twenty dollars today. Eighty years of payments already gets you to nineteen sixty.

That sounds like old maths.

Very old. Johan de Witt valued Dutch government life annuities as sums of discounted payments in 1671. The same sum now sets par swap rates and a swap's sensitivity to its fixed rate.

Any trap?

Duration is a straight-line approximation. For a small move it is accurate. For a big move the price curve bends away from it, and you need convexity.

So the one-line version?

Duration is a derivative, a perpetuity is a limit, and the annuity factor is a geometric series you can derive in three lines.

Section 2

Getting arbitrarily close, without ever needing to arrive

A limit is a genuinely honest idea, even stated in plain English.

A limit describes what a quantity approaches as some input is pushed arbitrarily close to a target — without that input ever needing to actually reach the target itself. Written formally: limx→a f(x) = L.
→

x → a

The input is being pushed toward a target value a — it can get closer than any distance you name, without necessarily ever landing on a exactly.

L

The value being approached

L is the single number f(x) settles arbitrarily close to as x closes in on a. The fully rigorous "epsilon-delta" version makes "arbitrarily close" precise; the plain-English reading is enough for everything below.

✓

Why bother formalizing it

Because the derivative and the integral — the two tools duration, DV01, and the annuity factor are all built from — are both just this same idea, applied to a ratio in one case and a sum in the other.

Two fixed-income pictures use exactly this idea, and both recur for the rest of this deck. Duration is "how fast a bond's price wiggles for a tiny wiggle in yield" — the value that gets approached as that wiggle shrinks to nothing. The annuity factor is "what a stream of $1-per-period payments is worth today" — a sum that, in the perpetuity case, gets pushed to literally infinitely many terms. Same idea, two different directions.

Section 3

The derivative, revisited: a limit in disguise

Calculus for Finance already built this. Here it is again, because everything else in this deck leans on it.

f(x+h) − f(x)
÷ h, then h → 0
f '(x)

f '(x) = limh→0 [f(x+h) − f(x)] / h

h

A shrinking gap

h is the input being pushed toward the target — here the target is 0, not some other number. h never needs to actually equal zero.

÷

The ratio being tracked

[f(x+h) − f(x)] / h is the average rate of change of f over the gap h — an honest number at every h, but not yet the derivative.

L

What the ratio approaches

As h shrinks toward zero, that average rate of change settles on a single value — the instantaneous rate of change, f '(x), at exactly the point x.

Where this is headed

Swap the generic f(x) for a bond's price as a function of yield, P(y), and this exact ratio-shrinking-toward-an-instant pattern becomes duration two sections from now — not a new idea, the same one, aimed at a formula this curriculum already uses.

Section 5

From discrete coupons to a continuous trickle of cash

The ordinary present-value sum is already a sum of discrete slices — one cash flow per coupon date.

Imagine a continuum of infinitesimal cash flows CF(t) dt arriving at every instant rather than on discrete coupon dates, each discounted continuously at rate r. Letting the gap between payment dates shrink to zero turns the discrete sum into exactly the integral from the previous slide:

P = Σₜ CFₜ / (1+r)ᵗ
gap → 0
PV = ∫₀ᵀ CF(t) e⁻ʳᵗ dt

Nothing about that integral is exotic once the discrete sum is already familiar — it's the identical accumulation idea, applied to a continuum of cash flows instead of a handful of discrete ones. This exact parallel gets reused two sections from now, when the annuity factor's own continuous-compounding limit turns out to be a special case of this same integral.

Section 6

Summing a geometric series of discounted cash flows

An annuity factor is the present value of a $1-per-period stream. Step through how its formula is actually derived — not asserted.

Define it as a finite geometric series with n terms and common ratio x = (1+y)⁻¹:

A(n,y) = Σt=1n (1+y)⁻ᵗ = x + x² + ⋯ + xⁿ

Same "multiply, subtract, solve" trick used to sum any finite geometric series — like weighing a pile of ever-smaller chocolate bars by comparing the pile to a slightly shifted copy of itself instead of stacking every bar on a scale.

Differentiating A(n,y) itself (via the quotient rule) gives Dmod = 1/y − n / [(1+y)((1+y)ⁿ−1)] — the same differentiation move used on a bond's price, aimed at the annuity factor directly. Duration isn't a separately memorized formula; it's this closed form, differentiated.

Section 8

Two limits hiding inside one formula

A(n,y) = [1 − (1+y)⁻ⁿ] / y has exactly two limits worth taking — two different things pushed toward infinity.

n → ∞ — the perpetuity limit

For y > 0, (1+y)⁻ⁿ → 0 as n → ∞ — raising a number greater than 1 to an ever-larger power sends its reciprocal to zero. So A(n,y) → 1/y. A stream of $1 payments running forever is worth exactly 1/y today.

m → ∞ within a period — continuous compounding

Compounding m times within a period sends each discrete discount factor (1+y)⁻ᵗ to e⁻ʸᵗ, giving Σ e⁻ʸᵗ. Spread the $1 per period continuously through each period as well, and the sum becomes the continuous-time integral ∫₀ⁿ e⁻ʸᵗ dt = [1 − e⁻ʸⁿ] / y — the same shape, (1+y)⁻ⁿ simply replaced by e⁻ʸⁿ.

Notice the structural echo: the continuous annuity integral above is just the general continuous-time PV integral from Section 5, PV = ∫₀ᵀ CF(t) e⁻ʳᵗ dt, with the constant cash-flow rate CF(t) = 1 plugged in. The derivative behind duration and the integral behind continuous discounting aren't separate ideas colliding by coincidence here — the annuity factor's continuous limit is a special case of that same integral.
Section 9

Duration: literally a derivative

Differentiate the bond-pricing formula with respect to yield, and duration falls out with nothing new added.

P(y) = Σₜ CFₜ/(1+y)ᵗ
d/dy
dP/dy = −Σₜ t·CFₜ/(1+y)ᵗ⁺¹
dP/dy

The raw derivative

Always negative for a plain bond — every term is a positive number with a minus sign in front, the algebraic reason price and yield move in opposite directions.

−1/P

Flip the sign, normalize by price

Turns "dollars per unit yield" into a positive, price-independent sensitivity that practitioners can quote and compare across bonds.

Dmod

Modified duration

Dmod = −(1/P)(dP/dy) — the quoted number, positive, in years, directly a derivative.

Worked example: a 2-year, $1,000 face bond with a 5% annual coupon, priced at a 5% yield (a par bond, P = $1,000 exactly). dP/dy = −[(1×50)/(1.05)² + (2×1,050)/(1.05)³] = −[45.35 + 1,814.06] = −1,859.41. So Dmod = −(1/1,000)×(−1,859.41) = 1.8594 years — every input already sat inside the pricing formula; nothing new was invented.

Duration answers one question: if the yield wiggled by a tiny amount, how much would the bond's price wiggle in response? It's a speedometer reading — the "road" is yield, the "distance traveled" is price. Differentiating the pricing formula is reading that speedometer directly off the math instead of guessing.

Section 11

Every fixed-income formula, wearing a trading-desk name

Duration, convexity, DV01, the annuity factor itself, and forward-rate extraction from a yield curve — every one of them is a limit, a derivative, or an integral, just dressed up in trading-desk vocabulary. Even the instantaneous forward rate, f(t) = d/dt[t·r(t)] = r(t) + t·r'(t), is nothing but the derivative of a fitted yield curve's cumulative yield.

Duration and DV01 are almost never hand-differentiated in production. Most real instruments — callable bonds, mortgage-backed securities, structured notes — have no clean closed-form price to differentiate. Practitioners instead compute effective duration numerically: bump the yield curve up, reprice; bump it down, reprice; take the finite difference — literally the same central-difference check used earlier on the annuity factor, applied where no closed form exists to check it against.

Deff ≈ −(1/P) × [P(y+ε) − P(y−ε)] / (2ε)

Where this needs qualifying

Duration is a local, first-order approximation — it degrades for large yield moves without a convexity (second-derivative) correction. Day-count and compounding conventions silently change an annuity factor's exact value for what colloquially gets called "the same bond." And Macaulay duration (a weighted-average time, in years) isn't modified duration (a price sensitivity) — they relate by Dmod = DMac / (1+y/m), never exactly 1, so swapping one for the other is never exactly correct.

Section 12

Quick check

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

In plain English, what does limx→a f(x) = L assert?

A limit describes what a quantity approaches, not a value it's required to reach — the whole point of the "arbitrarily close" language.
Question 2

Modified duration Dmod is defined as:

Differentiate bond price with respect to yield, flip the sign (dP/dy is always negative for a plain bond), then normalize by price so the quoted number is a positive, price-independent sensitivity.
Question 3

For A(n,y) = [1−(1+y)⁻ⁿ]/y with y > 0 fixed, what happens as n → ∞?

(1+y)⁻ⁿ → 0 as n grows, since raising a number greater than 1 to an ever-larger power sends its reciprocal to zero, leaving A → (1−0)/y = 1/y.
Question 4

A $10,000,000 position with modified duration 8.50 years has a DV01 of approximately:

DV01 = P × D_mod × 0.0001 = 10,000,000 × 8.50 × 0.0001 = $8,500.
Question 5

For annual compounding (m=1), how do Macaulay duration (D_Mac) and modified duration (D_mod) relate?

D_mod = D_Mac / (1+y/m); with m=1 that's D_Mac / (1+y). Treating them as interchangeable silently misstates the sensitivity by that factor.
Your call
Scenario drill · an illustrative trading day

You have just sold $10 million of 10-year notes

You make markets in 10-year Treasury notes and have just sold $10,000,000 face of a note priced near par, with a modified duration of 8.50 years. That is a DV01 of $8,500 per basis point. The 10-year future you hedge with has a DV01 of about $85 per contract. You want to go home flat on rates. What do you do?

  1. Sell 100 futures, so both positions match.
    Selling futures adds a second short. A 1bp fall in yield would now cost about $17,000 instead of nothing. A short bond is hedged with a position that gains when yields fall, which is a long future.
  2. Buy 100 futures: $8,500 ÷ $85 per contract.
    The hedge ratio is one derivative divided by another. If yields rise 1bp the short note gains $8,500 and the futures lose $8,500. If yields fall, the reverse. Net roughly flat either way.
  3. Buy 50 futures now and the rest only if yields start to fall.
    Half a hedge leaves about $4,250 per basis point open. That can be a deliberate view, but it is not going home flat, which is what you said you wanted.
  4. Buy 85 futures, one for each dollar of the contract's DV01.
    That confuses the divisor with the answer. The number of contracts is your DV01 divided by theirs, not the contract's DV01 itself. At 85 contracts you would still have $1,275 per basis point open.

What the lesson shows. This is an illustrative situation using the lesson's own numbers, not a dated event, and the $85 futures DV01 is itself an illustrative figure: real desks compute it from the cheapest-to-deliver bond. 100 contracts neutralise the first-order exposure. Because duration is a local slope, the hedge is only exact for small moves: after a large move both DV01s change, and the curvature that duration ignores is the subject of convexity.

What to carry forward

01

A limit formalizes "arbitrarily close, without needing to arrive" — every derivative and integral in this curriculum, duration and continuous discounting included, is built from exactly that one idea.

02

Duration is a derivative — D_mod = −(1/P)(dP/dy), the bond-pricing formula differentiated with respect to yield, sign-flipped and normalized by price.

03

04

Two limits sit inside that one formula — n→∞ gives the perpetuity value 1/y; compounding frequency m→∞ recovers continuous compounding and connects directly to e⁻ʳᵗ.

05

06

What this now lets you do

Differentiate any pricing formula to get its sensitivity directly instead of quoting duration from memory. Derive an annuity factor — and its perpetuity and continuous-compounding limits — from the geometric series instead of taking it on faith. Size a DV01 hedge from first principles. And recognize forward rates, convexity, and every other fixed-income "Greek" as the same handful of limit ideas, wearing different trading-desk names.
