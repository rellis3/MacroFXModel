Mood, the Quote and Forced Selling

A price is where the last willing buyer met the last willing seller. Most days both are choosing. In a crisis one side stops choosing: margin calls, redemptions and risk limits turn sellers into people who must sell — at any price, whatever they can.

Section 1

The quote is a meeting point, not a verdict

The printed price is the last trade — one marginal buyer and one marginal seller, for whatever small size they traded.

p̄ = Σ pᵢqᵢ / Σ qᵢ

A sell order bigger than the best bid fills it, then the next level down, and the next — it walks the book. Your average price is the size-weighted average of every level you touched; the last fill is lower still. Impact grows roughly with the square root of size relative to normal volume, scaled by volatility.

Section 2

Four kinds of move — the fourth gets forgotten

🙂

Optimism

People buying because they like what they see.

😟

Fear

People selling because they don't.

❓

Uncertainty

Nobody knows — shows up as volatility, not direction.

⚙

Systematic

Selling because a rule or contract says so: margin call, redemption, VaR breach, vol-target cut. Ask "was this forced?"

Section 3

Voluntary versus forced

Voluntary seller
  • Sells because their view changed
  • Has a floor price — below it, waits
  • Chooses what to sell
  • Can spread it over days
Forced seller
  • Sells because a rule says so
  • No floor price
  • Sells what turns into cash fastest — the liquid, the profitable, the safe
  • Must sell now
Forced sellers sell what they can, not what they want. That is why unrelated assets fall together — they share a holder raising cash — and why "correlations go to one".
Section 4

Margin calls: a 10% fall can force a 44% sale

Equity E = A − L. To get back to the lender's maximum leverage λ, sell S = A′ − λ·E′.

Try it
Loan = 400 · equity before = 100
After the fall: A′ = 450 · E′ = 50 · leverage = 9.0×
Forced sale S
200
Share of holdings
44%
Lenders also tend to cut λ when markets get volatile, which makes the required sale bigger exactly when selling is most costly.
Section 5

The dealer-inventory spiral

Q_max = VaR limit / (z·σ·√h)

Same risk limit, double the volatility → a dealer may hold half the inventory. In a dealer-run market like government bonds, that is a direct cut in how much selling the market can absorb.

ShockBond vol rises
→
DealersForced to hold less inventory
→
DealersSmaller size, wider spreads
→
OutcomeEach sale moves price further; vol rises again

Vol-targeting funds add a second loop: exposure w = σ*/σ̂, so when measured vol doubles, they halve exposure — mechanically, all at once.

Section 6

Walk the book — forced vs voluntary

Toy book: bid level i holds (20 + 12i)/m units at vol multiple m; at higher vol the top round(m)−1 levels are pulled.

300 units at 1×: last fill −30bp. Same 300 at 2×: −50bp. Voluntary seller at 2×: sells 140 and waits. The forced seller can't wait.
Section 7 · It actually happened

March 2020: the dash for cash

ShockCOVID-19: everyone needs cash at once
→
Funds, foreign holders, levered RV fundsSell the most liquid asset: Treasuries
→
DealersBalance sheets full; liquidity drains
→
15 & 23 MarchFed cuts to near zero, buys Treasuries without limit

For several days in mid-March, Treasury yields rose while stocks fell — the world's safe asset sold because it was the easiest thing to sell. Even gold fell for a stretch. The scramble subsided once a buyer with no risk limit arrived.

Section 8 · It actually happened

March 2021: Archegos and the race to sell first

A family office built huge, concentrated positions — ViacomCBS and several US-listed Chinese companies among them — through total return swaps at several prime brokers, so no bank saw the whole thing. ViacomCBS fell after announcing a share sale in the week of 22 March; Archegos missed margin calls; on Friday 26 March banks sold large blocks regardless of price.

Sold early

Better prices, smaller losses.

Sold late

Walked a book others had already emptied. Credit Suisse later reported a loss of about $5.5 billion; Nomura close to $3 billion.

Section 10

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Why can selling a large block average well below the quoted price?

The average fill is Σpᵢqᵢ/Σqᵢ over every level touched — below the best bid, with the last fill lower still.
Question 2

A dealer's VaR limit is fixed and volatility triples. Their maximum inventory…

Q_max = limit / (zσ√h), so it scales as 1/σ. Less inventory means a thinner book, bigger moves, and more volatility.
Question 3

Why did Treasuries fall alongside stocks in mid-March 2020?

Redemptions, foreign dollar needs and leveraged unwinds all raised cash from the most liquid asset. The selling was about who held Treasuries, not about Treasuries.
Question 4

Every index closes down today. What does this desk's evidence say about next week?

breadth-all-down: SPX500 +0.31 ATR, NAS100 +0.26 on next-5-session range. Five sessions later the market was up 58–61% of the time — the ordinary drift.
Section 11

What to carry forward

01

A quote is where the marginal buyer and seller met, for a small size. Big orders walk the book.

02

Add "systematic" to fear, optimism and uncertainty: margin calls, redemptions, VaR limits and vol targets force price-insensitive selling.

03

Leverage multiplies it: at 5×, a 10% fall forces a 44% sale. Higher vol shrinks dealer inventory as 1/σ, thinning the book.

04

Forced sellers sell what they can — Treasuries and gold fell with stocks in March 2020. Correlations go to one.

05

Validated here: an all-down day precedes a wider week (range, not direction). Tested null: retail squeeze fuel; fear-bid gold. Untested: leader leads the decline.
