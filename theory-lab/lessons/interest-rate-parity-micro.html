Interest Rate Parity, the No-Arbitrage Price of a Forward

A forward exchange rate isn't a forecast — it's an accounting identity, pinned down today by today's spot rate and the two currencies' interest rates, and enforced by the same riskless arbitrage that keeps a bond's price tied to its yield. This deck builds that identity from the two-route argument that forces it to be true, then shows what a genuine gap from it actually means.

Both routes start with $1 and end fully riskless at T. If the two payoffs weren't equal, a desk could borrow via the cheap route and invest via the rich one and pocket the gap with zero capital and zero risk — so trading forces them equal.
This is the entire argument behind covered interest rate parity. Nobody's expectations enter it anywhere — only two riskless routes forced to pay the same.

Think of an interest rate as the rental price of money. If euros rent cheaper than dollars, converting dollars to euros, earning the cheap rent, and converting back has to come with a correspondingly worse exchange rate on the way back — or the round trip would print riskless money out of nothing.

Section 2

The identity itself

Rearranging the no-arbitrage condition from the last slide gives the covered interest rate parity (CIP) formula.

F/S = (1+r_d)/(1+r_f)   ⟹   F = S × (1+r_d)/(1+r_f)
F

Forward rate

Agreed today for delivery at time T. Fully pinned down by the three quantities below — no belief or forecast required anywhere in the formula.

S

Spot rate

Today's observable exchange rate, quoted as domestic currency per one unit of foreign currency (e.g. USD per EUR).

r_d

Domestic rate

The domestic risk-free rate for the period T — e.g. the USD money-market rate.

r_f

Foreign rate

The foreign risk-free rate for the same period T — e.g. the EUR money-market rate.

With continuously compounded annual rates the same identity is written exactly as F = S·e^((r_d−r_f)T). Either form says the same thing: the forward is the spot, adjusted for the fact that money rents at different prices in the two currencies.

Not a forecast

F is completely determined today by S, r_d, and r_f — three quantities you can look up right now. It says nothing about what anyone expects the spot rate to actually be at T. That's a different question (uncovered interest rate parity), covered in the full lesson, and it is nowhere near as reliable as this identity.

Rates equal — the forward sits right on top of spot. Neither currency carries a forward premium or discount.
The beginner trap

It feels like the higher-yielding currency should be the one that gets more expensive forward — after all, it's the "better" one to hold. It's the opposite: when r_d > r_f, F/S > 1 means the foreign (lower-yielding) currency is priced at a premium, while the higher-yielding domestic currency trades at a forward discount. This falls straight out of the arbitrage argument — it isn't a judgment about either currency's quality, and it says nothing yet about where spot actually ends up later.

Section 4

Worked example — pricing the fair forward

USD domestic, EUR foreign. Spot S = 1.10, one-year r_d = 5.00%, one-year r_f = 3.00%.

Step by step
Ratio of growth factors: (1 + 0.05) / (1 + 0.03) = 1.019417
Fair forward: F* = 1.10 × 1.019417 ≈ 1.1214 USD/EUR
Forward premium on EUR: 1.1214 / 1.10 − 1 ≈ 1.94%
EUR — the lower-yielding currency — is priced forward at a premium (1.1214 > 1.10), exactly as Section 3 predicts. The 1.94% premium sits close to, but slightly under, the 2.00-point rate gap — the small gap between them is the compounding term. For small rates, F/S − 1 ≈ r_d − r_f is a handy rule of thumb.

Nothing here is a guess about the future — S, r_d, and r_f are all observable today, and F* is just arithmetic on those three numbers.

Section 5

Worked example — forcing a mispriced forward back into line

Suppose the market forward were quoted at 1.15 instead of the fair 1.1214 — deliberately exaggerated so the mechanics are easy to see.

The arbitrage, step by step
Borrow $1,000,000 at 5%: owe $1,050,000 in a year
Convert to EUR at spot 1.10: €909,090.91
Invest at 3%: €909,090.91 × 1.03 = €936,363.64
Sell forward at the mispriced 1.15: €936,363.64 × 1.15 = $1,076,818.18
Repay the $1,050,000 loan → pocket $26,818.18, riskless, from zero net capital
That is precisely the trade real desks run the instant F drifts away from F* — and precisely why it never gets anywhere near this large in liquid markets. A real G10 mispricing this size would not survive an instant; the trading itself closes the gap.

Section 7

When the identity doesn't hold exactly

Before 2008, in liquid G10 pairs under normal conditions, CIP held to within transaction costs (a few basis points) — but "almost exactly" isn't "always exactly."

Du, Tepper & Verdelhan (2018) documented persistent CIP deviations, often tens of basis points, across major currencies after 2008 — the cross-currency basis. The textbook arbitrage above assumes unlimited, cost-free balance sheet to execute the round trip; post-crisis capital and leverage-ratio rules made that balance sheet measurably more expensive for banks to supply, so a persistent gap survives, bounded by that funding cost rather than by a genuine unexploited free lunch.

Why anyone watches this

The basis is a live read on funding stress and dollar-funding scarcity in the banking system — it widens when banks' balance sheets get tighter or more expensive to deploy, which is exactly why desks and macro watchers track it as a signal in its own right, not merely as a rounding error in an otherwise-perfect formula.

Section 8

How this actually gets used

🏦

Covered interest arbitrage

Bank trading desks and cross-currency basis swap markets trade the small but real deviations from CIP directly — closer to relative value on regulatory funding costs than a directional currency bet.

📐

Forward points, quoted daily

FX desks quote forwards as "points" added to spot rather than an outright rate — the market's day-to-day expression of exactly this identity, updated as rates and spot move.

🧭

Rate-differential reasoning

"The dollar is strong because the Fed is hiking" is the same interest-differential intuition CIP formalizes, whether or not the speaker ever writes the equation down.

🎯

Not the same as the carry trade

The FX carry trade removes the forward hedge entirely and bets on where spot actually lands — a genuinely different, risk-bearing claim (uncovered interest rate parity) covered in the full lesson.

Reality check on this repo

The repo does have a carry signal: js/carryEngine.js is a real, unit-tested FX carry factor — inverse-vol-sized, rebalanced, costed, with its own IS/OOS split — wired to a live /api/fx-carry endpoint in server.js and used as a leg in the multi-factor blend. What still isn't built here is a standalone CIP forward-pricing tool or a cross-currency-basis monitor — this deck's calculator, not repo code, is what prices the identity itself. So: a carry signal exists and is tested; a CIP/basis tool does not.

Section 9 · It actually happened

Autumn 2008: the week the identity broke

It actually happened

After Lehman failed on 15 September 2008, banks stopped lending unsecured dollars to each other. Non-US banks still had dollar assets to fund, so they all raised dollars through FX swaps at once. The banks that would normally arbitrage the gap were short of dollars and balance sheet too. The dollar rate implied by swaps rose well above the cash rate, and CIP deviations in EUR/USD and USD/JPY ran many times wider than any normal cost band. The gap narrowed only after the Fed made its dollar swap lines with the ECB, BoE, SNB and BoJ unlimited in mid-October. In the same weeks the yen carry trade crashed: AUD/JPY lost something like 40–50% from its mid-2008 high.

Section 10

Where the textbook breaks, and what this desk found

The tempting claim: UIP fails, so holding the higher-yielder pays.

Desk test: too small to tell fx-factor-book-v2: the carry basket made +0.61 out of sample, which is 1.5 SE, inside the 2021-26 rate-divergence era (in-sample −0.12). Sharpe SE 0.41. Not an edge.
Desk data: base rate yen-into-yields: the carry-unwind shape, 35 episodes in 18 years, too few to score. AUD/JPY higher five sessions later 47%.
Where the textbook version breaks

CIP assumes someone can always borrow at a truly risk-free rate and has unlimited, free balance sheet to run the round trip. In 2008 neither held. UIP assumes a risk-neutral investor; the carry crash showed the "free" yield pickup is a premium for crashing in exactly the moments funding disappears.

Section 11

Quick check

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

What forces covered interest rate parity to hold?

Both routes in Section 1 start with $1 and end fully riskless at T. If they paid different amounts, a desk could lock in free money with zero capital and zero risk — and that trading itself closes any gap.
Question 2

If r_d = 6% and r_f = 2%, does the domestic currency trade forward at a premium or a discount?

F/S = (1+r_d)/(1+r_f) > 1 here, which makes the foreign (lower-yielding) currency the one at a premium — so the higher-yielding domestic currency is at a discount. This is exactly the beginner trap from Section 3.
Question 3

S = 1.20, r_d = 4%, r_f = 1%, T = 1 year. Using F ≈ S × (1 + r_d·T)/(1 + r_f·T), what's the fair one-year forward?

1.20 × (1.04/1.01) = 1.20 × 1.029703 ≈ 1.2356.
Question 4

Why doesn't a persistent cross-currency basis mean the no-arbitrage logic is wrong?

Du, Tepper & Verdelhan (2018): the identity is still the right starting model; the real-world deviation is a story about who can fund the arbitrage under post-crisis capital rules, not evidence the no-arbitrage logic itself failed.
Question 5

Is trading a genuine covered-interest-arbitrage gap the same thing as running the FX carry trade?

Covered arbitrage locks in every leg today, including the forward sale — it's enforced by no-arbitrage. The carry trade removes that forward hedge and takes an uncovered bet on where spot actually lands, which is a UIP question, not a CIP one.
Section 12

What to carry forward

01

CIP is a no-arbitrage identity, not a forecast — F = S×(1+r_d)/(1+r_f) is pinned down today by three observable prices, with nobody's expectations involved anywhere.

02

It's enforced by two routes forced to pay the same — stay domestic, or convert-invest-and-sell-forward — and any real gap between them is free, riskless money that trading closes fast.

03

The higher-yielding currency trades at a forward discount, mechanically — the opposite of the intuitive first guess, and a direct, unavoidable consequence of the arbitrage argument.

04

Forward points are the market's day-to-day quote of this exact identity — the same F − S gap this deck's calculator computes, updated live as spot and rates move.

05

Real CIP deviations are persistent, and about funding cost, not free lunches — the cross-currency basis reflects post-2008 balance-sheet constraints, and it's watched as a funding-stress signal in its own right.

06

Covered arbitrage and the carry trade are not the same claim — one is riskless and identity-enforced; the other drops the hedge and bears real, sometimes brutal, currency risk (the full lesson's subject).

07

It actually happened, autumn 2008: after Lehman, dollar funding vanished, nobody could arbitrage the gap, and CIP broke until the Fed's uncapped swap lines arrived. Carry crashed in the same weeks. The desk's carry test is UNDERPOWERED (+0.61 OOS, 1.5 SE): not an edge.

What this now lets you do

Price a fair forward rate from spot and two interest rates, and immediately recognize which currency should sit at a premium versus a discount, without guessing. Read a genuine, persistent CIP deviation as a funding-cost story rather than a broken formula. Tell covered interest arbitrage apart from the uncovered FX carry trade the moment either comes up — one is riskless, the other very much isn't.
