Judge the Decision, Not the Result

A good trade can lose and a reckless one can win. If you grade yourself on results, luck writes your rulebook. The cure is simple and unglamorous: write your reasoning down before the market answers.

Section 1

Outcome bias: "resulting"

Drive home drunk and arrive safely: good outcome, bad decision. Drive home sober and get hit by someone running a red light: bad outcome, good decision. Obvious on the road — invisible in a P&L.

The evidence

Baron and Hershey (1988): people rated the same decision, with the same information, as better when it happened to turn out well.

The name

Annie Duke, Thinking in Bets (2018), calls it resulting: treating the quality of the result as the quality of the decision.

Section 2

Two axes, four boxes

✅

Deserved success

Good decision, good outcome. Note it; don't over-learn it.

🎲

Bad luck

Good decision, bad outcome. Don't "fix" a sound process.

⚠

Dumb luck

Bad decision, good outcome. The dangerous one: it pays you to do the wrong thing.

✗

Just deserts

Bad decision, bad outcome. One of the two boxes a results-review gets right.

Section 4

Hindsight bias: "I knew it all along"

Fischhoff (1975) gave people an account of the 1814 British–Gurkha war. Those told the outcome rated it more likely — and believed they'd have predicted it — than those not told. They didn't notice their judgment had moved.

With Ruth Beyth, he asked people to estimate events around Nixon's 1972 trips to Beijing and Moscow. Afterwards, they remembered having given higher odds to what then happened.

After a loss the warning signs look obvious; after a win the logic looks inevitable. Either way, what you actually thought is gone — unless you wrote it down.
Section 5

One result tells you almost nothing

50/50 prior between a 55% trader and a 45% one. Each win multiplies the odds by 0.55/0.45 ≈ 1.22; each loss by 0.82.

Try it

One win: 50% → 55%. Seven of ten: 69%. On average a genuine 55% process needs about 147 trades to reach 95%.
Section 6

The decision journal — written before

01

What I expect — specific, checkable, with a horizon.

02

Why — the reasons, and the base rate: how often does this happen anyway?

03

How confident — a number. 60% means wrong four times in ten.

04

What would change my mind — decided now, not renegotiated later.

05

Pre-mortem — "it went badly; the most likely reason is…"

Score it later with the Brier score, BS = mean of (confidence − outcome)². 0 is perfect; always saying 50% scores 0.25. A fillable journal is in the full lesson.

Section 7

The pre-mortem, and the chain it breaks

Gary Klein's pre-mortem (HBR, 2007): before committing, imagine it has already failed and list why. It builds on "prospective hindsight" research (Mitchell, Russo & Pennington, 1989): imagining an event has happened helps people find more reasons for it.

Section 8 · It actually happened

Super Bowl XLIX, 1 February 2015

Seattle trail New England 28–24, under half a minute left, ball on the one-yard line. Everyone expects a run. Pete Carroll calls a pass; Malcolm Butler intercepts it at the goal line. The next day: "the worst call in Super Bowl history".

Duke's point

Interceptions from there are rare, an incomplete pass stops the clock and leaves time for more plays — a reasonable case exists that passing gave Seattle more chances to score. Caught, the same call would have been "clever". The decision was identical in both worlds; only the outcome changed.

Section 9 · It actually happened — on this desk

A prior written down, and beaten

Before running a test, the desk writes and commits its claim, definitions and its own expectation. In October 2026 it tested "when the Fed's Standing Repo Facility is drawn, markets get wider".

Written before

"Real, and the strongest cell here" — a wider S&P 500 range the next week.

What came back

Over 21 episodes the range was narrower than controls, and the facility is drawn on nearly a third of all sessions. Reported as a null against expectation.

No room to say "we expected that all along". That's hindsight bias beaten by a dated piece of paper.

Section 11

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

You revenge-size on a hunch and make +4R. Which box?

A rule-break that paid. The reward teaches you to repeat it — the most dangerous box.
Question 2

A signal is "right 54% of the time". What do you need before calling it an edge?

The desk's rates-pivot test: 54.1% hit rate, 54.1% base rate — zero edge.
Question 3

When should a decision-journal entry be written?

Written afterwards, it's a hindsight generator. And review winners too — that's where lucky mistakes hide.
Question 4

You said 80% on five calls; three came true. Brier score?

(3 × 0.04 + 2 × 0.64) / 5 = 0.28 — worse than always saying 50% (0.25). A hint of overconfidence, on a tiny sample.
Section 12

What to carry forward

01

Decision quality and outcome are different axes. Grade them separately.

02

Hindsight edits memory without telling you. Only a record written before beats it.

03

One result is weak evidence: ~150 trades to be 95% sure of a 55% edge.

04

Journal before, pre-mortem before, score after — always with the base rate.

05

The desk's own prior lost and was reported as a null; a 54.1% hit rate was the 54.1% base rate. Untested: whether journals raise P&L.

End of The Human Side

Six lessons, one habit

Loss aversion, the disposition effect, overconfidence, herding, drawdown panic and resulting all look different on paper — once you write it down first.
