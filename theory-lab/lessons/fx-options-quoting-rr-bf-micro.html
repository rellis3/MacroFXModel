FX Options Quoting, or Three Numbers That Draw a Smile

An FX options screen shows no prices and no strikes. It shows volatilities against deltas, and for each expiry just three headline numbers: the ATM vol, the risk reversal and the butterfly. This guide shows why the market quotes that way, how those three numbers rebuild the whole smile, which conventions change the answer, and how the sterling risk reversal told the Brexit story weeks before the vote.

The whole idea
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

I asked for a price on a one-month euro dollar option and the broker said twenty-one point six. That isn't a price.

It is, just not in money. FX options are quoted in implied volatility. Both sides agree the vol, then plug it into the currency version of Black-Scholes to get the premium.

Why not just quote the premium?

Because the premium changes every time spot ticks, and the vol barely does. A vol quote stays good while the market moves.

He didn't give me a strike either. He said twenty-five delta.

Same idea. Delta is a strike measured in risk. A twenty-five delta call is out of the money, far enough that it moves about a quarter as much as the currency. Name the strike by its delta and the name still means the same thing tomorrow.

So what is actually on the screen?

Three numbers per expiry. The at-the-money vol is the level. The risk reversal is the twenty-five delta call vol minus the put vol: the tilt. The butterfly is how far the two wings sit above the middle: the curvature.

Give me numbers.

At the money ten, risk reversal minus two, butterfly a half. Start at ten. Add the half to both wings, so ten and a half. Then split the risk reversal in two. The put gets one more, the call one less. Put eleven and a half, call nine and a half.

So minus two means the puts are dearer.

Right. The market is paying up for one side. A deep negative risk reversal says people want protection against the base currency falling, and will pay extra for it. It is the price of insurance, not a forecast.

Is that little formula exact?

Only for the smile's own strangle. The butterfly the broker quotes is defined through a strangle priced at a single vol. When the risk reversal is big, the two come apart. In one published dollar yen example from January two thousand and nine, the quoted butterfly was about point one eight, but the smile's own was about point four two.

And delta isn't even one thing, is it?

No. Dollar yen deltas include the premium, because the premium is paid in dollars, the base currency. Euro dollar deltas don't. Read the wrong convention and every strike lands in the wrong place.

Has a risk reversal ever told a real story?

The Brexit vote in June twenty sixteen. The one-month sterling risk reversal was about minus one point three on the twenty-fifth of May. The next day the one-month expiry first stretched past the vote, and it jumped to about minus five point four. By the tenth of June it was near minus eight. After the result, sterling fell more than eight percent in a day.

The one-line version?

Three numbers give you a smile: level, tilt and curvature. Check which delta and which at-the-money before you use them, and read the risk reversal as the price of fear, not a forecast.

Section 1

The quote is a volatility, not a price

Both sides agree an implied vol. The Garman–Kohlhagen formula (Black-Scholes with two interest rates) turns it into a premium.

📺

Four inputs are on a screen

Spot, the two currencies' interest rates and the time to expiry are all observable. The forward F = S·e^((r_d − r_f)τ) is covered interest parity.

🤝

One is negotiated

Implied volatility is the only ingredient up for debate, so it is the thing that gets quoted, like a yield instead of a bond price.

⏱

It survives a moving market

A premium changes every time spot ticks. A vol quote barely does, so thousands of quotes stay valid while spot moves underneath.

🧮

The formula bakes the price

v = φ[S·e^(−r_f τ)·N(φd₊) − K·e^(−r_d τ)·N(φd₋)], with φ = +1 for a call on the base currency and −1 for a put.

Section 2

Delta names the strike, and "ATM" is a straddle

A "25-delta call" is the out-of-the-money call whose value moves a quarter as much as the currency. The name means the same thing wherever spot is.

Follow it through
spot Δ = φ·e^(−r_f τ)·N(φd₊)
forward Δ = φ·N(φd₊)
Spot delta is the hedge in the spot market, forward delta the hedge in the forward. A 25-delta call has Δ = +0.25, a 25-delta put Δ = −0.25. With forward deltas, a 25-delta put is the same strike as a 75-delta call.
K = F·exp(−φ·N⁻¹(φ·e^(r_f τ)·Δ)·σ√τ + ½σ²τ)
The vol is inside the strike formula. A jumpier currency puts its 25-delta strike further from the forward. So you need the vol at that delta before you can find the strike, which is why smiles are quoted in delta space.
K_ATM = F·e^(+½σ²τ)
premium-adjusted: F·e^(−½σ²τ)
The default ATM for short-dated FX is the delta-neutral straddle: the strike where call and put deltas cancel, so the straddle is a pure vol trade. It is a few pips off the forward. Long-dated and many emerging-market quotes use the forward itself.

A positive risk reversal means calls on the base currency are dearer; a negative one, puts. Desks say "in favour of": "GBP/USD one-month at 7.9 GBP puts" is a risk reversal of −7.9. The same arithmetic works at 10 delta for the far wings.

Section 4

Tilt it and lift it yourself

Move the three quotes. The curve is Malz's parabola in forward delta, which passes through all three points.

Try it
ATM vol10.0%
25Δ risk reversal−2.0
25Δ butterfly0.50
25Δ put 11.50% · ATM 10.00% · 25Δ call 9.50%

Set the risk reversal to zero and the smile turns symmetric, both wings at ATM + BF. Out at 10 delta the curve is only the parabola's guess: real 10-delta quotes are separate numbers.

Section 6

Which delta? It depends on the pair

If the premium is paid in the base currency, the dealer already holds some of the hedge, so delta is "premium-adjusted": Δ_pa = Δ − premium/S.

Regular delta
  • Premium in the terms currency
  • EUR/USD, GBP/USD, AUD/USD (premium in USD)
  • ATM straddle strike just above the forward
Premium-adjusted delta
  • Premium in the base currency
  • USD/JPY, USD/CHF, USD/CAD, EUR/JPY, EUR/CHF, EUR/GBP, AUD/JPY, USD/BRL, USD/MXN
  • ATM straddle strike just below the forward

Spot or forward: for pairs of OECD currencies, spot delta out to and including one year, forward delta beyond. Pairs with an emerging-market currency use forward delta at every tenor. Exceptions exist, so check the broker's convention.

Section 7

Reading a risk reversal: the market paying up for one side

💥

Crash risk

A big move one way looks likelier, faster or gappier than the same move the other way.

🛡

Hedging demand

People with real exposure are buying protection, and dealers charge to carry it.

🔗

Spot–vol link

If vol rises when spot falls, puts gain twice in a sell-off, and the market prices that in.

🚫

Not a forecast

It is the price of insurance. In June 2016 sterling's risk reversal was deeply negative while spot rose for two weeks.

In money: a one-month 25-delta EUR/USD option on €10m had a vega of about $12,100 per vol point (January 2009 quote). A −0.5 risk reversal is about $6,000 between the put and the call.
Section 8

One smile per expiry: the term structure

Read down the ATM column and you get the term structure: upward-sloping in calm markets, inverted in a crisis, bumped wherever an event falls.

Variances add: σ₁→₂ = √((σ₂²T₂ − σ₁²T₁)/(T₂ − T₁)) = √((0.0144 × 2 − 0.01 × 1)/1) = 13.71%

The tenor whose expiry first covers an event usually moves most. A one-month risk reversal that jumps overnight with no news may simply have rolled over an election or a central-bank meeting. Check the calendar before reading it as new information.

Section 9

Where the shortcut stops being exact

The formula is exact for the smile's own strangle. The broker's butterfly is a "market strangle": both strikes found and priced at the single vol ATM + BF.

Where the textbook version breaks

USD/JPY one month, 20 January 2009 (Reiswich and Wystup): ATM 21.00, RR −5.3, quoted BF 0.184. The shortcut gives 18.534% and 23.834%. The smile that reprices the market strangle has 18.769% and 24.069%: a smile strangle of 0.419, both wings about 0.24 vol higher. For EUR/USD the same day (RR −0.5) the two strangles were 0.7375 and 0.7377.

Where this needs qualifying

Three quotes give three points, not a distribution. Before Brexit the true picture was two outcomes far apart, which no smooth smile through three points can show. That is why analysts fitted two-scenario mixtures, and desks fill the smile with vanna-volga or SABR.

Section 10 · It actually happened

Brexit, 2016: each tenor flinched when it covered the vote

It actually happened

The referendum was set on 20 February 2016 for 23 June. Clark and Amen tracked the GBP/USD smile. The one-month 25-delta risk reversal went from −1.3 on 25 May to −5.4 on 26 May, the day the one-month expiry first fell after the vote, and to −7.9 by 10 June with spot near 1.4390. Spot then rallied to 1.4877 as polls leaned Remain. Leave won; GBP/USD closed 24 June at 1.3622, about 8.4% lower, after briefly trading around 1.32 to 1.33, its weakest since 1985.

Section 11

Quick check

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

ATM 8.0, 25-delta risk reversal +1.0, butterfly 0.30. What is the 25-delta put vol?

σ_25P = 8.0 + 0.30 − ½(1.0) = 7.8. The call is 8.8. A positive risk reversal makes calls on the base currency dearer.
Question 2

For a short-dated EUR/USD quote, "at the money" usually means:

The strike where call and put deltas cancel, F·e^(½σ²τ) with regular deltas: a straddle there is a pure vol trade.
Question 3

Why are USD/JPY deltas premium-adjusted but EUR/USD deltas not?

Both premiums are paid in USD. In USD/JPY the dollar is the base currency, so the premium is part of the hedge quantity and shrinks the delta.
Question 4

A one-month risk reversal jumps from −1.3 to −5.4 overnight with no news. The likeliest reason?

GBP/USD, 25 to 26 May 2016: the one-month expiry first covered the 23 June vote, so the option started carrying the event's risk.
Question 5

Sterling's one-month risk reversal is −7.9. What does that tell you?

It is the price of insurance. In June 2016 it sat near −7.9 while spot rallied for two weeks before the vote.
Your call
Scenario drill · 10 June 2016

Sterling insurance has never looked so expensive

You run a US fund holding £50 million of UK assets, currency unhedged. The referendum is on 23 June. GBP/USD is near 1.4390, and the one-month 25-delta risk reversal is about −7.9: sterling puts cost about eight vols more than equivalent calls. A colleague says: "The puts are absurdly rich. Let's sell some and collect the premium." What do you do?

  1. Sell 25-delta GBP puts to harvest the rich skew.
    You already own the downside through the £50 million of assets. Selling puts doubles that bet, and the skew is rich precisely because the market sees a real chance of a large, fast fall. You would be paid well for standing in the road.
  2. Hedge some or all of the sterling with forwards, which carry no implied vol, and buy options only for the part where you want to keep the upside.
    The risk reversal says option protection on this side is expensive, not that you should go unprotected. A forward hedge costs nothing in vol terms; the price is giving up the rally if Remain wins. Using options only where you need the optionality keeps the skew bill small.
  3. Buy 25-delta GBP puts on the whole £50 million.
    It protects you, and it keeps the upside, but you pay the richest vol on the screen for all of it. Defensible for a fund that cannot give up the upside; expensive for one that can.
  4. Do nothing. A −7.9 risk reversal means the market is too bearish, so sterling should rally.
    The risk reversal is a price of insurance, not a forecast, and reading it as a contrarian signal is still a forecast. Staying unhedged into a binary event is a directional bet with the fund's whole sterling position.

What happened. Sterling first rallied, to 1.4877 by the close on polling day, as polls leaned towards Remain. Then Leave won. On 24 June GBP/USD closed at 1.3622, down about 8.4%, after trading briefly around 1.32 to 1.33, its weakest since 1985. Forward hedges locked in rates above 1.40; puts paid out; anyone who had sold sterling puts on top of a long sterling book lost twice. Figures from Clark and Amen (2017) and contemporary market reports.

Section 12

What to carry forward

01

FX options are quoted in implied vol, the one negotiable input to Garman–Kohlhagen, and strikes are named by delta so the names survive spot moving.

02

ATM usually means the delta-neutral straddle: F·e^(+½σ²τ) with regular deltas, F·e^(−½σ²τ) with premium-adjusted ones, the forward for many long-dated and emerging-market quotes.

03

σ_25C,25P = σ_ATM + σ_BF ± ½σ_RR: level, tilt and curvature. The 10-delta pair does the same for the far wings.

04

The shortcut is exact for the smile strangle only. With a big risk reversal the quoted butterfly differs (USD/JPY, Jan 2009: 0.184 quoted, 0.419 in the smile).

05

Check the delta convention: premium-adjusted for USD/JPY, EUR/JPY, USD/CHF, EUR/GBP; regular for EUR/USD, GBP/USD, AUD/USD; spot to one year then forward for OECD pairs.

06

A risk reversal is the market paying up for one side, timed by which expiry covers an event: sterling's one-month went −1.3 → −5.4 → −7.9 before the 2016 vote. A price of fear, not a forecast.

What this now lets you do

Read an FX vol screen: turn ATM, risk reversal and butterfly into wing vols, wing vols into strikes under the right convention, and a risk reversal or term structure into a statement about which side and which dates the market is worried about.
