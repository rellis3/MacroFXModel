Correlation & Linear Regression

Before you can ask whether two prices are cointegrated, whether a Kalman filter's hedge ratio is drifting, or whether a factor explains a return — you need the tool that measures "moves together" in the first place, and the tool that fits the best straight line through that relationship; this primer builds both from the ground up.

PRIMER — foundational background ~15 min read Prereqs: Probability & Random Variables

Why this matters

Nearly every piece of quantitative reasoning in this repo eventually rests on one of two questions: "do these two things move together?" and "if I had to draw one straight line through this relationship, what would it be?" The Cointegration lesson asks whether two price levels share a stable equilibrium — and its entire first step is an ordinary least squares regression. The Kalman Filter lesson tracks a hedge ratio that drifts over time — and that hedge ratio is a regression slope, just one that's allowed to update bar by bar instead of being fit once on a fixed window. Principal Component Analysis decomposes a basket of correlated return series into independent factors — and the correlation matrix it operates on is built from exactly the covariance and correlation formulas below. None of those three lessons re-derive this machinery; they assume it. This primer is where it gets derived, once, so it can be imported by reference everywhere else.

The two ideas are also easy to reach for carelessly, which is precisely why they need a careful foundation. A correlation coefficient is a single number that looks authoritative — "r = 0.87" sounds like a fact about the world — but it silently assumes the relationship it's summarizing is linear, and it says nothing whatsoever about which variable, if either, is causing the other. A regression line looks like it's revealing a structural truth, but it is, mechanically, nothing more than the line that minimizes one particular measure of vertical distance to a cloud of points — a description of a dataset, not a law of nature. Understanding exactly what these tools compute, and exactly what they don't claim, is what keeps the more advanced lessons that lean on them from being misapplied.

Plain-English intuition

Covariance is "do they move together," correlation is that idea rescaled to fit between -1 and 1

Imagine plotting two variables against each other, day by day — say a stock's daily return and the broad market's daily return. On a day when the market is up, is the stock usually also up? On a day the market is down, is the stock usually also down? If the answer is "usually yes," the two variables tend to be above their own averages together and below their own averages together. Covariance is just that idea turned into arithmetic: for each day, multiply how far the stock was from its own average by how far the market was from its own average, then average that product across all the days. When both variables tend to be on the same side of their average at the same time, most of those products are positive (positive times positive, or negative times negative, both give positive), so the average comes out positive — positive covariance. When they tend to be on opposite sides, the products are usually negative — negative covariance.

The problem with covariance as a report card is that its size depends on the units of whatever you're measuring — covariance between two variables measured in basis points is a different-looking number than the same relationship measured in percent, even though nothing about the underlying relationship changed. Correlation fixes that: it takes covariance and divides it by each variable's own standard deviation, which cancels the units out and rescales the result to always live between -1 (perfectly, linearly opposite) and +1 (perfectly, linearly together), with 0 meaning no linear tendency either way. That unitless, bounded number is what lets you compare "how related are these two FX pairs" against "how related are these two equity indices" on the same scale.

Regression answers a related but different question: not just "do they move together," but "if I know today's value of X, what's my single best straight-line guess for Y?" It draws one line through the cloud of points, chosen by a specific rule — minimize the total squared vertical distance from every point to the line — and that line's slope becomes the practical, tradeable number: for every one-unit move in X, how many units does Y tend to move.

The math, step by step

Everything here builds on the variance and expectation machinery from the Probability & Random Variables primer. The order is: covariance, then correlation as covariance rescaled, then the OLS regression line derived from minimizing squared residuals, then R² as the fraction of variance that line explains.

1. Covariance. For two random variables \(X\) and \(Y\) with means \(\mu_X = E[X]\) and \(\mu_Y = E[Y]\), covariance measures the average tendency of the two variables to deviate from their own means in the same direction at the same time:

\[ \mathrm{Cov}(X,Y) = E\big[(X-\mu_X)(Y-\mu_Y)\big] \]

Notice this is a direct generalization of variance, not a new idea bolted on next to it: variance is just the covariance of a variable with itself, \(\mathrm{Var}(X) = \mathrm{Cov}(X,X) = E[(X-\mu_X)(X-\mu_X)] = E[(X-\mu_X)^2]\). Everything the earlier primer said about variance — that it's built from squared/paired deviations from a mean, that it's estimated from a finite sample by averaging over observed deviations — is the same machinery here, just applied to two variables' deviations multiplied together instead of one variable's deviation squared. Given a finite sample of \(n\) paired observations \((x_i, y_i)\), the sample covariance is estimated the same way the sample variance is:

\[ \widehat{\mathrm{Cov}}(X,Y) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-\bar{x})(y_i-\bar{y}) \]

(Exactly as with sample variance, some software divides by \(n-1\) rather than \(n\) — the "Bessel's correction" for using an estimated mean rather than the true one. It matters for small-sample bias, not for the concept; this primer uses \(n\) throughout for arithmetic simplicity and because it cancels out of every ratio built below.)

The sign of covariance is meaningful — positive means "tend to move the same direction," negative means "tend to move opposite directions," zero means no linear tendency either way — but its magnitude is not, on its own, interpretable. A covariance of 4.0 between two variables measured in one unit and a covariance of 0.0004 between the same two variables rescaled to a different unit describe exactly the same underlying relationship. That's the problem correlation exists to solve.

2. The correlation coefficient. Divide covariance by the product of each variable's own standard deviation:

\[ r = \frac{\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)}{\sigma_X\,\sigma_Y} \]

Dividing by \(\sigma_X\) and \(\sigma_Y\) cancels the units of \(X\) and \(Y\) out of the result entirely — \(r\) is a pure, unitless number. It is also provably bounded, \(-1 \le r \le 1\), a direct consequence of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality applied to the deviations \((X-\mu_X)\) and \((Y-\mu_Y)\); this is why \(r=1\) (perfect positive linear relationship) and \(r=-1\) (perfect negative linear relationship) are the mathematical ceiling and floor, not just empirically rare extremes. \(r=0\) means no linear relationship — a qualifier this primer returns to explicitly in the pitfalls section below, because it is the single most common misreading of a correlation coefficient. This specific formalization — covariance rescaled by the two standard deviations — is due to Karl Pearson in the 1890s, which is why it is formally called the Pearson correlation coefficient to distinguish it from rank-based alternatives (Spearman's, Kendall's) that measure a broader "moves together" notion without assuming linearity.

\(\mu_X, \mu_Y\)The population means of \(X\) and \(Y\); estimated in a sample by \(\bar{x}, \bar{y}\).
\(\sigma_X, \sigma_Y\)The standard deviations of \(X\) and \(Y\) — the square roots of their variances.
\(\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)\)Covariance — the average product of \(X\) and \(Y\)'s deviations from their own means.
\(r\)The Pearson correlation coefficient — covariance rescaled to \([-1, 1]\), unitless.
\(\alpha, \beta\)The regression intercept and slope: \(Y = \alpha + \beta X + \epsilon\).
\(\epsilon\)The true, unobserved regression error term.
\(\hat\alpha, \hat\beta\)The OLS-estimated intercept and slope, fit from a sample.
\(\hat{y}_i\)The fitted value for observation \(i\): \(\hat{y}_i = \hat\alpha + \hat\beta x_i\).
\(e_i\)The residual for observation \(i\): \(e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i\) — the part of \(y_i\) the fitted line does not explain.

\(R^2\)Coefficient of determination — the fraction of SST explained by the regression, \(1 - \mathrm{SSR}/\mathrm{SST}\).

3. Ordinary least squares (OLS) regression. Correlation tells you how strongly two variables move together; regression goes one step further and gives you a specific, usable line — a rule for predicting \(Y\) from \(X\). The model is:

\[ Y = \alpha + \beta X + \epsilon \]

\(\alpha\) is the intercept (the predicted \(Y\) when \(X=0\)), \(\beta\) is the slope (the predicted change in \(Y\) per one-unit change in \(X\)), and \(\epsilon\) is the error term — everything about \(Y\) this simple straight-line model does not capture. Given a sample of paired observations \((x_i, y_i)\), fitting the model means choosing specific numbers \(\hat\alpha\) and \(\hat\beta\) for the intercept and slope. The residual for each observation is the vertical gap between the actual data point and the line's prediction for it:

\[ e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i = y_i - (\hat\alpha + \hat\beta x_i) \]

"Ordinary least squares" names the specific rule used to pick \(\hat\alpha\) and \(\hat\beta\): choose the values that minimize the sum of squared residuals across every observation in the sample —

\[ \mathrm{SSR}(\hat\alpha, \hat\beta) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} e_i^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(y_i - \hat\alpha - \hat\beta x_i\big)^2 \]

Two things are worth being explicit about before minimizing this. Why squared, rather than just summing the raw (signed) residuals or their absolute values? Summing raw residuals is a dead end — a line with huge positive and huge negative residuals that happen to cancel would score as "perfect," which is clearly wrong. Minimizing squared residuals instead of absolute residuals is a deliberate choice with consequences: squaring is smooth and differentiable everywhere (absolute value has a kink at zero, making the minimization harder to solve in closed form), and it penalizes large residuals disproportionately more than small ones — a residual twice as large contributes four times the penalty, which pulls the fitted line to avoid being far off on any single point, at the cost of being more sensitive to outliers than an absolute-error fit would be (a real trade-off, not a free lunch).

To actually minimize SSR, treat it as a function of \(\hat\alpha\) and \(\hat\beta\) and use ordinary calculus: take the partial derivative with respect to each, and set both to zero (the condition for a minimum of a smooth bowl-shaped function — and SSR, being a sum of squares of linear terms, is exactly that kind of function, so this always has a unique minimum). Differentiating with respect to \(\hat\alpha\):

\[ \frac{\partial \mathrm{SSR}}{\partial \hat\alpha} = -2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(y_i - \hat\alpha - \hat\beta x_i\big) = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \sum_i e_i = 0 \]

This first condition says the residuals must sum to exactly zero — the line has to run through the "center of mass" of the data. Dividing through by \(n\) and rearranging gives the intercept directly once \(\hat\beta\) is known:

\[ \hat\alpha = \bar{y} - \hat\beta\,\bar{x} \]

Differentiating with respect to \(\hat\beta\):

\[ \frac{\partial \mathrm{SSR}}{\partial \hat\beta} = -2\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i\big(y_i - \hat\alpha - \hat\beta x_i\big) = 0 \]

Substituting \(\hat\alpha = \bar y - \hat\beta \bar x\) from the first condition into this second condition and rearranging (the algebra consists of expanding the product and regrouping terms into deviations from the means — mechanical but not illuminating to spell out line-by-line here) collapses to a remarkably clean result:

\[ \hat\beta = \frac{\sum_i (x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)}{\sum_i (x_i - \bar x)^2} = \frac{\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)}{\mathrm{Var}(X)} \]

This is the payoff of the whole derivation: the least-squares slope is exactly the covariance between \(X\) and \(Y\), divided by the variance of \(X\) alone. It is also exactly why this primer had to build covariance and variance first — the OLS slope is not a separate concept from correlation, it is covariance, just normalized by \(X\)'s own spread instead of by both variables' spreads (which is what correlation does). This is the identical formula js/statsCore.js's linregSlope implements in this repo (see the repo box below) — there the "\(X\)" is simply the bar index (0, 1, 2, …), so the fitted slope is a per-bar drift/trend rate rather than a slope against another price series, but the underlying least-squares arithmetic is identical.

4. R² — the fraction of variance explained. Once a line is fit, a natural question is how good the fit actually is. Split the total variation in \(Y\) around its own mean, SST \(= \sum_i (y_i-\bar y)^2\), into two pieces: the part the regression line explains (how far the fitted values \(\hat y_i\) themselves vary) and the part left over in the residuals (SSR). \(R^2\) is the explained fraction:

\[ R^2 = 1 - \frac{\mathrm{SSR}}{\mathrm{SST}} = 1 - \frac{\sum_i (y_i-\hat y_i)^2}{\sum_i (y_i-\bar y)^2} \]

\(R^2 = 1\) means the line passes through every point exactly (zero residual variance); \(R^2 = 0\) means the line explains nothing beyond just always guessing \(\bar y\) (the regression's residual variance equals the raw variance of \(Y\)). For the specific case of simple linear regression (one predictor \(X\)), there's a direct algebraic identity connecting this back to the correlation coefficient from step 2:

\[ R^2 = r^2 \]

This is not a coincidence or an approximation — it falls directly out of substituting the OLS formulas for \(\hat\alpha\) and \(\hat\beta\) back into the SST/SSR decomposition above; the algebra is mechanical once you have \(\hat\beta = \mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)/\mathrm{Var}(X)\) in hand; it is stated here as a fact to use, and revisited concretely in the worked example below. The identity only holds for simple regression (a single \(X\)); once a second predictor is added, \(R^2\) and any one predictor's correlation with \(Y\) come apart — which is exactly the mechanism behind the "R² only ever goes up" pitfall discussed later in this lesson.

Worked example

Covariance, r, β, α, and R² by hand for five points

Take five paired observations that roughly follow \(y \approx 2x\) with a little noise:

\[ x = [1,2,3,4,5] \qquad y = [2.1,\ 3.9,\ 6.2,\ 7.8,\ 10.1] \]

Means. \(\bar x = (1+2+3+4+5)/5 = 3\). \(\bar y = (2.1+3.9+6.2+7.8+10.1)/5 = 30.1/5 = 6.02\).

Deviations. \(x_i - \bar x = [-2,-1,0,1,2]\). \(y_i - \bar y = [-3.92,\ -2.12,\ 0.18,\ 1.78,\ 4.08]\).

Covariance. Multiply each pair of deviations and average:

\[ (-2)(-3.92)+(-1)(-2.12)+(0)(0.18)+(1)(1.78)+(2)(4.08) = 7.84+2.12+0+1.78+8.16 = 19.9 \]
\[ \mathrm{Cov}(x,y) = 19.9 / 5 = 3.98 \]

Variance of x. Square and average the x-deviations: \((4+1+0+1+4)/5 = 10/5 = 2\), so \(\mathrm{Var}(x) = 2\), and \(\sigma_x = \sqrt{2} \approx 1.4142\).

Variance of y (needed for r). Square and average the y-deviations: \(15.3664+4.4944+0.0324+3.1684+16.6464 = 39.708\), so \(\mathrm{Var}(y) = 39.708/5 = 7.9416\) and \(\sigma_y = \sqrt{7.9416} \approx 2.8181\).

Correlation.

\[ r = \frac{3.98}{(1.4142)(2.8181)} = \frac{3.98}{3.9854} \approx 0.9986 \]

A correlation of about 0.999 — very close to a perfect straight-line relationship, exactly as expected from data built as \(2x\) plus a small amount of noise.

OLS slope and intercept.

\[ \hat\beta = \frac{\mathrm{Cov}(x,y)}{\mathrm{Var}(x)} = \frac{3.98}{2} = 1.99 \qquad \hat\alpha = \bar y - \hat\beta\,\bar x = 6.02 - (1.99)(3) = 0.05 \]

So the fitted line is \(\hat y = 0.05 + 1.99x\) — close to the "true" \(y=2x\) the data was built around, as it should be with only a little noise added.

Residuals and R² (cross-checking the \(R^2=r^2\) identity). Fitted values: \(\hat y = [2.04,\ 4.03,\ 6.02,\ 8.01,\ 10.00]\). Residuals \(e_i = y_i-\hat y_i = [0.06,\ -0.13,\ 0.18,\ -0.21,\ 0.10]\) (these sum to 0.00, confirming the first OLS condition derived above). Squared and summed: \(\mathrm{SSR} = 0.0036+0.0169+0.0324+0.0441+0.0100 = 0.107\). SST is \(n\cdot\mathrm{Var}(y) = 5 \times 7.9416 = 39.708\) (the same sum of squared y-deviations computed above). So:

\[ R^2 = 1 - \frac{0.107}{39.708} \approx 1 - 0.0027 = 0.9973 \]

Compare against \(r^2 = (0.9986)^2 \approx 0.9973\) — the two routes to \(R^2\) agree, confirming the identity from step 4 rather than just asserting it. About 99.7% of the variation in \(y\) is explained by its linear relationship with \(x\); the remaining 0.3% is noise the line doesn't capture.

How practitioners actually use this

Beta and factor exposure. The single most common use of this exact machinery in finance is estimating a "beta" — regress an asset's returns on a benchmark's returns (the market, a sector index, a macro factor) and the OLS slope \(\hat\beta\) is the asset's sensitivity to that factor: for every 1% the benchmark moves, the asset tends to move \(\hat\beta\)%. This is literally the CAPM beta, and it's the same identity as the trend slope computed for a single price series against time.

Hedge ratios. If you want to hedge a long position in one instrument with a short position in a correlated one, the regression slope of the instrument you're hedging against the hedge instrument tells you the ratio of units to use — regress \(Y\) on \(X\) and \(\hat\beta\) units of \(X\) offset one unit of \(Y\), on average, over the fitted sample. This is exactly Step 1 of the Engle-Granger cointegration procedure covered in the Cointegration lesson, and exactly the quantity the Kalman Filter lesson tracks as a time-varying version of the same idea, once a single fixed-window \(\hat\beta\) stops being good enough.

Risk/return decomposition. Regressing a strategy's or portfolio's returns on a set of known factors (value, momentum, carry, market beta) splits realized performance into an explained part (the factor exposures times the factor returns) and an unexplained residual — the part attributable to something other than the named factors, sometimes optimistically labeled "alpha." A high \(R^2\) means the named factors are doing most of the explanatory work; a low one means most of what's happening is not captured by that factor set.

Screening and pair discovery. A rolling correlation matrix across a universe of instruments is the standard first pass for finding candidate relationships worth investigating further — which pairs of currencies, or which currency and which macro series, tend to move together. It is a cheap, fast filter, and precisely because it's cheap and fast, it is also easy to over-trust; the next section on pitfalls, and the Cointegration lesson in full, exist because "correlated in returns" is a necessary but far from sufficient condition for a tradeable relationship.

Common pitfalls

Where correlation and regression get misread

Correlation is not causation. The classic textbook example: across cities and months, ice cream sales and drowning deaths are strongly positively correlated. Ice cream does not cause drowning, and drowning does not cause ice cream sales — both are driven by a third, unmeasured variable (warm weather: more people swim, and more people buy ice cream). This is called confounding, and it is the default explanation to rule out, not a rare edge case, any time a correlation is found between two variables without a clear mechanical or economic reason one should drive the other. In markets, two currencies can be correlated because one causes the other to move, because both are driven by a shared factor (broad USD strength, global risk sentiment), or by pure coincidence over the sample window looked at — the correlation coefficient itself cannot distinguish between these, and treating a high \(r\) as proof of a causal or structural link is the single most common misuse of this tool.

Correlation only captures linear relationships. A variable can depend on another extremely strongly and predictably while still showing \(r \approx 0\), if that dependence isn't linear. A textbook case: let \(x\) range symmetrically from \(-a\) to \(a\) and set \(y = x^2\) — every value of \(y\) is perfectly, deterministically determined by \(x\), yet because \(y\) is exactly as large for negative \(x\) as for the corresponding positive \(x\), the positive and negative deviation-products cancel out almost exactly, and the Pearson \(r\) comes out near zero. \(r=0\) means "no linear tendency," not "no relationship" — always look at a scatter plot, not just the coefficient, before concluding two variables are unrelated.

Adding predictors always weakly increases R², even if they're useless. \(R^2\) can never go down when you add another predictor to a regression, because OLS is free to set that predictor's coefficient to exactly zero if it adds nothing — so the fit can only improve or stay flat, never worsen, purely as a matter of the minimization having more freedom, regardless of whether the new predictor has any real relationship to \(Y\). A regression with twenty near-random predictors will report a deceptively high in-sample \(R^2\) for reasons that have nothing to do with any of them actually explaining \(Y\). This is exactly why a suspiciously high \(R^2\), on its own, should raise scrutiny rather than confidence — check the number of predictors relative to the sample size, and prefer an adjusted \(R^2\) (which penalizes added predictors) or true out-of-sample performance over the raw in-sample number, consistent with this repo's broader discipline of never trusting an in-sample metric as evidence of anything by itself.

Standard OLS inference assumes well-behaved errors. The textbook formulas for a regression's standard errors, t-statistics, and p-values are derived assuming the residuals are homoskedastic (constant variance across the whole sample) and not autocorrelated (one period's residual doesn't help predict the next period's). Financial return series routinely violate both — volatility clusters (heteroskedasticity), and residuals from a poorly specified model can trend in stretches (autocorrelation). When either assumption breaks, the standard errors the software reports are simply wrong (usually too small, making a relationship look more statistically significant than it is), even though the point estimate \(\hat\beta\) itself can still be a reasonable estimate. This repo's own build discipline calls for checking exactly this before trusting a regression coefficient's significance — Durbin-Watson for autocorrelation, Breusch-Pagan for heteroskedasticity — rather than reading a low p-value at face value.

Self-test

Q1. Why does \(R^2 = r^2\) hold for simple linear regression (one predictor), and why does that identity break once a second predictor is added?
It holds because \(R^2\)'s SST/SSR decomposition, once you substitute in the OLS formulas \(\hat\beta = \mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)/\mathrm{Var}(X)\) and \(\hat\alpha = \bar y - \hat\beta\bar x\), algebraically reduces exactly to the square of the Pearson correlation coefficient between \(X\) and \(Y\) — both quantities are, underneath, built from the same covariance-over-variances ratio, just normalized differently (\(r\) divides by both standard deviations; \(\beta\), and by extension \(R^2\), effectively normalizes only by \(X\)'s variance in a way that collapses back to \(r^2\) in the one-predictor case). With a second predictor, \(R^2\) becomes a joint statement about how much of \(Y\)'s variance the whole set of predictors together explains, which is no longer reducible to any single predictor's pairwise correlation with \(Y\) — the identity is a special case of the one-variable model, not a general law.
Q2. Give an example of two variables with a strong, deterministic relationship but a correlation coefficient near zero.
\(y = x^2\) with \(x\) ranging symmetrically around zero (e.g. \(x \in [-5, 5]\)). Every value of \(y\) is completely determined by \(x\) — there is no noise at all — but because \(y\) is identical for \(x\) and \(-x\), the positive and negative deviation-products that make up the covariance largely cancel, leaving \(\mathrm{Cov}(x,y)\), and therefore Pearson's \(r\), close to zero. This is the standard illustration that \(r\) measures only linear association; strong nonlinear (quadratic, cyclical, threshold-based) relationships can hide from it entirely.
Q3. Why does adding more predictor variables to a regression always weakly increase R², even if the new predictors are pure noise with no real relationship to Y?
Because OLS minimizes the sum of squared residuals over all available coefficients simultaneously, and it always has the option of setting a new, useless predictor's coefficient to exactly zero, which reproduces the old fit exactly. Since the minimization is free to do at least that well, and is sometimes able to do slightly better by finding some small spurious in-sample pattern in the new predictor purely by chance, SSR can only stay the same or fall as predictors are added — it can never rise. Since \(R^2 = 1 - \mathrm{SSR}/\mathrm{SST}\) and SST doesn't change, \(R^2\) can only stay the same or rise. This mechanical fact — not any real explanatory power — is why a raw in-sample \(R^2\) with many predictors is untrustworthy on its own, and why adjusted \(R^2\) or out-of-sample validation exist.
Q4. In the OLS derivation, why does setting the partial derivative of the sum of squared residuals with respect to the intercept to zero imply the residuals must sum to exactly zero?
\(\partial \mathrm{SSR}/\partial\hat\alpha = -2\sum_i (y_i - \hat\alpha - \hat\beta x_i) = -2\sum_i e_i\). Setting this equal to zero and dividing by \(-2\) directly gives \(\sum_i e_i = 0\) — it's the same expression, just relabeled as the residual definition. Intuitively, this is the condition that makes the intercept optimal: if the residuals summed to something other than zero, you could shift the whole line up or down by that average amount and strictly reduce the total squared error, so the minimum can only be at the point where that's no longer possible — where the positive and negative residuals exactly balance.
Q5. A friend says "our regression found a very significant relationship, p < 0.01" but the residuals turned out to be strongly autocorrelated. Why is that a problem, and what should you check for before trusting the coefficient?
The standard formula for a regression's t-statistics and p-values assumes the residuals are independent of each other (no autocorrelation) and of constant variance (homoskedastic). When residuals are autocorrelated, the effective amount of independent information in the sample is smaller than the raw observation count suggests — nearby residuals are echoing the same information rather than each contributing something new — so the standard formula understates the true uncertainty around \(\hat\beta\), producing standard errors, t-statistics, and p-values that look far more confident than they should. The point estimate \(\hat\beta\) itself can still be a reasonable estimate of the slope; it's the significance claim built on top of it that's unreliable. The fix is not to trust the p-value at face value — run a Durbin-Watson test for autocorrelation and a Breusch-Pagan test for heteroskedasticity on the residuals first, and use heteroskedasticity/autocorrelation-robust standard errors (or a different model specification) if either comes back flagged.

  • Francis Galton, "Regression Towards Mediocrity in Hereditary Stature," Journal of the Anthropological Institute, 1886 — Galton's original height-inheritance study; the source of both the term "regression" (tall parents' children tend to be shorter than their parents, "regressing" toward the population mean, and vice versa) and an early empirical treatment of what would become the correlation coefficient.
  • Karl Pearson, "Notes on Regression and Inheritance in the Case of Two Parents," Proceedings of the Royal Society of London, 1895 — Pearson's formalization of the correlation coefficient into the precise covariance-over-standard-deviations form used throughout this primer and universally today.
  • Jeffrey M. Wooldridge, Introductory Econometrics: A Modern Approach — the standard textbook treatment of OLS, its assumptions (including the homoskedasticity/no-autocorrelation conditions discussed in the pitfalls section), and formal statistical inference for regression; a natural next step for anyone wanting the full derivation with proofs.
  • Francis Anscombe, "Graphs in Statistical Analysis," The American Statistician, 1973 — the origin of "Anscombe's quartet," four datasets with nearly identical means, variances, correlations, and regression lines despite looking completely different when plotted; the canonical illustration of why a scatter plot, not just the summary statistics, always belongs in the analysis.
