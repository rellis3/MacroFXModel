Vector Error Correction Models, Who Actually Does the Correcting?

Cointegration proves two prices are tied together by a leash with a normal length. It never says which one moves when the leash goes taut. This is the first lesson in a brand-new category of the curriculum built entirely around that question — and a Vector Error Correction Model is the first real tool for answering it: an actual regression for how each series' next move depends on how far the pair currently sits from equilibrium.

Section 1

A leash proves nothing about who moves

Recap: cointegration shows a stationary spread exists. It stops there.

🐕

The leash exists (Cointegration)

Y_t − β·X_t − c is stationary — the spread has a stable home it keeps returning to. That's the entire content of the Cointegration lesson's Engle-Granger test.

❓

But who gets pulled?

A leash going taut doesn't say whether the dog gets yanked forward, the walker gets tugged back, or both. Cointegration is silent on this entirely.

📐

A VECM is the answer

Two regressions — one for ΔY, one for ΔX — each predicting this period's change from last period's spread. The coefficients say exactly who moves, and by how much.

🔭

Why a whole new category

"Which price is leading, which is following" is the organizing question of this entire 3-lesson category — VECM is the first, informal answer; Granger causality and Hasbrouck's Information Share (next two lessons) make it rigorous.

The Cointegration lesson even names this next step in one sentence of its own text: model each series' change partly on how far last period's spread sat from equilibrium. This deck is that sentence, spelled all the way out.
Section 2

The model: two equations, one shared term

Z_{t-1} is last period's cointegrating residual — the exact same spread from the Cointegration lesson, reused as a predictor.

The equations

ε_Y,t
ΔX_t
=
α_X · Z_{t-1}

Z_{t-1} = Y_{t-1} − β·X_{t-1} − c — nothing new, just last period's spread. α_Y and α_X are the new content this whole deck is about.

The "short-run lags" pieces are ordinary autoregressive terms — recent momentum in ΔY and ΔX feeding into the forecast — exactly the machinery the ARIMA & VAR lesson already builds. Nothing about them is specific to error correction, so this deck (like the full lesson) treats them at a conceptual level only and spends its time on α_Y and α_X instead.

Section 3

Two scenarios, same leash, different puller

Same spread, same magnitude of correction needed — three different ways it could actually play out.

Try it
α_Y = −0.35, α_X ≈ 0. Y falls back hard toward X; X barely reacts to the disequilibrium at all. X is behaving like a weakly exogenous anchor — Y is doing essentially all of the correcting.
α_X = +0.35, α_Y ≈ 0. The mirror image: X rises back toward Y, while Y ignores the gap almost entirely. Now Y is the anchor and X is the follower — same leash, opposite puller.
α_Y = −0.18, α_X = +0.17. Both series move roughly evenly to close the same gap — no clear anchor or follower, both sides sharing the adjustment almost equally.

All three scenarios are perfectly valid, cointegrated systems — nothing about cointegration itself prefers one over another. Only a fitted VECM's α_Y and α_X actually tell you which world a real pair is in.

Section 4

Watch the correction happen

Y overshoots well above X, then gets pulled back down. X barely moves throughout.

Visualize it
Y ranges far from X (the gap — Z — widens), then swings back down to reconverge, all while X drifts only gently. That asymmetry — one series doing almost all the round-trip work — is exactly a large |α_Y| paired with a small |α_X|.
Section 5

The formal guarantee: Granger Representation Theorem

Cited as a given result — not re-derived

Engle & Granger's 1987 paper — the same paper that defines cointegration itself — proves: any genuinely cointegrated pair must admit a valid error-correction representation of exactly this two-equation form, and conversely, a valid error-correction representation implies the pair is cointegrated. The two ideas are formally equivalent, not just related.

One consequence worth remembering: α_Y and α_X can't both be exactly zero. If neither series ever responded to the disequilibrium, nothing would pull Z back toward zero, and the pair wouldn't be cointegrated in the first place. At least one of the two has to be doing real work — the fitted model just says which, and how much.

Section 6

Comparing α_Y and α_X is the whole point

Large |α_Y|, small |α_X|
  • Y does almost all the correcting
  • X is the "anchor" — barely reacts to the gap
  • Y is the "follower"
Comparable |α_Y| and |α_X|
  • Both series share the adjustment
  • No clean anchor/follower split
  • Correction happens from both sides at once
Where this is heading next

This is exactly the question the next lesson in this category, Hasbrouck Information Share, formalizes properly — not for one pair, but for many simultaneous quote venues of the same asset, asking rigorously which one is actually leading price discovery. Comparing α_Y to α_X here is the direct, two-variable preview of that idea.

Section 7

α_Y = −0.35, α_X = +0.15 fixed. Only Z_{t-1} changes. Watch which bar moves further.

Try it

ΔY_t = −0.35 × 0.0050 = −0.00175  |  ΔX_t = 0.15 × 0.0050 = +0.00075

Drag Z_{t-1} toward either extreme and both bars grow — but the blue Y bar always grows more than twice as fast as the purple X bar, because |α_Y|=0.35 is more than twice |α_X|=0.15. That fixed ratio, not the size of any one shock, is what marks Y as the follower.

Section 10

Where this quietly goes wrong

🚫

Skipping the cointegration test

The theorem's guarantee only applies to genuinely cointegrated pairs. Fit this on two series never actually shown to cointegrate and the α's mean nothing.

📏

No significance check

A bigger |α_Y| in one small noisy sample isn't proof Y is "the" follower — the same standard-error caveats that apply to any OLS coefficient apply here too.

🔄

Sign convention matters

Flip how Z is defined and every sign above flips with it. α_Y>0 and α_X<0 together means the gap gets pushed wider, not corrected — not valid error correction at all.

✂️

Dropping lags in a real fit

This deck's calculator isolates α·Z for teaching clarity. A genuine fit (like this repo's own fitVECM) keeps the short-run lag terms — omitting them biases the estimated α itself.

Section 11

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

What is the error-correction term Z_{t-1}, exactly?

It's exactly the Cointegration lesson's own spread, lagged one period and reused as a regressor for this period's changes.
Question 2

α_Y = −0.50, α_X = −0.01 (statistically indistinguishable from zero). Which series is the "follower"?

A large-magnitude α_Y with α_X near zero is the textbook "weak exogeneity" case: X barely reacts to disequilibrium, Y does virtually all of the adjusting.
Question 3

What does the Granger Representation Theorem actually state?

Proven by Engle & Granger (1987): a cointegrated pair must admit a valid VECM representation, and a valid VECM representation implies cointegration.
Question 4

Given α_Y=0.30, α_X=−0.20, and Z_{t-1}=0.01>0, what's wrong with these particular signs?

With Z_{t-1}>0, correction needs α_Y≤0 and/or α_X≥0. Here Y is forecast to rise further and X to fall further — the gap widens, not closes — inconsistent with genuine cointegration.
Question 5

Does this repo have a real, live two-equation VECM system anywhere?

js/arma.js's fitVECM is a genuine, live single-equation error-correction regression (wired through computeARMAForecast into several production files) — but it forecasts one series against a price-implied fair value, not a jointly-fitted two-series α_Y/α_X system.
Section 12

What to carry forward

01

Cointegration proves a stationary spread exists between Y and X. A VECM is the dynamic regression built on top of it, forecasting each series' own change from that spread's last value.

02

The error-correction term Z_{t-1} is nothing new — it's exactly the Cointegration lesson's own spread, one period lagged and reused as a predictor.

03

Comparing |α_Y| and |α_X| is the actual payoff: the larger one marks the "follower" doing most of the correcting, the smaller one the relative "anchor."

04

The Granger Representation Theorem is the formal bridge — cointegration and valid error correction are equivalent, proven by Engle & Granger (1987), cited here rather than re-derived.

05

This repo already runs a genuine single-equation version live (js/arma.js's fitVECM) — the full two-equation system built here goes further than that code does.

What this now lets you do

Read a cointegrated pair's fitted α_Y and α_X as a real, informal answer to "which price is doing the adjusting" — and recognize that question as the exact seed of the next two lessons in this category, which make it formally rigorous.
