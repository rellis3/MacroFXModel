Lesson 12 · Options & Derivatives

Prices Don't Always Drift — Sometimes They Tear

Black-Scholes assumes an underlying's path is continuous — it can wiggle wildly, but it can never skip a level. Real FX doesn't obey that around a scheduled release: NFP, CPI, and central bank surprises make price gap, not drift, through dozens of pips in a heartbeat. Robert Merton's 1976 fix bolts a random-jump process onto ordinary diffusion so a model can finally admit that gap exists — and, along the way, explains a shape in real option prices that pure Black-Scholes never could.

Section 1

Black-Scholes has no way to represent a gap

"Continuous" means price must pass through every level between two points — no teleporting.

Under Black-Scholes, the underlying follows geometric Brownian motion: a constant drift plus continuous random noise. Zoom in as far as you like on that path and it never has a literal break — no instantaneous double-digit-pip vertical line. That assumption assigns vanishingly small probability to a large move happening in an infinitesimally short window of time.

Where this breaks in real FX

Scheduled macro releases are calendar events, known in advance down to the second, after which price can move 50, 100, even 200+ pips in a handful of seconds with no meaningful trading in between. That's not "fast diffusion" — it's a genuine discontinuity, and a pure Black-Scholes world has no mechanism to represent it at all.

Section 2

A calm river with the occasional flash flood

〜

Most of the time: diffusion

A river's water level drifts up and down a little with rainfall and evaporation — small, smooth, continuous changes. That's ordinary Brownian motion, the same noise term Black-Scholes already uses.

⚡

Rarely: a jump

Every so often a dam upstream releases water without warning. The level doesn't drift up over an hour — it jumps there in seconds. FX around a scheduled release is exactly this river.

+

Merton's move: add, don't replace

Keep the continuous diffusion for normal trading, and add a separate, independent process whose only job is to occasionally inject a sudden discrete jump.

✓

Both regimes, one model

The result tells two true stories about the same underlying at once: "most of the time this diffuses quietly" and "once in a while, without warning, it jumps."

At 8:30am ET on NFP Friday, the "dam" opens: a number hits the wire, algos and humans reprice in an instant, and the pair can gap through several pips of quoted spread and dozens of pips of "normal" range before the next real two-sided market forms.

Section 4

The formula: one new term bolted on

Black-Scholes' SDE, then Merton's addition.

Plain Black-Scholes

dS/S = μ dt + σ dW

Ordinary continuous drift (μ dt) plus continuous Brownian noise (σ dW). Smooth, never a break, whatever the zoom level.

Merton jump-diffusion

dS/S = μ dt + σ dW + dJ

The exact same two terms, plus dJ — zero almost all the time, and a sudden multiplicative jolt on the rare instant a jump actually lands.

Everything new lives inside dJ, and it's built from two independent ingredients stacked together: a process deciding when jumps happen, and a distribution deciding how big each one is once it happens.

λ

Jump intensity

The average rate of jump arrivals per unit time. Over a short interval dt, the chance of exactly one jump is λ·dt. Bigger λ = more frequent jumps.

μⱼ

Mean jump size

Controls direction and rough magnitude once a jump occurs. μⱼ = 0 means jumps are unbiased — as likely up as down.

σⱼ

Jump size spread

How variable jump sizes are, jump to jump. Small σⱼ = jumps are all about the same size; large σⱼ = some barely move price, others are violent.

λ or T below zero isn't a smaller jump risk — it's a nonsense input. Below zero, or non-numeric, both figures show "—" instead of a false answer.

This is the same Poisson process behind the coin-flip intuition: over a short interval, the chance of a jump is λ·dt, and stacking those tiny chances up over a full horizon T gives an expected count of λT jumps, with probability 1 − e^(−λT) of seeing at least one. A near-zero λ collapses this back to plain Black-Scholes — jumps essentially never happen.

Section 6

Why this produces fat tails, not just a wider bell curve

Mixing "usually calm" with "occasionally violent" is not the same as one wider normal distribution.

Pure diffusion (GBM) — thin tails Jump-diffusion — peaked center + fat tails

With probability roughly (1 − λΔt), no jump occurred and the return looks like ordinary diffusion — narrow, clustered near zero. With the remaining small probability, a jump landed on top of that diffusion, pushing the outcome further out. A mixture of "mostly narrow" and "occasionally wider" is not itself a single normal distribution — it has extra mass out in the tails relative to a normal with the same overall variance. That's excess kurtosis (leptokurtosis): a taller, narrower center plus fatter tails than Gaussian.

Section 7

The puzzle: why does the market charge more for far-out-of-the-money options?

Plug one flat σ into Black-Scholes and every strike should price consistently. Real markets don't behave that way.

If you back out the σ that would have produced each observed market price (the "implied volatility"), you don't get a flat line across strikes — you get a curve, usually higher at the wings than at the money. That's the volatility smile (or, when asymmetric, the volatility skew).

Deep out-of-the-money options only pay off on a large, sudden move before expiry — exactly the kind of move a pure diffusion process treats as nearly impossible over typical horizons, but that jump risk explicitly assigns real probability to. Price that option with plain Black-Scholes at one flat σ, and you systematically underprice it relative to a market that correctly prices in the chance of a jump.

The resolution

The market corrects for jump risk the flat-sigma formula can't represent by demanding a higher implied volatility at the wings — the only lever the Black-Scholes formula has to spit out the "correct," jump-aware price. The smile is, in this reading, the options market's fingerprint of jump-diffusion reality being forced through a Black-Scholes-shaped pricing formula. (Jump risk is one of two classical explanations — the other is stochastic volatility, not covered here.)

Section 8

NFP Friday versus an average Tuesday

The same currency pair, two very different honest values of λ.

Quiet Tuesday

No major scheduled release. Price moves are dominated by ordinary order flow and cross-market spillover — the diffusion term. A jump-diffusion fit on this kind of day alone would want λ close to zero: jumps essentially don't happen, and the diffusion-only assumption is a reasonably honest description.

8:30am ET, NFP Friday

The timing is certain — everyone knows when — but the number's value, and the market's reaction, is unknown until it prints. A large share of the day's total realized variance can concentrate into that one window, often with a real tradeable-liquidity gap rather than smooth ticks.

An honest jump-diffusion model wouldn't treat λ as one flat constant for the whole day — it would want λ to spike sharply for the few minutes around the release and sit near zero the rest of the time. That's exactly why option desks quote separate "event vol" for the days around a known release, distinct from the base term structure — and why "vol crush" hits right after the print, once the jump has either happened or the window has passed without one.

Section 9

This model versus what practitioners actually run

Merton's original formula
  • Closed-form, but leans on a diversifiability assumption that doesn't obviously hold for FX event risk (the same NFP print jolts other pairs, rates, and equities too)
  • λ, μⱼ, σⱼ are genuinely hard to separate from σ using only a historical return series — notoriously ill-conditioned estimation
  • Treats a jump as one clean instantaneous step, when real post-release action is often a fast, wide burst over a few seconds
What desks use day to day
  • Local/stochastic-vol and Lévy-process models calibrated directly to the observed smile, rather than assumed from theory
  • Quoting "event vol" directly — separate implied vols around known releases, without ever fitting λ, μⱼ, σⱼ explicitly
  • Kou's (2002) double-exponential model — separate, independently tunable up-jump and down-jump parameters for markets that gap harder one way than the other
Fat tails alone aren't proof a jump model would help

Seeing high excess kurtosis or a large historical CVaR in a trade-return series confirms the empirical symptom jump risk predicts. It is not, by itself, evidence that building a parametric jump model and layering it into a strategy would improve anything out-of-sample — that would need to be tested, not assumed from the theory being a good story.

Section 10

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

What does λ (lambda) control in the Merton jump-diffusion model?

λ is the jump intensity — the average rate of jump arrivals per unit time. Jump size is governed separately by μⱼ and σⱼ.
Question 2

Why can't plain Black-Scholes represent an NFP-style gap?

Geometric Brownian motion must pass through every intermediate price level — it has no term that can produce a genuine skip.
Question 3

Mixing rare jumps into ordinary diffusion produces a return distribution that is:

A mixture of "mostly narrow, no-jump" outcomes with "rare, wider, jump" outcomes is not a single normal distribution — it has excess kurtosis (leptokurtosis).
Question 4

Why does a pure Black-Scholes model systematically underprice deep out-of-the-money options?

Those options only pay off on a genuine jump; a diffusion-only model treats that scenario as nearly impossible, so its price comes out too low relative to a market that prices real jump risk — which is exactly what shows up as the volatility smile.
Question 5

With λ = 2 jumps/year and T = 0.5 years, what's the expected number of jumps and P(at least one)?

Expected jumps = λT = 2 × 0.5 = 1.0. P(≥1 jump) = 1 − e^(−λT) = 1 − e^(−1) ≈ 0.632, or 63.2%.
Section 11

What to carry forward

01

Black-Scholes paths are continuous by construction — no mechanism exists for a genuine gap, so it assigns near-zero probability to a large move in a short window.

02

Merton adds a jump term dJ on top of ordinary diffusion — zero almost always, a sudden multiplicative jolt on the rare instant a Poisson-governed jump lands.

03

Three new parameters drive it: λ (how often), μⱼ (average direction/size), σⱼ (how variable each jump is).

04

Mixing rare jumps into diffusion produces excess kurtosis — a taller, narrower center plus fatter tails than a normal curve, not just a wider bell.

05

Jump risk is one of the two classical explanations for the volatility smile — the market prices in jumps a flat-σ Black-Scholes model can't represent by charging more at the wings.

06

In practice, desks calibrate smile-matching models or quote "event vol" directly, far more often than fitting λ, μⱼ, σⱼ from Merton's original formula.

What this now lets you do

Recognise why a continuous-path model can't represent a genuine market gap, and name the mechanism (a Poisson jump term) that fixes it. Read the volatility smile as a symptom, not a market inefficiency — and connect fat left tails and elevated excess kurtosis in a real return series to the same underlying jump-risk story, whether or not a parametric jump model is ever fit.
