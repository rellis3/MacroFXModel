The IV Smile & Skew, and What It Does to Price

Implied volatility is not one number per expiry. Every strike carries its own, and plotted against strike the numbers form a curve. Its height says what a move costs, its tilt says which direction costs more, and, through vanna, a falling market that lifts vol can change how dealers hedge. This guide shows how to read the curve on the OI Lab chart, what it implies for price, and where the evidence stops.

The whole idea

One curve: lowest at the money, higher in the wings

Same expiry, same underlying, a different price of volatility at every strike. The tilt toward the left is the skew. On equity indices the downside wing sits higher, because buyers pay up for crash protection.

Section 1

What the chart is actually drawing

On the OI Lab terrain card each outer line is one expiry: a strike's implied vol minus the at-the-money vol, in vol points.

0

The pinch

At the money, the extra vol is zero by definition, so every line crosses there. The pinch is the at-the-money strike.

↔

Sideways distance

How far a line sits from the pinch is how many vol points that strike costs more than the at-the-money strike.

≋

One line per expiry

Near expiries (bright) bend hardest in the wings; far ones are flatter.

⚠

The in-the-money half is faint

It is noisy and mostly echoes the other side. Read the bright out-of-the-money half.

Section 2

Skew in two numbers

Practitioners summarise the curve rather than eyeballing it. Measured at the same distance x on each side:

Risk reversal — the tilt

RR(x) = IV(call at +x) − IV(put at −x). On equity indices it is negative: the downside wing is richer. The more negative, the steeper the skew.

Butterfly — the curvature

BF(x) = ½[IV(+x) + IV(−x)] − IV(ATM). How much both wings sit above the middle, whichever way they tilt. A large butterfly means the market is paying for tails on both sides.

RR → which side
+
BF → how much tail
Where these come from

FX desks quote risk reversals and butterflies directly, at the 25-delta strike. The Theory Lab's FX options quoting guide builds them from scratch.

Section 3

Build a smile, then move the market

IV(x) = σ + a·x + b·x², with x the percent distance from spot. Set the skew and the curvature, then move price and watch the at-the-money vol.

Try it
Skew a (vol points per 1%)−0.50
Curvature b0.05
Spot move−3%
At-the-money vol is held at 20%. The smile is assumed to stay fixed against strikes (a “sticky strike” assumption), so a fall in spot moves the new at-the-money point up the put wing.
Put at −3%: 21.95% · call at +3%: 18.95%
RR(3%) = −3.00 · BF(3%) = 0.45 · ATM vol after the move: 21.95%
Section 4

Why a falling market lifts vol, and what that does to hedging

The skew turns a spot move into a vol move. Vanna turns a vol move into hedging flow.

Spot falls
the old at-the-money strike is now out of the money
→
New at-the-money vol is higher
the smile stays put, spot slides up the put wing
→
Option deltas change
vanna: delta changes when vol changes
→
Dealers re-hedge
direction depends on who is short what
The last link is an assumption

Which way the extra hedging pushes price depends on whether dealers are net long or short those options. Open interest does not tell you. The OI Lab uses the standard convention (dealers long calls, short puts), and every sign flips if that is wrong.

Section 5 · worked read

SPX on 20 October 2026: the downside costs far more

Read off the OI Lab terrain card (values approximate).

Puts, below the pinch

The nearest expiry reaches +4 vol points at about 7620, roughly 1.3% below the pinch (7722).

Calls, above the pinch

The same expiry only reaches +4 near 7950, roughly 3% above it. The upside is far cheaper.

The read

Strong put skew: the market is paying much more for protection against a drop than for an equal rally. The put wall (7665.7) and major wall (7615.7) sit in that expensive zone, which is where a fall would reprice options fastest. This describes how options are priced. It does not say a fall is likely.

Section 6

How it trades

Traders do not trade “the smile” directly. They trade pieces of it.

RR

Risk reversal

Buy one wing, sell the other (a long call against a short put, for example). Pays when the skew moves your way. Often used to express a view on where the premium sits.

🛡

Hedging cost

Steep put skew means protection is expensive. Collars and put spreads exist to cut that cost by selling the other wing.

📅

Calendars

Near expiries bend harder than far ones. Calendar spreads trade that difference around an event.

📊

Relative value

Desks compare today’s skew with its own history. A steep skew against its usual range is read as unusual fear, not as a direction.

Section 7

What it does to price: the mechanism and the evidence

The textbook mechanism

Skew makes vol rise as price falls. That feeds vanna, which can change dealer hedging. Protection demand also shows up as extra put buying near the money in a selloff. These are real forces, but how big they are against everything else in the market is an empirical question.

What our own tests found

Implied vol forecasts range better than realised vol (7 of 7). The gamma sign predicts next-day range on NQ. Walls, flips and max pain did not beat placebo levels, and no positioning signal predicted direction.

Not tested yet

Whether skew steepness predicts the day’s range or its down-versus-up asymmetry. It is registered as a candidate, not a result. Use the smile as context for how much a move costs, not as a signal for which way price goes.

Section 8

Four ways to misread the fan

The pinch and your spot can differ

The smile is live from Cboe, 15 minutes delayed. Levels and open interest come from the last saved snapshot, so the pinch and the spot line can sit slightly apart.

Jagged wings are thin strikes

Far-out strikes trade rarely, so their implied vol jumps around. Do not read shape into kinks.

Only SPX, NDX and RUT have a smile here

Gold, FX and commodities come from CME, which gives one vol per expiry. The fan there shows gamma ridges, and vanna is only an estimate.

Skew is a price, not a forecast

A steep put wing says protection is expensive. It does not say the protection will pay.

Section 9

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Why do all the lines on the fan meet at one point?

The vertical axis is the extra vol over the at-the-money strike. At the money that difference is zero by construction, for every expiry, so every line crosses there.
Question 2

With IV(x) = 20 − 0.5x + 0.05x² (x in percent), what happens to the at-the-money vol if spot falls 3% and the smile stays fixed against strikes?

The new at-the-money point sits where the old curve read x = −3: 20 + 1.5 + 0.45 = 21.95. That is the spot-vol link: a fall in price lifts the vol at the money.
Question 3

A steep put skew on SPX tells you…

Skew is a price of protection. It reflects demand and risk appetite. Our tests found no positioning signal that predicts direction, and the dealer sign behind any hedging story is an assumption.
Section 10

What to carry forward

01

Implied vol is a curve across strikes: lowest at the money, higher in the wings. The fan draws one curve per expiry as extra vol over the at-the-money strike.

02

Risk reversal measures the tilt, and the butterfly measures the curvature. Equity indices carry a negative risk reversal because protection is bought.

03

With the smile fixed against strikes, a fall in spot moves the at-the-money point up the put wing, so at-the-money vol rises.

04

Vanna turns that vol change into hedging flow, but its direction rests on an assumption about who is short what.

05

Evidence so far: implied vol forecasts range, and the gamma sign relates to range. Skew predicting range or direction has not been tested.

06

Read the bright out-of-the-money half, expect noise in thin wings, and remember that only SPX, NDX and RUT have a real smile on the lab.
