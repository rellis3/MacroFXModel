Multiple Testing & the Look-Elsewhere Effect

If you run 70 statistical tests at the usual 5% threshold, you should expect several to look "significant" even when nothing real is happening — this lesson is about why, and what to do instead.

CONCEPT — a validation discipline, not a signal ~13 min read Prereqs: Bayesian Inference (helpful for the FDR section)

Why this matters

Every statistical test you run has some chance of being wrong even when it is done correctly. That is not a flaw in the method — it is the price of using probability at all. A single well-designed test with a 5% false-positive rate is a reasonable, disciplined thing to run. The problem starts when you don't run one test. You run seventy: one per currency pair, one per regime, one per parameter combination, one per session-of-day, one per year. Each one individually still carries only a 5% chance of a false alarm. But the chance that at least one of the seventy false-alarms compounds fast — and by the time you've run seventy tests, finding a "significant" result is no longer surprising. It is close to guaranteed, whether or not there is anything real to find.

This matters here specifically because the natural way to explore an FX/macro idea is exactly the shape that breaks naive significance testing: slice by pair, slice by regime, slice by session, slice by holding period, slice by day-of-week. Each slice is a separate look at the data, and each look is a separate roll of the dice. If you don't account for how many looks you took, you will regularly convince yourself that noise is signal — and you will do it with a clean, defensible-looking p-value in hand. This lesson gives you the machinery to tell the difference: how fast the false-positive rate grows with the number of tests, and the two standard corrections (Bonferroni and False Discovery Rate) for keeping it under control.

Plain-English intuition

The lottery-ticket analogy

Imagine a lottery where each ticket has a 5% chance of winning, purely by chance, with no skill involved. If you buy one ticket, a win would be a small surprise — 1-in-20 odds. If you buy seventy tickets, a win is not a surprise at all; in fact you'd be a little unlucky not to get at least one winner. Now suppose someone hands you a folder of seventy tickets, only shows you the one winning ticket, and says "look, I found a winner — this system works." You'd rightly ask: how many tickets did you buy in total, and how many of them won? Backtesting many pair/regime/parameter slices and reporting only the ones that cleared your significance bar is functionally the same move. The "winning ticket" (the significant slice) is not evidence of skill unless you can also show it survives after accounting for how many tickets you bought.

The math, step by step

Start with a single hypothesis test. You have a null hypothesis — call it \(H_0\) — which represents "nothing interesting is happening" (e.g. "this strategy's mean return is zero," or "this slice has no edge"). You compute a p-value: the probability, if \(H_0\) were actually true, of seeing a result at least as extreme as the one you observed, purely from sampling noise. You then compare that p-value to a significance threshold \(\alpha\) (commonly 0.05). If \(p < \alpha\), you call the result "statistically significant" and reject \(H_0\).

Crucially, \(\alpha\) is defined as the false-positive rate of a single test: if \(H_0\) is really true, the test will still incorrectly reject it with probability exactly \(\alpha\), by construction. A 5% threshold means a 5% chance of a false alarm on any one test where there is genuinely nothing there. That is an acceptable, well-understood risk for one test. The question this lesson answers is: what happens to that risk when you run many tests at once?

\(H_0\)the null hypothesis for one test — "no real effect / no edge in this slice"
\(\alpha\)the significance threshold for a single test (the false-positive rate you accept per test, e.g. 0.05)
\(p\)the p-value of a single test — probability of a result this extreme under \(H_0\)
\(m\)the number of independent hypothesis tests run (pairs × regimes × parameter sets × …)
FWERfamily-wise error rate — probability of at least one false positive across all \(m\) tests
\(\alpha_{\text{Bonf}}\)the Bonferroni-corrected per-test threshold, \(\alpha / m\)
FDRfalse discovery rate — the expected proportion of "discoveries" (rejections) that are actually false positives
\(p_{(1)}, \dots, p_{(m)}\)the \(m\) p-values sorted from smallest to largest (rank 1 = smallest)
\(k\)the rank of a p-value in the sorted (Benjamini-Hochberg) list — used to compute its critical threshold
\(q\)the target FDR level (the BH analogue of \(\alpha\), e.g. 0.10 for "allow 10% of discoveries to be false")

Deriving the family-wise error rate

Suppose you run \(m\) tests, each independently testing a true null hypothesis (nothing real, by construction, in every single one) at threshold \(\alpha\). For any one test, the probability of not producing a false positive is \(1-\alpha\) (95% of the time, at \(\alpha=0.05\), it correctly stays below the significance bar). If the tests are independent, the probability that all \(m\) of them avoid a false positive is the product of each individual "avoid it" probability:

\[ P(\text{no false positives in } m \text{ tests}) = (1-\alpha)^m \]

The family-wise error rate (FWER) is the complement — the probability that at least one of the \(m\) tests produces a false positive purely by chance:

\[ \text{FWER} = 1-(1-\alpha)^m \]

This function grows toward 1 quickly as \(m\) increases, even though each individual test is still exactly as reliable as it was on its own — \(\alpha\) per test never changed. What changed is that you gave chance more opportunities to produce a fluke, and the probability that none of those opportunities pays off shrinks fast. This is the mathematical core of the "look-elsewhere effect" — a phrase from particle physics, where searching across many bins of a histogram (many possible masses a new particle could appear at) for a bump inflates the chance of finding a "significant" bump somewhere purely from statistical fluctuation, even with no new particle present. The 2012 Higgs boson discovery announcement is well known partly because physicists were explicit about correcting for exactly this effect before claiming a discovery — the same look-elsewhere correction this lesson describes for FX slices.

The Bonferroni correction

The simplest fix is to shrink the per-test threshold so that the family-wise error rate, not the per-test error rate, is held at \(\alpha\). The Bonferroni correction sets the per-test threshold to:

\[ \alpha_{\text{Bonf}} = \frac{\alpha}{m} \]

and only calls a test significant if \(p < \alpha_{\text{Bonf}}\). This is derived from a simple inequality (Boole's inequality / the union bound): the probability of at least one false positive across \(m\) tests is at most the sum of the individual probabilities, so \(\text{FWER} \le m \cdot \alpha_{\text{Bonf}} = \alpha\) by construction — this bound holds regardless of whether the tests are independent, which is Bonferroni's real virtue. The cost is conservatism: as \(m\) grows, the bar for any single test to count as significant becomes very high, and Bonferroni can be needlessly strict when the tests are correlated rather than independent — as they usually are in FX. Seventy pair/regime slices are not seventy independent draws: EURUSD, GBPUSD and AUDUSD all move partly off a shared USD factor, so a macro surprise that moves the dollar can make several "slices" light up together. That shared variation means the tests carry less independent information than \(m\) implies, so Bonferroni's worst-case correction (built for the independent case) ends up over-punishing real, correlated effects — treating seventy correlated looks as if they were seventy independent ones.

False Discovery Rate (Benjamini-Hochberg)

Bonferroni controls the probability of any false positive at all, which is a very strict bar once \(m\) is large. In many practical settings — including exploratory research across many slices — a more useful control target is the false discovery rate (FDR): not "never have a false positive," but "keep the proportion of my discoveries that are false positives below some target \(q\)." If you flag 10 slices as significant and your FDR is controlled at 10%, the guarantee is that on average roughly 1 of those 10 flagged slices is expected to be a false positive — not that any specific one is safe, and not that zero false positives will occur across your whole research program.

The standard procedure, from Benjamini & Hochberg (1995), works like this:

  1. Run all \(m\) tests and collect their p-values.
  2. Sort them from smallest to largest: \(p_{(1)} \le p_{(2)} \le \dots \le p_{(m)}\), so rank \(k\) is the position of \(p_{(k)}\) in that sorted order.
  3. Find the largest rank \(k\) such that:
\[ p_{(k)} \le \frac{k}{m} \, q \]
  1. Declare every test with rank \(1, 2, \dots, k\) (i.e. every p-value at or below that cutoff p-value) significant. Everything ranked above \(k\) is not.

Intuitively, the threshold each p-value has to clear scales up with its rank: the smallest p-value only has to beat \(q/m\) (close to Bonferroni's strictness), but the tenth-smallest gets to beat the more lenient \(10q/m\), and so on. This staircase is what lets BH flag more true effects than Bonferroni while still keeping the proportion of false flags under control — it trades "zero tolerance for any false positive" for "a bounded, known rate of false positives among what I call a discovery," which is usually the more useful guarantee for exploratory, many-slice research.

Worked example

Seventy slices, one significance threshold

Suppose you slice your data 70 ways — say 7 pairs × 5 regimes × 2 holding periods, or any similar breakdown — and for each slice you run a test of "does this slice have non-zero mean return" at the standard \(\alpha = 0.05\). Assume, for the sake of the calculation, that in truth none of the 70 slices has any real edge — every one of them is pure noise around zero. Using the FWER formula from above with \(m=70\):

\[ \text{FWER} = 1-(1-0.05)^{70} = 1 - (0.95)^{70} \approx 1 - 0.0275 \approx 0.972 \]

That is a 97.2% chance that at least one of the 70 slices comes back "statistically significant" at the 5% level, purely from noise, even though nothing real is there. If you expect a false positive rate of roughly \(\alpha\) per slice, the expected number of false positives across 70 pure-noise slices is \(70 \times 0.05 = 3.5\) — so finding "3 out of 70 slices look significant" is not a discovery. It is almost exactly what you'd expect to see if there were no edge anywhere at all. This is the numeric backbone of the house rule already written into this repo's own project memory: "finding a few 'winners' among 70 slices is what noise does." The math above is why that sentence is true, not just a cautious-sounding guess.

Now apply the corrections. Bonferroni: \(\alpha_{\text{Bonf}} = 0.05 / 70 \approx 0.000714\) — a slice's raw p-value would need to be below roughly 0.07% (about 70× stricter than the uncorrected 5%) to count as significant. That is a demanding bar, and given that FX pairs share common factors (a correlated, not independent, set of 70 tests), it is probably stricter than it needs to be. Benjamini-Hochberg is more forgiving: if, say, your smallest p-value among the 70 slices is \(p_{(1)} = 0.0009\), it needs to beat \(\frac{1}{70}(0.10) \approx 0.00143\) to survive at a 10% target FDR — a rank-1 test almost matches Bonferroni's strictness — but a slice ranked 10th only needs to beat \(\frac{10}{70}(0.10) \approx 0.0143\), roughly 20× more lenient than Bonferroni's flat bar for every rank. That staircase is the entire mechanical difference between the two corrections.

How practitioners actually use this

In quantitative finance this problem has its own well-developed vocabulary, most notably backtest overfitting: trying many strategy variants (parameter sets, entry rules, universes) against the same historical data and reporting only the ones that looked good. Marcos López de Prado and collaborators formalized this with the Probability of Backtest Overfitting (PBO) and the Deflated Sharpe Ratio (DSR) — a Sharpe ratio adjustment that explicitly discounts for the number of trials run and the variance across them, conceptually the same move as Bonferroni/BH but tailored to Sharpe ratios instead of p-values. The practical habits that follow from this literature, and from the physics/biostatistics multiple-testing tradition more broadly, are: (1) log every test you run, including the ones that failed — a file of "here are the 70 slices I checked" is what lets you compute \(m\) honestly later; (2) pre-register what counts as a discovery before looking at results, so you can't quietly redefine "significant" after seeing which slice happened to win; (3) treat the single best-performing slice out of many with real suspicion — its rank alone (being #1 of 70) tells you almost nothing, because the best of many noise draws is expected to look good by construction, regardless of whether any of the draws contain real signal; and (4) prefer out-of-sample confirmation on data that had no part in the slicing/selection process over any post-hoc statistical correction — corrections manage the false-positive rate of the searches you already ran, they do not manufacture the OOS evidence a real effect would produce on fresh data.

That sentence is asking you to do, in your head, exactly the FWER calculation worked through above: count \(m\) (the number of cells you sliced the data into), compute or at least estimate what noise alone would produce at that \(m\), and only treat a slice as a real survivor if it clears that chance-baseline and holds up in-sample consistently and, per Lego Principle 5, on a genuine out-of-sample split.

Common pitfalls

Where this goes wrong in practice

Reporting only the winners. The single most common failure is simply not disclosing \(m\) — showing the 3 significant slices out of a study without mentioning it checked 70. Without \(m\), a reader (including future-you) cannot tell a real finding from an expected noise artifact.

Treating "best of many" as "significant." The slice with the lowest p-value out of 70 is, definitionally, expected to have an unusually low p-value even under pure noise — someone has to be the minimum of 70 draws. A low p-value on your single best slice, considered alone and out of context of the other 69, is close to uninformative.

Applying Bonferroni honestly to correlated tests and getting nothing. Bonferroni assumes worst-case independence; FX slices sharing USD, EUR, risk-sentiment or a volatility regime are far from independent. Applying Bonferroni naively to 70 heavily correlated slices can suppress real effects along with the noise — it is a safe but blunt instrument, not the uniquely correct answer. This is precisely why BH/FDR, or slice-count reduction via grouping correlated slices together first, is usually the more practical tool in FX work than literal Bonferroni.

Re-running the same test after seeing the data ("p-hacking"). Multiple testing isn't only about slicing by pair or regime — trying several lookback windows, thresholds, or filters on the same slice until one clears significance is the same problem in disguise, and it inflates \(m\) just as surely as adding more pairs does, even though it can feel like "refining one idea."

Confusing statistical significance with economic significance. Even a correctly-FDR-controlled discovery only tells you the effect is unlikely to be pure noise — it says nothing about whether the effect size survives realistic costs, is large enough to trade, or remains stable outside the sample it was found in. This correction is necessary, not sufficient (see Lego Principle 5's cost/OOS discipline).

Self-test

Q1. In your own words, what does \(\alpha = 0.05\) promise you for a single hypothesis test — and what does it NOT promise once you've run many tests?
It promises that if the null hypothesis is actually true, there is a 5% chance this one test will incorrectly reject it — a false-positive rate of 5% per test. It does not promise anything about the chance of a false positive across a whole batch of tests: run enough tests at \(\alpha=0.05\) and a false "significant" result becomes likely, even though each individual test is still exactly as reliable as advertised (5% per test never changed). The mistake is applying the single-test guarantee to a multi-test situation.
Q2. Derive, don't just state: why is the family-wise error rate \(1-(1-\alpha)^m\) rather than simply \(m\alpha\)?
For independent tests, the probability that all \(m\) tests avoid a false positive is the product of each one avoiding it: \((1-\alpha)^m\), since each test's outcome doesn't affect the others. FWER is "at least one false positive," which is the complement of "zero false positives," so \(\text{FWER} = 1-(1-\alpha)^m\). The simpler \(m\alpha\) is actually the Bonferroni union-bound approximation — it's an upper bound on the true FWER (via \(P(A \cup B) \le P(A)+P(B)\)) that holds even when tests are correlated, and it's close to \(1-(1-\alpha)^m\) when \(\alpha\) is small and \(m\) isn't huge, but they are not the same quantity and \(m\alpha\) can exceed 1 while a true probability cannot.
Q3. Why is Bonferroni often too strict when your 70 tests are correlated — for instance, when all your slices are FX pairs that share USD exposure?
Bonferroni's \(\alpha/m\) threshold is derived to guarantee FWER \(\le \alpha\) in the worst case, which is when all \(m\) tests are independent and therefore each one carries the maximum possible amount of unique information. When tests are correlated — as EURUSD, GBPUSD and AUDUSD are, sharing a common USD factor — a single macro shock can move several "independent-looking" slices together, so the effective number of independent pieces of information is less than \(m\). Bonferroni still divides by the full \(m\), producing a stricter (smaller) threshold than the correlation structure actually requires, which can bury a real, shared effect along with the noise.
Q4. What does controlling the False Discovery Rate at \(q=0.10\) actually guarantee — and what does it NOT guarantee?
It guarantees that, on average, no more than about 10% of the tests you flag as "significant" (your discoveries) are expected to be false positives — a bound on the expected proportion of false discoveries among your discoveries. It does NOT guarantee that any specific flagged slice is real, does not bound the absolute number of false positives (a large batch can still produce several false discoveries even at 10%), and does not say anything about tests you didn't flag. It's a rate guarantee over the discovered set, not a certificate on any individual result.
Q5. Why doesn't a single very low p-value on your BEST slice out of 70 tell you anything on its own?
Even if all 70 slices are pure noise with no real effect, one of them has to be the minimum p-value of the batch — that's simple order statistics, not evidence. The expected minimum p-value among 70 independent noise draws is already fairly small purely by chance (roughly on the order of \(1/(m+1) \approx 0.014\) for the smallest of 70 uniform p-values under the null). Reporting only that one number, without disclosing it was chosen as the best of 70 candidates, hides exactly the information (the value of \(m\), and where this p-value ranked) needed to judge whether it's a real finding or the expected extreme of a noise distribution.
Q6. You test 70 slices and find the smallest p-value is 0.0009. Using the worked example's Benjamini-Hochberg numbers, would this survive at rank 1 with a target FDR of \(q=0.10\)? What has to also be true for you to trust it beyond the statistics?
Rank 1's BH critical value is \(\frac{1}{70}(0.10) \approx 0.00143\). Since \(0.0009 < 0.00143\), it passes the BH cutoff at rank 1 (and, for reference, it also clears Bonferroni's flat \(0.000714\) bar only if it's below that — 0.0009 is actually just above Bonferroni's 0.000714, so it would fail the stricter Bonferroni test while passing BH, illustrating exactly how the two corrections can disagree). Passing either correction only means the result is unlikely to be pure multiple-testing noise. To actually trust it as a tradeable edge you still need it to be in-sample consistent (not carried by one lucky sub-period), to hold up on a genuine out-of-sample split with realistic costs, and to have an economically sensible mechanism — statistical survival is necessary, not sufficient.
