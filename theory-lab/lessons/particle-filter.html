The Particle Filter (Sequential Monte Carlo)

When a hidden state's dynamics stop being linear and its noise stops being Gaussian, the Kalman filter's neat closed form breaks — a weighted swarm of simulated guesses can track the state anyway.

CONCEPT — not built or tested anywhere in this repo ~18 min read Prereqs: Kalman Filter & Adaptive Relationships, Bayesian Inference
TL;DR
  • The Kalman filter's closed-form predict/update recursion only works when the state transition and observation equations are linear and the noise is Gaussian. Break either assumption and there is generally no closed form for the posterior at all.
  • A particle filter approximates the posterior with a weighted swarm of samples ("particles") instead of a mean and a variance — each particle is a candidate value for the hidden state, and its weight is how much the observed data actually supports it.
  • One cycle is four steps: initialize particles from the prior, propagate each through the (possibly nonlinear) state transition, weight each by the likelihood of the observation given that particle's state, and resample proportional to weight when the swarm has gotten too lopsided.
  • The effective-sample-size diagnostic \(N_{\text{eff}} = 1/\sum_i w_i^2\) measures how lopsided the weights are — it drops toward 1 as one particle absorbs nearly all the credibility (particle degeneracy) and stays near \(N\) when weight is spread evenly; resampling is triggered when it falls below a threshold, commonly \(N/2\).
  • The worked example tracks a state whose process noise is a fat-tailed mixture (mostly calm, occasionally a large jump) — exactly the kind of non-Gaussian shock a linear Kalman filter's single Gaussian \(Q\) cannot represent honestly.
  • This is a concept lesson only: explaining Sequential Monte Carlo is not a claim it produces edge in this repo. Nothing here is built, prototyped, or validated.
Section 01

Why this matters

The Kalman filter lesson built a clean recursion — predict, then update — for tracking a hidden state like a drifting hedge ratio. That recursion has a closed form only because of a specific, convenient mathematical coincidence: when the prior belief is Gaussian and the observation is related to the state linearly, with Gaussian noise, the posterior comes out exactly Gaussian too (conjugacy). That is precisely why two numbers — a mean \(\hat{x}\) and a variance \(P\) — are enough to summarize the entire belief at every step.

That coincidence stops being available the moment either assumption breaks. Two common ways it breaks, both routine in real systems: the state transition or observation relationship is nonlinear (a squared term, a saturating function, a regime-dependent coefficient), or the noise is not Gaussian — it has fat tails, or it is a mixture of "mostly small nudges, occasionally a large jump" rather than one single bell curve. Once that happens, the true posterior can be skewed, multimodal (genuinely two or more separate "clusters" of plausible states, not one bump), or otherwise shaped nothing like a Gaussian — and there is generally no formula for it. A linear Kalman filter forced onto such a problem doesn't fail loudly; it quietly returns a Gaussian approximation that can misestimate both the state and, just as importantly, how confident to be about it.

The particle filter — the workhorse algorithm of a broader family called Sequential Monte Carlo (SMC) — sidesteps the need for a closed form entirely. Instead of representing the belief about the hidden state with two numbers, it represents it with many concrete guesses, each one scored by how well it explains what was actually observed. No linearity is required, and no particular noise shape is required, because the algorithm never needs to solve an integral in closed form — it approximates that integral with samples instead. The price is that the answer is now a Monte Carlo approximation with its own sampling variance, not an exact number — a trade-off worth naming plainly before going further.

💡
Plain-English intuition

A crowd of guessers instead of one confident estimate

Recall the Kalman filter's boat-at-night analogy: one radar ping a minute, one running estimate of position, blended with each new ping by a derived gain. Now imagine a different setup — the boat's drift is not simple straight-line motion (maybe it's caught in a current that curves unpredictably), and occasionally, rarely, the boat doesn't drift at all but instead gets shoved hard by a wave (a large, sudden jump — not a small Gaussian nudge). A single confident estimate is a poor tool for this: it can't represent "probably here, but there's a real chance it just got shoved somewhere else entirely."

So instead of one guesser, put a thousand guessers on the problem, each holding their own candidate position. Each minute, every guesser moves their own candidate forward using their own understanding of how the boat drifts (including, for some fraction of guessers, "maybe a wave just hit"). Then the radar ping arrives. Score every guesser by how consistent their current candidate is with the ping — a guesser sitting right where the ping implies the boat should be gets a high score; a guesser far off gets a low one. If the scores get badly lopsided — one guesser wildly more credible than everyone else, meaning the crowd has collapsed onto a single opinion — replace the crowd: keep the credible guessers (cloning some of them), and quietly retire the ones who turned out to be way off, so the finite population of guessers is spent representing possibilities that are actually still in play, not ones that have already been ruled out. That's a particle filter: a crowd of guesses, re-scored every step, periodically refreshed so the crowd doesn't waste itself on a few dead ends.

Section 02

The math, step by step

The setup generalizes the Kalman filter's state-space model by allowing the transition and observation functions to be nonlinear, and the noise to be drawn from any distribution at all — not only Gaussian:

\[ x_t = f(x_{t-1}, w_t), \qquad y_t = h(x_t, v_t) \]
f(·)the (possibly nonlinear) state transition function
h(·)the (possibly nonlinear) observation function
w_t, v_tprocess/observation noise — drawn from any distribution, not necessarily Gaussian

Compare this directly to the Kalman filter lesson's \(x_t = x_{t-1} + w_t\) and \(y_t = H_t x_t + v_t\): those are the special linear-Gaussian case of exactly this same state-space idea. Nothing about "a hidden state that evolves and produces noisy observations" has changed — only the shape of \(f\), \(h\), and the noise has been generalized.

Instead of tracking a mean and variance, a particle filter represents the belief about \(x_t\) — formally, the posterior distribution \(p(x_t \mid y_{1:t})\) given everything observed through time \(t\) — as a weighted collection of \(N\) concrete sample values, the particles:

\[ p(x_t \mid y_{1:t}) \approx \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_t^{(i)}\, \delta\!\left(x_t - x_t^{(i)}\right) \]
x_t^{(i)}particle i's candidate value for the hidden state at time t
w_t^{(i)}particle i's weight — how much credibility the data gives that candidate, normalized so all weights sum to 1
δ(·)the Dirac delta — a spike of probability located exactly at the particle's value, with zero width

Read this as: "my belief about the hidden state is a pile of spikes, one at each particle's location, each spike as tall as that particle's weight." More particles concentrated in one region of the state space, with higher weight, means that region is believed more strongly — this is a discrete, sample-based stand-in for a full continuous probability density, and it can approximate any shape — skewed, multimodal, fat-tailed — given enough particles, unlike a Gaussian, which can only ever be one symmetric bump.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Instead of describing your belief with one tidy sentence ("it's probably around here, give or take this much"), imagine describing it with a pile of sticky notes scattered on a table, each one a guess, and some notes stuck down harder than others to show how confident you are in that particular guess. A pile of sticky notes can show two separate clumps of guesses, or a lopsided smear toward one side — shapes a single tidy sentence just can't capture. That messy pile of weighted guesses is exactly what a particle filter uses instead of a clean mean-and-variance summary.

🎲

1. Initialize

Draw \(N\) particles \(x_0^{(i)}\) from the prior distribution over the starting state, and give each an equal weight, \(w_0^{(i)} = 1/N\).

➡️

2. Propagate

Push each particle through the state transition, \(x_t^{(i)} = f(x_{t-1}^{(i)}, w_t^{(i)})\), drawing a fresh noise sample for every particle independently. This is the "predict" step's generalization — no data used yet.

⚖️

3. Weight

Once \(y_t\) arrives, score each particle by the likelihood of that observation given its own candidate state, \(w_t^{(i)} \propto w_{t-1}^{(i)}\, p(y_t \mid x_t^{(i)})\), then normalize so the weights sum to 1.

🔁

4. Resample

Check \(N_{\text{eff}}\). If it has fallen too low, draw a fresh set of \(N\) particles proportional to weight, and reset every weight to \(1/N\) — replacing the discredited guesses with clones of the credible ones.

Step 3's weight update deserves unpacking. In the common special case where every step resamples (weights are reset to \(1/N\) every time), the recursive formula collapses to the simpler \(w_t^{(i)} \propto p(y_t \mid x_t^{(i)})\) — "this step's weight is just this step's likelihood," because the previous weight was already uniform going in. The general recursive form \(w_t^{(i)} \propto w_{t-1}^{(i)} p(y_t \mid x_t^{(i)})\) is needed only when resampling is skipped for one or more steps (common when \(N_{\text{eff}}\) is still healthy, to avoid the small amount of diversity loss resampling always costs — see the pitfalls below).

The degeneracy problem and the effective-sample-size diagnostic

Left unchecked, the weighting step has a well-known failure mode called particle degeneracy: after enough steps, one particle's weight tends to dominate almost completely while the rest decay toward zero — the swarm has effectively collapsed to a single guess, wasting almost the entire population of \(N\) particles on candidates that no longer matter. The standard diagnostic for how close the swarm is to this collapse is the effective sample size:

\[ N_{\text{eff}} = \frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{N} \left(w_t^{(i)}\right)^2} \]
N_effeffective sample size — how many particles are meaningfully contributing right now
Σ (w_i)²the sum of squared weights — larger when weight is concentrated in fewer particles

Two extremes make this formula easy to sanity-check. If every particle has the same weight, \(w_i = 1/N\), then \(\sum w_i^2 = N \cdot (1/N)^2 = 1/N\), so \(N_{\text{eff}} = N\) — the healthiest possible state, every particle equally informative. If a single particle holds essentially all the weight (\(w_1 \approx 1\), the rest \(\approx 0\)), then \(\sum w_i^2 \approx 1\), so \(N_{\text{eff}} \approx 1\) — total degeneracy, only one particle is actually doing anything. A common rule of thumb triggers resampling whenever \(N_{\text{eff}}\) drops below \(N/2\): resample too rarely and the swarm degenerates; resample every single step regardless of \(N_{\text{eff}}\) and you pay an avoidable diversity cost (see pitfalls) even when the swarm was still healthy.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine a group project where, on paper, there are nine people contributing — but in practice one person is doing all the real work and the other eight are just nodding along. The group "looks" like nine people but is effectively worth about one. \(N_{\text{eff}}\) is a number that catches exactly this: it counts how many particles are actually pulling meaningful weight right now, versus just being along for the ride, so you know when it's time to bring in fresh contributors instead of relying on the same one guess.

A common, low-variance way to actually draw the resampled particles (rather than picking each one independently, which itself adds unnecessary Monte Carlo noise) is systematic resampling: lay the particles' cumulative weights end to end on \([0,1]\), draw a single random offset \(u \sim \text{Uniform}(0, 1/N)\), then pick the particles landed on by the \(N\) evenly spaced points \(u,\ u+\tfrac1N,\ u+\tfrac2N,\ \ldots\) This guarantees a particle with, say, twice the average weight gets picked roughly twice as often — but deterministically spread across the \([0,1]\) line rather than by \(N\) separate coin-flips, which is what makes it lower-variance than naive multinomial resampling.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Resampling is like a raffle where credible particles bought more tickets: the ones the data liked get cloned into the next round, and the ones it didn't get quietly dropped. Systematic resampling is a smarter way to run that raffle — instead of drawing each winner separately (which can randomly favor or shortchange someone by luck alone), it lines every ticket up in a row and picks winners at evenly spaced intervals, so someone who bought twice as many tickets reliably wins about twice as often, with far less luck-of-the-draw noise.

\(x_t\)The hidden state at time \(t\) — the thing being tracked, never observed directly.

\(h(\cdot)\)The observation function — possibly nonlinear, unlike the plain Kalman filter's linear \(H_t x_t\).

\(w_t^{(i)}\)Particle \(i\)'s normalized weight at time \(t\) — how much credibility the observed data lends to that particular candidate, with \(\sum_i w_t^{(i)} = 1\).

Worked example

Weighting a 5-particle swarm against one observation, then checking N_eff

Suppose the true dynamics have a fat-tailed process noise the linear Kalman filter cannot represent: 90% of the time the state nudges by a small \(\mathcal{N}(0, 0.01)\) amount, but 10% of the time it takes a much larger jump, \(\mathcal{N}(0, 1.0)\) — a two-component mixture, not one Gaussian. A particle filter handles this cleanly: each particle simply draws its own noise from that mixture during the propagate step, so the swarm as a whole naturally represents "mostly small moves, occasionally a big one," without needing a single tidy formula for the mixture's overall shape.

After the propagate step, suppose 5 particles hold these candidate values, each starting with equal weight \(w^{(i)}_{\text{prior}} = 0.20\):

\(x^{(1)}=1.05,\ x^{(2)}=1.15,\ x^{(3)}=1.20,\ x^{(4)}=1.25,\ x^{(5)}=1.40\)

The observation arrives: \(y = 1.30\), with observation variance \(R = 0.05\). Score each particle by the Gaussian likelihood \(p(y \mid x^{(i)}) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi R}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{(y-x^{(i)})^2}{2R}\right)\), where \(1/\sqrt{2\pi R} = 1/\sqrt{0.3142} \approx 1.784\):

\[ \begin{aligned} p(y\mid x^{(1)}) &= 1.784 \times e^{-0.0625/0.1} \approx 0.955 \\ p(y\mid x^{(2)}) &= 1.784 \times e^{-0.0225/0.1} \approx 1.425 \\ p(y\mid x^{(3)}) &= 1.784 \times e^{-0.0100/0.1} \approx 1.614 \\ p(y\mid x^{(4)}) &= 1.784 \times e^{-0.0025/0.1} \approx 1.740 \\ p(y\mid x^{(5)}) &= 1.784 \times e^{-0.0100/0.1} \approx 1.614 \end{aligned} \]

Multiply each by its prior weight (0.20) and normalize so the five raw weights sum to 1 (raw sum \(\approx 1.4695\)):

\[ w^{(1)}\approx 0.130,\quad w^{(2)}\approx 0.194,\quad w^{(3)}\approx 0.220,\quad w^{(4)}\approx 0.237,\quad w^{(5)}\approx 0.220 \]

Particle 4 (\(x=1.25\), closest to the observation) picked up the most weight, as expected, but no single particle dominates — the weights range only from 0.130 to 0.237. Compute the effective sample size:

\[ \sum_i \left(w^{(i)}\right)^2 \approx 0.0169+0.0376+0.0483+0.0561+0.0483 = 0.2072 \qquad N_{\text{eff}} = \frac{1}{0.2072} \approx 4.83 \]

With \(N=5\) particles, \(N_{\text{eff}}\approx 4.83\) is close to the maximum possible — the swarm is healthy, and (using the common \(N/2 = 2.5\) threshold) no resampling is needed yet. Now imagine instead that the fat tail actually fired that step — a genuine jump occurred, and only one particle happened to be sitting near where the state actually jumped to. That particle's likelihood would dwarf the other four's, its weight would approach 1, the others would collapse toward 0, \(\sum_i w_i^2\) would jump toward 1, and \(N_{\text{eff}}\) would plunge toward 1 — exactly the degeneracy signal that triggers a resample, replacing the four now-irrelevant particles with fresh clones drawn near the one candidate the jump actually vindicated.

A named scenario

A hedge ratio that mostly drifts calmly but occasionally re-prices discontinuously around a rate decision

Picture the same drifting-hedge-ratio problem as the Kalman filter lesson, but for a pair whose relationship is known to behave in two distinct ways: on an ordinary day it drifts slowly and smoothly, but around a scheduled central-bank rate decision it can re-price in a single discontinuous jump rather than a gradual walk — and, crucially, it isn't always obvious in advance whether a given post-announcement move is the start of a genuine repricing or a transient overreaction that fades back within the same session. A linear Kalman filter, built on one fixed Gaussian process-noise variance \(Q\), has no way to represent "probably a small drift, but there's a real chance this is one of the rare large jumps" — it can only ever produce one symmetric bell curve, which either overreacts to ordinary noise (if \(Q\) is set large to accommodate the jumps) or underreacts to a genuine jump (if \(Q\) is set small for the ordinary days). A particle filter can represent both branches at once — a cluster of particles tracking "ordinary drift continues" alongside a separate cluster tracking "the jump was real" — and let the next observation's weighting decide which cluster the data actually supports, without having to commit to one branch in advance. This is presented as an illustration of what the method is for, not as a claim that this exact setup has been built, backtested, or shown to add anything over the existing linear approach.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

Particle filters originate in the same tracking-and-estimation lineage as the Kalman filter, and the bootstrap particle filter specifically (Gordon, Salmond & Smith, 1993, cited below) was one of the first practical algorithms to make Sequential Monte Carlo usable outside of pure theory. The canonical non-finance use cases mirror the boat/GPS analogy directly: robot localization (a robot with a rough map and noisy sensors, uncertain exactly where on the map it sits — "Monte Carlo Localization" is a direct particle-filter application), radar and sonar tracking of a maneuvering target whose motion isn't well described by a straight line, and object tracking in video where a target can be temporarily occluded (hidden behind something) and reappear, a genuinely multimodal situation a single Gaussian estimate handles poorly.

In quantitative finance, particle filters show up wherever a state-space model is genuinely nonlinear or non-Gaussian and a closed-form filter isn't available: estimating the latent volatility path in a stochastic volatility model (where volatility itself is a hidden, continuously evolving state — a close cousin of the GARCH lesson's volatility-clustering idea, but treating volatility as unobserved rather than a deterministic function of past squared returns), fitting jump-diffusion models where price paths can jump discontinuously, and — looking ahead to the next lesson in this category — approximating regime-switching state-space models as an alternative to the moment-matching approximation those models otherwise require. A further, more advanced technique worth knowing by name: particle Markov Chain Monte Carlo (particle MCMC), which uses a particle filter as a component inside a larger procedure for estimating a model's fixed parameters (not just its time-varying hidden state) from data — useful when even the parameters themselves are uncertain and need to be learned, not just assumed.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where Sequential Monte Carlo quietly goes wrong

Particle impoverishment after resampling. Resampling fixes weight degeneracy by cloning high-weight particles — but cloning is not the same as generating genuinely new information. After several resample cycles, especially with process noise \(Q\) that's small, the swarm can end up as many near-identical copies of just a handful of original particles, collapsing the very diversity the method depends on. This is a real cost of resampling, not a free fix, which is exactly why resampling only when \(N_{\text{eff}}\) actually drops below a threshold — rather than at every single step regardless — is standard practice.

The curse of dimensionality. The number of particles needed to adequately cover the space of plausible states grows very fast as the hidden state's dimension grows — a handful of particles can represent a 1-dimensional state reasonably, but the same handful is hopelessly inadequate for a state with many correlated dimensions. This is a genuine, well-documented limitation, not a tuning problem to engineer around casually.

Choosing the resample threshold, or \(N\) itself, by grid-searching backtest performance. This is the same trap the Kalman filter lesson flags for \(Q\) and \(R\): the resample threshold and the particle count are chosen inputs, and tuning them post hoc against how good a backtest looks is overfitting wearing a Monte Carlo disguise, not a principled choice.

Treating the output as exact. A particle filter's estimate is a Monte Carlo approximation with genuine sampling variance — rerun it with a different random seed and, especially with a modest \(N\), the answer will move around. More particles reduce this variance but never eliminate it; reporting a particle-filter estimate as if it were a single deterministic answer, the way a closed-form Kalman estimate is, overstates its precision.

Worth remembering: a particle filter is a strictly more general tool than the Kalman filter — it can be applied to a linear-Gaussian problem too, and will (with enough particles) converge to the same answer the closed-form Kalman filter gives instantly and exactly. That generality is not free: for a problem that genuinely is linear-Gaussian, the plain Kalman filter is both cheaper and exact, and reaching for particles there adds Monte Carlo noise for no benefit.

Key Takeaways

✓A particle filter replaces "mean and variance" with a weighted swarm of concrete guesses — it works for nonlinear transitions and non-Gaussian noise precisely because it never needs a closed-form posterior formula.
✓The cycle is four steps: initialize from the prior, propagate through the (possibly nonlinear) dynamics, weight by the observation's likelihood, resample when the swarm gets lopsided.
✓\(N_{\text{eff}} = 1/\sum_i w_i^2\) measures swarm health — it runs from \(N\) (every particle equally informative) down to 1 (total degeneracy, one particle carries everything).
✓The worked example's swarm stayed healthy (\(N_{\text{eff}}\approx 4.83\) of 5) against an ordinary observation, but a genuine fat-tail jump would collapse it toward 1 — exactly the case a resample step exists to catch.
⚠Resampling fixes degeneracy but costs diversity (particle impoverishment) — it is a real trade-off, not a free correction, which is why it should trigger on \(N_{\text{eff}}\), not run unconditionally every step.
⚠Not built, not prototyped, not validated in this repo. Explaining Sequential Monte Carlo is not a claim it produces edge here — any real use would need to beat the incumbent on true out-of-sample performance first, per this project's Lego Principle.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. Why doesn't the Kalman filter's closed-form recursion work once the state transition or observation function is nonlinear, or the noise isn't Gaussian?
The Kalman filter's closed form relies on conjugacy: a Gaussian prior combined with a Gaussian likelihood that's linearly related to the state produces an exactly Gaussian posterior, summarized completely by a mean and a variance. Break linearity or the Gaussian-noise assumption and the true posterior can become skewed, multimodal, or otherwise non-Gaussian — there is generally no formula for its mean and variance in closed form, so two numbers are no longer enough to describe the belief.
Q2. What does a particle's weight represent, and what does resampling do to those weights?
A particle's weight is how much the observed data supports that particle's specific candidate value for the hidden state — proportional to the likelihood of the observation given that candidate, normalized so all weights sum to 1. Resampling draws a fresh set of \(N\) particles proportional to the current weights (so high-weight particles get cloned, low-weight ones are dropped) and then resets every weight back to \(1/N\) — it doesn't change what the swarm currently believes on average, it reallocates the finite particle budget toward the candidates the data actually supports.
Q3. Compute \(N_{\text{eff}}\) for four particles with weights 0.25, 0.25, 0.25, 0.25, and separately for weights 0.70, 0.10, 0.10, 0.10. What does the difference tell you?
Equal weights: \(\sum w_i^2 = 4\times 0.25^2 = 0.25\), so \(N_{\text{eff}} = 1/0.25 = 4\) — every particle is fully informative, the maximum possible for \(N=4\). Lopsided weights: \(\sum w_i^2 = 0.70^2 + 3\times 0.10^2 = 0.49 + 0.03 = 0.52\), so \(N_{\text{eff}} = 1/0.52 \approx 1.92\) — even though there are still 4 particles, the swarm is behaving as if it only had about 2 meaningfully distinct guesses, because one particle dominates the weight. This is exactly the degeneracy the diagnostic is built to catch.
Q4. Why is systematic resampling generally preferred over picking each of the N new particles independently at random (proportional to weight)?
Picking each new particle independently is itself a random process with its own sampling variance — by chance, a particle with the true average weight could still get picked more or fewer times than its weight implies. Systematic resampling instead lays the cumulative weights end to end and picks particles at \(N\) deterministically evenly-spaced points (offset by one shared random draw), which guarantees a particle gets picked close to exactly as many times as its weight implies — a lower-variance way to do the same reallocation.
Q5. What is "particle impoverishment," and why is resampling every single step, regardless of \(N_{\text{eff}}\), not automatically the safest choice?
Particle impoverishment is the loss of diversity that resampling causes: because resampling clones high-weight particles rather than generating new information, after repeated resample cycles the swarm can end up as many near-duplicate copies descended from just a few original particles. Resampling unconditionally every step maximizes how often this cloning happens, even during periods when the swarm was already healthy (\(N_{\text{eff}}\) close to \(N\)) and didn't need it — which is why resampling is normally triggered only when \(N_{\text{eff}}\) actually drops below a threshold.
Q6. A particle filter is strictly more general than a Kalman filter — so why not just always use a particle filter, even for a problem that is genuinely linear-Gaussian?
For a genuinely linear-Gaussian problem, the Kalman filter's closed form gives the exact answer instantly and cheaply, with no sampling variance at all. A particle filter applied to the same problem would, with enough particles, converge toward that same answer — but it pays for that generality with Monte Carlo noise (the answer depends on the random seed and the particle count) and greater computational cost, for a problem that didn't need any of that generality in the first place. Reach for particles only once linearity or Gaussian noise genuinely fails to hold.
