The Fokker-Planck Equation, Itô's Lemma Turned Inside Out

The earlier Itô's Lemma visual guide answered one question: given a random process, how does a function of it evolve, one path at a time? This deck is that lesson's direct sequel and mirror image — it asks how the process's own probability density evolves, across every possible path at once, not just one. The answer turns out to be a completely ordinary, deterministic partial differential equation. No randomness survives in it at all — which is exactly what makes it worth thirteen slides on its own.

Section 1 · How it reached trading

Reading the market's whole distribution

Bachelier · 1900 · Breeden & Litzenberger · 1978 · Dupire · 1994

How it reached trading

Pricing runs backward: one payoff, one price. Calibration needs the other direction: what distribution does the model imply at each date, and does it match the smile? Bachelier's 1900 bond thesis already had probability spreading like heat.

Breeden and Litzenberger (1978) read the density from call prices; Dupire (1994) wrote the forward equation in strikes, giving the local vol that fits every vanilla. Desks still calibrate local and local-stochastic vol this way.

Section 2

One path's transformation vs. the whole cloud's shape

Both questions start from the exact same SDE, dXₜ = μ(Xₜ,t)dt + σ(Xₜ,t)dWₜ. They just ask completely different things about it.

Itô's Lemma — one path, transformed

Given a function f, how does f(Xₜ) evolve along a single realized path? ito-lemma-micro.html works this out fully: keep the (dWₜ)²=dt term ordinary calculus drops, and out comes df = (f′μ + ½f″σ²)dt + f′σ dWₜ. The output is itself still random — one path in, one still-random path out.

Fokker-Planck — the whole population, evolved

Forget any one path. Run the SDE from the same start a million times and ask what the resulting cloud of outcomes looks like at each instant. The density p(x,t) describing that whole population obeys its own equation — and that equation has no dWₜ term anywhere in it.

Same μ, same σ, same SDE underneath both questions. The only thing that changes is which object gets asked to evolve — one random path, or the density describing every possible path at once.
Section 3

The Fokker-Planck equation, term by term

p(x,t) is the probability density that the process sits near x at time t. Two terms describe everything that can happen to that curve's shape.

The equation
∂p/∂t
=
−∂/∂x[μp]
+
½∂²/∂x²[σ²p]
Also called the forward Kolmogorov equation — both names are standard for the same object.
→

Drift / advection term

−∂/∂x[μ(x,t)p(x,t)] shifts the density's location, carrying probability mass in the direction μ points, without changing its shape — smoke carried sideways by a steady breeze.

≈

Diffusion term

½∂²/∂x²[σ²(x,t)p(x,t)] spreads the density out. Structurally it's the ordinary heat equation's Laplacian, with σ² playing the role of thermal conductivity — the same smoke blurring outward on its own, breeze or no breeze.

Every individual particle inside that cloud is still bouncing around unpredictably. The equation above describes something else entirely — the population-level shape of where the particles are — and that shape's evolution is exactly as deterministic as the heat equation it structurally resembles.

Section 4

Where the equation actually comes from

Not asserted from nowhere — it's Itô's Lemma, averaged and turned inside out.

1

Apply Itô's Lemma to a test function, then take expectations

The dWₜ term has zero mean, so it drops out, leaving d/dt 𝔼[f(Xₜ)] = 𝔼[μf′ + ½σ²f″]. The bracket, ℒf = μf′ + ½σ²f″, is called the generator of Xₜ — exactly the deterministic half of Itô's Lemma.

2

Write both sides as integrals against p, then integrate by parts

𝔼[f(Xₜ)] = ∫f(x)p(x,t)dx. Integrating by parts (twice, for the second-order term) moves the derivatives off f and onto μp and σ²p instead — at the cost of a sign flip each time a derivative crosses the integral sign.

The result, ℒ*p = −∂ₓ[μp] + ½∂ₓ²[σ²p], is called the formal adjoint of the generator ℒ — "adjoint" in exactly that integration-by-parts sense. It's the same underlying object Itô's Lemma produces, moved algebraically from acting on a test function f onto the density p itself.
Where this needs qualifying

This slide carries the idea, not the full rigor — the complete derivation needs boundary conditions ensuring p → 0 fast enough at x = ±∞ that the integration-by-parts boundary terms actually vanish. The full lesson works through every algebraic step; this is the shape of the argument.

Section 5

Forward vs. backward — the one distinction to get right

A close relative runs the opposite direction — and it's exactly the type of equation Black-Scholes is.

Forward — Fokker-Planck

Unknown: a density p(x,t). Fixed data: an initial condition at t₀. Runs forward, increasing t. Operator: the adjoint ℒ*. Answers: "given where I start, what's the whole distribution of where I might end up?"

Backward — Kolmogorov

Unknown: an expected payoff u(x,t) = 𝔼[g(X_T)|Xₜ=x]. Fixed data: a terminal condition at T. Runs backward from T. Operator: the plain generator ℒ, un-adjointed. Answers: "given what I want at the end, what's it worth now?"

The rule that prevents the mix-up

"Given where I start, where might I end up" is forward — Fokker-Planck. "Given what I want at a fixed future time, what's it worth right now" is backward — Kolmogorov. The Black-Scholes PDE is precisely a backward-Kolmogorov-type equation for u=V, with one addition: a −rV discounting term that turns "expected terminal payoff" into "expected terminal payoff, discounted to today's dollars." Same generator, opposite direction, different unknown.

Section 7

A mean-reverting process settles into a fixed width

Ornstein-Uhlenbeck: dXₜ = 1·(0 − Xₜ)dt + 1·dWₜ, starting at x₀ = 2. Three snapshots of its own Fokker-Planck solution, drawn to a shared scale.

■ t=0.3 (mean 1.48, std 0.47)   ■ t=1 (mean 0.74, std 0.66)   ■ t=3 (mean 0.10, std 0.71)

From t=0.3 to t=1 the peak drops sharply — fast spreading. From t=1 to t=3 the peak barely moves — the density has nearly reached its stationary distribution, width σ/√(2θ) = 1/√2 ≈ 0.707, exactly what t=3's std of 0.71 already sits at. A driftless Brownian motion's Fokker-Planck solution spreads forever; a mean-reverting one settles into a fixed shape and stays there.

Solve the Fokker-Planck density yourself

For dXₜ = μ dt + σ dWₜ starting at x₀, the closed-form solution to the Fokker-Planck equation is Normal(x₀+μt, σ²t). Change any input.

Section 10

How this actually gets used

📊

Projecting a risk factor's distribution

"Where might this rate or price plausibly be in N days" is, conceptually, evolving a density forward — whether via a closed-form Fokker-Planck solution, a numerical PDE solve, or a Monte Carlo histogram standing in for the same thing.

🧭

Checking which direction a PDE solver runs

Before trusting a numerical PDE solver, ask whether it's set up forward (adjoint operator, initial condition) or backward (plain generator, terminal condition) — conflating the two gives a stable computation of the wrong quantity, not a crash.

🔬

Closed forms are the exception

GBM, Ornstein-Uhlenbeck, and CIR happen to have known closed-form densities. Most SDEs with more general coefficients don't — the equation is still well-posed, but solving it needs numerical PDE methods or simulation instead.

Checked against this repo

The full lesson notes that the Fokker-Planck equation is "rarely solved by hand" in practice and that a risk system typically stands in with Monte Carlo simulation instead. That claim was re-checked directly against this codebase for this deck: no Fokker-Planck, Kolmogorov-equation, or explicit density-evolution PDE solver exists anywhere in this repo's source outside these two lesson pages — so the claim holds, and nothing here needed correcting.

Section 11

Where this gets misapplied

Ø

Mixing up forward and backward

By far the most common mistake — both equations share the same μ, the same σ, and a visually similar PDE structure. Tie the question to the concrete direction, every time.

⧉

Treating the equation itself as stochastic

It isn't. p(x,t) is completely deterministic, even though the process Xₜ it describes is random — the randomness has already been fully absorbed into p's shape. A stochastic PDE (next in this series) is a genuinely different object: one with its own explicit noise term.

≠

Assuming a closed-form density always exists

GBM, OU, and CIR are special. Most SDEs with general μ(x,t), σ(x,t) don't admit a formula to look up — the equation still governs the true density, it just needs numerical or simulation methods to solve.

⚑

Skipping the well-posedness prerequisite

If the SDE doesn't have a unique solution in the first place (Lipschitz and growth conditions from earlier in this curriculum), there's no well-defined p(x,t) for the Fokker-Planck equation to even be about.

Section 12

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

What does the Fokker-Planck equation actually describe?

That second and third description is Itô's Lemma's job. The Fokker-Planck equation is the mirror-image question — the population-level density p(x,t), not any single path.
Question 2

Which term in ∂p/∂t = −∂ₓ[μp] + ½∂ₓ²[σ²p] spreads the density out rather than shifting its location?

The drift term is a pure translation, like smoke carried by a breeze. The diffusion term is structurally the heat equation's Laplacian — it's the one that blurs the density outward.
Question 3

A colleague asks: "given today's spot price, what's the distribution of where it might be in six months?" Is this a forward or backward problem?

"Given where I start, where might I end up" is exactly the Fokker-Planck / forward-Kolmogorov structure — initial condition, evolved forward.
Question 4

Is the Black-Scholes PDE a forward or backward equation, and why?

Black-Scholes fixes V(S,T)=max(S−K,0) at expiration and solves backward to today's value — precisely a backward-Kolmogorov-type equation, with one extra −rV discounting term.
Question 5

For dXₜ = μdt + σdWₜ starting at x₀=0 with μ=0.1, σ=0.5, what is Var(X₃)?

Variance = σ²t = 0.5² × 3 = 0.25 × 3 = 0.75. The mean, by contrast, is x₀ + μt = 0 + 0.1×3 = 0.3 — that's a different number, answering a different question.
Section 13

What to carry forward

01

Itô's Lemma asks how a function of a process evolves along one path; the Fokker-Planck equation asks how the process's whole density evolves across every path — same SDE, mirror-image question.

02

∂p/∂t = −∂ₓ[μp] + ½∂ₓ²[σ²p] is fully deterministic — drift shifts the density's location, diffusion spreads it out, and no dWₜ term survives anywhere in the equation.

03

It's the formal adjoint of the generator that shows up in Itô's Lemma — averaging Itô's Lemma and integrating by parts moves the derivatives off a test function and onto the density.

04

Forward (Fokker-Planck: density, initial condition) and backward (Kolmogorov: expected payoff, terminal condition) are mirror images, not the same equation — Black-Scholes is a backward one, plus discounting.

05

For Brownian motion with drift, the density is Normal(x₀+μt, σ²t) — mean and variance both grow linearly, forever. For a mean-reverting process, the density instead settles into a fixed stationary width.

06

How it reached trading: Bachelier (1900), then Breeden & Litzenberger (1978) and Dupire (1994) — the forward equation is how local-vol models are calibrated to the smile.

What this now lets you do

Read "the density evolves forward" and "the value is computed backward" as two structurally different PDE problems, not variations on one — and check which one any solver you're handed actually is before trusting its output. Compute a closed-form transition density for the handful of processes (Brownian motion, OU, GBM, CIR) that admit one, and know exactly why most SDEs won't hand you a formula instead.
