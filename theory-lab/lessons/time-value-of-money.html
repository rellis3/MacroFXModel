Time Value of Money

A dollar today is worth more than a dollar next year — not as a saying, but as an arithmetic fact with a precise formula. That formula is where the risk-free rate \(r\) in Black-Scholes comes from, and it is the entire foundation Interest Rate Parity is built on.

DEEPER FOUNDATION — read before Black-Scholes or Interest Rate Parity ~13 min read Prereqs: Returns, Log-Returns & Compounding
TL;DR
  • Future value compounds: \(FV = PV(1+r)^t\); present value runs it in reverse, \(PV = FV/(1+r)^t\), and the multiplier \(DF(r,t)=1/(1+r)^t\) is the discount factor — always strictly between 0 and 1 when \(r,t>0\).
  • Compounding infinitely often (continuously) gives \(FV=PV\cdot e^{rt}\); taking logs shows \(\ln(FV/PV)=rt\), meaning a continuously-compounded rate \(r\) is a log return per unit time — the same object the Returns & Compounding primer defined.
  • Worked example: $1,000 at a nominal 6% for 5 years grows to \$1,338.23 compounded annually vs. \$1,349.86 compounded continuously — an \$11.63 gap from the same stated rate, purely from compounding frequency.
  • USD/JPY scenario: spot 150.00, USD deposits at 5%, JPY at 0.1% — the no-arbitrage 3-month forward is about 148.19 (≈ −181 forward points), below spot, because the higher-rate currency (USD) must trade forward at a discount or a riskless borrow-JPY/invest-USD arbitrage opens up.
  • The forward discount is not a directional forecast — it's the mechanical PV/FV adjustment that keeps the interest-rate differential from being free money, and it's exactly the machinery behind Black-Scholes's \(Ke^{-rT}\) term and the upcoming Interest Rate Parity lesson.
Section 01

Why this matters

The Black-Scholes lesson in this series introduces a term \(Ke^{-rT}\) — "the strike price discounted back to today at the risk-free rate" — and moves on quickly, because deriving option pricing theory isn't the place to also derive what discounting is. That derivation belongs here. Every discount factor, every present-value calculation, every "the market is pricing in X%" statement in finance rests on the same four-hundred-year-old idea: money has a price, that price is the interest rate, and moving money across time changes its value in a way you can compute exactly. Get this mechanical and it stops being a footnote you skip past in every other lesson — it becomes the thing you instantly recognize whenever an \(r\), an \(e^{-rt}\), or a "3-month forward" shows up anywhere else in this series.

This lesson also exists specifically to set up the next one. Interest Rate Parity — the theory that pins down FX forward rates from nothing more than two countries' interest rates — is entirely a time-value-of-money argument applied to two currencies at once. If "a dollar today is worth more than a dollar in a year because it earns interest" isn't automatic and comfortable by the end of this page, IRP will feel like a new, separate idea instead of what it actually is: this same idea, run twice, once per currency, and compared.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Why a dollar today beats a dollar next year

Suppose someone offers you a choice: $100 in your hand right now, or a guaranteed $100 exactly one year from today. Take the $100 now. Not because a bird in hand is worth two in the bush as a matter of psychology, but because of what you can do with the $100 in the meantime: put it in a bank account paying, say, 5% interest, and in a year it has become $105. The person who waited for their $100 is now strictly poorer than you, by $5, for having made an economically identical promise a year earlier. "A dollar today is worth more than a dollar tomorrow" is just this: today's dollar can be lent, invested, or deposited, and interest is the price the world pays you for the use of it while you wait.

Turn that around and you get discounting for free. If $100 today grows to $105 in a year at 5%, then $105 arriving in a year must be worth exactly $100 today — no more, no less, because $100 today can be turned into exactly $105 in a year and nothing about waiting for the $105 changes that equivalence. Every present-value calculation in finance is this same statement run in reverse: given a future amount and a rate, what amount today would grow into it? That single question — and its mirror image, what does today's amount grow into? — is the entire subject of this lesson.

Section 02

The math, step by step

1. Future value — the compounding argument

Let \(PV\) be an amount of money today, and let \(r\) be the interest rate it earns per period (a year, unless stated otherwise). After one period, that amount has grown by \(r\) — the original amount plus its interest:

\[ PV + PV\cdot r = PV(1+r) \]

The important step is what happens in period two. The interest paid in period two is not earned only on the original \(PV\) — it is earned on the whole balance carried forward, \(PV(1+r)\), including the interest period one already added. That is the entire meaning of "compounding": interest earns interest. So after period two:

\[ \big[PV(1+r)\big](1+r) = PV(1+r)^2 \]

Repeating this \(t\) times — each period multiplying the running balance by another factor of \((1+r)\) — gives the future value formula:

\[ FV = PV(1+r)^t \]

What PV compounds into — the entire lesson's central equation
\(PV\)Present value — an amount of money today.
\(FV\)Future value — what that amount grows into after \(t\) periods of compounding at rate \(r\).
\(r\)The interest (or discount, or risk-free) rate per period, expressed as a decimal (5% = 0.05). This is the exact same symbol \(r\) that appears in the Black-Scholes formula's \(Ke^{-rT}\) term.
\(t\)The number of compounding periods — years, if \(r\) is an annual rate. \(t\) does not have to be a whole number; three months is \(t=0.25\) years, used directly below in the FX forward scenario.
\((1+r)^t\)The growth factor — how many dollars \(t\) periods from now one dollar today turns into. Always strictly greater than 1 whenever \(r>0\) and \(t>0\).

Notice the formula is exponential — \((1+r)\) raised to the power \(t\) — not \(PV(1+rt)\), which would just add \(r\) once per period rather than letting each period's gain itself start earning \(r\). That distinction, "simple" versus "compound" interest, is exactly the additive-versus-multiplicative gap the Returns & Compounding primer built its entire volatility-drag argument around, just running in the opposite direction: there, compounding a volatile return series pulled the geometric mean below the arithmetic mean; here, compounding a fixed, certain interest rate every period makes an investment grow faster than simply adding \(r\) once per period would. Same multiplicative mechanism, opposite-looking consequence, because certainty and variance behave differently once you start multiplying instead of adding.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Picture a snowball rolling down a snowy hill. Each bit of extra snow it picks up on the way down doesn't just sit on top loosely — it becomes part of the ball, and now picks up even more snow of its own next time around. That's compounding: interest doesn't just get added on flatly each year, it joins the pile and starts earning interest itself, which is why money grows faster and faster instead of by the same amount every single period.

2. Present value — discounting

Present value is nothing more than the future-value formula solved for \(PV\) instead of \(FV\). Divide both sides of \(FV = PV(1+r)^t\) by \((1+r)^t\):

\[ PV = \frac{FV}{(1+r)^t} \]
FVthe known future amount
(1+r)ᵗdivide by the same growth factor to run it in reverse

The quantity \((1+r)^{-t} = 1/(1+r)^t\) that \(FV\) gets multiplied by here is called the discount factor:

\[ DF(r,t) = \frac{1}{(1+r)^t} \]
DF(r,t)discount factor — always strictly between 0 and 1

A discount factor answers one specific question: "what is one dollar, to be received \(t\) periods from now, worth today?" Multiply any future cash flow by the discount factor for its arrival date and you get its present value — this is literally the entire operation underneath every bond price, every discounted-cash-flow valuation, and (as the Black-Scholes lesson's \(Ke^{-rT}\) term shows) every option-pricing formula that involves a future strike or payoff.

A discount factor is always strictly less than 1 whenever \(r>0\) and \(t>0\). This isn't a modeling choice, it falls straight out of the algebra: if \(r>0\) then \((1+r)>1\), and raising a number greater than 1 to a positive power \(t\) produces a number still greater than 1, so dividing 1 by it must give something strictly between 0 and 1. Two limiting cases are worth holding onto because the chart below is built entirely out of them: as \(t\to 0\) (nothing to wait for), \(DF\to 1\) — a dollar due immediately is worth a dollar; as \(t\to\infty\) with \(r>0\) fixed, \(DF\to 0\) — a dollar due arbitrarily far in the future is worth arbitrarily little today, no matter how large it nominally is. The higher \(r\) is, the faster that decay toward zero happens for any given \(t\), which is exactly the "low-rate curve declines slowly, high-rate curve declines fast" shape in the chart.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

If $100 today turns into $105 next year in the bank, flip the question around: $105 arriving next year must be worth exactly $100 today — no more, no less. A discount factor is a shrinking number, always somewhere between 0 and 1, that answers "how much is a dollar arriving later actually worth right now." The longer you have to wait, or the higher the interest rate, the smaller that number gets, because waiting always costs you something.

3. Continuous compounding — the limit, and why it's the same object as a log return

So far compounding has happened once per period (once per year, say). Nothing forces that — a bank could compound semiannually, quarterly, monthly, daily, or in principle infinitely often. If a nominal annual rate \(r\) compounds \(n\) times per year, each sub-period earns \(r/n\), and over \(t\) years there are \(nt\) sub-periods, so:

\[ FV = PV\left(1+\frac{r}{n}\right)^{nt} \]

(Note this "nominal" means the stated annual rate before adjusting for how often it compounds — a different sense of "nominal" from the nominal-vs-real, inflation-adjusted meaning used in the next section; both uses are standard in finance and worth keeping straight.) Taking the number of compounding periods \(n\) to infinity — compounding continuously — invokes a standard calculus limit (the same limit that defines the constant \(e\) itself, not re-derived here):

\[ \lim_{n\to\infty}\left(1+\frac{r}{n}\right)^{n} = e^{r} \quad\Rightarrow\quad FV = PV\cdot e^{rt} \]
e^rthe limit of ever-more-frequent compounding
PV·e^{rt}continuous-compounding future value — the closed-form limit

This convergence is fast and, past a point, practically invisible. Take \(r=6\%\) over one year and watch the effective annual growth factor as compounding frequency \(n\) increases:

\(n=1\) (annual)\((1.06)^1 = 1.06000\)
\(n=2\) (semiannual)\((1.03)^2 = 1.06090\)
\(n=4\) (quarterly)\((1.015)^4 \approx 1.06136\)
\(n=12\) (monthly)\((1.005)^{12} \approx 1.06168\)
\(n=365\) (daily)\((1+0.06/365)^{365} \approx 1.06183\)
\(n\to\infty\) (continuous)\(e^{0.06} \approx 1.06184\)

Daily compounding is already within a thousandth of the continuous limit — which is exactly why quoted textbook formulas (including Black-Scholes) default to continuous compounding: it's the clean, closed-form limiting case of something real-world compounding already approximates closely at any ordinary frequency.

Here is the connection the Returns & Compounding primer set up directly. Take logs of both sides of \(FV = PV\cdot e^{rt}\):

\[ \ln\!\left(\frac{FV}{PV}\right) = rt \]
ln(FV/PV)the log return over the horizon — same object as the Returns primer's
rtrate times time — the constant-rate accrual that produces it

Recall the primer's definition of a log return over a horizon: \(r_t = \ln(P_t/P_{t-1})\), generalized to any horizon as \(\ln(P_{\text{end}}/P_{\text{start}})\). The left-hand side above, \(\ln(FV/PV)\), is exactly that log return, applied to a risk-free deposit growing from \(PV\) to \(FV\). The right-hand side, \(rt\), says that log return accrues at a constant rate \(r\) per unit time. This is not an analogy or a resemblance — a continuously-compounded interest rate is a log return per unit time, the identical mathematical object, and the reason it's additive across time (2 years at a continuously-compounded 5% gives exactly the same growth as two separate 1-year periods at 5% each, with no cross term to worry about) is the exact log-additivity property the primer derived from \(\ln(ab)=\ln a + \ln b\). A "risk-free rate of 5%, continuously compounded" is precisely a claim that a riskless bond's log return accrues at a constant 0.05 per year — nothing more exotic than that.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Compounding "continuously" just means imagining interest getting added in infinitely tiny drips all the time, instead of once a year in one big splash — like a tap that never stops dripping instead of a bucket poured in once. All those tiny drips add up to one clean, exact number (built from a special math constant called e), and that number turns out to secretly be the same thing as a "log return" — a way of measuring growth that adds up neatly across time.

4. Nominal vs. real rates — a forward-reference to Fisher and Purchasing Power Parity

Everything above assumes the interest rate \(r\) buys you something real — that $105 next year genuinely lets you purchase 5% more goods than $100 buys today. That's only true if prices haven't risen in the meantime. If inflation runs at rate \(\pi\) over the same period, part of that 5% growth is just keeping pace with rising prices, not actually making you richer in purchasing-power terms. The relationship between the nominal rate you're quoted, the real rate you actually experience, and inflation is the Fisher equation (named for economist Irving Fisher, cited in Further Reading):

\[ (1+i_{\text{nominal}}) = (1+i_{\text{real}})(1+\pi) \]
i_nominalthe quoted rate — what a market or deposit actually states
i_realthe purchasing-power rate you actually experience
πinflation over the same period

which rearranges to \(i_{\text{real}} = \dfrac{1+i_{\text{nominal}}}{1+\pi}-1\), and for small rates is well approximated by simple subtraction: \(i_{\text{real}} \approx i_{\text{nominal}} - \pi\). A nominal rate of 5% with 3% inflation gives an exact real rate of \(1.05/1.03 - 1\approx 1.94\%\), close to (but not exactly) the naive \(5\%-3\%=2\%\) approximation. The point to carry forward, not derive fully here: every \(r\) used in this lesson's formulas is, strictly, a nominal rate unless stated otherwise, and the compounding math is identical either way — only the economic interpretation of what you actually gained changes. This exact nominal/real split, and how it plays out when two countries have different inflation rates, is the subject of the upcoming Purchasing Power Parity lesson; it isn't needed for Interest Rate Parity (which works entirely with quoted nominal rates), but it's the natural next question once you've internalized that "the rate" a market quotes is never automatically "the value you actually gain."

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

A nominal rate is just the number printed on your bank statement. A real rate is how much richer you actually got, once you account for prices going up too. If your money grows by 5% but the stuff you'd want to buy also got 3% more expensive, you didn't really get 5% richer — you got about 2% richer, because part of that growth just kept up with rising prices instead of buying you anything extra.

Discount factor vs. time, at two different rates
Low rate (r = 3%) High rate (r = 10%)
Both curves are \(DF(t) = e^{-rt}\) (the continuous-compounding discount factor), starting at exactly 1.0 when \(t=0\) — a dollar due right now needs no discounting. The 10% curve falls to roughly 0.37 by year 10 and under 0.14 by year 20; the 3% curve is still above 0.74 at year 10 and above 0.54 at year 20. The rate you discount at changes how quickly a distant dollar becomes economically negligible — the same shape difference that makes long-dated bonds far more sensitive to interest-rate moves than short-dated ones.
🧮
Worked example

Growing $1,000 for 5 years at 6% — annual vs. continuous compounding

Take \(PV = \$1{,}000\), \(r = 6\%\) (0.06), \(t = 5\) years, compounded annually first:

\[ FV = 1000\times(1.06)^5 = 1000\times1.3382256 \approx \$1{,}338.23 \]

Check the discount factor runs the arithmetic in reverse: \(DF = 1/(1.06)^5 = 1/1.3382256 \approx 0.74726\), and \(1338.23\times 0.74726 \approx \$1{,}000.00\) — the original \(PV\), recovered exactly, which is exactly what "present value" and "future value" being inverses of each other means.

Now compound the same nominal 6% continuously instead of annually, everything else unchanged:

\[ FV = 1000\times e^{0.06\times 5} = 1000\times e^{0.3} \approx 1000\times1.349859 \approx \$1{,}349.86 \]

Continuous compounding gives \(\$1{,}349.86\) versus annual compounding's \(\$1{,}338.23\) — a difference of \(\$11.63\) on the same stated 6% rate, purely because continuous compounding lets interest start earning interest immediately rather than waiting a full year between compounding dates. This is the same convergence table from above, just carried out to 5 years instead of 1: the stated ("nominal") rate is identical in both cases, but the effective annual growth differs because compounding frequency is a separate lever from the quoted rate itself — exactly the distinction flagged when continuous compounding was introduced.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

Time value of money is the mechanical foundation underneath essentially all of fixed income and corporate finance: a bond's price is the sum of its coupon and principal cash flows, each individually discounted back to today by the discount factor for its payment date; a company's net present value (NPV) for a capital project is its projected future cash flows, discounted the same way, minus the upfront cost; a yield curve is nothing more than a table of discount factors (or, equivalently, the rates that produce them) at different maturities, extracted from observed bond prices.

💵

Bond pricing

Sum of coupon and principal cash flows, each discounted back to today by the discount factor for its payment date.

🏗️

Capital project NPV

Projected future cash flows, discounted the same way, minus the upfront cost.

📈

The yield curve

Nothing more than a table of discount factors (or the rates producing them) at different maturities, extracted from observed bond prices.

None of this requires a forecast of where markets are going — like the risk-neutral pricing in Black-Scholes, discounting is a mechanical, no-forecast-needed operation on known future cash flows and a quoted rate. The judgment in real finance is almost entirely about which rate to use and how certain the future cash flow really is — the arithmetic of discounting itself is not in dispute.

🎯
Real-world trading scenario

Why does the 3-month USD/JPY forward differ from spot with zero directional view?

Suppose USD/JPY spot is \(S_0 = 150.00\) (150 yen buys 1 dollar), the USD deposit rate is 5% annualized, and the JPY deposit rate is 0.1% annualized. A trader with no opinion at all about which way USD/JPY is headed still needs to know: what is the fair 3-month (\(t=0.25\) year) forward rate? The answer is not 150.00, even with zero directional view, because the two currencies pay different rates of interest, and the forward rate must exactly reflect that PV/FV gap or a riskless arbitrage opens up.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine two banks down the street from each other, one paying almost no interest and one paying a lot. If you could borrow for free from the low-interest bank and just park that money in the high-interest one, everyone would do it and print free money. The "forward rate" — the exchange rate agreed today for a swap that happens later — has to be set at exactly the number that closes that loophole, so borrowing from the cheap currency and parking it in the expensive one doesn't actually make anyone free money once the exchange-rate change is factored in.

Here is the no-arbitrage argument, run with these exact numbers. Suppose (hypothetically, incorrectly) the forward rate were quoted equal to spot, 150.00. A trader could: borrow 150 JPY for 3 months at 0.1% (owing back \(150\times(1+0.001\times0.25) = 150\times1.00025 = 150.0375\) JPY); immediately convert the borrowed 150 JPY to \$1 at spot; invest that \$1 at the 5% USD rate for 3 months, growing to \(1\times(1+0.05\times0.25) = 1.0125\) USD; then convert that \$1.0125 back to JPY at the (mispriced) 150.00 forward, receiving \(1.0125\times150 = 151.875\) JPY. Repaying the 150.0375 JPY loan leaves \(151.875 - 150.0375 = 1.8375\) JPY of pure, riskless profit, for zero net capital committed — free money, funded entirely by borrowing the low-rate currency and investing in the high-rate one.

The market closes that gap by pricing the forward below spot instead. The correct no-arbitrage forward solves for the rate at which this trade earns exactly zero:

\[ F = S_0\times\frac{1+r_{\text{JPY}}\,t}{1+r_{\text{USD}}\,t} = 150\times\frac{1+0.001\times0.25}{1+0.05\times0.25} = 150\times\frac{1.00025}{1.0125} \approx 148.19 \]
S₀spot rate today
1+r_JPY tgrowth factor of the low-rate currency (numerator)
1+r_USD tgrowth factor of the high-rate currency (denominator)
Fthe no-arbitrage forward — the higher-rate currency trades at a discount

That's a forward rate about 1.81 yen below spot — roughly −181 forward points (in the standard 0.01-yen "pip" convention for USD/JPY) for a 3-month tenor. Check the arbitrage really is closed at this rate: converting the same \$1.0125 back to JPY at 148.185 gives \(1.0125\times148.185\approx150.0375\) JPY — exactly enough to repay the 150.0375 JPY loan, with nothing left over. The forward discount is not a market forecast that USD will weaken against JPY; it is the precise present-value/future-value adjustment that makes borrowing the low-rate currency to fund an investment in the high-rate currency a break-even trade rather than free money. A rough rule of thumb — forward points \(\approx -S_0\times(r_{\text{USD}}-r_{\text{JPY}})\times t = -150\times0.049\times0.25\approx-1.84\) yen — lands close to the exact figure and is useful for a quick sanity check, but the exact ratio formula above is what actually holds without approximation. This is precisely what the upcoming Interest Rate Parity lesson formalizes and generalizes: forward points are a direct, mechanical readout of an interest-rate differential, not a view on direction.

The trap: a forward trading below spot is not a market call that the currency will weaken. It's the mechanical PV/FV adjustment that keeps borrowing the low-rate currency to fund the high-rate one from being free money — nothing more, nothing less.
⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where time-value reasoning quietly goes wrong

Reading a forward discount or premium as a directional forecast. The scenario box above should make this concrete: USD/JPY trading forward below spot says nothing about anyone expecting the dollar to weaken. It is the mechanical consequence of USD paying a higher deposit rate than JPY, full stop — exactly the same category of mistake as reading a Black-Scholes price as a market forecast when \(\mu\) never entered that formula either.

Conflating nominal-vs-compounding-frequency with nominal-vs-real (inflation). This lesson uses "nominal" in two genuinely different, both-standard senses: the stated rate before adjusting for compounding frequency (Section 3), and the stated rate before adjusting for inflation (Section 4, the Fisher equation). They are unrelated adjustments that happen to share a name. Mixing them up — for instance, assuming continuous compounding somehow "accounts for" inflation — produces confidently wrong numbers.

Ignoring how much a small rate difference compounds over long horizons. The discount-factor chart makes this visible directly: at 3 years the 3% and 10% curves are still fairly close (0.914 vs. 0.741), but by 20 years they've diverged to 0.549 vs. 0.135 — a 4x gap that started as a 7-percentage-point rate difference. Present values of long-dated cash flows (a 30-year bond, a pension liability, a long-dated FX forward) are far more sensitive to the discount rate assumption than short-dated ones, purely because \((1+r)^t\) is exponential in \(t\).

Using simple interest and compound interest interchangeably. Money-market conventions (the FX forward scenario above, most bank deposits under a year) typically use simple interest, \((1+rt)\), for a single sub-annual period — there's no compounding date to speak of within the term. Multi-year investments almost always compound, \((1+r)^t\). Applying the wrong convention to the wrong horizon is a common, avoidable source of a wrong present value, even though a 3-month gap between the two conventions is usually small.

Key Takeaways

✓\(FV = PV(1+r)^t\) is compounding — interest earning interest. That's why growth is exponential in \(t\), not the linear \(PV(1+rt)\) of simple interest.
✓Present value just runs future value backward. The discount factor \(DF(r,t)=1/(1+r)^t\) is always strictly between 0 and 1 whenever \(r,t>0\).
✓A continuously-compounded rate IS a log return per unit time. \(\ln(FV/PV)=rt\) is the identical object the Returns & Compounding primer already defined — not an analogy.
✓Nominal ≠ real. The Fisher equation strips out inflation to get the purchasing-power rate you actually experience — and this "nominal" is a different split from nominal-vs-compounding-frequency.
✓An FX forward is priced by no-arbitrage, not by forecast. The higher-rate currency always trades forward at a discount — otherwise a riskless borrow-low/invest-high trade would print free money.
⚠Don't read a forward discount as a directional view. USD/JPY trading below spot says nothing about anyone expecting the dollar to weaken — it's mechanical, full stop.
⚠Small rate gaps compound into large present-value gaps over long horizons. A 7-point rate difference is a modest gap at 3 years but a 4x discount-factor gap by year 20 — long-dated cash flows are far more rate-sensitive than short-dated ones.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. Why is future value \(PV(1+r)^t\) rather than the simpler \(PV(1+rt)\), and what economic mechanism does the exponent capture that the simpler formula misses?
\(PV(1+r)^t\) captures compounding: interest earned in an earlier period becomes part of the balance that itself earns interest in later periods. Each period multiplies the running balance by another factor of \((1+r)\), which is what turns the growth into an exponential function of \(t\) rather than a straight line. \(PV(1+rt)\) — simple interest — only ever pays \(r\) on the original \(PV\), never on previously accrued interest, so it grows linearly and understates true compounded growth for any \(t>1\) period (the two formulas agree only within a single period, where there's no prior interest yet to compound).
Q2. Why is a discount factor always strictly between 0 and 1 whenever \(r>0\) and \(t>0\), and what happens to it in the limits \(t\to 0\) and \(t\to\infty\)?
\(DF(r,t)=1/(1+r)^t\). Since \(r>0\) makes \((1+r)>1\), raising it to any positive power \(t\) keeps it greater than 1, so its reciprocal must lie strictly between 0 and 1. As \(t\to 0\), \((1+r)^t\to 1\), so \(DF\to 1\) — a dollar due essentially immediately needs essentially no discounting. As \(t\to\infty\) with \(r\) fixed and positive, \((1+r)^t\to\infty\), so \(DF\to 0\) — an arbitrarily distant dollar is worth arbitrarily little today, however large its face amount, which is exactly the asymptotic behavior both curves in the chart show.
Q3. In what precise sense is "a continuously-compounded interest rate" the same mathematical object as "a log return per unit time" from the Returns & Compounding primer?
Continuous compounding gives \(FV=PV\cdot e^{rt}\). Taking the natural log of both sides gives \(\ln(FV/PV)=rt\). The primer defines a log return as \(\ln(P_{\text{end}}/P_{\text{start}})\) over any horizon; \(\ln(FV/PV)\) is exactly that quantity, applied to a bond growing from \(PV\) to \(FV\). So \(rt\) — rate times time — is the log return over the horizon \(t\), meaning \(r\) itself is a log return per unit time. This is why continuously-compounded rates are additive across time with no cross term (two years at 5% continuously compounded gives exactly the same total growth as year one at 5% plus year two at 5%) — it's the identical log-additivity property the primer proved from \(\ln(ab)=\ln a + \ln b\), not a separate fact that happens to look similar.
Q4. If the nominal interest rate is 7% and inflation runs at 4%, what is the approximate real rate, what is the more precise real rate, and why does someone who cares about purchasing power need the real rate rather than the nominal one?
The quick approximation is \(i_{\text{real}}\approx i_{\text{nominal}}-\pi = 7\%-4\%=3\%\). The exact Fisher relationship, \(i_{\text{real}}=(1+i_{\text{nominal}})/(1+\pi)-1\), gives \(1.07/1.04-1\approx 2.88\%\), slightly below the naive subtraction — the approximation always overstates the real rate a little because it ignores the cross term in the exact formula. The nominal rate tells you how many more dollars you'll have; the real rate tells you how much more you can actually buy with them, since prices rose too. A 7% nominal return sitting next to 4% inflation is a much smaller genuine gain in purchasing power (about 2.9%) than the headline 7% figure alone suggests.
Q5. In the USD/JPY scenario (USD 5%, JPY 0.1%, spot 150.00), why does the 3-month forward come in below spot rather than above it — and what specific arbitrage would exist if the forward were instead quoted equal to spot?
The forward comes in below spot because USD pays the higher interest rate. If it were quoted equal to spot instead, a trader could borrow JPY (the cheap, low-rate currency), convert to USD at spot, invest the USD at the higher 5% rate, and convert the proceeds back to JPY at the (too-generous) 150.00 forward — netting a riskless profit funded entirely by the interest-rate gap, as the arithmetic in the scenario box shows explicitly (a 1.8375 JPY profit on zero net capital). The market prevents this by pricing the forward at roughly 148.19 instead, exactly low enough that converting the USD proceeds back to JPY at that rate only just covers the JPY loan repayment — zero arbitrage profit, which is the actual condition that pins down the forward rate. The general rule: the higher-yielding currency (here, USD) always trades at a forward discount, never a premium, given this setup.
Q6. The Black-Scholes call formula contains the term \(Ke^{-rT}\). Using only the time-value-of-money concepts from this lesson — not the option-pricing logic itself — what does that term compute, and why must some version of it appear in any correctly-priced claim on a future cash flow?
\(Ke^{-rT}\) is simply the present value of the strike price \(K\), to be potentially paid \(T\) years from now, discounted back to today using the continuous-compounding discount factor \(e^{-rT}\) at the risk-free rate \(r\) — exactly the \(PV=FV\times DF(r,t)\) machinery derived in this lesson, with \(FV=K\), rate \(r\), and horizon \(T\). Some version of this term must appear in any correctly-priced claim on a future cash flow because comparing a dollar amount due in the future to a price quoted today, without first converting them to the same point in time via discounting, is comparing two different kinds of quantities as if they were the same thing — exactly the "$100 now vs. $100 in a year" mistake this lesson opened with, just embedded inside a more complicated formula.
