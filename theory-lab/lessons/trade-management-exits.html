Managing the Trade: R-Multiples, Stops & Exits

Once you are in, every decision is an exit decision. Measure trades in R, put stops where the idea is wrong rather than where it starts to hurt, and know what targets, trailing stops and partial exits really do to your expectancy.

CONCEPT — a framework for exits, not a tested strategy ~19 min read Prereqs: Kelly Criterion, Drawdowns
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • Measure every trade in R, the amount you planned to lose: \(R = (\text{exit} - \text{entry})/(\text{entry} - \text{stop})\). Expectancy is \(E = p\,\bar W - (1-p)\,\bar L\). Win 40% of the time at +2R against −1R losers and \(E = +0.20R\) a trade; the break-even win rate is 33%.
  • A stop is an invalidation point, not a pain threshold. Put it where the reason for the trade is wrong, and beyond ordinary noise. On a random walk a stop 0.4 daily standard deviations away is touched within a day 69% of the time; at 1.5 it is 13%. Scale stops with volatility, not with a fixed number of pips.
  • Exits reshape outcomes; they do not create edge. On a random walk a +2R target is hit before a −1R stop one time in three, and \(E = \tfrac13(2) - \tfrac23(1) = 0\). Targets, trailing stops, time stops and partial exits only add value when the market has persistence or your idea has an edge. Partial exits and break-even stops smooth the ride (in this lesson's example the hit rate rises from 33% to 50%), but never lift expectancy above the better of the parts.
  • Set stops and targets from evidence. Maximum adverse and favourable excursion (MAE, MFE) of past trades show how far winners usually went against you, and how far losers went for you before turning.
  • Management leaks are expensive. Close half your winners at +1R instead of +2R and let 1 loser in 5 run to −3R, and the same entries fall from +0.20R to −0.24R a trade.
  • Real-world episode: on 15 January 2015 the Swiss National Bank dropped its 1.20 floor under EUR/CHF without warning, and EUR/CHF fell below 0.90 within minutes. A stop is an order, not a guarantee: a 50-pip stop that fills 1,050 pips away is a 21R loss.

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

I spend hours on entries. Once I'm in, I just sort of manage it by feel.

Then most of your results are being decided by feel. Every choice after the entry is an exit choice: where the stop goes, whether to take profit, whether to trail, when to give up.

So how do I even compare trades? One was fifty pips, one was two hundred.

Measure them in R. One R is the loss you planned when you placed the stop. A trade that makes twice its planned risk is plus two R. A stopped-out trade is minus one R. Now every trade speaks the same language.

And then?

Then expectancy is simple. Say you win forty percent of the time, winners make two R, losers lose one R. Forty percent of two is zero point eight. Sixty percent of one is zero point six. You make a fifth of an R per trade, on average.

Only forty percent winners, and it still makes money.

Yes, because the winners are bigger. Now the bad news. Close half your winners early at one R, and let one loser in five run to minus three because you moved the stop. Same entries. You now lose about a quarter of an R per trade.

Ouch. So where should the stop go?

Where the reason for the trade is wrong. Not where it starts to hurt. And outside ordinary noise. If a day's typical move is fifty pips, a twenty pip stop gets touched by pure noise most days, about seven times in ten on a random walk.

What about targets versus letting winners run? Everyone argues about that.

Here's the uncomfortable part. If prices were pure noise, no exit rule would help. A two R target before a one R stop gets hit one time in three, and that works out to exactly zero. Exits only shape the outcomes. The edge has to come from the idea.

And partial exits? Taking half off feels safer.

It is smoother. You win more often and the swings are smaller. But the expectancy is just the average of the two halves. Scaling out can't beat the better half.

And the stop always protects me?

A stop is an order, not a guarantee. In January twenty fifteen the Swiss central bank dropped its floor under the franc without warning. The euro fell from one twenty to below ninety centimes in minutes. Stops just under one twenty filled far lower. A fifty pip stop filled a thousand pips away is a twenty one R loss.

So the one-line version?

Count in R, put the stop where the idea dies, size for the gap, and never move a stop further away.

Section 01

Why this matters

Most trading education is about entries: what to buy, when, and why. But an entry is one decision. Everything that happens afterwards, every adjustment to the stop, every early profit, every "let me give it until Friday", is an exit decision, and there are many more of them. Two traders with identical entries can end up with opposite results purely from how they manage the position. The picture above is not a stretch: the leaks in it are the ones the behavioural finance literature has documented for decades.

This lesson gives you a language and a toolkit for the part after the click. The language is the R-multiple: every result expressed as a multiple of the risk you planned. The toolkit is the set of exits (stops, targets, trailing stops, time stops, partial exits) and the maths that says what each one does to the distribution of your results. The most important single fact in it is a negative one. On a market with no edge, no exit rule can create one. Exits redistribute: they trade hit rate for payoff, or smoothness for upside. That is still worth a great deal, because a trader who can stick to a plan is worth more than one with a better plan they abandon.

The admin lesson From a View to a Trade Plan covers the five numbers to write down before entry (entry, invalidation, stop, target, size). This lesson picks up from there.

💡
Plain-English intuition

A stop is where the idea dies, not where it starts to hurt

Every trade starts with a reason: "EUR/USD should rise because the rate gap has turned in its favour", or "this range should hold". Somewhere on the chart there is a price at which that reason is plainly wrong. If the range breaks, the range trade is over. That price is the invalidation point, and the stop belongs just beyond it.

A pain threshold is different: "I don't want to lose more than 20 pips." The market does not know about your pain. If ordinary noise moves 50 pips a day, a 20-pip stop will be hit by noise most days, while your reason for the trade is still intact. You will be right about the idea and still lose. The fix is not a tighter or looser stop. It is to put the stop where the idea fails, and then choose the position size so that hitting it costs an amount you can accept. Fear belongs in the size, never in the stop.

Once the stop is set, it defines 1R, your unit of risk. Every exit after that, a target, a trailing stop, a half-off at +1R, is a choice about how to turn a stream of uncertain price paths into a stream of R results. The maths below shows which of those choices can matter, and which ones only feel like they do.

The math, step by step

Watch the formula build
Expectancy in R, and the win rate you need, one term at a time
\[ E = \color{#fbbf24}{p\,\bar W} \]

Start with the winners. A share \(p\) of trades win, and the average winner makes \(\bar W\) times your planned risk.

\[ E = p\,\bar W \color{#fbbf24}{\;-\;(1-p)\,\bar L} \]

Subtract the losers. The other \(1-p\) of trades lose \(\bar L\) R on average. If every stop is honoured and fills at its level, \(\bar L = 1\).

\[ E = p\,\bar W - (1-p)\,\bar L \color{#fbbf24}{\;-\;c} \]

Pay the costs. Spread and slippage per round trip, measured in R, come off every trade, winner or loser.

\[ E = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \color{#10b981}{p^* = \frac{\bar L + c}{\bar W + \bar L}} \]

Set expectancy to zero and solve for the win rate. With +2R winners, −1R losers and no costs, \(p^* = 1/3\): you need to win more than one trade in three.

The R-multiple. Fix the stop before you enter. The distance from entry to stop is your planned risk per unit, and it becomes the unit for everything that follows. For a long position:

\[ R = \frac{\text{exit} - \text{entry}}{\text{entry} - \text{stop}} \]
Rthe trade's result as a multiple of its planned risk
exit − entrywhat the trade actually made or lost per unit
entry − stopthe planned risk per unit, fixed before entry (1R)

For a short, flip both signs. A trade bought at 1.0850 with a stop at 1.0775 risks 75 pips; exiting at 1.1000 makes 150 pips, which is +2R. Stopped out at 1.0775, it is −1R. If the stop gaps and fills at 1.0700, it is −150/75 = −2R. Because R divides out the position size and the instrument, a 50-pip trade in EUR/USD and a 300-point trade in an index can sit in the same journal and be compared directly. The idea was popularised by Van Tharp, whose books made "R-multiples" and "expectancy" standard trading vocabulary.

With \(p = 0.40\), \(\bar W = 2\) and \(\bar L = 1\): \(E = 0.40 \times 2 - 0.60 \times 1 = +0.20R\). If you risk 0.5% of the account per trade, that is +0.10% of the account per trade on average. 🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Before each game you decide you could lose one sweet. That one sweet is "1R". If you win two sweets, that's +2R. If you win two sweets 4 games out of 10 and lose one sweet the other 6, after 10 games you have 8 − 6 = 2 extra sweets: a fifth of a sweet per game. You lose more often than you win, and you still come out ahead, because your wins are bigger.

How noisy is an expectancy? A single trade's result swings far more than its average. For the two-outcome case (win \(\bar W\), lose \(\bar L\)) the variance per trade is:

\[ \sigma_R^2 = p\,\bar W^2 + (1-p)\,\bar L^2 - E^2 \]
σ_R²variance of one trade's result, in R²
pW̄² + (1−p)L̄²average squared result
− E²minus the squared average

For the 40%, +2R, −1R system: \(0.4 \times 4 + 0.6 \times 1 - 0.04 = 2.16\), so \(\sigma_R = 1.47R\). The average is 0.20R; the typical swing is seven times bigger. Over 200 trades the expected total is +40R with a standard deviation of \(1.47\sqrt{200} = 20.8R\), so about 1 run in 37 (2.7%) still finishes below zero. To see the edge two standard errors clear of zero you need roughly \(n = (2\sigma_R/E)^2 = (2 \times 1.47/0.20)^2 \approx 216\) trades. That is why judging an exit rule on the last twenty trades tells you almost nothing.

Stops: noise, volatility and the reflection principle. How often does pure noise touch a stop? Treat price over a few days as a random walk with daily standard deviation \(\sigma\) and no drift. The reflection principle (the classic result for Brownian motion) gives the chance that the price touches a level \(d\) below entry at some point within \(T\) days:

\[ P(\text{touch within } T) = 2\,\Phi\!\left(-\frac{d}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}\right) \]
dstop distance from entry, in pips
σ√Thow far noise typically wanders in T days
2Φ(−·)twice the one-sided normal tail: paths that touch and come back count too

The ratio \(d/\sigma\) is all that matters. With a daily \(\sigma\) of 50 pips, a 20-pip stop (\(d/\sigma = 0.4\)) is touched within one day 69% of the time. A 75-pip stop (\(1.5\sigma\)) is touched within a day 13% of the time, and within five days 50% of the time. If volatility doubles to 100 pips a day, the same 20-pip stop is touched within a day 84% of the time, while a stop that doubles with it (150 pips) is still at 13%. A volatility-based stop, a fixed multiple of \(\sigma\) or of the average true range (ATR), keeps the stop's meaning constant as markets calm down and heat up. A fixed-pip stop does not: it is wide in a quiet market and a coin toss in a busy one.

ATR, introduced by J. Welles Wilder in 1978, is the average of each day's true range (high to low, stretched to include any gap from the previous close). For a random walk the expected daily high-to-low range is \(\sqrt{8/\pi}\,\sigma \approx 1.6\sigma\), so as a rough guide an ATR of 80 pips goes with a daily \(\sigma\) of about 50. Real markets have fat tails and gaps, so ATR runs a little differently, but the scaling holds: stops in ATR or \(\sigma\) multiples move with the market.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine a puppy on a long walk, wandering left and right. If you draw a line just one step to its left, it will cross it almost straight away, even if it is heading forwards. Draw the line five steps away and it only crosses if it really turns around. A stop is that line. A bouncy puppy (a busy market) needs the line further away.

Costs scale with tightness too. A round-trip cost of 2 pips is \(c = 2/10 = 0.20R\) on a 10-pip stop, \(0.10R\) on a 20-pip stop and \(0.027R\) on a 75-pip stop. A tight stop pays the spread on a bigger position, so the same pips of cost take a much bigger bite out of every trade's expectancy.

The trap: moving a stop further away is not "giving it room". Once the stop is beyond the invalidation point, the reason for the trade has already failed. Moving it is a new, bigger bet on a broken idea, made at the moment you are least able to judge it. Let 1 loser in 5 run to −3R and \(\bar L\) rises from 1.0 to \(0.8 \times 1 + 0.2 \times 3 = 1.4\); a +0.20R system becomes \(0.8 - 0.6 \times 1.4 = -0.04R\).

Why exits cannot create an edge from noise. Put a stop \(a\) below entry and a target \(b\) above it on a driftless random walk. The chance of reaching the target first is the gambler's-ruin result:

\[ P(\text{target first}) = \frac{a}{a+b} \quad\Rightarrow\quad E = \frac{a}{a+b}\cdot b - \frac{b}{a+b}\cdot a = 0 \]
adistance down to the stop
bdistance up to the target
a/(a+b)chance the target is hit first, with no drift
E = 0whatever a and b you choose

With a 1R stop and a 2R target, \(P = 1/3\) and \(E = \tfrac13 \times 2 - \tfrac23 \times 1 = 0\). With a 1R stop and a 0.5R target, \(P = 2/3\) and \(E = \tfrac23 \times 0.5 - \tfrac13 \times 1 = 0\). The hit rate moves from 33% to 67%; the expectancy does not move at all. The general statement is Wald's identity (the optional stopping theorem): if price drifts by \(\mu\) a day while you hold it, then for any exit rule that does not see the future,

\[ E[\text{profit per trade}] = \mu \cdot E[\tau] \]
μdrift: the expected move per day in your favour while you hold
E[τ]average holding time under your exit rule
μ·E[τ]no drift, no profit, whatever the exit

So with no drift, every stop, target, trail or time rule has zero expectancy before costs, and negative after. With a real, constant drift, an exit rule only changes how long you are exposed to it. Exits matter in three situations: when the drift is not constant (an edge that fades, so a time stop ends exposure once it is gone), when price action carries information about whether the idea is right (adverse moves mean "probably wrong", so a stop cuts the wrong ideas early while right ones run), and when the path has persistence or reversal (trends reward letting winners run; ranges reward taking profits). Kaminski and Lo (2014) make the stop-loss version of this precise: under a random walk a stop-loss rule always lowers expected return, while with momentum in returns it can add value.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

If a game is pure luck, you can't win by choosing when to stop playing. Stopping when you're two sweets up only happens one time in three; the other two times you hit "one sweet down" first. It always evens out. Stopping rules only help when the game isn't pure luck.

Targets, letting winners run, and the cost of a trailing stop. A profit target caps the winner at \(b\): high hit rate, capped payoff. "Letting winners run" means no fixed target, usually with a trailing stop that follows the best price at a distance \(d\). A trailing stop always exits \(d\) below the best price reached (ignoring gaps), so every trade gives back exactly \(d\) from its peak. On a driftless walk the best price reached before a pullback of \(d\) averages exactly \(d\) above entry (it is exponentially distributed with mean \(d\); see Lehoczky, 1977), so the trail's expectancy is again zero. With an upward drift \(\mu\) the peak averages \(\frac{\sigma^2}{2\mu}\left(e^{2\mu d/\sigma^2} - 1\right)\), a little more than \(d\), and the trail earns the difference. Two costs follow. A tight trail is stopped by noise long before a trend can develop (the reflection numbers above apply to every pullback). A wide trail hands back a lot at the end of each good trade. Which wins depends on whether your market trends after your entries: that is an empirical question, not a matter of taste.

Time stops. Most edges have a lifetime: a reaction to news, a mean reversion that should play out within days, a seasonal flow. Once that window has passed, the drift is gone, and by Wald's identity each extra day in the trade adds variance (about \(\sigma^2\) per day) and no expected profit. A time stop, "exit after \(N\) days if neither stop nor target has been hit", ends exposure when the reason has expired. It also frees the risk budget for the next trade.

Partial exits and scaling. Scaling out, say half the position at +1R and the rest at a further target, is a blend of two exit rules. Expectancy is linear in position size, so the blend's expectancy is the weighted average of the parts:

\[ E_{\text{blend}} = w\,E_1 + (1-w)\,E_2 \]
wshare of the position closed by rule 1
E₁, E₂expectancy of each exit rule on its own
E_blendcan never beat the better of E₁ and E₂

What scaling out does change is the shape. Take a driftless walk, a −1R stop and three plans, worked out with the \(a/(a+b)\) rule above:

PlanOutcomes (probability)Hit rateEσ per trade
All out at +2R+2R (1/3), −1R (2/3)33%01.41R
Half at +1R, half at +3R+2R (1/4), 0 (1/4), −1R (1/2)25%01.22R

The last plan wins half the time instead of a third, and its swings are a fifth smaller, but its expectancy is still exactly zero. On a market where your entries do have an edge, the same arithmetic says partials and break-even stops trade some expectancy for smoothness. That can be a good trade, if smoothness is what keeps you following the plan, but it is a trade, not a free improvement. Scaling in (adding to a winner) is the mirror image: it raises size after a favourable move, which pays only if moves persist, and it changes your total risk. After adding, recompute where the combined position's stop is and what hitting it would cost; it is often more than the original 1R.

MAE and MFE: set stops and targets from evidence. For every closed trade, record two more numbers in R or in \(\sigma\): the maximum adverse excursion (how far it went against you at worst) and the maximum favourable excursion (how far it went for you at best). John Sweeney's 1996 book made this the standard way to tune exits. The test is simple. If eventual winners rarely went more than about 1σ against you, a stop at 1σ costs you few winners and cuts losers early. If many losers were at +1R at some point before turning, a partial exit or a trail would have banked some of that. The chart below runs this study on a simulated set of trades so you can see how it reads.

Gap risk. All the maths above assumes the price passes through your stop, so the stop fills at its level. Markets do not always oblige. Over a weekend, through a central bank announcement or in a sudden liquidity hole, price can jump past the stop, and a stop order then fills at the next available price. If a share \(g\) of losers fill at \(G\) R instead of 1R:

\[ \bar L = (1-g)\cdot 1 + g\cdot G \]
gshare of losing trades that gap through the stop
Gsize of a gapped loss, in R
L̄average loser, now above 1R

With 5% of losers gapping to −3R, \(\bar L = 0.95 + 0.15 = 1.10\) and the 0.20R system drops to \(0.80 - 0.60 \times 1.10 = 0.14R\). The rare, very large gap is a sizing problem rather than an expectancy problem: if one trade can fill at −20R, the risk per trade has to be small enough that −20R is survivable.

Worked example

One EUR/USD plan, in R, with a volatility stop

Illustrative numbers. EUR/USD is at 1.0850 and you are long. The analysis says the idea is wrong below about 1.0790. A day's \(\sigma\) is about 50 pips (an ATR of about 80). You place the stop at 1.0775, \(1.5\sigma = 75\) pips away, just beyond the invalidation level, and a target at 1.1000, 150 pips away. Costs are 2 pips a round trip.

\[ 1R = 75 \text{ pips}, \qquad \text{target} = \frac{150}{75} = 2R, \qquad c = \frac{2}{75} = 0.027R \]
1R = 75 pipsthe stop distance, 1.5 daily σ
2Rthe target in units of risk
c = 0.027R2 pips of cost on a 75-pip risk unit

Suppose past trades like this won 40% of the time. Expectancy and break-even:

\[ E = 0.40(2) - 0.60(1) - 0.027 = +0.173R, \qquad p^* = \frac{1 + 0.027}{2 + 1} = 34.2\% \]
E = +0.173Rabout 13 pips a trade on average
p* = 34.2%the hit rate below which the plan loses

Risking 0.5% of the account per trade, that is \(0.173 \times 0.5\% = 0.087\%\) of the account per trade. On noise alone the stop is touched within a day 13% of the time and within five days about half the time, so a multi-day idea will often need its full stop distance. Now compare a "safe" 20-pip stop with the same 150-pip target. The target is 7.5R, but noise touches the stop within a day 69% of the time, and costs are \(2/20 = 0.10R\) a trade. The tight stop did not reduce risk. It changed what the trade is: a lottery ticket on an immediate move, instead of a bet on the idea.

What the simulator shows. Set "Win rate & payoff" to 40% and 2.0R with seed 42 (the defaults). That run wins 41.5% of its 200 trades, earns +0.245R a trade (+49R in total) and has a worst drawdown of 10.0R. The theory says +0.20R: one run of 200 trades is noisy. Across 1,000 runs the median worst drawdown is 13R and 1 run in 20 sees 23R or more, from a system with a genuine edge. Now set "Losers that blow through" to 20%. Theory drops to −0.04R a trade, the same seed's run still happens to end +17R, and the median worst drawdown jumps to 31R. Small, invisible leaks; large, very visible drawdowns.

Exit rules in the simulator. Switch to "Price paths & exit rules", keep seed 42, set the trend to 0 (pure noise) and pick "Trailing stop". This seed's 200 trades make +19.2R, +0.096R a trade, and look like a strategy. The long-run line underneath says −0.057R ± 0.026: on noise, every exit rule earns minus its costs. Now set the trend back to 0.15. In the long run the 2R target earns +0.051R, the trailing stop +0.047R and "let it run" to the 10-day time stop +0.145R, each give or take 0.03 to 0.05. In this toy, where a right idea keeps trending, letting winners run pays best; yet seed 42's 200-trade runs for the target and "let it run" rules both finish slightly negative. Exit choices are real but small next to the noise of a few hundred trades.

Real-world trading scenario

Up 1.5R the day before a central bank meeting

Illustrative. You are long EUR/USD from the worked example, entry 1.0850, stop 1.0775 (1R = 75 pips), target 1.1000. Four days in, it trades at 1.0960: +1.47R. Tomorrow the ECB announces its decision. Three instincts arrive at once: "move the stop to break-even so this can't become a loser", "take half off", and "it's going my way, let it run".

Run each through the lesson. A break-even stop is an exit rule like any other: on noise it raises the hit rate and lowers expectancy by exactly nothing, and with a real edge it gives some up, because a normal pullback of 1.47R (about 2.2 daily σ, not rare over several days) now ends a trade whose reason is still intact. Taking half off is a blend: smoother, never better than the better half. Neither question is the right first question. The right first question is is the reason still valid, and what happens over the event? A central bank decision is a scheduled gap risk. If the plan's gap line says a 150-pip jump through the stop is possible, the position is sized for 1R but exposed to 3R.

A disciplined answer: if the trade was never meant to hold through the decision, a time or event rule closes or cuts it today, whatever the open profit. If it was, keep the stop at invalidation (not at a feel-good level) and cut the size to what you can lose on a gap. Either way, the decision comes from the plan, not from the open P&L, which is exactly the anchor the disposition effect pulls on.

Section 03

It actually happened: the Swiss franc, 15 January 2015

Every formula above assumes a stop fills at its level. The clearest modern case of that assumption failing, at scale and in a major currency, is the day the Swiss National Bank abandoned its floor under EUR/CHF.

It actually happened

The floor that "could not break"

On 6 September 2011, with money pouring into the franc as a safe haven, the Swiss National Bank set a minimum exchange rate of 1.20 francs per euro and said it was prepared to buy foreign currency in unlimited quantities to defend it. For more than three years EUR/CHF sat just above 1.20. To many traders the floor looked like the perfect invalidation level: buy EUR/CHF near 1.20, put a stop a little below, risk a few dozen pips against a central bank's promise.

On the morning of 15 January 2015 the SNB announced it was discontinuing the minimum exchange rate and cut its deposit rate to −0.75%. There was no warning. EUR/CHF fell from 1.20 to below 0.90 within minutes, with almost no trading in between, before recovering part of the way. Stop orders just below 1.20 were triggered and then filled wherever the next real price was, far below their levels. Some retail clients ended the day owing their brokers money. FXCM, one of the largest US retail FX brokers, was left with client balances below zero and needed a $300 million rescue loan; Alpari UK went into insolvency.

The invalidation logic was right: below 1.20, the trade's reason was dead. What failed was the assumption that the exit would happen near the invalidation level. A stop is an order to trade at the next available price, not a promise of a price.

⚠️
Where the textbook version breaks

Continuous prices, fills at the level, and a stop that is always there

The reflection principle, the \(a/(a+b)\) rule and the trailing-stop results all assume price moves continuously, so it must pass through your stop and you fill there. Real prices jump: at weekends, at scheduled announcements, at unscheduled policy changes, and whenever liquidity disappears. Your fill is the next real trade, which can be many R away. Pegs and floors are the extreme case, because they suppress volatility right up until the moment they release it all at once, so a volatility-based stop measured during the peg is far too small. The defences are outside the exit rule: a smaller size wherever a gap is plausible, a written "gap line" for each event, and, where the cost is worth it, protection that does not depend on liquidity, such as a bought option or a broker's guaranteed stop (which some brokers sell for a premium).

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

You tell a friend: "If the price of sweets drops below 10p, sell mine." Then one morning the shop shuts, and when it opens again sweets cost 7p. Your friend can't sell at 10p, because nobody is buying at 10p any more. They sell at 7p. A stop is your friend's instruction, not a magic floor.

Section 04

How practitioners actually use this

Journal in R. Professionals who manage discretionary trades typically log every trade's planned risk, result in R, MAE and MFE. Summary statistics (hit rate, average win and loss, expectancy, the distribution of R) then describe the trading itself, independent of position sizes that changed over time. A loss larger than −1R is flagged automatically: either a gap, or a stop that was not honoured.

Volatility-scaled stops and exits. Systematic traders almost always set stops in units of volatility. The best-known published example is the Turtle traders' rules from the 1980s, documented by Curtis Faith: position size set from N (a 20-day average true range), stops at 2N from entry, and exits on a breakout in the other direction (a 10- or 20-day low for longs), which acts as a trailing exit. The "chandelier exit", associated with Chuck LeBeau, trails a stop a multiple of ATR below the highest high since entry. Both keep the stop's meaning constant across calm and busy markets.

Exits are tested like entries. A serious exit study holds the entries fixed and replays each trade's actual price path under alternative exits (fixed targets, trailing stops, break-even moves, time stops), scored in R on the same trades, with costs, on one sample, and then checks the winner on a second. Unless the entries have a real edge, expect the differences between sensible exits to be small relative to the noise: that is what Wald's identity predicts.

On the desk, backtest-exit-study.html does this for the bot's trades: it replays each trade's M1 path under fixed 1R to 3R targets, two trailing rules, a break-even move at +1R and 4-hour and 8-hour time stops, scores every rule in R against the actual exit, and labels itself exploratory (one window of a few hundred trades), a candidate to validate out of sample rather than a change to ship.

Gap risk is handled in the size. Around weekends, central bank decisions and elections, and in currencies with a peg or band, risk managers size for the plausible gap rather than for the stop, use options where a jump is the main risk, and accept that a stop's only job is to define 1R in normal conditions.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where trade management quietly goes wrong

Moving stops away, and cutting winners early. These are the disposition effect acted out inside a single trade: selling winners too early and riding losers too long (Shefrin and Statman, 1985; Odean, 1998). The cure is mechanical: write the stop and the exit rule before entry, and allow stops to move only in the direction that reduces risk.

The automatic break-even stop. Moving the stop to entry "once it's working" feels free. It is an exit rule with zero effect on noise and a cost with an edge: it turns ordinary pullbacks into scratched trades. Use it if your own MAE data say winners rarely come back to entry, not by reflex.

Comparing exits without costs, or across stop widths. Tight stops look wonderful in a costless backtest because R shrinks while spread does not. Always include spread and slippage, measured in R.

Tuning stops and targets on the same trades you judge them on. MAE and MFE studies overfit easily. Choose the rule on one period and test it on another, and prefer round, volatility-scaled numbers to a precisely optimised 1.37 ATR.

Judging an exit rule on 20 or 30 trades. With a per-trade swing of 1.5R, the standard error of the average after 30 trades is about 0.27R, bigger than most real edges.

Treating a stop as a guaranteed exit. Gaps, pegs and thin markets fill stops far beyond their level. Size for the gap when one is possible.

Scenario drill · 23 June 2016

Referendum night, and your trade is in profit

The UK votes on leaving the European Union today, and polls close at 10pm. Earlier in the week you bought GBP/USD at 1.4700 (an illustrative position) with a stop at 1.4550, 150 pips, so 1R = 150 pips. Late in the evening sterling trades around 1.50 as markets lean towards Remain: you are up about 2R. Results will arrive overnight, in thin markets. What do you do with the position before the results?

  1. Move the stop up to entry at 1.4700. Now the trade can't lose.
    A break-even stop is an order, not a floor. If the result shocks the market, price can fall far through 1.4700 before a fill. The stop changes the hit rate on a normal night; it does not cap the loss on an abnormal one.
  2. Add to the position. The polls point to Remain, and adding to winners is how trends pay.
    Scaling in only pays when moves persist. A binary event is not a trend: adding raises the size just before the largest gap risk of the year, and the combined position's real risk is now several times 1R.
  3. Close the trade, or cut it to a size whose loss you could accept if the price gapped many hundreds of pips.
    The trade was not built to hold a binary vote result. An event rule, like a time stop, ends exposure the plan never priced. If you keep any, size it for the gap line, not for the stop.
  4. Keep the trade but buy a short-dated sterling put as protection.
    Defensible: an option caps the loss whatever the gap. But protection is expensive the night everyone wants it, and it does not answer whether the trade belongs in the book at all.

What happened. Sterling traded around 1.50 against the dollar shortly after the polls closed. As results came in overnight showing a vote to leave, it fell to about 1.32 on 24 June, its lowest level since 1985, a fall of more than 10% within hours. A long from 1.4700 faced a move of roughly 1,500 pips, about 10R on a 150-pip stop; how much of that a stop saved depended on where it filled in a fast, thin market.

Section 05

Key Takeaways

✓Count every trade in R. \(R = (\text{exit} - \text{entry})/(\text{entry} - \text{stop})\) makes trades of any size and instrument comparable, and turns a journal into evidence.
✓Expectancy, not hit rate, pays. \(E = p\bar W - (1-p)\bar L - c\). 40% winners at +2R earn +0.20R a trade; break-even is \(p^* = (\bar L + c)/(\bar W + \bar L)\).
✓Stops go at invalidation, outside the noise, scaled by volatility. On a random walk a 0.4σ stop is touched within a day 69% of the time; a 1.5σ stop, 13%. Fear belongs in the size, not the stop.
✓Set exits from your own MAE and MFE, chosen on one sample and checked on another.
⚠Exits reshape; they do not create edge. With no drift, every stop, target, trail and time rule has zero expectancy before costs (\(E = \mu E[\tau]\)). Partial exits raise the hit rate and lower variance but cannot beat the better part.
⚠Judging exit rules needs hundreds of trades. A 1.47R swing per trade means about 216 trades to see a 0.20R edge two standard errors clear of zero.
✗Moving stops and cutting winners are expensive. Both together take the same entries from +0.20R to −0.24R a trade.
✗A stop is an order, not a guarantee. On 15 January 2015 EUR/CHF fell from 1.20 to below 0.90 in minutes; a 50-pip stop filled at 1.10 is −21R. Size for the gap.
Section 06

Self-test

Q1. A system wins 30% of the time. Winners average +2.5R and losers −1R, with costs of 0.05R a trade. Is it profitable, and what is its break-even win rate?
\(E = 0.30 \times 2.5 - 0.70 \times 1 - 0.05 = 0.75 - 0.70 - 0.05 = 0.00R\): exactly break-even. The break-even win rate is \(p^* = (1 + 0.05)/(2.5 + 1) = 30\%\). A low hit rate is fine with big enough winners, but here costs eat the whole edge. Widening the stop (so the same pips of cost are a smaller share of R) or improving the entry would be the levers, not a target change made without evidence.
Q2. Why is "I don't want to lose more than 20 pips" a poor way to set a stop on a pair whose daily σ is about 50 pips?
Because the stop is set by your pain, not by where the idea fails, and it sits inside ordinary noise. On a random walk a stop 0.4σ away is touched within a day about 69% of the time (\(2\Phi(-0.4)\)), so you are stopped out of trades whose reason is still intact, and 2 pips of cost are already 0.10R. Put the stop beyond the invalidation level and control the money at risk through position size instead.
Q3. On a driftless random walk, you exit at a +2R target or a −1R stop. What is the hit rate and the expectancy? What changes if the target is +0.5R?
The chance of hitting the target first is \(a/(a+b) = 1/3\), so the hit rate is 33% and \(E = \tfrac13 \times 2 - \tfrac23 \times 1 = 0\). With a +0.5R target the hit rate rises to \(1/1.5 = 67\%\) and \(E = \tfrac23 \times 0.5 - \tfrac13 \times 1 = 0\). The hit rate moved; the expectancy did not. Exits only redistribute outcomes; with no drift, no exit rule creates an edge (Wald's identity, \(E = \mu E[\tau] = 0\)).
Q4. A trader takes half off at +1R on every trade "to lock in profits". What does this do to expectancy, hit rate and variance?
Expectancy is the weighted average of the two halves' expectancies, so it can never exceed the better of them; if the trader's edge comes from letting trades run, the half-off gives some of it up. The variance falls, and the share of trades that bank something rises: on a driftless walk with a −1R stop, half at +1R and half at +3R wins 25% outright with another quarter scratching to zero (σ 1.22R against 1.41R for all-out at +2R), and adding a break-even stop after the first half lifts the hit rate to 50% and cuts σ to 1.12R, all at an expectancy of exactly zero. It is a trade of upside for smoothness, worth making only if the smoothness keeps you on the plan.
Q5. Your MAE study shows that 90% of your eventual winners never went more than 0.8 ATR against you, but your stop sits at 2 ATR. What might you change, and how should you check it?
A stop nearer 0.8 to 1 ATR would cost few winners and cut most losers at a smaller loss, and because 1R shrinks, the same risk budget buys a bigger position. But choose the new level on one period of trades and test it on a separate period, include costs (a tighter stop makes each pip of cost a bigger share of R), and check the stop still sits beyond a sensible invalidation point. An MAE threshold tuned and judged on the same trades will look better than it is.
Q6. You were long EUR/CHF at 1.2050 with a stop at 1.2000 on 15 January 2015. Why did the stop fail to limit the loss to 1R, and what should the plan have done differently?
When the SNB scrapped the 1.20 floor, the price jumped far below 1.20 with almost no trading in between. A stop is an order that fills at the next available price, so it filled far below its level: a fill at 1.1000 is −1,050 pips, or −21R on a 50-pip stop. The invalidation level was right; the assumption of a continuous exit was wrong. The plan needed a gap line, the loss if the floor broke, and a position size small enough to survive it, or protection that does not rely on liquidity, such as an option.
