When an Edge Has Stopped Working

Every edge has losing stretches, and some edges really do die. This lesson separates the two with arithmetic you set up in advance — streak odds, a test of the live record, a CUSUM alarm, and an honest count of how many trades it takes — and ends with a decay verdict you can act on.

CONCEPT ~22 min read Prereqs: Drawdowns and Risk of Ruin; Normal distribution
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • A losing streak is weak evidence. At a tested 55% win rate, a run of 7+ losses turns up somewhere in 180 trades about 31% of the time. Streaks tell you about variance, not about the edge.
  • Test the whole live record against the tested one. \(z = (W - np_0)/\sqrt{np_0q_0}\). 88 wins from 180 against a tested 55% is \(z = -1.65\): suggestive, not conclusive.
  • Small decays take hundreds of trades to see. Telling 55% from 50% reliably (5% false alarm, 80% power) needs about 615 trades; 63% from 50% needs about 88. Work this number out before you go live.
  • CUSUM watches trade by trade. Add a little for each loss, take a little off for each win, never go below zero, and stop at a threshold \(h\) chosen for an acceptable false-alarm rate. With \(h = 3\), false alarms in 300 trades are about 3.5%, and a fall from 55% to 50% is caught within 1,000 trades about 95% of the time.
  • Write the rule first, act on the rule. Green / amber (half size, review) / red (stop, re-test). Never re-fit the strategy on the live sample to make the alarm go away.
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

My system just lost seven trades in a row. It tested at fifty-five percent wins. I think it's broken.

Maybe. But the streak is the weakest evidence you have. At fifty-five percent, a run of seven losses turns up somewhere in a hundred and eighty trades about three times in ten. Edge or no edge, streaks look much the same.

So what should I look at instead?

The whole record. How many trades so far, and how many wins?

A hundred and eighty trades, eighty-eight wins.

At fifty-five percent you would expect ninety-nine wins. Luck alone typically moves that by about seven either way. You are eleven short, so about one and two thirds of those typical swings below.

Is that bad?

It is amber. Not nothing, not proof. The rule you write in advance says amber means halve the size and look for a cause: wider spreads, worse fills, a data change, a market the backtest never saw.

Why not just stop?

Because a hundred and eighty trades can barely tell. To separate a fifty-five percent edge from a coin flip reliably, you need around six hundred trades. At a hundred trades a year, that is six years.

Six years? Then how do I ever know?

You set an alarm that watches every trade. Each loss adds a little to a running total, each win takes a little off, and it never goes below zero. When the total crosses a level you chose in advance, you stop and re-test.

Like a smoke alarm.

Exactly like one. Set it low and toast sets it off. Set it high and the real fire takes longer to catch. You can't have fast and quiet together, so you pick the trade-off before you start.

Has this gone wrong for real money?

August two thousand and seven. Many quant equity funds took sudden, heavy losses in the same few days while the market barely moved. Much of it reversed soon after, but some funds had already cut near the worst point. And underneath, the same kind of strategy had been earning less for years as more money chased it.

So a sharp shock that wasn't decay, and a slow decay nobody saw.

Both in one episode. A rule that only watched the last week would have read them exactly backwards.

The one-line version?

Write down what broken means before you go live, judge the whole record against the tested edge, and never re-fit the system on its losing stretch.

Section 01

Why this matters

Drawdowns and Risk of Ruin showed that a system with a real edge still loses eight in a row and still sits underwater for months — and that people abandon good systems at the bottom. That lesson ended with an instruction: define "broken" in advance. This one builds that definition.

The failure runs both ways. Switch off too readily and you abandon every edge in its first ordinary slump. Switch off too reluctantly and you keep paying for an edge that crowding, a new regime or a change in costs has already removed. The tools here are the ones statisticians built for exactly this question — has the process changed, or is this the usual noise? — and they connect to change-point detection (the general theory), Base Rates First (how to weigh a live record against a prior), and Scoring Your Own Calls (the same discipline, applied to forecasts rather than trades).

💡
Plain-English intuition

A smoke alarm needs a threshold

A smoke alarm that goes off every time you make toast is useless — you take the battery out, and then it misses the real fire. One that only goes off when the kitchen is fully ablaze is useless too. A good alarm is set so false alarms are rare and real fires are caught quickly, and you cannot have both perfectly: lower the threshold and you get more toast alarms; raise it and real fires take longer to notice.

Watching a trading edge is the same problem. Every loss is a little smoke. Most of it is toast. The job is to decide, before any of it happens, how much smoke over how long counts as a fire — and to know how long a real fire would take to set the alarm off.

How it reached trading

Is the manager unlucky, or has the skill gone?

CUSUM: E. S. Page, 1954 · sequential testing: Abraham Wald, 1940s · into manager monitoring: Philips, Yashchin & Stein, 2003

Allocators and risk managers have always faced the question: a strategy or a manager has had a bad year — fire them, or hold? Comparing annual returns with a benchmark answers it badly. A year is too short to separate skill from luck, and looking again every month quietly raises the chance of a false alarm each time you look.

The tools came from industrial quality control, where a factory needs to know as soon as a machine drifts out of tolerance without stopping the line for every odd part: Page's cumulative-sum (CUSUM) chart of 1954, built on Wald's sequential probability ratio test. In 2003 Thomas Philips, Emmanuel Yashchin and David Stein set out a CUSUM scheme for monitoring active managers' excess returns, tuned so that a manager with real skill rarely triggers it while one who has lost it does within a predictable time.

The same machinery applies to any trading rule with a tested record: a strategy's live trades are the factory's parts, and the tested win rate or average return is the tolerance.

The math, step by step

Watch the formula build
The record test on the worked example, one term at a time
\[ \color{#fbbf24}{n\,p_0} = 180 \times 0.55 = 99 \]

Start with what the tested edge expects. After 180 live trades at a tested 55%, you would expect 99 wins.

\[ \color{#fbbf24}{W} - n\,p_0 = 88 - 99 = -11 \]

Subtract it from the wins you actually have. The live record is 11 wins short.

\[ \frac{W - n\,p_0}{\color{#fbbf24}{\sqrt{n\,p_0\,q_0}}} = \frac{-11}{\sqrt{180 \times 0.55 \times 0.45}} = \frac{-11}{6.67} \]

Divide by the noise: how far luck alone typically moves the win count after 180 trades. Here that is about 6.7 wins.

\[ \color{#10b981}{z = -1.65} \quad \Rightarrow \quad -2.33 < z \le -1.28: \text{ amber} \]

The shortfall is 1.65 typical swings. That is suggestive, not conclusive: the pre-set rule says halve the size and review, not stop.

1. Streaks: compute them, do not read them

With a win rate \(p\) and loss rate \(q = 1 - p\), a run of \(k\) losses starting at one particular trade has probability \(q^k\). The number that matters is the chance of such a run somewhere in \(n\) trades, which comes from the recursion in the drawdowns lesson:

\[ P(\text{run} \ge k \text{ in } n) = 1 - a_n, \qquad a_n = \sum_{j=0}^{k-1} p\,q^{j}\,a_{n-j-1}, \quad a_n = 1 \text{ for } n < k \]
aₙchance that n trades contain no losing run of k or more
p qʲj losses, then the win that ends the run
1 − aₙchance the run turns up somewhere

At 55%, a run of 7 starting on one given trade is \(0.45^7 \approx 0.0037\), about 1 in 268. Over 180 trades it is 30.6%; over 300 trades, 46.0%. A streak is a fact about the shape of the record, not its average. A system with no edge at all produces streaks of almost the same length.

2. The record test: is the live win rate still the tested one?

The question that does carry information is whether the whole live record is consistent with the tested edge. If the tested win rate is \(p_0\) and you have won \(W\) of \(n\) live trades, then under "nothing has changed" \(W\) has mean \(np_0\) and standard deviation \(\sqrt{np_0q_0}\):

\[ z = \frac{W - n\,p_0}{\sqrt{n\,p_0\,q_0}} \]

For a strategy measured in R (profit per trade in units of the amount risked) rather than wins, use the average: \(z = (\bar{x} - \mu_0)/(s/\sqrt{n})\), with \(\mu_0\) the tested average and \(s\) the standard deviation of live trades. Either way, the test compares the live record with the tested number, not with zero.

3. How many trades before you can tell

Fix the false-alarm rate \(\alpha\) (switching off a healthy edge) and the power \(1-\beta\) (catching a dead one). To tell a tested \(p_0\) from a decayed \(p_1\), you need roughly

\[ n \approx \left( \frac{z_{\alpha}\sqrt{p_0q_0} + z_{\beta}\sqrt{p_1q_1}}{p_0 - p_1} \right)^{2} \]
z_α1.645 for a 5% one-sided false-alarm rate
z_β0.842 for 80% power — catching a real decay four times in five
p₀ − p₁the size of the decay; halve it and n roughly quadruples

The same arithmetic in R: a fall of 0.1R per trade, when one trade's standard deviation is 1R, needs about \((2.487/0.1)^2 \approx 619\) trades; a fall of 0.2R, about 155. The lesson in the table is uncomfortable and important: a thin edge decaying a little is, for practical purposes, invisible for a year or more. If your strategy takes 100 trades a year and its edge is 55% against a 50% break-even, no honest test can tell you within six months that it has died.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Is a coin a tiny bit unfair? Flip it ten times and you cannot tell. Flip it a thousand times and you start to. The smaller the unfairness, the more flips you need — and an edge is just a slightly unfair coin.

4. CUSUM: an alarm that watches every trade

Testing the record once a month has a hidden cost: every look is another chance of a false alarm. Page's CUSUM looks after every trade with a known false-alarm rate. For a win/loss record, give each trade a score — the log of how much likelier that outcome is under "decayed" (\(p_1\)) than under "as tested" (\(p_0\)) — and keep a running sum that is never allowed below zero:

\[ \lambda_t = \begin{cases} \ln(p_1/p_0) & \text{win} \\ \ln(q_1/q_0) & \text{loss} \end{cases}, \qquad S_t = \max(0,\ S_{t-1} + \lambda_t), \qquad \text{alarm when } S_t \ge h \]
λa win scores negative, a loss positive. For 55% vs 50%: win −0.095, loss +0.105
max(0, …)good runs cannot bank credit forever; the sum forgets old wins, so it reacts to a recent change
Sₜthe evidence that the edge has dropped since the last time the record looked healthy
hthe alarm level; higher means fewer false alarms and slower detection

Set \(p_1\) to the break-even win rate — for a payoff ratio \(b\) (average win ÷ average loss) that is \(1/(1+b)\) — and the CUSUM is asking the question you care about: has this stopped making money? Choosing \(h\) is the smoke-alarm trade-off. Simulated for a tested 55% against a break-even 50% (40,000 paths each, seed 3):

5. One number for the whole record: the likelihood ratio

The CUSUM's running sum is built from a likelihood ratio. Applied to the whole record at once, it says how many times likelier the live record is under "decayed" than under "as tested":

\[ \text{LR} = \left(\frac{p_1}{p_0}\right)^{W} \left(\frac{q_1}{q_0}\right)^{n-W} \]
W, n − Wwins and losses in the live record
LRabove 1 favours "decayed"; multiply your prior odds by it (Bayes in one line)

That links straight to Base Rates First: a strategy that passed an honest out-of-sample test deserves prior odds in its favour, and the live record moves those odds by LR — no more.

Section 04

Worked example: a decay verdict

🧮
Worked example

180 live trades, 88 wins, and seven losses in a row

A EUR/USD system, tested out of sample at \(p_0 = 55\%\) with average win equal to average loss (\(b = 1\), break-even 50%). Live: 180 trades, 88 wins (48.9%). The first 120 trades went roughly to plan (67 wins); the last 60 have gone badly (21 wins, 39 losses), ending in seven straight losses.

1

The streak

\(P(\text{run} \ge 7 \text{ in } 180) = 30.6\%\) at 55%. Roughly one live record in three has one. On its own: not evidence.

2

The record

Expected wins \(180 \times 0.55 = 99\); standard deviation \(\sqrt{180 \times 0.55 \times 0.45} = 6.67\). \(z = (88 - 99)/6.67 = -1.65\), a one-sided p of about 0.05. In the amber band.

3

How much should that move you?

\(\text{LR} = (0.50/0.55)^{88}(0.50/0.45)^{92} = e^{1.306} = 3.7\): the record is 3.7 times likelier if the edge has gone. Start from even odds and that is a 79% chance the edge has gone. But the system passed an honest out-of-sample test — say prior odds of 3 : 1 that it is real — and the chance it has gone is \( (3.7/3)/(1 + 3.7/3) \approx 55\%\). A coin flip, not a verdict.

4

Could 180 trades even tell?

Power to detect 55% → 50% at a 5% false-alarm rate with \(n = 180\) is only about 38%. You would need about 615 trades for 80%. The record is suggestive because the test is weak, not because the evidence is clean.

5

The CUSUM

With \(h = 3\), tuned to 50%: the bad 60-trade stretch alone adds \(39 \times 0.105 - 21 \times 0.095 = +2.11\). The CUSUM reads at least 2.1 — close to the alarm, not over it.

6

The usable output

Decay verdict: AMBER — review. Halve the size now. Check for a cause you can name: wider spreads or worse fills, a data or code change, a regime the backtest never saw. Keep trading at half size and let the rule decide: red (stop and re-test) if the CUSUM reaches 3 or \(z\) falls to −2.33; back to green and full size if \(z\) recovers above −1.28.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

A fishing spot can stop working because other people found it and caught all the fish. You can't tell from one bad afternoon — fishing is like that. But if you write down every trip, you can see when the good spot has really gone quiet, and move before you waste the whole summer.

It actually happened

August 2007: the quant quake, and the slow decay before it

In the second week of August 2007 many quantitative long/short equity funds — firms running different models, apparently independently — suffered sudden, severe losses in the same few days, while the broad stock market barely moved. Much of the loss reversed shortly afterwards, but not before some funds had cut positions near the worst point.

Amir Khandani and Andrew Lo's study, "What Happened to the Quants in August 2007?", reconstructed the episode with a simple short-term contrarian strategy as a stand-in for the funds' models. Their explanation: many funds held similar positions, and the rapid unwinding of one or more large portfolios pushed prices against all of them at once. They also found something slower: the stand-in strategy's returns had been falling for years before 2007 as more capital pursued similar trades, so that funds needed more leverage to earn the same returns — which made the crowded unwind more violent when it came.

Both kinds of "stopped working" are in this one episode. The gradual erosion was real decay, visible only over years of data. The August losses looked like decay but were a crowding shock, and much of them came back. A rule that only watched the last week would have read the two exactly backwards.

It actually happened

2016: publication as a decay event

R. David McLean and Jeffrey Pontiff studied 97 published variables that had been shown to predict stock returns ("Does Academic Research Destroy Stock Return Predictability?", Journal of Finance, 2016). They compared each one's returns in the original study's sample, in the period after that sample but before publication, and after publication. Returns were 26% lower out of sample and 58% lower after publication. The authors read the first drop as partly statistical luck in the original studies and the larger post-publication drop as investors trading on what they had read. Edges decay for a reason you can name — someone else found them — and the decay is usually partial, not a switch from on to off.

Section 07

How practitioners actually use this

📐

Power before launch

Work out how many live trades it would take to see a decay to break-even. If the answer is five years, accept that the live record will not rescue a bad decision — the research has to be right.

🚦

Graded responses

A watch level that cuts size and triggers a review, and a stop level that switches off. Cutting size on amber limits the cost of being wrong in either direction.

🔁

Replay the live period

Run the live dates through the backtest engine. If the backtest also loses there, it is the market; if it wins, the gap is execution, data or code — not decay.

🧭

Name the mechanism

Decay has causes: publication, crowding, a regime change, cost changes. A statistical alarm plus a named cause is a much stronger case than either alone (see change-point detection and regime switching).

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where decay calls go wrong

Judging by the streak. The streak is the most vivid part of the record and the least informative. Use the whole record.

Testing against zero instead of the tested edge. "Still profitable" can hide a fall from 60% to 52%. Compare with \(p_0\), and with break-even.

Looking every week with a fixed 5% test. Each look is another chance of a false alarm; after a year of weekly looks a healthy system will very likely "fail" once. Use a CUSUM or a threshold set for repeated looking.

Expecting a quick answer. A 55%-to-50% decay takes hundreds of trades to show. Plan for that rather than deciding on six weeks.

Ignoring clustering. Real losses bunch in bad regimes, so streaks run longer and records wander further than the independent-trade formulas say. Treat the numbers as the optimistic case.

Re-fitting on the loss. Changing parameters until the live stretch looks good is in-sample optimisation on the worst sample you have. It resets nothing and guarantees the next decay.

Scenario drill · the week of 6 August 2007

Your quant strategy has its worst few days ever, and the market barely moved

You run a quantitative long/short equity strategy with a long tested record. In the space of a few days this week it has suffered sudden, severe losses, far outside anything in its history. The broad stock market has hardly moved. Other quant funds, running different models, are reporting the same thing. What do you do?

  1. The edge is dead. Liquidate everything today.
    A few days of losses is the most vivid part of the record and the least informative about the edge. When many funds hold the same positions, selling into the unwind means selling near the worst prices.
  2. Cut to a size you can survive under a pre-set rule, change nothing in the model, and judge decay separately on the long record.
    This keeps the two questions apart. The sudden loss looks like a crowding shock, which calls for survival, not a verdict. Whether the edge itself has been eroding is a slower question that only the long record, tested against the tested edge, can answer.
  3. Hold full size. It is only noise and it will come back.
    It might come back, but holding full size through a crowded unwind assumes you will survive the path, and nothing guarantees it. It also skips the slower question of whether the edge has been shrinking.
  4. Re-fit the model on this week's data so it stops losing.
    That is in-sample optimisation on the worst sample you have. It fits the shock, not the edge, and resets nothing.

What happened. In the second week of August 2007, many quant long/short equity funds took sudden, severe losses in the same few days while the broad market barely moved. Much of the loss reversed shortly afterwards, but some funds had already cut positions near the worst point. Khandani and Lo later explained the losses as a crowded unwind: many funds held similar positions, and the rapid selling of one or more large portfolios pushed prices against all of them. They also found a slower decay: their stand-in strategy's returns had been falling for years as more capital chased similar trades. The week was a shock, not the decay; the decay was the quiet trend underneath.

Key Takeaways

✓Streaks are not verdicts. At 55%, a run of 7 appears in 180 trades about 31% of the time, edge or no edge.
✓Test the whole record against the tested edge: \(z = (W - np_0)/\sqrt{np_0q_0}\), with LR to say how far it should move your odds.
✓Know the trades needed in advance. 55% → 50% needs about 615; 63% → 50% about 88. Small decays are slow to see.
✓CUSUM watches every trade with a known false-alarm rate. Choose \(h\) from the trade-off table, not from how you feel.
✓Grade the response. Amber halves size and looks for a cause; red stops and sends the idea back to an out-of-sample re-test.

Section 09

Self-test

Q1. A system tested at 60% wins has won 105 of its first 200 live trades. What is z, and which band is it in?
Expected \(200 \times 0.6 = 120\); standard deviation \(\sqrt{200 \times 0.6 \times 0.4} = 6.93\). \(z = (105 - 120)/6.93 = -2.17\). Amber (between −1.28 and −2.33): half size and review, close to red.
Q2. Why is "eight losses in a row" weaker evidence of decay than "won 48% of 400 trades against a tested 55%"?
A long streak is common in long records whether or not there is an edge (at 55%, a run of 8 is about 24% likely in 300 trades). The 400-trade record uses every trade: expected 220, standard deviation 9.95, observed 192, so \(z = -2.81\) — beyond the red line.
Q3. About how many trades do you need to tell a 55% edge from 50% at a 5% false-alarm rate with 80% power? What does that imply for a strategy that trades 100 times a year?
About 615. At 100 trades a year that is six years. The live record cannot rescue a bad research decision on any useful timescale, so the out-of-sample test before launch carries most of the weight.
Q4. In a CUSUM for 55% against 50%, what does one win and one loss do to the score, and why is the sum floored at zero?
A win adds \(\ln(0.50/0.55) = -0.095\); a loss adds \(\ln(0.50/0.45) = +0.105\). The floor stops a long good stretch from banking so much credit that a later decay takes forever to show. The CUSUM measures evidence of a fall since the record last looked healthy.
Q5. You lower the CUSUM threshold from 4 to 2 to catch decay faster. What do you pay?
False alarms. For 55% against 50%, the chance a healthy system trips the alarm within 300 trades rises from about 0.5% to about 21%; median time to catch a real decay falls from about 475 to about 194 trades. Faster and quieter cannot both improve.
Q6. A strategy's backtest won 80% of trades; live it has won 42% from the very first trade. Decay?
Almost certainly not. A gap that large from day one points at the backtest: look-ahead, survivorship, fills or stops that could not happen live. Replay the live period through the backtest and audit what it could see at decision time before using any decay test — a decay test assumes the tested number was real.

Further reading
  • E. S. Page, "Continuous Inspection Schemes", Biometrika, 1954 — the original CUSUM.
  • Abraham Wald, Sequential Analysis, Wiley, 1947 — the sequential probability ratio test the CUSUM is built on.
  • Thomas K. Philips, Emmanuel Yashchin and David M. Stein, "Using Statistical Process Control to Monitor Active Managers", Journal of Portfolio Management, 2003 — CUSUM applied to manager performance.
  • Amir E. Khandani and Andrew W. Lo, "What Happened to the Quants in August 2007?", 2007 (later in the Journal of Investment Management and the Journal of Financial Markets) — crowding, unwinds and the slow decline of a contrarian strategy's returns.
  • R. David McLean and Jeffrey Pontiff, "Does Academic Research Destroy Stock Return Predictability?", Journal of Finance, 2016 — how much published effects shrink out of sample and after publication.
  • Michèle Basseville and Igor V. Nikiforov, Detection of Abrupt Changes: Theory and Application, Prentice Hall, 1993 — the standard reference on CUSUM and change detection.
  • Drawdowns and Risk of Ruin, Change-Point Detection and Base Rates First — the streak arithmetic, the general theory, and how to weigh a live record against a prior.
