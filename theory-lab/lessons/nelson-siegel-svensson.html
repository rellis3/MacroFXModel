Curve Fitting: Nelson-Siegel & Svensson

Bootstrapping gives you a curve that fits your bonds exactly and kinks unpredictably in between them. Nelson-Siegel and Svensson trade a sliver of that exact fit for a lot of stability — four (or six) numbers that describe an entire yield curve's level, slope and hump at once, and that central banks publish every single trading day.

CONCEPT — not built or tested in this repo ~16 min read Prereqs: Bootstrapping the Yield Curve
TL;DR
  • Pure bootstrapping connects zero rates point-to-point through the exact bonds you feed it — between sparse or noisy inputs the resulting curve can kink, wiggle, or become unstable to extrapolate, especially where few liquid bonds exist at a given maturity.
  • Nelson-Siegel (1987) replaces that piecewise construction with one smooth function of four parameters — \(\beta_0\) (long-run level), \(\beta_1\) (slope), \(\beta_2\) (curvature/hump), \(\lambda\) (decay rate) — describing an entire curve's shape at once.
  • Each parameter has a clean economic reading: \(\beta_0\) is where the curve settles at very long maturities, \(\beta_0+\beta_1\) is the very-short-end rate (so \(\beta_1\)'s sign sets whether the curve tilts up or down), \(\beta_2\) controls a single hump or trough in the belly, and \(\lambda\) controls where along the maturity axis that hump sits.
  • Svensson (1994) adds a second curvature term with its own decay parameter \(\lambda_2\), giving the curve two independent humps — the extra flexibility central banks like the ECB and the Fed actually use in their own published curves.
  • Fitting either model to observed bond yields is a nonlinear least-squares problem with no closed-form solution — solved iteratively, and prone to different local optima depending on the starting guess, especially for \(\lambda\).
  • The worked example plugs \(\beta_0=4.5\%,\ \beta_1=-2.0\%,\ \beta_2=1.0\%,\ \lambda=2.0\) into the formula by hand at 1Y, 2Y, 5Y and 10Y, producing 3.11%, 3.50%, 4.05% and 4.29% — a smooth, upward-sloping, gently humped curve generated entirely from four numbers.
Section 01

Why this matters

Bootstrapping a yield curve — stripping zero-coupon rates out of a ladder of coupon-bearing bond prices, one maturity at a time — is exact by construction: feed it real bond prices and it hands back a curve that reprices every one of those bonds perfectly. But "exact" and "well-behaved" are different properties. Bootstrapping only knows about the specific maturities it was fed. Between two widely spaced input bonds, or across a patch of the curve where the available bonds are thin, illiquid, or slightly noisy, the piecewise result can kink sharply, overshoot, or even imply an economically strange forward rate for a stretch of maturities nobody actually traded. A single stale or off-the-run quote can visibly distort the curve around it, and extrapolating a bootstrapped curve beyond its longest input bond is close to guesswork.

Parametric curve fitting takes a completely different approach: instead of connecting the dots exactly, pick a smooth mathematical shape — one flexible enough to describe the handful of ways a real yield curve actually looks (rising, falling, humped, inverted) — and find the small number of parameters that make that shape fit the observed bond prices as closely as possible, without being forced through every single one of them exactly. Nelson-Siegel is the classic version of that idea: a four-parameter curve that is smooth everywhere by construction, stable to extrapolate, and immune to a single noisy input bond distorting the whole shape. Svensson is the natural one-term extension once four parameters aren't flexible enough.

💡
Plain-English intuition

A curve shape with only four knobs

Nearly every real yield curve, on nearly every day, looks like one of a small number of shapes: roughly flat, tilted up (short rates below long rates — the normal case), tilted down (an inversion, often read as a recession signal), or one of those two shapes with a bump or a dip somewhere in the middle. Nelson-Siegel's insight is that you don't need to specify every single point of that curve independently — you can describe the whole family of shapes with just four dials: how high does the curve eventually settle (level), how much lower or higher does the short end sit relative to that (slope), is there a bump or a dip in the belly, and how big (curvature), and roughly where along the maturity axis does that bump sit (decay rate).

Turning those four dials produces a smooth curve every time — there's no way to accidentally create a jagged kink, because the underlying function is smooth by construction. The cost is that a four-dial shape can't perfectly reproduce every last basis point of every bond's quoted yield — it wasn't built to. It was built to capture the shape a real curve actually takes, cheaply, robustly, and with very few numbers to store, transmit, or forecast.

Bootstrapping — exact, but locally fragile

Reprices every input bond exactly. Between inputs, the curve is only as well-behaved as the specific bonds and interpolation rule used to connect them — sparse or noisy inputs can produce visible kinks, and extrapolating past the last input maturity has no principled anchor.

Parametric (Nelson-Siegel / Svensson) — smooth, but approximate

A handful of parameters describe the entire curve's shape at once. Smooth and stable everywhere, including extrapolation, and immune to any single noisy quote distorting the whole curve — at the cost of not exactly repricing every input bond.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Bootstrapping is like connecting dots with a ruler — perfectly accurate at each dot, but the line between two far-apart dots can zig and zag oddly. Nelson-Siegel instead draws one smooth curve that generally passes close to all the dots without insisting on hitting every single one exactly — like sketching a smooth hill instead of a jagged connect-the-dots picture.

Section 02

The math, step by step

Nelson & Siegel (1987) proposed modeling the instantaneous forward rate curve as the solution to a particular second-order differential equation with equal real roots, which — after integrating to get the corresponding yield (spot rate) at each maturity — produces one remarkably compact closed-form expression for the yield \(y(\tau)\) at maturity \(\tau\):

\[ y(\tau) = \beta_0 + \beta_1\left(\frac{1-e^{-\tau/\lambda}}{\tau/\lambda}\right) + \beta_2\left(\frac{1-e^{-\tau/\lambda}}{\tau/\lambda} - e^{-\tau/\lambda}\right) \]
\(\beta_0\)
Level
The long-run yield the curve settles toward as maturity grows
\(\beta_1\)
Slope
Short end minus long-run level — its sign flips the curve's tilt
\(\beta_2\)
Curvature
The size (and sign) of a single hump or dip in the belly
\(\lambda\)
Decay rate
Controls where along the maturity axis the hump sits

It helps to name the recurring building block once. Let \(x=\tau/\lambda\) and define the decay function \(e^{-x}\) and the slope loading \(L(\tau)=\dfrac{1-e^{-x}}{x}\). The formula above is then just \(y(\tau)=\beta_0+\beta_1 L(\tau)+\beta_2\big(L(\tau)-e^{-x}\big)\) — three additive pieces, each multiplying a fixed "loading" function of maturity by a parameter that says how much of that shape to include.

x = τ/λmaturity rescaled by the decay parameter — unitless
e⁻ˣdecays from 1 (at τ=0) toward 0 as τ grows — the "how much short-term behavior is left" weight
L(τ)the slope loading — starts at 1, decays smoothly to 0
L(τ) − e⁻ˣthe curvature loading — zero at both ends, positive (or negative) in between
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

The whole formula is just three ingredients stacked together: a baseline number that never changes with maturity, a piece that matters a lot for short maturities and fades away for long ones, and a piece that's zero at both ends but bulges up or down somewhere in the middle. Mixing different amounts of those three ingredients is literally all it takes to draw almost any realistic yield curve shape.

What each parameter does, and why — the two limiting cases

The cleanest way to see what \(\beta_0\), \(\beta_1\) and \(\beta_2\) actually mean is to evaluate the formula at the two extremes of maturity, \(\tau\to 0\) and \(\tau\to\infty\), where the loadings collapse to simple numbers.

1

As \(\tau\to 0\): the instantaneous short rate

For small \(x\), a first-order expansion of the exponential, \(e^{-x}\approx 1-x\), gives \(1-e^{-x}\approx x\), so \(L(\tau)=\dfrac{1-e^{-x}}{x}\to 1\). Meanwhile \(e^{-x}\to 1\) too, so the curvature loading \(L(\tau)-e^{-x}\to 1-1=0\). The formula collapses to:

\[ y(0) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \]

The instantaneous short-end rate is \(\beta_0+\beta_1\) — this is exactly why \(\beta_1\) is called the slope: if \(\beta_1<0\), the short end sits below the long-run level \(\beta_0\) (a normal, upward-sloping curve); if \(\beta_1>0\), the short end sits above it (an inverted curve).

2

As \(\tau\to\infty\): the long-run level

As \(x\to\infty\), \(e^{-x}\to 0\), so \(L(\tau)=\dfrac{1-e^{-x}}{x}\to 0\) as well (the numerator approaches 1 while the denominator grows without bound), and the curvature loading \(L(\tau)-e^{-x}\to 0-0=0\). Both the slope and curvature terms vanish entirely, leaving:

\[ y(\infty) = \beta_0 \]

\(\beta_0\) is exactly the yield the curve converges to at very long maturities — its long-run level. Note this also means \(\beta_0\) alone should be economically sensible on its own (a plausible long-run rate) — a fit that produces an implausible \(\beta_0\) is a sign something's gone wrong with the fit, not a quirk to shrug off.

3

In between: the hump, and where it sits

The curvature loading \(L(\tau)-e^{-x}\) is exactly zero at both \(\tau=0\) and \(\tau=\infty\) (shown above) but nonzero in between — it rises from 0, peaks somewhere in the middle of the maturity range, then decays back to 0. That single hump-shaped loading, scaled by \(\beta_2\), is what lets the curve bow away from a straight line connecting its short and long ends: a positive \(\beta_2\) adds a hump (the belly sits above where a straight short-to-long line would put it); a negative \(\beta_2\) creates a dip. \(\lambda\) sets the maturity scale of the whole curve — it rescales exactly where, along the maturity axis, that hump's peak sits (a smaller \(\lambda\) pulls the hump toward shorter maturities; a larger \(\lambda\) pushes it toward longer ones), and it does so for the slope loading \(L(\tau)\) too, controlling how quickly the short-end behavior decays away as maturity grows.

Short end minus long-run level, \(y(0)-\beta_0\). Negative ⇒ normal upward-sloping curve; positive ⇒ inverted.

Size and direction of a single hump (positive) or dip (negative) in the belly of the curve.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Think of the four numbers as four dials on a machine that draws yield curves. One dial sets how high the curve eventually flattens out far in the future. One dial sets whether short-term rates start above or below that flat level. One dial adds a bump or a dip somewhere in the middle. The last dial slides that bump left or right along the timeline. Four dials, one smooth curve every time — no way to accidentally draw a jagged line.

The trap Svensson exists to fix: the curvature term in plain Nelson-Siegel is mathematically capable of producing exactly one hump (or one dip) — no more. A genuinely two-humped real curve (for instance, one segment priced for an expected near-term hiking cycle followed by a segment priced for expected cuts further out) cannot be fit well by four Nelson-Siegel parameters no matter how they're tuned.

The Svensson extension — a second, independent hump

Svensson (1994) adds exactly one more curvature term, structurally identical to Nelson-Siegel's, but built on its own separate decay parameter \(\lambda_2\):

\[ y(\tau) = \beta_0 + \beta_1\left(\frac{1-e^{-\tau/\lambda_1}}{\tau/\lambda_1}\right) + \beta_2\left(\frac{1-e^{-\tau/\lambda_1}}{\tau/\lambda_1} - e^{-\tau/\lambda_1}\right) + \beta_3\left(\frac{1-e^{-\tau/\lambda_2}}{\tau/\lambda_2} - e^{-\tau/\lambda_2}\right) \]
β₃the size (and sign) of a second, independent hump
λ₂a second decay parameter — sets where the second hump sits, separate from λ₁

(The original Nelson-Siegel's single decay parameter is relabeled \(\lambda_1\) here purely to distinguish it from Svensson's \(\lambda_2\) — some sources keep the original notation \(\lambda\) for the first term and simply add \(\beta_3,\lambda_2\).) Because the two curvature terms are structurally identical up to which decay parameter they use, Svensson is best read as "Nelson-Siegel, twice, sharing one level and one slope" — six parameters in total (\(\beta_0,\beta_1,\beta_2,\beta_3,\lambda_1,\lambda_2\)) instead of four, buying a second independent hump at a plausibly different maturity than the first. This is the extra flexibility central banks like the ECB and the U.S. Federal Reserve actually use in the curves they publish daily.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Plain Nelson-Siegel can only draw a curve with one bump. Svensson just bolts on a second, independent bump-making dial, so the curve can have two separate bulges at two different points along the timeline instead of being stuck with just one.

Fitting the curve: nonlinear least squares

Neither Nelson-Siegel nor Svensson can be fit with ordinary linear regression, because \(\lambda\) (and \(\lambda_2\)) appear inside the exponential and division — the model is linear in \(\beta_0,\beta_1,\beta_2,(\beta_3)\) for a fixed \(\lambda\), but nonlinear once \(\lambda\) itself is also being estimated from the data. In practice, \(\lambda\) is solved for jointly with the betas by minimizing the sum of squared differences between the model's fitted yields (or, more precisely in most implementations, fitted bond prices) and the actually observed ones:

\[ \min_{\beta_0,\beta_1,\beta_2,\lambda} \ \sum_{i=1}^{n} \Big(y_i^{\text{obs}} - y(\tau_i;\ \beta_0,\beta_1,\beta_2,\lambda)\Big)^2 \]
yᵢᵒᵇˢthe i-th observed bond/instrument yield
nhow many observed yields/bonds are being fit at once

This is exactly the nonlinear least-squares optimization problem covered in this curriculum's Numerical Methods material — there is no closed-form solution, so it is solved iteratively (Gauss-Newton, Levenberg-Marquardt, or a general-purpose nonlinear optimizer), starting from an initial guess and refining it step by step until the sum of squared errors stops improving meaningfully. Because the objective is genuinely nonlinear in \(\lambda\) (and doubly so in Svensson's \(\lambda_1,\lambda_2\) pair), the optimizer can converge to different local optima depending on where it starts — a real practical wrinkle covered in the pitfalls section below.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Fitting the curve means trying different dial settings until the drawn curve lands as close as possible to all the real, observed bond yields at once — like adjusting a car radio's tuning knob until the static clears up the most, except here a computer tries thousands of settings automatically instead of you turning the knob by hand.

Worked example

Plugging in a parameter set by hand

Take \(\beta_0=4.5\%\), \(\beta_1=-2.0\%\), \(\beta_2=1.0\%\), \(\lambda=2.0\) — the same parameters the chart above starts on. Compute the yield at \(\tau=1,2,5,10\) years.

1

τ = 1 year

\(x=\tau/\lambda=1/2=0.5\), \(e^{-0.5}\approx 0.6065\).

\[ L(1) = \frac{1-0.6065}{0.5} = \frac{0.3935}{0.5} = 0.7870 \]
\[ y(1) = 4.5 + (-2.0)(0.7870) + 1.0\big(0.7870-0.6065\big) = 4.5 - 1.574 + 0.1805 = 3.1065\% \]
2

τ = 2 years

\(x=2/2=1.0\), \(e^{-1.0}\approx 0.3679\).

\[ L(2) = \frac{1-0.3679}{1.0} = 0.6321 \]
\[ y(2) = 4.5 - 2.0(0.6321) + 1.0(0.6321-0.3679) = 4.5 - 1.2642 + 0.2642 = 3.5000\% \]
3

τ = 5 years

\(x=5/2=2.5\), \(e^{-2.5}\approx 0.0821\).

\[ L(5) = \frac{1-0.0821}{2.5} = 0.3672 \]
\[ y(5) = 4.5 - 2.0(0.3672) + 1.0(0.3672-0.0821) = 4.5 - 0.7344 + 0.2851 = 4.0507\% \]
4

τ = 10 years

\(x=10/2=5.0\), \(e^{-5.0}\approx 0.0067\).

\[ L(10) = \frac{1-0.0067}{5.0} = 0.1987 \]
\[ y(10) = 4.5 - 2.0(0.1987) + 1.0(0.1987-0.0067) = 4.5 - 0.3973 + 0.1920 = 4.2946\% \]

Notice the curve is already closing in on \(\beta_0=4.5\%\) by 10 years, and would sit at 4.4333% at 30 years — consistent with the \(\tau\to\infty\) limit derived above.

🎯
Real-world trading scenario

Why the ECB and the Fed both publish a Svensson-family curve, not a bootstrap

The European Central Bank publishes a daily euro area AAA-rated government bond yield curve fitted with a Svensson-family parametric model, built from a set of underlying bonds that is often sparse at some maturities and changes composition over time as bonds mature and new ones are issued. A pure bootstrap of that same bond set, on a day where (say) the 7-year and 9-year segments are thinly represented, could easily produce a visible kink or an implausible forward rate right where liquidity is weakest — a genuine distortion, not a real market view. Refitting a smooth four-or-six-parameter curve to the same data instead produces a curve that changes shape gradually and predictably as the input bonds change day to day, and that extrapolates sensibly beyond the longest available bond.

In the U.S. Treasury market, Federal Reserve staff economists Refet Gürkaynak, Brian Sack and Jonathan Wright built (and the Fed still updates) a widely used daily Treasury yield curve dataset fitted with exactly this Svensson-style extended Nelson-Siegel form — used throughout academic and practitioner research as the standard reference "smooth" Treasury curve, precisely because it is far more stable, and far easier to interpolate or extrapolate from, than stitching together whichever specific on-the-run and off-the-run bonds happened to be liquid on a given day.

A rates desk pricing a bond or swap at an odd maturity — say 17 years, between the market's usual round 15-year and 20-year points — reads that rate straight off the fitted parametric curve rather than re-running a bootstrap, precisely because the parametric curve was built to behave sensibly everywhere, not just exactly at the maturities of the bonds that happened to be used to fit it.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

Daily curve publication. Central banks and Treasury departments that publish an official smooth reference curve overwhelmingly use Nelson-Siegel or, more often, the Svensson extension — refit fresh each trading day from that day's observed bond prices via the nonlinear least-squares procedure above.

Interpolation and extrapolation. A rates desk that needs a discount rate or forward rate at a maturity nobody happens to be quoting reads it straight off the fitted curve, rather than inventing an ad-hoc interpolation rule between two bootstrapped points — the parametric curve is smooth and economically sensible by construction at every maturity, including beyond the longest bond used to fit it.

A compact risk-factor decomposition. Because \(\beta_0,\beta_1,\beta_2\) map directly onto "level, slope, curvature," risk managers use the three fitted numbers themselves as a compact summary of how the whole curve moved — level risk, slope (steepener/ flattener) risk, and curvature (butterfly) risk — for hedging and scenario analysis, without tracking every individual maturity point separately. It's a genuinely useful coincidence (not proven here, but well documented in the term-structure literature) that empirical principal component analysis of historical yield curve changes — covered elsewhere in this curriculum's Principal Component Analysis lesson — tends to find that the same three shapes, level, slope and curvature, are in fact the first three empirical factors driving real yield curve movements.

Forecasting the curve, not just describing it. Diebold and Li (2006) turned the static Nelson-Siegel fit into a dynamic forecasting model: treat the fitted \(\beta_0(t),\beta_1(t),\beta_2(t)\) as three separate time series (holding \(\lambda\) fixed across time), forecast each with a simple univariate time-series model, then reassemble a forecast yield curve for a future date from the three forecast betas — a widely cited applied technique for the specific problem of forecasting an entire curve's shape rather than one point on it at a time.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where curve fitting goes wrong in practice

Chasing every wiggle with more parameters. Adding Svensson's second hump term (or further ad-hoc terms beyond it) to force the fit closer to every observed bond defeats the entire purpose of a parametric smoothing method — a six-parameter fit to eight bonds is barely different from an unconstrained interpolation, and loses the stability that was the whole point of fitting a smooth shape in the first place.

\(\lambda\) (and \(\lambda_2\)) estimation instability. The optimization is genuinely nonlinear in the decay parameters, and the least-squares surface can have multiple local minima or long flat ridges — different reasonable starting guesses can converge to visibly different \(\lambda\) values with similar fit quality. This gets worse in Svensson when \(\lambda_1\) and \(\lambda_2\) end up close to each other: the two curvature terms become nearly collinear (nearly the same shape, just relabeled between \(\beta_2\) and \(\beta_3\)), and the fit can no longer tell the two humps apart reliably. Practitioners commonly bound or separate the two decay parameters, or grid-search several starting values and keep the best-converged fit, rather than trusting a single optimizer run from one arbitrary starting point.

Mistaking the fitting residual for a trading opportunity. Unlike a bootstrap, a Nelson-Siegel or Svensson fit generally does not reprice its own input bonds exactly — a small residual between the fitted curve and any individual input bond's actual yield is normal and expected. Treating that residual as a "mispriced bond" to trade against the fitted curve is trading fitting noise, not a real market inefficiency; the curve was never built to match that bond exactly in the first place.

Forcing a genuinely two-humped curve into plain Nelson-Siegel. As shown above, the classic four-parameter form is mathematically capable of exactly one hump. If the real curve has two distinct bumps (for instance, priced-in policy expectations for a hike cycle followed by cuts further out), plain Nelson-Siegel will visibly under-fit no matter how its four parameters are tuned — that is specifically what motivates reaching for Svensson, not adding more Nelson-Siegel-style terms of the same shape.

Key Takeaways

✓Parametric fitting trades a little exact-fit for a lot of stability. Four numbers replace a piecewise construction that can kink between sparse or noisy inputs.
✓Each parameter has a clean economic reading. \(\beta_0\) is the long-run level (\(\tau\to\infty\)), \(\beta_0+\beta_1\) is the short-end rate (\(\tau\to 0\)), \(\beta_2\) sizes a single hump/dip, and \(\lambda\) places it along the maturity axis.
✓Svensson's \(\beta_3,\lambda_2\) term buys a second, independent hump — the flexibility central banks like the ECB and the Fed actually use in their own published curves.
✓Fitting is nonlinear least squares, not a closed-form solve. \(\lambda\) sits inside the exponential, so it's estimated jointly with the betas by iterative optimization.
⚠\(\lambda\) (and \(\lambda_2\)) are the hard part. The optimization surface is prone to local minima and near-collinearity between the two decay parameters in Svensson — a single optimizer run from one starting guess isn't enough to trust.
⚠The fitted curve is a smoothed approximation, not an exact reprice. Small residuals against the input bonds are normal — don't mistake fitting noise for a trading edge.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. Why can a pure bootstrap produce a visibly kinked curve, and how does Nelson-Siegel avoid that by construction?
A bootstrap only knows about the exact bonds it's given — between two widely spaced or thinly traded input maturities, the piecewise construction can kink, overshoot, or imply an odd forward rate, because nothing constrains the shape in between beyond whatever interpolation rule is used. Nelson-Siegel instead evaluates one smooth, continuous function of \(\tau\) everywhere — since \(y(\tau)\) is built from smooth exponential terms with no piecewise switching, there is no mechanism by which the fitted curve could develop a kink, no matter how sparse or noisy the underlying bonds used to fit it are.
Q2. What does the sign of \(\beta_1\) tell you about the curve's slope, and why?
Evaluating the formula at \(\tau\to 0\) gives \(y(0)=\beta_0+\beta_1\) — the instantaneous short rate. Since \(\beta_0\) alone is the long-run level (\(\tau\to\infty\)), a negative \(\beta_1\) means the short end sits below the long-run level: a normal, upward-sloping curve. A positive \(\beta_1\) means the short end sits above the long-run level: an inverted curve. The sign of \(\beta_1\) is therefore a direct, literal readout of the curve's overall tilt.
Q3. What new flexibility does Svensson's \(\beta_3,\lambda_2\) term add over plain Nelson-Siegel, and what real curve feature motivates adding it?
Plain Nelson-Siegel's curvature term is mathematically capable of producing exactly one hump or dip. Svensson adds a second curvature term, structurally identical but built on its own decay parameter \(\lambda_2\), which lets the fitted curve have two independent humps at two different maturities. This matters when the real curve genuinely has two distinct bumps — for example, market pricing that reflects an expected near-term hiking cycle followed by an expected cutting cycle further out — a shape plain Nelson-Siegel cannot represent well no matter how its four parameters are tuned.
Q4. Why is fitting Nelson-Siegel or Svensson to observed bond yields a nonlinear least-squares problem rather than something solved with ordinary linear regression?
For a fixed, known value of \(\lambda\), the model is linear in \(\beta_0,\beta_1,\beta_2\) (and \(\beta_3\) in Svensson) — it's just a weighted sum of fixed loading functions. But \(\lambda\) itself is also unknown and needs to be estimated from the data, and it appears inside an exponential and a division, which ordinary linear regression cannot handle. The full problem — estimating the betas and \(\lambda\) jointly — is therefore nonlinear, has no closed-form solution, and must be solved with an iterative numerical optimizer.
Q5. A fitted Svensson curve doesn't exactly reprice the bonds used to fit it — is that a red flag, or evidence of a mispriced bond to trade against the curve?
Neither, generally. Unlike a bootstrap, a parametric fit was never built to reprice every input bond exactly — it was built to capture the curve's overall shape smoothly and stably using only a handful of parameters, which is a fundamentally different goal from an exact interpolation. A small residual between the fitted curve and any one bond's actual yield is the normal, expected cost of that trade-off. Treating that residual as a real arbitrage opportunity is trading fitting noise, not a genuine market inefficiency, unless it's confirmed through independent means (e.g. a genuinely stale quote, a real liquidity premium, or a documented persistent basis) rather than just reading it off the fit's own residual.

  • Refet S. Gürkaynak, Brian Sack, and Jonathan H. Wright, "The U.S. Treasury Yield Curve: 1961 to the Present," Federal Reserve Board Finance and Economics Discussion Series, 2007 — documents the Fed's own Svensson-family fitted Treasury curve dataset referenced in the scenario above.
