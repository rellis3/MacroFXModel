White's Reality Check & Hansen's SPA Test, Bootstrapping the Whole Candidate Set

The Deflated Sharpe Ratio corrects one reported Sharpe using an estimate of how many trials produced it. These two tests skip the estimate — they keep every candidate strategy's full return history and bootstrap the entire panel directly, resampling all of them together and asking how often pure luck alone would produce a "best" result this good.

Section 1

Same question, a completely different tool

Both fixes target the exact same danger: the best of many tries looks good even with zero real edge anywhere.

📐

Deflated Sharpe Ratio

Compresses each trial to one Sharpe ratio, then plugs an estimated trial count N and dispersion σSR into an extreme-value-theory formula for what the best-of-N Sharpe should look like by luck.

🎲

White's Reality Check / Hansen's SPA

Keeps every candidate's full period-by-period return history and directly bootstraps the entire panel — no Gaussian-maximum formula, no separate dispersion estimate. The real data supplies its own distribution.

Same disease, two honest treatments. This deck covers the bootstrap-the-whole-panel branch — reusing the block bootstrap this curriculum's own Bootstrapping & Monte Carlo lesson already built, now pointed at a maximum across many candidates instead of one series alone.
Section 2

The setup: many candidates, one shared clock

N candidate strategies, T shared time periods, one fixed benchmark.

The statistic
dk,t = Rk,t − Rtbench
→
V̄ = maxk mean(dk)
Every candidate's excess return over the SAME benchmark, on the SAME periods. The test statistic is simply whichever candidate's own average excess return is currently the largest.
Why not a textbook critical value?

Two problems stack up: real returns are usually autocorrelated (breaking the usual √T standard error even for one series), and V̄ is a maximum across N candidates that are often cross-correlated too — variations on a theme share the same underlying bets. There's no simple formula for "the max of many dependent things." So instead of a formula, bootstrap it.

Section 3

One shared resample, applied to every candidate at once

The key trick: draw ONE random block-index path, and reuse it across all N candidates in the same replicate.

Visualize it
Compute each candidate's mean on this SAME resample, take the max across candidates — that's one bootstrap replicate of the WRC/SPA statistic. Repeat thousands of times.
Section 4

WRC's weak spot: junk candidates widen the null

Demeaning EVERY candidate at zero gives even the obviously-bad ones an equal shot at "winning" the replay.

What WRC does
  • Every one of the N candidates gets recentered at exactly zero before resampling
  • A clearly-dominated candidate still competes for "largest resampled mean" in every replicate
  • More junk candidates in the panel → wider bootstrap null → higher bar for the real winner to clear
  • Correct, but conservative — real statistical power is left on the table
What this costs in practice
  • A real parameter sweep is rarely N equally-plausible candidates
  • Most sweeps are one or two promising ideas plus a long tail of obviously weak variants
  • That long tail is exactly what inflates WRC's null the most
  • Hansen's fix targets this directly — next
Section 5

Hansen's fix: studentize, and bench the clear losers

📏

Studentize before the max

Divide each candidate's mean by its own bootstrap-estimated spread before comparing — puts a jumpy, high-vol candidate and a smooth, low-vol one on equal footing, the same idea a Sharpe ratio applies to a single strategy.

🚫

Don't recenter a clear loser at zero

A candidate whose own studentized statistic is convincingly negative keeps something closer to its own real (bad) performance in the bootstrap world — denying it a chance to drift up and pretend it could have tied for best.

Judging a footrace where three runners visibly tripped: pretending, in an imagined rerun, that they "might have won" is silly once you've already seen them fall. SPA stops giving obviously-last-place candidates that benefit of the doubt, and puts every remaining runner's time in context of how consistent that runner normally is.
Section 6

WRC versus SPA, side by side

Same bootstrap engine, same null hypothesis — SPA is the field's preferred modern successor, not a rival idea.

Compare
WRC: V̄ = maxk mean(dk) — raw sample means, unadjusted for each candidate's own volatility.
SPA: Z = maxk mean(dk)/SEk — studentized, so a low-vol candidate's edge is judged relative to its own normal wobble, not on raw scale.
WRC: every candidate demeaned to exactly zero before resampling — full benefit of the doubt for everyone, junk included.
SPA: only candidates that weren't clearly inferior get recentered at zero; a convincingly-bad candidate keeps its own real (negative) mean instead.
WRC: correct, but its null widens — and its power falls — as more obviously-weak candidates are added to the panel.
SPA: recovers that lost power on the exact same data, since junk candidates can no longer inflate the null — generally the preferred choice today.
Section 7

A toy panel: one real edge, one deceptive Sharpe

Five candidates, 16 periods each, benchmark = 0. Watch what raw Sharpe alone would have you believe.

A

Momentum — the real edge

mean 0.469, Sharpe 1.19, t = 4.75 — clearly the biggest mean in the panel.

C

Carry — the trap

mean 0.156 (a third of Momentum's), yet Sharpe 0.93 — second-highest in the panel, purely because its realized variance happened to come out tiny.

B·D·E

The rest — plain noise

means near zero or negative, Sharpes near zero or negative — no story here, exactly what an edge-free candidate should look like.

The reveal

Bootstrapping this exact panel: Momentum's mean survives comfortably (p ≈ 0.003 under WRC, p ≈ 0.0002 once studentized under SPA). Carry's own mean, tested the identical way, comes back p ≈ 0.24 — nowhere near significant. An eye-catching Sharpe and a statistically real edge are not the same claim.

Section 10

The test is only as honest as the candidate set

Quietly curated candidate set
  • Run 50 variations, keep the 5 that looked best, discard the other 45
  • Feed WRC/SPA only those 5
  • Test computes a confident-looking p-value
  • Completely uninformative — it answered "best of 5?" not "best of 50?"
The honest candidate set
  • Every variation genuinely tried goes into the panel — junk included
  • The bootstrap null correctly reflects the real scale of the search
  • SPA's inferior-candidate handling keeps the honest panel from losing power
  • The p-value now actually means what it claims to mean
No formula can detect a cherry-picked candidate set from the outside — it will happily return a small, confident p-value on the wrong question. This is a discipline problem, identical in spirit to the Deflated Sharpe lesson's warning about undercounting N.
Section 11

What this repo actually has — and doesn't

Verified against this repo's actual code

This repo's statsCore.js ships a real, working stationary (Politis–Romano) block bootstrap, reused across backtestStats.js, scripts/freeze_expectation.mjs, js/volReversionCore.js, and analysis/cot_factor/factor_test.py. Every single one of those points the bootstrap at ONE series or statistic at a time. The one place this repo corrects for testing several things at once (volReversionCore.js's best-of-4-horizons check) uses a parametric Bonferroni adjustment, not a bootstrap of a max statistic. And this repo's own honest self-assessment names the sharper version of this exact problem — "best-of-N ranked on the same data... best of ~600 random configs per pair" — as its single biggest unaddressed risk, proposing purged cross-validation plus a deflated-Sharpe adjustment as the fix. No genuine WRC/SPA-style bootstrap of a full candidate panel exists anywhere in this codebase — the building block (the bootstrap itself) is already here; assembling it into this specific test has not been done.

Section 12

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

What is White's Reality Check's test statistic?

V̄ = max_k mean(d_k) — the best candidate's own sample mean excess return, taken across every candidate actually run.
Question 2

Why can't a normal-distribution critical value judge this statistic directly?

Two separate problems: serial correlation breaks a single series' own standard error, and taking a MAX across cross-correlated candidates has no simple closed-form distribution. Bootstrap the real data instead of assuming a shape.
Question 3

What specifically does Hansen's SPA test fix about White's Reality Check?

WRC demeans every candidate at zero, letting obviously-bad ones inflate the null. SPA studentizes by each candidate's own spread and denies a convincingly-inferior candidate the "benefit of the doubt" recentering — recovering statistical power on the same data.
Question 4

In the toy panel, why did Carry's impressive-looking Sharpe (0.93) fail a bootstrap test of its own mean?

Carry's mean (0.156) was about a third of the real edge's (0.469) — its high Sharpe was a low-variance artifact of a short sample, exactly the kind of thing five noise-driven candidates can produce purely by chance.
Question 5

A researcher tests only the best 5 of 50 variations actually tried. Is the resulting bootstrap p-value trustworthy?

No formula can detect a curated candidate set from the outside. It will compute a confident-looking answer to the wrong question ("best of 5?" instead of "best of 50?") — a discipline failure, not something the arithmetic can catch.
Section 13

What to carry forward

01

WRC and SPA test the full candidate panel's actual return histories directly — a genuine bootstrap in place of the Deflated Sharpe Ratio's estimated trial count and extreme-value-theory formula.

02

The test statistic is a maximum across candidates, which is exactly why no textbook single-series critical value applies — a stationary block bootstrap, applied jointly across the whole panel, builds the real null instead.

03

WRC is honest but conservative once junk candidates are included; Hansen's SPA studentizes and stops clearly-inferior candidates from padding the null, recovering real power on the same data.

04

An impressive raw Sharpe is not the same claim as a statistically real edge — this deck's own toy panel showed a noise strategy's Sharpe nearly matching a real edge's while its mean failed the bootstrap outright.

05

Both tests are worthless on a dishonestly curated candidate set — the honesty of the candidate list is a discipline problem no formula can rescue after the fact.

What this now lets you do

Recognize when a "best backtested variant" claim needs a bootstrap test of the whole search, not just a look at the winner's own Sharpe ratio. Understand why WRC and SPA are the same idea at different power levels, not competing methods. Spot a Sharpe ratio that's flattering purely because of low realized variance, and know exactly what test would catch it.
