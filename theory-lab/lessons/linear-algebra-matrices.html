Linear Algebra & Matrices

Vectors, matrices, and eigenvectors are not a math-class prerequisite this repo makes you sit through for its own sake — they are the exact vocabulary the PCA, Kalman Filter, and Hidden Markov Model lessons in this Theory Lab quietly assume you already have.

DEEPER FOUNDATION — read before PCA, Kalman Filter, or Hidden Markov Models ~15 min read Prereqs: none
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • A matrix is a machine that takes every vector in the plane and moves it somewhere else (rotate, stretch, shrink, shear); an eigenvector is the special direction the matrix only stretches or shrinks along, never rotates off, and the stretch amount is its eigenvalue.
  • Matrix-vector multiplication (Av) and matrix-matrix multiplication are worked by hand, and matrix multiplication is shown to be non-commutative: MN ≠ NM for concrete 2×2 examples.
  • The worked example solves A = [[3,1],[1,3]]: the characteristic equation λ² - 6λ + 8 = 0 factors to eigenvalues λ₁ = 4 (eigenvector (1,1)) and λ₂ = 2 (eigenvector (1,-1)), which come out perpendicular because A is symmetric.
  • Reframed as a two-FX-pair covariance matrix (correlation ≈ 0.33), those eigenvalues split the book's total variance (trace = 6) into 66.7% shared-direction and 33.3% divergence-direction, giving 1.8 effective independent bets via (Σλ)²/Σλ² = 36/20.
  • This vocabulary is the direct prerequisite for three other Theory Lab lessons: PCA (eigenvectors of a correlation matrix), the Kalman Filter (chained matrix multiplication), and Hidden Markov Models (the stationary distribution is the eigenvector with eigenvalue 1).
Section 01

Why this matters

Open the PCA lesson in this Theory Lab and within a few paragraphs you hit "eigenvector," "eigenvalue," and a 3×3 covariance matrix. Open the Kalman Filter lesson and you hit a state vector, a transition matrix, an observation matrix, and a covariance matrix that gets multiplied through every update step. Open the Hidden Markov Model lesson and you hit an \(N \times N\) transition matrix whose rows are probability distributions, and a "stationary distribution" that turns out to be nothing more exotic than a specific eigenvector of that matrix. None of those three lessons stop to explain what a matrix actually is or why "eigenvector" means anything at all — they assume it, the same way a lesson on options pricing assumes you already know what a derivative (calculus, not the financial kind) is.

This lesson is the "Deeper Foundations" tier's answer to that gap: not a new trading concept, but the mathematical vocabulary underneath several of the concepts this Theory Lab already covers. It is written for zero prior linear algebra, built up geometrically rather than as a wall of matrix-algebra rules to memorize, and it worked every piece of arithmetic below by hand so you can check it against your own. Read it once, and "eigenvector of the correlation matrix" in the PCA lesson, "propagate the covariance matrix" in the Kalman Filter lesson, and "stationary distribution of the transition matrix" in the Hidden Markov Model lesson should all read as instances of the same small set of ideas rather than three separate walls of jargon.

💡
Plain-English intuition

A vector is an arrow, a matrix is a machine that moves arrows

A vector is just an ordered list of numbers — \((3, 1)\), say. Two equivalent ways to picture it, and you'll want both: as a point in space (walk 3 units along one axis, 1 unit along the other, and put a dot there), or as an arrow from the origin to that point, carrying both a direction and a length. In this repo, a vector might be two FX pairs' daily returns on a single day, a 2-D hidden-state estimate in a Kalman filter, or a row of loadings across several instruments — different meanings, identical arithmetic.

A matrix is a rectangular table of numbers, but the useful way to think about it is not "a table of numbers" — it's a machine that takes any vector in and produces another vector out, always by the same fixed rule (a "linear transformation"). Feed a matrix a vector, and it hands back a new vector that has been rotated, stretched, shrunk, sheared, or some mix of all of those, in a way that's completely determined by the matrix's entries. A 2×2 matrix acting on the whole 2-D plane at once is the picture to hold in your head for everything that follows: it takes every point in the plane and moves it somewhere else, all in one consistent motion — circles become ellipses, squares become parallelograms, straight lines stay straight lines (that's what "linear" means here), but the origin never moves.

Almost everything below is one question asked about that machine: is there some special direction the machine doesn't rotate at all — a line through the origin that the machine only stretches or shrinks, but never tilts off of itself? If such a direction exists, it's called an eigenvector, and the amount of stretching along it is called an eigenvalue. That's the whole punchline of this lesson, arrived at properly below.

Section 02

The math, step by step

Work through this in order — matrix-vector multiplication first (the basic operation), then matrix-matrix multiplication and the identity matrix (composing two machines, and the machine that does nothing), and only then eigenvectors and eigenvalues, which are a question you can only ask once you understand what the machine does to an arbitrary vector.

\(v\)A vector — an ordered list of numbers, e.g. \(v = (v_1, v_2)\) in 2-D. Written as a column when it multiplies a matrix.
\(A\)A matrix — here always \(2 \times 2\), i.e. 2 rows and 2 columns, entries \(A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}\). "\(m \times n\)" always means \(m\) rows, \(n\) columns.
\(Av\)The matrix \(A\) applied to the vector \(v\) — "\(A\) acting on \(v\)," read right to left. This is matrix-vector multiplication, defined below. The output is always another vector.
\(I\)The identity matrix, \(\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\) in 2-D — the one matrix for which \(Iv = v\) for every \(v\). The matrix equivalent of multiplying a number by 1.
\(\det(A)\)The determinant of \(A\) — for a \(2\times2\) matrix, \(\det(A) = ad - bc\). Tells you (among other things) whether \(A\) is invertible: \(\det(A) = 0\) means \(A\) squashes the plane down onto a line or a point, losing information, and cannot be undone.
\(\lambda\)(lambda) An eigenvalue of \(A\) — a scalar (an ordinary number) such that some nonzero vector is only scaled, not rotated, by \(A\).
\(v\) (as eigenvector)An eigenvector of \(A\) — a nonzero vector whose direction is unchanged by \(A\); it may be lengthened, shortened, or flipped end-to-end, but it stays on the same line through the origin.
\(\det(A - \lambda I) = 0\)The characteristic equation — the algebraic condition that pins down every eigenvalue of \(A\), derived below.

Matrix-vector multiplication, by hand

For a \(2\times2\) matrix \(A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}\) and a vector \(v = \begin{pmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \end{pmatrix}\), the product \(Av\) is defined by:

\[ Av = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} a v_1 + b v_2 \\ c v_1 + d v_2 \end{pmatrix} \]
Athe matrix — the fixed machine doing the moving
vthe input vector, written as a column
Avthe output — each row of A dotted with v

Read it as: the first entry of the output is "row 1 of \(A\), dotted with \(v\)" (multiply matching entries, add the results); the second entry is "row 2 of \(A\), dotted with \(v\)." Concretely, with \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 1 & 4 \end{pmatrix}\) and \(v = \begin{pmatrix} 5 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\):

\[ Av = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 1 & 4 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 5 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2(5) + 3(1) \\ 1(5) + 4(1) \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 13 \\ 9 \end{pmatrix} \]
(5, 1)the input arrow — mostly horizontal, barely tilted up
(13, 9)the output arrow — a different length, a different direction

The input arrow \((5,1)\) — mostly horizontal, barely tilted up — comes out as \((13,9)\), a different length pointing in a different direction. That's the whole operation: two multiplications and one addition per output row, always.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

A matrix is like a recipe card that tells you exactly what to do with a pair of numbers to make a new pair of numbers. You take the input's two numbers, follow the recipe (multiply and add in a fixed pattern), and out comes a brand-new pair — a new arrow that usually points a different way and is a different length than the one you started with.

Matrix-matrix multiplication: chaining two machines

If \(A\) is one transformation and \(B\) is another, applying \(B\) first and then \(A\) to the result is itself a single linear transformation — call it \(AB\) — computed by treating each column of \(B\) as a vector, multiplying \(A\) into it, and stacking the results back up as columns:

\[ (AB)_{ij} = \sum_k A_{ik} B_{kj} \quad\text{(row } i \text{ of } A \text{ dotted with column } j \text{ of } B\text{)} \]
Bapplied first — its columns are the vectors being fed in
Aapplied second, to whatever B just produced
ABthe single composite machine — one matrix, same rule as before

The dimensions have to line up: an \(m \times n\) matrix can only multiply an \(n \times p\) matrix (the inner dimensions must match), and the result is \(m \times p\). For two \(2\times2\) matrices this is always defined. The one fact that trips people up the first time: matrix multiplication is generally not commutative — \(AB \neq BA\) in general, unlike ordinary numbers where \(3 \times 5 = 5 \times 3\). "Apply \(B\) then \(A\)" is a genuinely different machine from "apply \(A\) then \(B\)." Check it with \(M = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\) and \(N = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\):

\[ MN = \begin{pmatrix} 1(1)+2(1) & 1(0)+2(1) \\ 0(1)+1(1) & 0(0)+1(1) \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 2 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \qquad NM = \begin{pmatrix} 1(1)+0(0) & 1(2)+0(1) \\ 1(1)+1(0) & 1(2)+1(1) \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix} \]
MNapply N first, then M — one specific composite machine
NMapply M first, then N — a different composite machine
MN ≠ NMorder matters here, unlike ordinary number multiplication

\(MN = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 2 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\) and \(NM = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\) are not the same matrix — order matters. This is why, in the Kalman Filter lesson, the exact order a state vector gets multiplied through a transition matrix, an observation matrix, and a covariance matrix is written with care rather than treated as interchangeable arithmetic.

The identity matrix \(I = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\) is the one matrix that changes nothing: \(Iv = v\) for any vector \(v\), and \(AI = IA = A\) for any matrix \(A\). It is the matrix equivalent of the number 1, and it shows up below as the tool for isolating eigenvalues.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Chaining two matrices together is like lining up two toy machines end to end, so whatever pops out of the first one drops straight into the second. But the order matters — squish-then-twist is not the same as twist-then-squish, even with the exact same two machines. The identity matrix is the one machine that's really just an empty tube: whatever goes in comes out exactly the same, untouched.

Eigenvectors and eigenvalues, built geometrically

Take the "machine" picture seriously: a \(2\times2\) matrix \(A\) takes every vector in the plane and moves it somewhere else. Pick almost any starting vector and its direction will change — the output arrow points a different way than the input arrow did. But for most matrices that actually arise from real data (in particular, for the symmetric matrices — where \(A\) equals its own transpose, \(A_{ij}=A_{ji}\) — that covariance and correlation matrices always are), there exist one or more special directions where this doesn't happen: feed the matrix a vector lying exactly along one of those directions, and the output is a scaled copy of the same vector, still on the exact same line through the origin, just longer, shorter, or flipped to point the opposite way.

That special vector is an eigenvector of \(A\), and the scaling factor is its eigenvalue \(\lambda\). Formally:

\[ Av = \lambda v \qquad (v \neq 0) \]
A
The machine
The matrix — its full action is rotate-and-scale, in general
v
The eigenvector
The special direction A only scales, never rotates off its own line
λ
The eigenvalue
The plain number A stretches v by along that direction

Read this literally: "the matrix \(A\) acting on \(v\) produces the same thing as just multiplying \(v\) by the plain number \(\lambda\)." The left-hand side is a full matrix-vector multiplication (rotate-and-scale, in general); the right-hand side is pure scaling, no rotation at all. An eigenvector is precisely a \(v\) for which those two operations — the matrix's full action, and mere scaling — happen to produce an identical result. A generic 2×2 matrix has (at most) two such directions, and, critically, a matrix only has eigenvectors in this sense because it is square — \(Av\) and \(v\) have to be able to sit in the same space and be directly compared (and possibly be equal) for "\(Av = \lambda v\)" to even be a meaningful statement, which requires \(A\) to map that space back into itself.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine a room full of arrows pointing every which way, and a machine that grabs every single one and moves it. Almost all of them get swung around to point somewhere new. But one or two special arrows just get longer or shorter — they never get swung to a new direction, only stretched or shrunk along the same line they were already on. Those special arrows are eigenvectors, and how much they got stretched is their eigenvalue.

Solving for eigenvalues: the characteristic equation

\(Av = \lambda v\) can be rewritten with everything on one side. Since \(\lambda v = \lambda I v\) (multiplying by the identity first changes nothing), subtract:

\[ Av - \lambda v = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad Av - \lambda I v = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad (A - \lambda I)v = 0 \]
A − λIA with λ subtracted off its diagonal entries
vthe (unknown, nonzero) eigenvector being solved for
= 0the zero vector — A−λI must crush v flat

This says: some matrix, \((A - \lambda I)\), multiplied by a nonzero vector \(v\), produces the zero vector. A matrix can only crush a nonzero vector down to zero if it "squashes" some direction entirely flat — which is exactly the condition \(\det(A - \lambda I) = 0\) (recall from the symbols table: zero determinant means the matrix loses a dimension). For a general \(2\times2\) matrix \(A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}\):

\[ \det(A - \lambda I) = \det\begin{pmatrix} a-\lambda & b \\ c & d-\lambda \end{pmatrix} = (a-\lambda)(d-\lambda) - bc = 0 \]
det(A−λI)determinant of the shifted matrix
(a−λ)(d−λ)−bcthe ordinary 2×2 determinant formula, ad−bc, applied to the shifted entries
= 0the condition that pins down every eigenvalue of A

Expanding \((a-\lambda)(d-\lambda) = ad - a\lambda - d\lambda + \lambda^2\) and collecting terms gives the characteristic equation, a plain quadratic in \(\lambda\):

\[ \lambda^2 - (a+d)\lambda + (ad - bc) = 0 \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad \lambda^2 - \operatorname{trace}(A)\,\lambda + \det(A) = 0 \]
trace(A)sum of the diagonal entries, a+d
det(A)ad − bc, the determinant
λsolve this ordinary quadratic to get every eigenvalue of A

where \(\operatorname{trace}(A) = a + d\) (the sum of the diagonal entries). Solve this ordinary quadratic for \(\lambda\) and you have every eigenvalue of \(A\) — an ordinary \(2\times2\) matrix has (up to) two of them. Once a specific \(\lambda\) is known, plug it back into \((A - \lambda I)v = 0\) and solve that (now perfectly ordinary) system of two linear equations for \(v\) to get the matching eigenvector. The full arithmetic, with real numbers instead of letters, is worked end to end in the box below.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Finding the eigenvalues is like a puzzle: keep subtracting some unknown number off the matrix's diagonal until the machine stops swinging things around normally and instead squashes one whole direction completely flat, like a pancake. The special number that makes it go flat is an eigenvalue — and there's a plain equation (the characteristic equation) that tells you exactly which numbers those are, without any guessing.

The dashed circle is every unit vector, all directions at once. The matrix \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\) (the same matrix worked by hand below) sends that whole circle to the purple ellipse. Follow any one of the grey spokes: the vector pointing due right (0°) lands on the dashed grey line pointing up and to the right — its direction changed, it got rotated. But the two colored spokes, sitting exactly on the ellipse's long and short axes, land on themselves, only longer: the red spoke is stretched exactly 4× and the blue spoke exactly 2×, and neither one rotates even slightly off its own line. Those two directions are \(A\)'s eigenvectors, and 4 and 2 are their eigenvalues — this picture is \(Av = \lambda v\).

Worked example

Finding both eigenvalues and eigenvectors of a symmetric 2×2 matrix, by hand

Take the matrix from the chart above, chosen to be symmetric (so its eigenvectors come out guaranteed perpendicular — more on why below) and to produce clean integers throughout:

\[ A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix} \]
3, 3diagonal entries — each pair's own variance in the trading scenario below
1off-diagonal — the shared covariance term
A = Aᵀsymmetric — guarantees real eigenvalues and perpendicular eigenvectors, proven below

Step 1 — set up the characteristic equation. Using the general formula derived above, \(\lambda^2 - \operatorname{trace}(A)\lambda + \det(A) = 0\), with \(\operatorname{trace}(A) = 3+3 = 6\) and \(\det(A) = (3)(3) - (1)(1) = 9 - 1 = 8\):

\[ \lambda^2 - 6\lambda + 8 = 0 \]
trace(A) = 63+3, sum of the diagonal
det(A) = 8(3)(3)−(1)(1)
λ²−6λ+8=0the concrete quadratic to solve

Step 2 — solve the quadratic. This factors cleanly: \(\lambda^2 - 6\lambda + 8 = (\lambda - 4)(\lambda - 2)\) — check it by expanding: \((\lambda-4)(\lambda-2) = \lambda^2 - 2\lambda - 4\lambda + 8 = \lambda^2 - 6\lambda + 8\), which matches. So:

\[ \lambda_1 = 4, \qquad \lambda_2 = 2 \]
λ₁ = 4larger eigenvalue — more stretch, the dominant direction
λ₂ = 2smaller eigenvalue — less stretch

Step 3 — find the eigenvector for \(\lambda_1 = 4\). Substitute into \((A - \lambda I)v = 0\):

\[ (A - 4I) = \begin{pmatrix} 3-4 & 1 \\ 1 & 3-4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix} \]
A − 4IA with 4 subtracted off its diagonal
−v₁+v₂=0row 1 of the resulting system — pins down the eigenvector's direction
v = (1, 1)the eigenvector for λ=4 — equal components, "both pairs move together"

Multiplying this by \(v = (v_1, v_2)\) and setting the result to zero, the first row gives \(-v_1 + v_2 = 0\), i.e. \(v_2 = v_1\) (the second row, \(v_1 - v_2 = 0\), says exactly the same thing — expected, since a singular \(2\times2\) matrix's two rows must be proportional). Any vector with equal components works; take the simplest, \(v = (1, 1)\), or its unit-length version \(\left(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}, \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\right)\). Check: \(Av = \begin{pmatrix} 3(1)+1(1) \\ 1(1)+3(1) \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix} = 4 \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = 4v\). Confirmed.

Step 4 — find the eigenvector for \(\lambda_2 = 2\).

\[ (A - 2I) = \begin{pmatrix} 3-2 & 1 \\ 1 & 3-2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \]
A − 2IA with 2 subtracted off its diagonal
v₁+v₂=0row 1 of the resulting system
v = (1, −1)the eigenvector for λ=2 — opposite components, "pairs diverge"

The first row gives \(v_1 + v_2 = 0\), i.e. \(v_2 = -v_1\). Take \(v = (1, -1)\), or unit-length \(\left(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}, -\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\right)\). Check: \(Av = \begin{pmatrix} 3(1)+1(-1) \\ 1(1)+3(-1) \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ -2 \end{pmatrix} = 2 \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix} = 2v\). Confirmed.

Notice the two eigenvectors, \((1,1)\) and \((1,-1)\), are perpendicular — their dot product is \((1)(1) + (1)(-1) = 0\). That's not a coincidence of these particular numbers: it's a general theorem that any symmetric matrix (\(A = A^\top\)) has mutually perpendicular eigenvectors, and real eigenvalues. Covariance and correlation matrices are always symmetric, which is exactly why the PCA lesson in this Theory Lab can promise that its principal components come out perpendicular to each other with no extra assumption required — it's inherited directly from this fact.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

A symmetric matrix is one that looks exactly the same if you flip it across its own diagonal, like folding a square napkin along the corner. For those matrices, the two special stretch-only directions always end up pointing exactly perpendicular to each other, like the corner of a book — that's not a coincidence of these particular numbers, it's a rule that's always true for this kind of matrix, and it's exactly the kind of matrix a set of returns' covariances always makes.

🎯
Real-world trading scenario

The same matrix, reframed as two FX pairs' return covariance

Now put a trading interpretation on the exact numbers just solved by hand. Suppose \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\) is the daily return covariance matrix of two positions in your book — say EUR/USD and GBP/USD — in some return-variance units. Diagonal entries are each pair's own variance (3 and 3 — by coincidence of these clean numbers, equal daily variance for both pairs); the off-diagonal entry, 1, is their covariance. That implies a correlation of \(1/\sqrt{3 \times 3} = 1/3 \approx 0.33\) — a real, moderate, unremarkable positive correlation for two dollar-crosses, not an extreme one.

The eigen-decomposition just computed by hand says something concrete about that book that the raw covariance numbers alone don't make obvious. The total variance of the two-pair book is \(\operatorname{trace}(A) = 3+3 = 6\), and the eigenvalues split that total exactly along the two eigenvector directions: \(\lambda_1=4\) along \((1,1)/\sqrt2\) — both pairs moving in the same direction by similar amounts, a shared "risk-on / dollar" move — and \(\lambda_2=2\) along \((1,-1)/\sqrt2\) — the two pairs moving in opposite directions, i.e. genuinely diverging from each other. As a share of the book's total variance: \(4/6 \approx 66.7\%\) shared-direction, \(2/6 \approx 33.3\%\) divergence-direction.

Concretely: if you're holding roughly equal-sized long EUR/USD and long GBP/USD positions, your combined book sits close to the \((1,1)\) direction — the dominant eigenvector — almost by construction, since "both pairs up together" is that direction. That means about two-thirds of your daily P&L variance from this pair of trades is coming from one shared factor (call it "long the dollar bloc"), and only about one-third is coming from the pairs actually behaving differently from each other. Using the same effective-bets formula this project already computes for a strategy book (\(\text{effectiveBets} = (\Sigma\lambda)^2/\Sigma\lambda^2\)): \((\Sigma\lambda)^2/\Sigma\lambda^2 = 6^2/(4^2+2^2) = 36/20 = 1.8\). Two nominal positions, but only about 1.8 effective, independent bets — not the dramatic "really just one big bet" story a much higher correlation would tell (that would need \(\lambda_1\) much closer to 6 and \(\lambda_2\) close to 0), but a real, quantified statement that this "two positions" book is somewhat less diversified than two fully independent trades would be, and by exactly how much. If you sized both legs assuming two uncorrelated units of risk, you are carrying about 10% more concentrated exposure to the shared factor than that assumption implies — worth knowing before you also add a third dollar-cross and assume it adds a full third independent bet.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

Nobody on a trading desk solves a characteristic equation by hand day to day — software libraries (or, in this repo, a hand-rolled numerical routine) do the arithmetic. What the desk-level intuition actually is: an eigenvector of a covariance or correlation matrix is a portfolio — a specific combination of the underlying instruments — and its eigenvalue is how much of the book's total variance that portfolio accounts for. Ranking eigenvectors by eigenvalue, largest first, answers "if I had to describe this basket's risk in the fewest possible independent moves, what are those moves, and how much does each one matter?" — which is precisely what the PCA lesson in this Theory Lab builds out in full, instrument by instrument, for a basket of FX pairs.

The same machinery reappears with a different vocabulary in two other lessons here. In the Kalman Filter, the state vector is repeatedly multiplied through a transition matrix (predicting the next state) and a covariance matrix is propagated through matrix multiplication at every step — the filter's entire update cycle is matrix-vector and matrix-matrix multiplication, nothing more exotic. In the Hidden Markov Model lesson, the transition matrix's "stationary distribution" — the long-run fraction of time spent in each regime — is literally the eigenvector of the transition matrix (transposed appropriately) whose eigenvalue is exactly 1; every valid transition matrix is guaranteed to have such an eigenvector because its rows are probability distributions that must sum to 1. Once you've solved \(Av = \lambda v\) by hand once, "the stationary distribution is the eigenvector for eigenvalue 1" stops being a magic phrase and becomes a direct application of the definition above.

More generally, quant risk teams use eigen-decomposition anywhere they need to answer "how many genuinely different things are actually happening in this basket," which is the mathematical core of Markowitz-style portfolio theory (diversification benefit comes from off-diagonal covariance terms, and eigen-decomposition is exactly how you separate "shared" variance from "idiosyncratic" variance) and of statistical factor-risk models like Barra's (covered in more depth in the PCA lesson's further reading).

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where the intuition here breaks if pushed too far

Not every matrix has "nice" eigenvectors. The guarantee that eigenvalues are real and eigenvectors are perpendicular is specific to symmetric matrices. A general (non-symmetric) matrix can have complex eigenvalues, or eigenvectors that aren't perpendicular, or in degenerate cases fewer independent eigenvectors than its dimension. Covariance and correlation matrices are always symmetric by construction, so this repo's uses of eigen-decomposition stay in the well-behaved case — but the geometric picture in this lesson (an ellipse, two perpendicular axes) is specifically the symmetric-matrix picture, not the fully general one.

Only square matrices have eigenvectors in this sense. \(Av = \lambda v\) requires \(Av\) and \(v\) to live in the same space so they can be compared and potentially be scalar multiples of each other. A non-square matrix maps between spaces of different dimension (say, 3 instruments' returns projected down to 2 factor scores) and simply has no eigenvectors to solve for — a different, related toolkit (singular value decomposition) is the generalization used there, and is out of scope for this lesson.

Equal eigenvalues mean "no preferred direction," not "an error." If a \(2\times2\) symmetric matrix's two eigenvalues come out equal (\(\lambda_1 = \lambda_2\)), the transformation stretches every direction by the same amount — geometrically, the "ellipse" is actually a circle, and any direction (not just two special ones) is an eigenvector. This happens for a correlation matrix precisely when the two series are exactly uncorrelated (a diagonal matrix with equal diagonal entries) — a case worth recognizing rather than treating as a computational glitch.

An eigen-decomposition describes the matrix you fed it, not a law of nature. A correlation matrix estimated from a rolling window of returns is a sample statistic — it changes as the window rolls forward, and its eigenvectors and eigenvalues change with it, sometimes sharply, especially when two eigenvalues are close together (small perturbations to the data can then swap or rotate the corresponding eigenvectors noticeably, even though the eigenvalues themselves barely move). Treat any single eigen-decomposition as a snapshot of a particular estimation window, not a permanent structural fact about the instruments involved — the same discipline this repo applies to any other rolling-window estimate.

"Stretch factor" is about the linear map, not about price moving in a straight line. None of this says anything about whether a price series trends, mean-reverts, or does either predictably over time — that's a different question (see the OU, Hurst/variance-ratio, and day-type lessons). Eigen-decomposition here describes the instantaneous covariance structure across instruments at a point in time, not their dynamics through time.

Section 04

Key Takeaways

✓A matrix is a machine that transforms every vector. Feed it any vector in the plane and it hands back a rotated, stretched, shrunk, or sheared version — always by the same fixed rule, and the origin never moves.
✓An eigenvector is the one direction that only gets scaled, never rotated. Formally, \(Av = \lambda v\): the matrix's full action and plain multiplication by a number produce an identical result.
✓The eigenvalue \(\lambda\) is the amount of stretch. Larger \(\lambda\) means that direction is stretched more — and, in a covariance matrix, accounts for more of the total variance.
✓Symmetric matrices are guaranteed well-behaved. Covariance and correlation matrices are always symmetric (\(A = A^\top\)), which guarantees real eigenvalues and mutually perpendicular eigenvectors — no extra assumption required.
✓Eigenvalues of a covariance matrix split total variance across independent directions. The worked example's \(\lambda_1=4\), \(\lambda_2=2\) splits a two-pair book's variance 66.7%/33.3% across a shared-direction and a divergence-direction, working out to 1.8 effective independent bets.
✓This vocabulary is the direct prerequisite for PCA, the Kalman Filter, and Hidden Markov Models. "Eigenvector of the correlation matrix," "propagate the covariance matrix," and "stationary distribution" are all instances of the same small set of ideas covered here.
⚠An eigen-decomposition is a snapshot, not a permanent structural fact. It describes one rolling estimation window and can shift — sometimes sharply — when two eigenvalues are close together, even if the eigenvalues themselves barely move.
⚠Non-square matrices have no eigenvectors in this sense. \(Av = \lambda v\) requires \(Av\) and \(v\) to live in the same space; singular value decomposition (SVD) is the generalization for non-square matrices, out of scope here.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. Why must a matrix be square for "eigenvectors" (in the \(Av = \lambda v\) sense) to make sense at all?
\(Av = \lambda v\) requires comparing \(Av\) directly to \(v\) — asking whether \(Av\) happens to be a scalar multiple of \(v\) itself. That comparison only type-checks if \(Av\) lives in the same space as \(v\) does, which requires the matrix to map that space back into itself (an \(n \times n\) matrix takes an \(n\)-dimensional vector to another \(n\)-dimensional vector). A non-square, say \(3 \times 2\), matrix maps a 2-D vector to a 3-D output — there is no meaningful sense in which a 3-D vector could equal \(\lambda\) times the original 2-D input, so the equation \(Av = \lambda v\) is not even well-formed for a non-square matrix.
Q2. What does it mean geometrically if a symmetric 2×2 matrix has two equal eigenvalues, \(\lambda_1 = \lambda_2\)?
It means the transformation stretches every direction in the plane by the exact same factor — there is no "preferred" axis at all. The unit circle maps to another circle (a degenerate ellipse whose major and minor axes are equal), not a genuinely elongated ellipse, and literally every nonzero vector is an eigenvector with that shared eigenvalue, not just two special ones. In the correlation-matrix interpretation, this is exactly what happens when two series are completely uncorrelated with equal variance: there is no direction along which they move together more than any other, so no single portfolio combination of the two stands out as the dominant risk factor.
Q3. Why is matrix multiplication generally not commutative (\(AB \neq BA\)), and why does that matter in practice rather than just being a textbook curiosity?
Each matrix represents a specific transformation of the plane, and "apply \(B\), then apply \(A\) to the result" (that's \(AB\), read right to left) is a genuinely different composite motion from "apply \(A\), then apply \(B\)" (\(BA\)) — rotating then stretching is not the same overall move as stretching then rotating, in general. This is worked concretely above with \(M\) and \(N\), which give different results (\(MN \neq NM\)) despite both being simple integer matrices. It matters in practice anywhere a computation involves multiple matrices chained together — for instance, a Kalman filter's predict/update equations multiply a state through a transition matrix, an observation matrix, and covariance matrices in a specific, load-bearing order; silently swapping the order of two matrix multiplications there is a real bug, not a harmless simplification.
Q4. In the worked example, \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\) has eigenvalues 4 and 2, with eigenvectors \((1,1)\) and \((1,-1)\). Without recomputing from scratch, what would you expect to happen to the eigenvalues (and the eigenvectors) if you instead used \(2A = \begin{pmatrix} 6 & 2 \\ 2 & 6 \end{pmatrix}\)?
The eigenvalues would double, to 8 and 4, while the eigenvectors stay exactly the same, \((1,1)\) and \((1,-1)\). This follows directly from \(Av = \lambda v\): if \((2A)v = 2(Av) = 2(\lambda v) = (2\lambda)v\), then \(v\) is still an eigenvector of \(2A\), now with eigenvalue \(2\lambda\) instead of \(\lambda\) — scaling a matrix by a constant scales every eigenvalue by that same constant and leaves every eigenvector's direction untouched. This is exactly why the effective-bets ratio \((\Sigma\lambda)^2/\Sigma\lambda^2\) used in the trading scenario above is scale-invariant: multiplying every eigenvalue by the same constant leaves that ratio unchanged, which is what lets it be computed indifferently from a covariance matrix or a rescaled correlation matrix.
Q5. A trader says "these two positions are basically one bet." Translate that sentence into a precise statement about the eigenvalues of their 2×2 covariance matrix.
It translates to: one eigenvalue is close to the matrix's full trace (nearly all of the combined variance), and the other eigenvalue is close to zero (almost no variance left along the perpendicular direction) — the "ellipse" of possible joint moves has become almost a flat line segment, because the two positions move together almost perfectly. In the effective-bets formula, \((\Sigma\lambda)^2/\Sigma\lambda^2\) then comes out close to 1 rather than close to 2, quantifying "basically one bet" as a number instead of a figure of speech. The worked scenario in this lesson (eigenvalues 4 and 2, giving 1.8 effective bets) is deliberately the more moderate, honest middle case, not this extreme one.
Q6. Why does a Hidden Markov Model's transition matrix always have an eigenvalue of exactly 1, and why is the corresponding eigenvector called the "stationary distribution"?
Every row of a valid transition matrix is a probability distribution (the probabilities of moving from that state to every state, including itself) and therefore sums to exactly 1; this structural property guarantees the matrix always has 1 as an eigenvalue (a standard result for any matrix whose rows, or whose columns depending on convention, sum to 1). The eigenvector paired with that eigenvalue-1, once normalized to sum to 1 itself, is a distribution over states that the transition matrix maps back to itself unchanged: \(A^\top \pi = 1 \cdot \pi = \pi\). That is precisely the definition of a stationary distribution — the long-run mix of time spent in each regime that, once reached, the dynamics no longer disturb. It is exactly the same equation, \(Av = \lambda v\), just with \(\lambda\) fixed at 1 and the vector reinterpreted as a probability distribution rather than a return or a price.
