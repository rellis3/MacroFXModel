Policy Gradients & Actor-Critic, Without the Max

The last lesson learned values from experience by repeatedly comparing a small menu of discrete actions and keeping the best one. That trick quietly breaks the moment the action itself is a continuous number — an exact position size, not "buy, sell, or hold." This lesson is the direct sequel: it shows how to learn a policy directly instead of a value table, how pairing that policy (the "actor") with a value estimate (the "critic") tames the resulting noise, and why none of that machinery says one word about whether the thing being optimized is actually a good idea.

Section 1

When "pick the best action" stops being cheap

Q-learning's update — and acting on it — leans on one operation at every single step.

Recall from Reinforcement Learning 2
Q(s, a)
→
maxa′ Q(s′, a′)
Q-learning's TD update, and acting greedily from a learned Q-function, both need this max over actions — computed fresh, every step.

When the action set is a short discrete menu — Stay vs. Open, Hold vs. Close — that max is comparing a handful of numbers and keeping the largest. Trivial. But real position sizing isn't a three-item menu; it's a continuous number, anywhere along a line. Computing maxa Q(s,a) over a continuous a is no longer a lookup — it's its own optimization problem, nested inside every decision step, with no closed form in general.

The actual bottleneck

It isn't that Q-learning "can't handle" continuous actions in principle — a Q-function can still be evaluated at any action. The problem is finding the max: searching an infinite set of candidates fast enough to act in real time, at every single step, forever.

This is the entire motivation for everything that follows: keep the max, and continuous actions force a nested optimization problem into every step. Drop the max — parameterize the policy itself and improve it with gradient ascent — and the continuous case stops being special at all.

Section 3

The fix: learn the policy directly

π

The policy is the thing being learned now

πθ(a|s) — a function, with its own parameters θ, mapping a state straight to an action or a distribution over actions. No value table sits in between.

↑

Improved by gradient ascent

Define J(θ) as the expected return under πθ, then nudge θ in whatever direction increases J(θ) — the same gradient-ascent machinery used to train nearly every other model in this curriculum.

✓

One sentence is the whole idea

Raise the probability of actions that led to a higher-than-expected return; lower the probability of ones that led to a lower-than-expected return.

∞

Works natively on a continuum

For a Gaussian policy over a continuous action, its log-probability and that log-probability's gradient are both plain closed-form expressions — no max over an infinite set ever appears in the method.

Picture a policy as a slot machine with a dial. Pull the lever, see which action got picked and what return followed. Better than expected → turn the dial slightly toward that action. Worse than expected → turn it slightly away. Repeat this thousands of times and the dial settles on whatever's actually been paying off. That is the policy gradient theorem — the dial is just a vector of parameters θ, and "turn it toward what worked" is precise calculus (this exact estimator is called REINFORCE, Williams 1992).
Section 4

Actor and critic, drawn as a loop

Two networks, one cycle, repeated every step.

The actor decides; the critic grades. The grade — not the raw, noisy return — is what actually updates the actor's parameters.

The actor is the policy πθ itself, updated by the policy gradient. The critic is a separately learned value function, typically trained with the exact TD(0) update from the previous lesson: V(s) ← V(s) + α[r + γV(s′) − V(s)]. The bracketed term there — the TD error — is, to a first approximation, the same quantity as the advantage used to weight the actor's update. Two lessons, converging on one number.

Section 5

Why a baseline makes the signal usable

Raw REINFORCE works, but the weight it uses — an entire trajectory's return — is noisy.

Raw return as the weight
  • Weight = the full observed return G — all of the trajectory's randomness lands in one number
  • A generally excellent state inflates every one of its actions' weight equally
  • No way to tell "this action was fine" from "this state is just fine"
Advantage as the weight
  • A(s,a) = Q(s,a) − V(s) — subtract the critic's own baseline for this state
  • Only rewards actions that beat this state's own average, not absolute return
  • Subtracting any action-independent quantity leaves the gradient's expectation unchanged, but can sharply cut its variance
Grading a test score against zero tells you little; grading it against how the class usually does on that exact test tells you something. The advantage does this for actions — it compares an action's outcome to what this state usually delivers, so only genuinely above- or below-average choices push the policy around.

A > 0 means the outcome beat what the critic expected — the parameter moves up. A < 0 moves it down. A learning rate of zero freezes it exactly in place.

This is a deliberately simplified stand-in for the real update — the actual policy gradient scales the step by the score function ∇θlog πθ(a|s), not by a flat 1-to-1 nudge — but the shape of the logic is identical: advantage decides direction and rough magnitude, the learning rate decides how much any single sample is allowed to move things.

Section 8

How practitioners actually use this

🛡

PPO & TRPO constrain each step

A single large gradient step can catastrophically change the policy — and the data used to estimate the next gradient along with it. Proximal Policy Optimization and Trust Region Policy Optimization cap how far one update is allowed to move.

📊

GAE blends bias and variance

Generalized Advantage Estimation mixes multi-step returns with the critic's baseline, trading the two off more carefully than a single raw TD step.

🤖

RLHF is this exact machinery

Aligning large language models with human preferences trains a reward model from human comparisons, then runs a policy gradient (commonly PPO) against it — same theorem, different "environment."

🔁

All elaborations, same two ideas

Every production method here is still just a score-function gradient plus a learned baseline to cut its variance — nothing above replaces either core idea, only stabilizes it.

Section 9

A powerful optimizer says nothing about what it's optimizing

Think of RL as an incredibly obedient genie: whatever wish you phrase as a reward, it grants exactly and only that wish, as literally as possible, with zero judgment about whether the wish was a good idea. Ask it to maximize profit with no mention of risk, and it will happily find the riskiest possible way to make money look great in a test environment — not because it misunderstood, but because it did precisely what it was asked, and nothing more.

This is not a caveat about a weak implementation — it's the structural nature of what an optimizer is. A reward defined as raw PnL with no drawdown penalty will train a policy that takes on catastrophic tail exposure, because nothing in the reward said not to. A simulated environment with no transaction costs, no market impact, or any look-ahead will get "solved" beautifully — the optimizer did exactly what it was asked, and what it was asked for was wrong. This failure mode has a name: reward hacking, or specification gaming, and it is one of the single most-documented ways applied reinforcement learning quietly goes wrong.

State this every time, not just once

Reinforcement learning is a method for solving a decision problem you've already specified correctly. It cannot supply the missing edge, the missing risk penalty, or the missing realism in the environment it was handed — and "we trained a policy that maximizes reward in our simulator" is evidence the method works as an optimizer, never evidence the underlying strategy has real edge.

Checked against this repo's actual code

Position sizing across this repo's live bots (see pylego/sizing.py: risk% × balance ÷ (stop-distance × pip value), discounted by a decay score, clamped to a max lot) is a fixed formula — not a learned policy. There is no policy-gradient, actor-critic, or other RL-trained code anywhere in this codebase. Section 10

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Why does Q-learning become awkward once the action space is continuous?

The max over a small discrete set is a cheap comparison. The same max over an infinite continuum has no closed form and would need re-solving at every single step.
Question 2

In actor-critic, which role is which?

The actor is π_θ(a|s), updated by the policy gradient. The critic is a separately learned V(s) (or Q), whose feedback weights that update.
Question 3

Why does subtracting the critic's baseline V(s) reduce variance without introducing bias?

V(s) depends only on the state, not the action, so removing it removes signal common to every action in that state — exactly the part that's uninformative about which action to prefer.
Question 4

What is the concrete link between the TD error from the previous lesson and the advantage used here?

Same expression, two names: TD learning calls it "how wrong the value estimate was"; actor-critic uses the identical number as "how much better than expected this action was."
Question 5

A team reports "our RL agent maximizes reward perfectly in our simulator." What does that actually prove?

An optimizer optimizes exactly what it's given. A reward with no risk penalty, or a simulator with unrealistic fills, gets "solved" just as eagerly as a well-specified one — solving it proves nothing about real-world validity.
Section 11

What to carry forward

01

Q-learning's max over actions is cheap for a short discrete menu and intractable for a continuous action space — it becomes a nested optimization problem at every single step.

02

Policy gradient methods sidestep the max entirely by parameterizing the policy π_θ(a|s) directly and improving it with gradient ascent on expected return.

03

The whole theorem is one sentence: raise the probability of actions that beat expectation, lower the probability of ones that didn't.

04

Actor-critic pairs that policy (the actor) with a learned value function (the critic), using the critic's advantage estimate to sharply cut the update's variance.

05

The previous lesson's TD error and this lesson's advantage are, to a first approximation, the same number — two lessons converging on one quantity.

06

None of this is a trading edge. RL faithfully optimizes whatever reward and environment it's handed — a misspecified reward or an unrealistic simulator gets "solved" beautifully while still failing live.

What this now lets you do

Read "continuous action space" in any RL discussion and know immediately why it rules out plain Q-learning and points toward a policy gradient method instead. Recognise an actor-critic architecture the moment you see one — a policy network and a value network, trading a gradient signal — and connect its advantage term straight back to last lesson's TD error. And hold the line, every time this machinery gets proposed for a real strategy, that a well-optimized reward is not the same claim as a well-specified one.
