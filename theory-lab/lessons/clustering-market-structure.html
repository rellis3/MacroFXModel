Clustering for Market Structure: k-Means & Hierarchical Clustering

Two ways of asking the same question with no labeled answer key: which of these things behave alike? k-means picks a number of groups and iterates toward them; hierarchical clustering builds a full tree of every possible grouping at once. Both are honest tools for finding structure — and both are unsupervised, which means neither one can tell you on its own whether what it found is real.

CONCEPT — not built or tested anywhere in this repo ~17 min read Prereqs: Hierarchical Risk Parity, Correlation & Linear Regression primer
TL;DR
  • k-means (MacQueen, 1967; standard practical algorithm from Lloyd, 1957/1982) repeats a two-step loop — assign every point to its nearest centroid, then move each centroid to the mean of its assigned points — until assignments stop changing.
  • Its honest limitation: you must choose \(k\), the number of clusters, before running it. The elbow method and the silhouette score are the two standard heuristics for picking \(k\), and neither gives a provably correct answer — both are read-the-chart-and-judge tools, not calculators.
  • Hierarchical (agglomerative) clustering builds a full dendrogram instead: start with every point as its own cluster, repeatedly merge the two closest clusters by some linkage rule (single, complete, or average), and record every merge — no \(k\) needs to be chosen upfront, only where to cut the finished tree.
  • This Theory Lab's own Hierarchical Risk Parity lesson already uses exactly this dendrogram-building technique — for one specific purpose, allocating portfolio weight down the tree. This lesson is the general-purpose version of the same tool: the same dendrogram machinery applies just as directly to regime discovery, grouping similar instruments by behavior, or any other "what clusters with what" question that has nothing to do with position sizing.
  • The mandatory caveat: clustering is unsupervised — there is no ground-truth label to check a clustering's answer against. A clean-looking split into groups is not, by itself, evidence that the groups mean anything; every clustering result has to be sanity-checked against real domain knowledge, never trusted just because an algorithm produced it.
Section 01

Why this matters

Every regression, classifier, or diagnostic test covered elsewhere in this Theory Lab is supervised: it has a known target — a return to predict, a regime label to classify, a residual whose "correct" behavior (patternless noise) is well defined — and it can be scored against that target. Clustering is a different kind of question entirely. It starts from a set of things (instruments, trading days, factor exposures) with no labels attached at all, and asks only: which of these look alike, and which look different? There is no "right answer" file to check the output against, because the entire point is to discover a structure nobody labeled in advance.

That makes clustering genuinely useful for a specific kind of question that supervised methods can't answer — "are there natural groups here I haven't already hypothesized?" — and it also makes clustering genuinely easy to misuse, because an algorithm will confidently produce groups from literally any input, including pure noise, with no built-in signal that says "these groups don't actually mean anything." Both honest facts belong in the same sentence: clustering is a real, useful discovery tool, and its output is never self-validating.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Sorting a box of mixed buttons with no picture on the box

Imagine a box of buttons dumped on a table, no instructions, no "correct" sorting provided anywhere. You could decide upfront "I want exactly 4 piles" and start grouping buttons into 4 piles by whatever makes them look similar — moving a button between piles whenever it's clearly closer to a different pile's "typical" button — and keep adjusting until the piles stop changing. That's the k-means idea: decide the number of groups first, then iterate toward a sensible split.

Or you could work differently: find the two most similar buttons on the whole table and physically push them together into a pair. Then find the next two most similar things — could be two more individual buttons, or a button and the pair you just made — and merge those. Keep going, always merging whatever's currently most alike, until everything on the table is one giant pile, and write down the order you merged things in. That record of merges is a full family tree of the buttons' similarity, from the most obvious pairs all the way up to "everything, eventually." That's hierarchical clustering — you never had to decide the number of piles upfront, because the whole tree is built regardless, and you choose how many piles you want only at the very end, by picking where to cut the tree.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

k-means is like being handed exactly 4 empty boxes and told "sort your toys into these 4 boxes so similar toys end up together" — you have to decide on 4 boxes before you even start. Hierarchical clustering is different: you just keep taping the two most similar toys together, over and over, until it's all one giant taped-together pile, and you can look at the tape lines afterward to decide where you'd want to cut it into however many groups you actually want.

Section 02

The math, step by step

k-means — the assign/update loop

k-means, introduced by MacQueen (1967) and standardly implemented via the iterative algorithm described by Stuart Lloyd (developed in 1957, published in 1982) — "Lloyd's algorithm" is the version almost every practical k-means implementation actually runs — has one job: given a dataset and a chosen number of clusters \(k\), find \(k\) centroids and an assignment of every point to one centroid that minimizes the total squared distance from each point to its own centroid:

\[ \min_{\{\mu_1,\dots,\mu_k\}} \ \sum_{i=1}^{k} \sum_{x \in C_i} \|x - \mu_i\|^2 \]
μᵢthe centroid (mean position) of cluster i
Cᵢthe set of points currently assigned to cluster i
‖x−μᵢ‖²squared Euclidean distance from a point to its cluster's centroid — the "within-cluster sum of squares" (WCSS), a.k.a. inertia

Solving that minimization exactly is computationally hard (it's a combinatorial problem — every possible way of partitioning \(n\) points into \(k\) groups), so in practice Lloyd's algorithm finds a good approximate solution with a simple, repeated two-step loop:

1

Initialize

Pick \(k\) starting centroids — commonly \(k\) random data points, or a smarter spread-out initialization like k-means++.

2

Assign

Assign every point to whichever centroid it is currently closest to (by Euclidean distance).

3

Update

Recompute each centroid as the mean position of all points currently assigned to it.

4

Repeat

Alternate steps 2 and 3 until no point changes its assignment — at that point the algorithm has converged.

Each of the assign and update steps can only ever decrease (or leave unchanged) the total WCSS from the previous step — assigning to the nearest centroid is the best possible assignment given the current centroids, and the mean is the position that minimizes squared distance to a fixed set of points — so the loop is guaranteed to converge. What it is not guaranteed to do is converge to the single best possible clustering: it can settle into a local optimum that depends on where the centroids started, which is why practical implementations run the whole loop several times from different random initializations and keep the result with the lowest final WCSS.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Drop k flags randomly on a map. Every town joins whichever flag is closest to it. Then move each flag to sit at the average location of the towns that joined it. Repeat — towns keep re-joining whichever flag is now closest, flags keep re-centering on their towns — until nobody wants to switch flags anymore. That settled-down map is the answer k-means gives you.

Choosing k — the elbow method and silhouette score, honestly

k-means needs \(k\) supplied before it starts, and the algorithm itself has no way to tell you the "right" number — a larger \(k\) can always reduce WCSS further (in the extreme, \(k=n\), one cluster per point, drives WCSS to exactly zero), so minimizing WCSS alone would always push toward the largest \(k\) available, which defeats the purpose. Two standard heuristics try to find a defensible \(k\) instead, and it's worth being direct that both are judgment calls dressed up as a procedure, not exact answers:

Elbow method

Plot WCSS against \(k\) for a range of candidate values. WCSS always decreases as \(k\) grows, but often with sharply diminishing returns after some point — the "elbow" where the curve visibly bends. Pick the \(k\) at the bend. The honest problem: real data frequently produces a smooth curve with no obvious bend at all, and different people looking at the same chart can reasonably disagree on exactly where the elbow is.

Silhouette score

For each point, compare its average distance to points in its own cluster against its average distance to points in the nearest other cluster, producing a score from −1 (poorly matched) to +1 (well matched); average across all points for a given \(k\), and pick the \(k\) that maximizes it (Rousseeuw, 1987). The honest problem: it can disagree with the elbow method, and it can be maximized at a small \(k\) (even \(k=2\)) that is technically the "cleanest" split by this metric while being useless for the actual question being asked.

Neither method is a calculator for the correct k. Both are heuristics for narrowing down a judgment call, and both can point to different answers on the same data. The defensible practice is to look at both, plus the actual clusters they produce, plus whatever domain knowledge exists about how many groups should plausibly be there — not to treat either chart's suggested \(k\) as a proven, final number.

Hierarchical (agglomerative) clustering — build the whole tree

Hierarchical clustering sidesteps the "choose \(k\) upfront" problem entirely by building every possible level of grouping at once, from the bottom up:

1

Start with every point as its own cluster

\(n\) points, \(n\) singleton clusters.

2

Merge the two closest clusters

"Closest" is defined by a chosen linkage rule: single linkage (the distance between the closest pair of points, one from each cluster), complete linkage (the distance between the farthest pair), or average linkage (the mean distance across every cross-cluster pair). Record the distance at which the merge happened.

3

Repeat until one cluster remains

Every merge is recorded, building a dendrogram — a tree whose leaves are the original points and whose internal nodes are merges, each labeled with the distance at which it happened.

4

Choose k only at the end, by cutting the tree

Draw a horizontal line across the dendrogram at any chosen height; the number of vertical branches it crosses is the resulting number of clusters at that level of granularity. Different cut heights give different \(k\) — all from the exact same tree, computed once.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Single linkage asks "what's the closest that any member of group A gets to any member of group B?" — even one close pair is enough to merge. Complete linkage asks the opposite: "what's the farthest apart any member of A and any member of B are?" — it only merges groups if even their most different members aren't too far apart, which tends to build tighter, more compact groups. Average linkage just takes the average of every possible A-to-B pairing — a middle ground between the other two.

This is the exact same technique the Hierarchical Risk Parity lesson in this Theory Lab already builds, step by step — correlation turned into distance, then agglomerative clustering into a dendrogram. That lesson uses the resulting tree for one very specific downstream purpose: recursively splitting portfolio weight down the branches. The clustering machinery itself has nothing to do with portfolios — the same dendrogram-building procedure applies identically whenever the question is "which of these things behave alike," whether the "things" are assets being allocated weight, trading days being grouped into regimes, or instruments being grouped purely to understand market structure with no allocation decision attached at all.
\(k\)The number of clusters — must be chosen before running k-means; only chosen after the fact (as a tree cut height) in hierarchical clustering.
\(\mu_i\)The centroid (mean position) of cluster \(i\) in k-means.
\(C_i\)The set of points currently assigned to cluster \(i\).
WCSS / inertiaWithin-cluster sum of squares — total squared distance from every point to its own cluster's centroid; what k-means minimizes.
Silhouette scorePer-point measure (−1 to +1) of how much better-matched a point is to its own cluster than to the nearest other cluster; averaged to score a choice of \(k\).
DendrogramThe tree produced by hierarchical clustering — leaves are individual points, internal nodes are merges labeled by merge distance.

Worked example

k-means by hand, from a deliberately bad initialization

Six 2D points: \(A(1,1)\), \(B(1,2)\), \(C(2,2)\), \(D(6,6)\), \(E(7,7)\), \(F(6,8)\) — visually two obvious clusters, \(\{A,B,C\}\) and \(\{D,E,F\}\). Run \(k=2\) starting from a deliberately poor initialization — both starting centroids inside the same visual cluster — to see the assign/update loop correct for it.

1

Bad initialization, iteration 1

Start with \(\mu_1=A=(1,1)\), \(\mu_2=B=(1,2)\) — both centroids sitting inside the left-hand cluster. Assigning every point to its nearest centroid (by squared distance) gives cluster 1 = \(\{A\}\) only, and cluster 2 = \(\{B,C,D,E,F\}\) — a clearly wrong split, since \(D\), \(E\), and \(F\) are much closer to each other than to \(B\) or \(C\), but they still land in cluster 2 simply because both starting centroids happened to be on the same side.

2

Update, then reassign

Recompute centroids as the mean of each cluster's current members: \(\mu_1 = \text{mean}(A) = (1,1)\) (unchanged, only one point); \(\mu_2 = \text{mean}(B,C,D,E,F) = (4.4,\ 5.0)\) — pulled noticeably up and to the right by \(D\), \(E\), and \(F\). Reassigning every point to its now-nearest centroid: \(B\) and \(C\) are now closer to \(\mu_1=(1,1)\) than to the shifted \(\mu_2=(4.4,5.0)\), so they switch. New clusters: \(\{A,B,C\}\) and \(\{D,E,F\}\) — exactly the visually obvious split, reached purely by the centroid shifting toward the true cluster it was straddling.

3

Converge

Update centroids again: \(\mu_1 = \text{mean}(A,B,C) = (1.33,\ 1.67)\), \(\mu_2 = \text{mean}(D,E,F) = (6.33,\ 7.0)\). Reassigning every point against these new centroids changes nothing — every point is still closest to the same centroid it was just assigned to. Assignments have stopped changing, so the algorithm has converged, landing on the natural two-cluster split despite starting from centroids that were both on the same side of the data.

This particular bad start still self-corrected in two iterations — but that's not guaranteed. A worse or unluckier initialization can converge to a genuinely worse local optimum and stay there, which is exactly why practical k-means runs several random restarts and keeps the lowest-WCSS result rather than trusting any single run.

Real-world trading scenario

Discovering regime structure in a feature panel, without a label to check against

A researcher builds a daily feature vector for a market — realized volatility, trend strength, cross-asset correlation, credit spread momentum — with no pre-assigned "regime" label attached to any day at all. Running k-means with \(k=3\) on two years of these feature vectors produces three clusters that, on inspection, roughly line up with what a human would call "calm trending," "calm range-bound," and "stressed/high-correlation" days. That mapping feels satisfying and intuitive — but it is exactly the moment to remember that k-means would have produced three clusters of some kind from this data regardless of whether real regime structure exists in it or not, because producing \(k\) groups is what the algorithm always does when asked for \(k\) groups.

The honest next step is not to declare victory, but to check the finding against independent domain knowledge the clustering never saw: do the days actually assigned to the "stressed" cluster line up with dates the researcher already knows were volatile from other evidence (news flow, VIX levels, known market events)? Does the split hold up if the feature set is perturbed slightly, or if \(k=4\) is tried instead? A regime map that survives that kind of independent sanity check is a genuinely useful discovery. A regime map that's only ever been checked by looking at the same clustering that produced it is not evidence of anything — it's the algorithm confirming its own output.

The mandatory caveat, stated plainly: clustering is unsupervised. There is no ground-truth label anywhere in the process to check a clustering's answer against, unlike a classifier that can be scored against known outcomes. A clean, well-separated-looking cluster plot is not proof the clusters are meaningful — it only proves the algorithm found some grouping that reduced its objective function, which it will do on essentially any input, including data with no real structure at all. Every clustering result needs an independent sanity check against real domain knowledge before it's treated as a finding, not just an algorithm's output.
Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

Clustering as an exploratory first pass, not a final answer. The typical workflow is to run both k-means (across a small range of \(k\)) and hierarchical clustering on the same data, look at the elbow and silhouette diagnostics, look at the actual dendrogram, and use all of that together to form a hypothesis about structure — then go check that hypothesis against something the clustering algorithm never had access to: known event dates, an independently built classification, or a domain expert's judgment. Clustering generates candidate structure efficiently; it does not validate that structure.

Beyond portfolio construction — the general-purpose uses. The Hierarchical Risk Parity lesson in this Theory Lab is a real, worked example of hierarchical clustering solving one specific problem: turning a correlation matrix into a tree and allocating weight down it. The same tree-building machinery, entirely unchanged, is just as directly useful for questions that have nothing to do with allocating capital — grouping trading days into behavioral regimes (the scenario above), grouping a large universe of instruments by return-behavior similarity to build a smaller set of representative baskets, or identifying which of many candidate signals are effectively duplicates of each other before feeding them into a model. Recognizing "this is the same clustering problem, just applied to a different set of things" is the actual transferable skill — the algorithm doesn't know or care whether the points being clustered are assets, days, or signals.

Feature scaling matters as much here as it does for regularized regression. Both k-means and hierarchical clustering measure distance directly, and any feature with a larger raw numeric range will dominate that distance calculation regardless of whether it's actually more informative — the same standardization discipline this Theory Lab's LASSO & Ridge lesson requires for its penalty terms applies here for exactly the same reason.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where clustering goes wrong in practice

Treating a clustering's output as validated just because it looks clean. The single most important pitfall on this page, restated: a well-separated-looking plot of clusters is not evidence the clusters are real or meaningful on their own. Always check against independent domain knowledge before treating a clustering result as a finding.

k-means assumes roughly round, similarly-sized clusters. Because it minimizes squared Euclidean distance to a single centroid per cluster, k-means implicitly favors compact, blob-shaped groups of roughly comparable size — it will badly mis-cluster data with elongated shapes, very different cluster sizes, or clusters nested inside each other, even when a human would find the true structure obvious by eye.

Not standardizing features before clustering. As noted above, unscaled features distort every distance-based decision the algorithm makes, in either method — the same failure mode as skipping standardization before LASSO or Ridge.

Not running multiple random initializations for k-means. A single run can converge to a poor local optimum purely from an unlucky starting point — the worked example above happened to recover cleanly, but that outcome isn't guaranteed, and production use requires comparing several restarts (or a smarter initialization like k-means++) rather than trusting one run.

Forgetting that the linkage rule changes the dendrogram. As the Hierarchical Risk Parity lesson notes for its own use case, a different linkage choice (single, complete, average, Ward) can produce a meaningfully different tree from the identical distance matrix — "a" dendrogram is not "the" dendrogram, and the choice should be stated and justified, not left as an unexamined default.

Key Takeaways

✓k-means loops: assign to nearest centroid, then update each centroid to the mean of its assigned points. Guaranteed to converge, not guaranteed to reach the global optimum.
✓Hierarchical clustering builds the entire tree bottom-up by repeatedly merging the closest pair of clusters. No k needs to be chosen upfront — only where to cut the finished dendrogram.
✓This lesson generalizes the Hierarchical Risk Parity lesson's own technique. Same dendrogram machinery, applicable to regime discovery, instrument grouping, or signal deduplication — not only portfolio weight allocation.
⚠Neither the elbow method nor the silhouette score gives a provably correct k. Both are heuristics for a judgment call, and they can disagree with each other on the identical data.
⚠k-means assumes roughly round, similarly-sized clusters and needs standardized features and multiple restarts. None of these are optional in a serious application.

Section 05

Self-test

Q1. State the two-step loop that Lloyd's k-means algorithm repeats, and explain why it's guaranteed to converge.
The two steps are: (1) assign every point to the centroid it's currently closest to, and (2) update every centroid to the mean position of the points now assigned to it. It converges because each step can only decrease or leave unchanged the total within-cluster sum of squares — assigning to the nearest centroid is the best possible assignment given the current centroids, and the mean is provably the position that minimizes total squared distance to a fixed set of points, so the objective never gets worse and eventually stops improving, at which point assignments stop changing.
Q2. Why can't you just pick the k that minimizes within-cluster sum of squares (WCSS) directly, the way you'd pick a model by its lowest error?
WCSS is not comparable across different values of k the way an out-of-sample error is comparable across models — WCSS can only decrease (or stay the same) as k increases, because more clusters always give the algorithm more centroids to fit the data more tightly, and at the extreme k=n (one cluster per point) WCSS is exactly zero. Minimizing WCSS directly would therefore always push toward the largest k available, which produces a meaningless, maximally fragmented clustering rather than a useful one — this is why the elbow method looks at the shape of the WCSS-vs-k curve (where it stops improving much) rather than its raw minimum.
Q3. What's the difference between single, complete, and average linkage in hierarchical clustering?
All three define "distance between two clusters" (as opposed to distance between two points) differently. Single linkage uses the distance between the single closest pair of points, one from each cluster — even one close pair is enough to trigger a merge, which can produce long, straggly clusters. Complete linkage uses the distance between the single farthest pair — it only merges when even the most dissimilar members of the two groups aren't too far apart, tending to produce tighter, more compact clusters. Average linkage uses the mean distance across every possible cross-cluster pair, a middle ground between the other two. The same set of points can produce a meaningfully different dendrogram depending on which rule is used.
Q4. Why is validating a clustering result fundamentally different from validating a supervised classifier?
A supervised classifier can be scored directly against known, independently observed labels — you can compute an accuracy or an error rate because the "right answer" for each observation exists and is available to check against. Clustering has no such labels anywhere in the process; it only ever measures internal consistency (how tightly grouped points are relative to how far apart different groups are), and an algorithm will produce some grouping that improves that internal measure from essentially any input, including data with no real underlying structure. The only way to validate whether a clustering result means something is to check it against evidence the clustering process itself never had access to — independent domain knowledge, known event dates, or a separately built classification — not by looking at the clustering's own internal metrics again.
Q5. Give an example, other than portfolio weight allocation, of a legitimate use for the same hierarchical-clustering/dendrogram technique the Hierarchical Risk Parity lesson uses.
Any "group things by behavioral similarity" question works: grouping trading days into behavioral regimes from a feature panel (realized volatility, trend strength, cross-asset correlation), grouping a large universe of instruments into a smaller set of representative baskets by return-behavior similarity, or identifying near-duplicate candidate signals before feeding them into a model — all of these use the identical build-the-dendrogram-then-cut-it procedure, just applied to a different set of "things" and with no portfolio weight computed anywhere in the process.
Q6. Why can two runs of k-means on the identical dataset with the identical k produce different final clusters?
k-means only ever converges to a local optimum of its objective function, and which local optimum it lands on depends on where the centroids started. Because standard practice initializes centroids randomly (or via a randomized smarter scheme like k-means++), two runs can start from different initial positions, follow different assign/update paths, and settle into different final clusterings — which is exactly why production use compares several random restarts and keeps the one with the lowest final WCSS, rather than trusting the result of any single run.
