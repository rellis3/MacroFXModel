Conjugate Priors: Updating a Belief Without Ever Leaving Algebra

The Bayesian Inference lesson updated a single number — a 20% chance of a trend day became a 50% chance once a classifier fired. But most real beliefs aren't one number; they're a whole range of plausible values, like "the true trend-day rate is probably near 30%, but I'm not sure if it's 20% or 40%." Updating an entire distribution like that usually needs numerical machinery. Conjugate priors are the special, exactly-solvable case — the one every textbook starts with, because you can watch a belief visibly narrow, update after update, using nothing but addition.

Section 1

From one number to a whole curve of belief

Bayesian Inference & Updating asked "how confident should I be in this one flag?" This lesson asks a harder question.

Instead of "there's a 30% chance today is a trend day" (one number), imagine saying "the true underlying rate of trend days is somewhere around 30%, but I'm genuinely unsure whether it's 20% or 40%." That fuzzy belief isn't a number — it's a shape: a curve that's higher near 30% and tapers off toward the edges. Updating that shape as real data arrives, one observation at a time, is what the rest of applied Bayesian statistics is built on.

In general, combining an arbitrary belief-shape with an arbitrary likelihood produces a posterior with no tidy formula — recovering it needs numerical integration or simulation. Conjugate priors are the handful of special cases where the prior and likelihood are matched so the posterior lands in exactly the same distribution family as the prior, and only its parameters change. That turns the update into an algebra problem instead of a numerical-methods problem.

Direct sequel

This is Bayesian Extensions 1, and it assumes the prior/likelihood/posterior vocabulary from the earlier Bayesian Inference & Updating lesson. If "prior," "likelihood," and "posterior" don't already feel concrete, that lesson is the one to read first.

Section 2

"Conjugate" has one precise meaning

A prior is conjugate to a likelihood when the posterior comes out of the same family as the prior — only the numbers describing it change.

The general case
  • Prior × likelihood can land almost anywhere — no guaranteed named family
  • Getting a mean, a variance, or a plot out of it needs numerical integration or simulation
  • This is the normal, unavoidable situation for most real-world models
  • It's exactly what the next lesson in this series — MCMC — exists to handle
The conjugate case
  • Prior family and posterior family are the same, by construction
  • Updating means recomputing a couple of parameters — pure arithmetic
  • Only exists for a short, specific list of matched pairs
  • Beta + Binomial and Normal + Normal are the two covered here
Why every course starts here

Conjugacy is a mathematical convenience, not a law of nature — most real-world likelihoods have no conjugate prior at all. It's taught first purely because it's the version you can compute by hand and actually watch narrow, before reaching for the simulation-based methods that handle everything else.

Section 3

The Beta distribution: a shape for an unknown rate

θ is an unknown rate between 0 and 1 — say, the true long-run fraction of days that are trend days. Beta(α,β) is a flexible curve over exactly that range.

α

"Successes I've already imagined"

A pseudo-count of successes the prior assumes, before any real data arrives.

β

"Failures I've already imagined"

A matching pseudo-count of failures — together, α and β set the prior's shape and its confidence.

α+β

The prior's weight

How many pseudo-observations the prior is worth. Beta(2,3) is worth 5 — a soft, easily-moved opinion.

α/(α+β)

The prior's mean

Beta(2,3) has mean 0.40 — "I lean toward thinking trend days are a bit under half of all days."

Beta(2,3)
mean
2/5 = 0.40
variance
0.04 (sd 0.20)

Think of α and β as two jars of pretend tokens, filled in before any real data shows up. Every real success or failure observed drops one more matching token into the right jar — the new totals are the new belief. No shape gets redrawn from scratch.

Section 4

Watch the posterior fall out of two kernels multiplied

Bayes' theorem says posterior ∝ prior × likelihood. Drop the parts that don't involve θ and watch what's left.

Try it — step through the algebra
Beta(α,β) prior
kernel
θ^(α−1)(1−θ)^(β−1)
The normalizing constant B(α,β) doesn't involve θ at all — it can be dropped and restored at the very end.
Binomial(n,θ) likelihood
kernel
θ^s(1−θ)^(n−s)
s successes, n−s failures observed. The combinatorial count C(n,s) also doesn't involve θ — drop it too.
θ^(α−1)(1−θ)^(β−1)
×
θ^s(1−θ)^(n−s)
Multiplying same-base powers just adds exponents: (α−1)+s on θ, and (β−1)+(n−s) on (1−θ).
θ^(α+s−1)(1−θ)^(β+n−s−1)
is exactly
Beta(α+s, β+n−s)
That last expression is a Beta kernel — since a Beta distribution is fully determined by its kernel, the posterior must be Beta(α+s, β+n−s). No integration was ever needed.
This is the entire proof. The prior family (Beta) and the posterior family (also Beta) came out identical because the exponents just added — that's what "conjugate" means, made concrete.

Add all counts to the original prior — Beta(2+12, 3+28) = Beta(14,31), same answer whether the 40 days arrive in one batch or two. sd: 0.20 → 0.118 → 0.068 — it more than halved, then nearly halved again.
The mean drifts toward the data (0.40 → 0.311, close to the true 0.30) while the spread collapses — a confidence statement with no separate "confidence" calculation bolted on. The width of the curve is the confidence statement.

Section 8

Normal-Normal: the same trick for a continuous mean

Instead of an unknown rate, suppose the unknown is a mean μ — say, a strategy's true average daily return — with a known variance σ² for each observation.

τ

Precision = 1/variance

Working in precision instead of variance matters because precisions from independent sources simply add — variance combines through a much less intuitive reciprocal-sum.

τ₀+τd

Posterior precision

Prior precision plus data precision. Information only ever accumulates — it never disappears.

μₙ

Precision-weighted mean

The posterior mean sits between the prior mean and the sample mean, weighted by how precise each source is — automatically, with nobody deciding the weight by hand.

μₙ = (τ₀μ₀ + τd·x̄) / (τ₀+τd)
A quick check: prior μ₀=0%, σ₀=0.5% (τ₀=4). Observe n=20 days, sample mean x̄=0.15%, known σ=1.0% (τd=20/1=20). μₙ = (4×0 + 20×0.15)/24 = 0.125% — pulled most of the way toward the data because its precision (20) dwarfs the prior's (4), but not all the way. Posterior variance = 1/24 ≈ 0.0417, so σₙ ≈ 0.204% — narrower than either source alone.

Section 10

Why closed form is worth having at all

⚡

One line of arithmetic

No simulation, no numerical integration — just add pseudo-counts, or add precisions. It runs as fast as the data arrives.

🔍

Every quantity is readable

The pseudo-counts, the precision — each intermediate number has a plain-English meaning, unlike an opaque simulation trace.

📐

Exact, not approximate

The posterior isn't estimated from samples — it's derived algebraically, so there's no sampling noise to worry about.

🚧

But narrow in scope

Conjugacy only exists for a handful of matched pairs. The moment a real likelihood doesn't algebraically match a standard prior family, it breaks.

That last row is the whole reason this is "Bayesian Extensions 1" and not the only lesson in the series. The instant a model needs a mixture of behaviors, a bounded-but-skewed variance, or any likelihood with no algebraic closed form, conjugacy is gone — which is exactly the gap that Markov Chain Monte Carlo, the next lesson in this trio, is built to fill: computing a posterior numerically when no formula for it exists.

Section 12

Where conjugate updating quietly goes wrong

Easy to get wrong
  • Treating consecutive days as independent when volatility clusters — this overstates how fast the posterior should narrow
  • Forgetting Normal-Normal needs σ² known — if it's estimated too, the real answer is a fatter-tailed Student-t, not a Normal
  • Picking α,β after peeking at the data — p-hacking wearing a Bayesian costume
What stays true
  • A "flat" Beta(1,1) prior still asserts every rate 0-100% was equally plausible — that's a real claim, not a neutral default
  • A narrow posterior quantifies confidence about the observed sample, not about whether the future looks the same
  • State the prior and its justification before looking at the evidence, every time
A tight posterior after 200 observations is not validation. It says "I'm confident about what happened in this sample" — it says nothing about whether that period was representative, or whether the process is even stationary. Conjugate updating narrows a belief about the past; it doesn't certify the future will match.
Section 13

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

What does it mean for a prior to be "conjugate" to a likelihood?

Conjugacy is a property of a specific prior/likelihood pairing — Beta with Binomial, Normal with Normal — not a general fact about all Bayesian updates.
Question 2

Prior is Beta(4,6). You observe 8 successes in 20 trials. What's the exact posterior?

Beta(4+8, 6+12) = Beta(12,18) — the pseudo-counts and the real counts just add.
Question 3

Why is precision (1/variance) easier to reason with than variance in Normal-Normal updating?

Posterior precision = prior precision + data precision — that additivity is exactly why the posterior mean is naturally a precision-weighted average.
Question 4

Why does a Beta(40,60) prior resist new data more than a Beta(2,3) prior?

The posterior mean is (α+s)/(α+β+n) — a real sample of n=20 barely moves a 100-pseudo-observation prior, but dominates a 5-pseudo-observation one.
Question 5

Why doesn't conjugate updating exist for every prior/likelihood pair?

Most real likelihoods — especially anything nonlinear — simply don't multiply into a tidy algebraic match with any standard prior family. That gap is exactly why simulation-based methods like MCMC exist.
Section 14

What to carry forward

01

Conjugate = same family in, same family out — Beta prior + Binomial likelihood → Beta posterior; Normal prior + Normal likelihood (known variance) → Normal posterior.

02

Beta-Binomial updating is just addition: posterior is Beta(α+successes, β+failures) — pseudo-counts and real counts combine directly.

03

Normal-Normal updating is a precision-weighted average: posterior precision = prior precision + data precision; the more confident source gets more say automatically.

04

A "flat" prior is still an assumption — Beta(1,1) asserts every rate is equally plausible beforehand, which is itself a real claim.

05

Conjugacy is the exception, not the rule — most real likelihoods have no matching conjugate prior, which is the entire motivation for MCMC, next.

06

A narrow posterior is not a validated edge — it quantifies confidence about the observed sample, not whether that sample generalizes forward.

What this now lets you do

Track a rate or a mean as small batches of real evidence arrive, in exact closed form, and watch the belief's spread shrink update by update with no separate confidence calculation bolted on. Recognise when a "confident-sounding" narrow posterior is only confident about the past. And know exactly where this machinery stops working — the moment a real likelihood doesn't match a standard prior family — which is precisely where the next lesson picks up.
