Corporate Actions & Series Continuity

A stock split, a dividend, or — closest to home for an FX/macro/futures desk — a futures contract roll all put a fake, non-tradeable jump into a raw price series, without a single bad tick anywhere in it. The standard fix removes that jump. This lesson is about the part almost nobody mentions until it bites them: the fix that saves your return calculation can quietly break every other calculation built on the actual, historically-quoted price level.

Section 1

A jump that never actually happened

The previous lesson was about catching prices that are simply wrong — bad ticks, stale feeds, impossible values. This one is stranger: every price below is perfectly accurate, correctly timestamped, and free of any error at all — and the series is still unusable as-is.

✂️

A stock split

A 2-for-1 split turns one $100 share into two $50 shares. Nothing about the company changed — the raw chart still shows a 50% overnight "loss."

💵

A dividend payment

The price legitimately drops by roughly the dividend on the ex-date — but a shareholder received that value in cash, they didn't lose it.

🔄

A futures roll

The expiring contract and the next-nearest one almost never close at the same price. Splicing them raw reproduces the exact same fake-jump problem.

All three have the same shape: the raw data isn't lying about any single print, but stitching those accurate prints into one continuous series manufactures a jump that no position anyone held ever actually experienced.
Section 2

The roll gap is real — it just isn't a return

A futures contract expires. A continuous history has to be built by splicing the expiring contract onto the next one that becomes the tradeable front month.

Worked example — a crude oil roll
Expiring contract
$78.40
roll →
New front month
$78.90
That $0.50 gap is cost-of-carry and curve shape — a real, expected feature of futures pricing, not an error. A trader who rolled a continuous position captured roughly that spread; they did not experience a discontinuous $0.50 gain overnight.

Splice the two contracts' raw prices directly at the roll date, though, and the series shows exactly that: a $0.50 jump that looks, to any naive return calculation, like a real one-day move. Multiply this by every quarterly roll across a multi-year history and the fake jumps stack up fast.

Why this isn't a Data Cleaning problem

Nothing here is an outlier or a bad tick — both $78.40 and $78.90 are genuine, correctly-recorded closing prices. The problem is structural: two different instruments were glued together at the seam, not a single instrument misreported.

Section 4

Two named fixes, two different tradeoffs

Both remove the jump at the roll date. They preserve different things about the history.

Back-adjustment — "Panama method"

Shift every price before the roll by a constant additive offset (the $ gap at the roll). Absolute price differences stay exactly correct — a $2 move is still a $2 move — no matter how far back you look.

Ratio-adjustment

Scale every price before the roll by a constant multiplicative ratio instead. Percentage returns stay exactly correct across the roll — a 2% move is still a 2% move — at the cost of shifting absolute levels multiplicatively.

Neither is "more correct." Back-adjustment protects dollar/point P&L math; ratio-adjustment protects percentage-return math. Pick based on what the downstream calculation actually needs — not once, as a single infrastructure decision, but per use case.

Section 7

Fixing continuity can break the price level

The key warning

Back-adjustment fixes returns by design — that's the whole point. But anything downstream that needs the actual, historical price level to mean something can silently break: a percentage return divides by that level, a support/resistance line is drawn on it, a price-level-based signal is triggered by it. A back-adjusted series can hand all three a number that no trade ever actually happened at.

%

Percentage returns

A % return divides by the price level. Divide by a level that's been shifted (or gone negative), and the "return" is meaningless, even though the point-to-point $ difference is still exactly correct.

📏

Support / resistance

A level drawn on a back-adjusted chart may not correspond to any price that was ever actually quoted on that historical date — the chart is real, the level on it isn't.

🚩

Any price-level trigger

A rule that fires "if price crosses X" needs X and the series it's compared against to live in the same, real price space — back-adjustment can quietly break that match far enough back.

Section 8

Practitioners keep both series, on purpose

Serious futures desks don't pick one adjustment method once — they maintain both, and choose per calculation.

Use back-adjusted (Panama)
  • Absolute dollar / point P&L
  • Position-sizing math
  • Anything comparing price differences
Use ratio-adjusted (or raw, per contract)
  • Long-horizon percentage-return statistics
  • Correlation across very different price eras
  • Anything dividing by a historical level
A trend-following backtest built on a naively-spliced, unadjusted series can show a suspiciously reliable "breakout" clustering right on every quarterly roll date — the strategy isn't detecting momentum, it's detecting its own data pipeline's roll jump. Re-run it on a properly adjusted series and the pattern vanishes.

Section 10

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Why does a raw, unadjusted futures series show a jump at every roll date, even though nothing was ever a "bad tick"?

Both prices are genuine. The problem is structural — gluing two instruments together at the seam, not a data error in either one.
Question 2

What does back-adjustment (the "Panama method") preserve, and what does ratio-adjustment preserve instead?

Additive offsets keep $ differences exact; multiplicative ratios keep % returns exact. That's the entire tradeoff between the two methods.
Question 3

Why can a long back-adjusted futures history eventually show a negative price?

The offsets are genuine and small individually, but they stack. A negative back-adjusted price is an adjustment artifact, not a real historical quote.
Question 4

Pre-roll price $110.00, post-roll price $108.50. What's the back-adjusted value of an older raw price of $95.00?

Offset = 108.50 − 110.00 = −1.50. Adjusted = 95.00 + (−1.50) = $93.50.
Question 5

A researcher needs 15 years of percentage-return correlation across two commodity futures, each with many rolls. Which series should they use?

Percentage-return statistics need the level-preserving-as-ratio property, which is exactly what ratio-adjustment protects and Panama-style back-adjustment does not.
Section 11

What to carry forward

01

A raw series can be 100% accurate at every print and still be unusable — splits, dividends, and futures rolls all manufacture a jump that isn't a real, tradeable return.

02

Splits/dividends are fixed by a cumulative back-adjustment factor, restating historical prices in consistent terms.

03

Futures rolls have two named fixes — back-adjustment ("Panama," additive, preserves $ differences) and ratio-adjustment (multiplicative, preserves % returns).

04

Neither fix is free — a back-adjusted series can drift to unusual or negative levels far in the past, which breaks any calculation that needs a real price level, not just a real price difference.

05

Choose the method per calculation, not once as an infrastructure default: dollar P&L wants back-adjustment, percentage-return statistics want ratio-adjustment.

06

This repo currently sidesteps the whole problem for its gold pair by trading spot instead of an unadjusted continuous future — a legitimate third option, not just theory.

What this now lets you do

Recognise a fake roll or corporate-action jump the moment you see one in a raw series, instead of mistaking it for a real return. Pick the right adjustment method — back-adjusted or ratio-adjusted — for whatever calculation you're actually running. And before trusting any price-level-based signal (support/resistance, a percentage return, a level trigger) on adjusted data, check whether that level still corresponds to something that was ever actually quoted.
