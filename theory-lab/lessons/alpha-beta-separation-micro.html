Alpha vs. Beta, Split Apart Honestly

Every return stream is secretly two things added together: a bet on the market you could have bought directly and cheaply (beta), and whatever is left over once that bet is honestly subtracted out (alpha). This deck builds the regression that separates the two, the technique — portable alpha — for hedging one away to isolate the other, and the ways that separation quietly goes wrong.

Section 1

The rising-tide test

"A rising tide lifts all boats." If the tide rises 10% and your boat rises 12%, was that your rowing — or a slightly leakier boat sitting a bit higher on the same tide?

Beta is the tide's contribution — measurable, and available to literally anyone who buys the tide directly with an index future or a cheap ETF. Alpha is whatever's left after you honestly subtract the tide's effect: the actual rowing. Most of what looks like skill in a rising market is beta wearing a costume.
📈

18% in a 16% year isn't automatically skill

A long-only strategy that beat the market by 2 points may have simply held a slightly more aggressive version of "the market" — and the market went up.

📉

A loss isn't automatically failure

A macro book that lost money in a risk-off quarter may just have carried the same directional exposure every other risk asset was carrying that quarter.

🎯

The one-line test

If you could have bought this return stream directly and cheaply with an index future, it's beta, not skill. Alpha is only what survives after that's subtracted out.

Section 2

One regression, two components

Regress a strategy's excess returns (return minus the risk-free rate) on the market's excess returns. The slope is beta; the intercept is alpha.

The market-model regression

This is the same linear-regression machinery as the Correlation & Linear Regression primer, just applied to excess returns — both sides measure compensation for risk taken above the safe rate, not raw return.
β

Beta — the slope

Literally Cov(rᵢ, rₘ) / Var(rₘ) — how many units of market risk one unit of the strategy carries. Cheap to buy directly with an index future or ETF; no skill required.

α

Alpha — the intercept

The average return left over once β × the market's move has been subtracted out, period by period. This is "Jensen's alpha" — what an allocator is actually paying a manager for.

ε

Epsilon — residual noise

Whatever the market factor alone doesn't explain, period to period. Not skill, not exposure — just noise that averages toward zero over enough periods.

Section 6

Portable alpha: sever the alpha from its beta

Once β is known, it can be cancelled out with a short position — leaving (in expectation) just the alpha stream.

Before hedging
  • Full β = 1.2 exposure rides every market swing, up and down
  • The ≈2% alpha is buried inside a much bigger, much noisier number
  • An allocator can't tell "skill" from "leverage on the market" just by looking at the total return
After hedging — beta neutralized
  • Short 1.2 units of market notional (an index future) per unit of the strategy
  • The two β(rm−rf) terms cancel; ≈2% alpha (before hedging costs) is what's left
  • That isolated stream can now sit under any beta exposure the allocator actually wants — or none at all
This is why it's called "portable": once alpha is hedged market-neutral, it's no longer glued to the specific market it was measured against. An allocator can keep this manager's stock-picking alpha while separately holding bond-market beta instead of equity beta — without accepting the manager's equity exposure as a package deal.

Observed return was 20% — the extra 0.8 points is residual noise (ε) for that specific year, not extra alpha. The manager's genuine, hard-won contribution was the 2%, not the 20%.

13.2 of those 20 points came purely from carrying 1.2× the market's own excess return — exposure anyone could buy directly and cheaply with a futures contract. That's the number a fee should never be paid for.

What the full lesson says: alpha-beta separation is a pure concept here, not yet computed for any strategy.

What the code actually does now: the beta side is live. bot/modules/beta_estimator.py runs a rolling-window OLS regression (identical in form to the α/β regression on this slide's earlier equation) against three factor proxies every 120 seconds, then smooths it with a Kalman filter — the exact "adaptive beta" fix the full lesson describes only as a hypothetical. bot/modules/portfolio_beta.py aggregates that beta across every open position, and bot/modules/beta_deviation.py compares the book's net exposure against regime-conditional target betas (BULL/BEAR/RANGE/CHOP), wired into bot/main.py.

What's still conceptual: the regression's alpha term is computed internally (see _ols_beta()) but immediately discarded — never persisted, scored, or reported anywhere. No code in this repo isolates a hedged, market-neutral alpha stream or executes a portable-alpha overlay. So: beta estimation and monitoring is real and running; alpha isolation and portable alpha remain exactly what the full lesson calls them — a concept.

Section 9

Where this goes wrong

⚠

A noisy intercept isn't proven skill

A positive α̂ with a wide standard error — short sample, high residual volatility — can be statistically indistinguishable from zero. Screening many strategies to find one "with alpha" makes this worse, not better.

↕

Beta drifts

The β̂ estimated over the last two years may not be today's true exposure — markets and strategies both change regime. A hedge sized to a stale β under- or over-hedges the real current exposure.

🕳

Survivorship & backtest inflation

"Alpha" measured only on the data a strategy was built on is the same in-sample-vs-out-of-sample trap that shows up everywhere else in this curriculum.

💸

Hedging isn't free

Shorting the beta leg carries real financing/borrow costs and slippage. The deliverable "pure alpha" is the theoretical intercept minus those costs — always a little less than the regression printed.

Section 10

How allocators actually use this

Institutional allocators run exactly this regression on every manager in a portfolio before deciding how much to pay for what. A manager whose "outperformance" is mostly beta in disguise is, in effect, an expensive way to buy an index. Portable-alpha overlays let an allocator hold, say, bond-market beta while still capturing an equity manager's genuine stock-picking alpha — instead of being forced to accept the manager's equity beta as a package deal.
Where this needs qualifying

Everything here uses a single market factor. Fama & French's multi-factor extensions (size, value, and later factors) generalize the exact same regression logic to several factors at once — necessary once one market factor can't explain a strategy's exposure on its own. Not covered in depth here.

Section 11

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

In ri − rf = α + β(rm−rf) + ε, what does β represent?

β is the regression slope: Cov(rᵢ,rₘ)/Var(rₘ), how many units of market risk one unit of the strategy carries. The intercept, α, is the skill component.
Question 2

A strategy returns 20% when the market returns 15%, rf=4%, and β=1.2. How much of that 20% is beta-driven, explained purely by market exposure?

β × (r_m − r_f) = 1.2 × (15% − 4%) = 13.2%. That's exposure anyone could buy directly and cheaply — not skill.
Question 3

To hedge a strategy's β=1.2 exposure to zero, what should you do?

Shorting β units of the market makes the two β(r_m−r_f) terms cancel, leaving (in expectation) just α plus noise — a market-neutral version of the same strategy.
Question 4

A positive α̂ from a regression is automatically real, deliverable skill.

A positive α̂ estimated over a short, noisy sample can be statistically indistinguishable from zero. Screening many strategies to find "the one with alpha" makes this worse, not better.
Question 5

What makes portable alpha "portable"?

Hedging β to zero severs the alpha stream from the specific market it was measured against — it can then sit on top of a completely different beta exposure, or none at all.
Section 12

What to carry forward

01

Every return stream is beta plus alpha — regress excess returns on the market's excess returns to split the two apart: ri−rf = α + β(rm−rf) + ε.

02

Beta is cheap — it's exposure you could buy directly with an index future or ETF, no skill required. Alpha is the residual left after that's subtracted, and the part actually worth a fee.

03

Beta is hedgeable — short the market sized to the estimated β and the β terms cancel, leaving (in expectation) just α plus noise: a market-neutral version of the same strategy.

04

"Portable" alpha means alpha severed from any one beta — once hedged market-neutral, it can sit on top of any beta exposure you actually want, or none at all.

05

This repo's beta estimation is live (rolling OLS + Kalman filter, monitored against regime targets) — but alpha isolation and portable-alpha hedging remain conceptual; the regression's alpha term is computed and thrown away, not acted on.

06

It's a repackaging technique, not a source of edge — noisy intercepts, drifting betas, survivorship, and hedging costs can all quietly erase the "alpha" it claims to isolate.

What this now lets you do

Look at any return stream and honestly ask how much of it is beta in disguise before crediting it as skill. Size and price a hedge that isolates a genuine alpha stream from its market exposure. Recognize why an allocator would rather pay for 2% of hedged alpha than 20% of unhedged beta — and know exactly which pitfalls (noisy intercepts, drifting betas, survivorship, hedging costs) can make that 2% smaller, or not real, by the time it's delivered.
