Theta Decay

Theta isn't a separate fact about options — it falls straight out of the same Black-Scholes PDE that gives delta and gamma, and once you rearrange it, "theta pays for gamma" stops being a slogan and becomes an equation.

CONCEPT — not built or tested in this repo ~19 min read Prereqs: Black-Scholes-Merton Option Pricing, Gamma Exposure & Dealer Hedging Flows
TL;DR
  • Theta is not an independent Greek to memorize — rearranging the Black-Scholes PDE gives \(\Theta = rC - rS\Delta - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2S^2\Gamma\) directly, the same equation that produces delta and gamma.
  • The closed-form theta for a non-dividend European call is \(\Theta = -\dfrac{S\varphi(d_1)\sigma}{2\sqrt{\tau}} - rKe^{-r\tau}N(d_2)\) — state it, don't re-derive the integral.
  • The key insight: for a delta-hedged position, \(\Theta \approx -\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2S^2\Gamma\) — theta and gamma are two views of the same convexity. This is the formal version of "theta pays for gamma": if realized volatility matches implied, the gamma-scalping P&L and the theta bleed offset almost exactly on average.
  • Theta accelerates near expiry the same way gamma does, through the same \(1/\sqrt{\tau}\) term — a worked comparison at 30, 7 and 1 day to expiry shows the daily decay roughly doubling, then accelerating again, matching the acceleration already shown for gamma in the previous lesson.
  • Calendar spreads are built specifically to harvest that acceleration differential between a near and a far expiry; weekend theta is a related, non-universal practitioner convention worth knowing about, not treating as law.
  • New in this lesson: combining \(\Theta\approx-\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2S^2\Gamma\) with the previous lesson's "dealers are typically net short gamma" gives, term by term, "dealers are typically net long theta" — the same PDE, two different partial derivatives, not two separate facts.
Section 01

Why this matters

The Black-Scholes-Merton lesson derives delta and gamma as the option-pricing formula's first and second partial derivatives with respect to the underlying, and states the Black-Scholes PDE itself. It mentions theta only in passing — "time decay" — without showing where the closed-form theta comes from or why it matters beyond "options lose value as expiry approaches." The Gamma Exposure & Dealer Hedging Flows lesson goes deep on gamma specifically: how it aggregates across an open-interest book into dealer hedging flow, and why that flow intensifies as \(1/\sqrt{\tau}\) near expiry. Neither lesson asks the natural next question: if gamma is the cost of convexity a dealer is exposed to, what is theta, and how do the two connect?

This lesson answers that directly. Theta is not a fact bolted onto the model after the fact — it is a term in the exact same PDE that gives delta and gamma, and rearranging that PDE turns "options decay over time" into a precise statement about how much and why. The practical payoff is the gamma-theta identity: a delta-hedged option position's theta bleed is, to a very good approximation, just its gamma times a constant — which is the entire economic logic behind why market-makers get paid to sell convexity, why "theta pays for gamma" is said the way it is, and — the genuinely new material in this lesson — why the previous lesson's "dealers are usually short gamma" and "dealers are usually long theta" are not two separate empirical observations. They are the same underlying fact, read off two different partial derivatives of one equation. This lesson does not re-derive delta or gamma from scratch; for those, see the Black-Scholes lesson linked above.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Rent, not a random tax

An option is a wasting asset: hold everything else fixed, and it loses a little value every single day purely because there's one fewer day left for the underlying to move in your favor. That's theta. It's tempting to treat it as a kind of tax you pay for holding the position, disconnected from everything else about the option — but that's not quite right. Theta is better understood as rent: the price you pay for the right to a position that gains disproportionately from big moves in either direction (that's gamma — convexity). Nobody rents you that upside for free. The option seller is, in effect, charging you the fair market rent for convexity, and that rent is exactly what theta measures.

Flip it around and the whole "dealer hedging" story from the previous lesson reads differently too. A dealer who wrote (sold) you that option gave up the convexity — they're short gamma — and in exchange they collect the rent — they're long theta. It isn't a coincidence that "short gamma" and "long theta" tend to travel together for the same position; by the end of this lesson that pairing will be a literal equation, not just an observed pattern.

Section 02

The math, step by step

Three things build on top of the Black-Scholes PDE: the rearrangement that isolates theta, the closed-form solution for a European call, and the delta-hedged approximation that connects theta back to gamma. Take them in order, then look at why theta accelerates near expiry the same way gamma does.

1. Theta falls out of the PDE — it isn't a separate fact

The Black-Scholes lesson states the no-arbitrage PDE that any correctly priced option's value \(V(S,t)\) must satisfy at every instant, where \(t\) is calendar time:

\[ \frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + rS\frac{\partial V}{\partial S} + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} - rV = 0 \]

Each term is already named after a Greek in the Black-Scholes lesson: \(\partial V/\partial S = \Delta\), \(\partial^2V/\partial S^2 = \Gamma\), and \(\partial V/\partial t = \Theta\) (theta, defined directly as this partial derivative with respect to calendar time — the same convention the Black-Scholes lesson uses). Substituting those names in place of the raw derivatives turns the PDE into a statement purely about the Greeks:

\[ \Theta + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2S^2\Gamma + rS\Delta - rC = 0 \]
Θtime decay — what this lesson isolates
½σ²S²Γthe convexity term — gamma's contribution
rSΔ − rCthe financing terms — carrying the hedge and the option itself at the risk-free rate

Rearranging to isolate \(\Theta\) — moving every other term to the other side — gives the result this lesson is built around:

\[ \Theta = rC - rS\Delta - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2S^2\Gamma \]

−rSΔ

Read this the same way the Black-Scholes lesson reads its own PDE: this is not a new assumption layered on top of delta and gamma, it is a pure algebraic consequence of the same no-arbitrage replication argument. If you already know \(C\), \(\Delta\) and \(\Gamma\) for an option, this equation hands you \(\Theta\) for free — nothing new needs to be estimated or assumed. One notational note worth being precise about: the PDE's \(\partial V/\partial t\) is with respect to calendar time \(t\) moving forward; the closed-form solution below is naturally expressed in terms of \(\tau\), the time remaining to expiry (\(\tau = T - t\), the same quantity the Black-Scholes lesson calls \(T\)). That sign relationship (\(\partial/\partial t = -\partial/\partial \tau\)) is already baked into the closed-form formula next — it doesn't require re-deriving anything, just keeping straight that "time passing" and "time remaining" move in opposite directions.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Theta isn't a separate ingredient someone tossed into the option-pricing recipe — it just falls out of the same equation that already gives you delta and gamma, the way the third side of a triangle is determined the moment you've fixed the other two. If you already know an option's price, delta, and gamma, theta comes along for free; nothing extra needs to be measured or guessed.

2. The closed-form theta for a European call

Solving the PDE explicitly (the same derivation that produces the \(C=S_0N(d_1)-Ke^{-r\tau}N(d_2)\) formula in the Black-Scholes lesson, just differentiated once more with respect to time instead of price) gives the standard closed-form theta for a non-dividend European call, using the same \(d_1, d_2\) as the Black-Scholes lesson:

\[ \Theta = -\frac{S\varphi(d_1)\sigma}{2\sqrt{\tau}} - rKe^{-r\tau}N(d_2) \]
−Sφ(d₁)σ / (2√τ)the convexity/diffusion term — always negative for a long call
−rKe⁻ʳᵀN(d₂)the financing term — pulling in the direction of −rC + rSΔ combined
φ(d₁)standard normal PDF at d₁, same as the Black-Scholes lesson's gamma formula uses
\(\Theta\)Theta — \(\partial C/\partial t\), how much the option's value changes per unit of calendar time passing, everything else held fixed. Negative for a long call in the standard case, meaning value bleeds away as time passes.
\(\tau\)Time remaining to expiry, in years — the same quantity the Black-Scholes lesson calls \(T\), renamed here to keep the calendar-time-vs-remaining-time distinction visible.
\(S, K, r, \sigma\)Spot, strike, risk-free rate and volatility — identical meanings to the Black-Scholes lesson.
\(\varphi(\cdot)\)The standard normal probability density function (the bell curve's height, not its cumulative area) — \(\varphi(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-x^2/2}\). Different from \(N(\cdot)\), the cumulative distribution used elsewhere.
\(d_1, d_2\)The same intermediate quantities from the Black-Scholes closed-form price, unchanged — see that lesson for their definitions and interpretation.
\(\Delta, \Gamma\)Delta and gamma, as derived in the Black-Scholes lesson — this lesson uses them, never re-derives them.

Both terms are negative for a standard long call with \(r,K,S,\sigma \tau>0\): the first because \(\varphi(d_1)\) is always positive, the second because it's minus a product of positive quantities. That's the formal version of "an option is a wasting asset" — under the model's own assumptions, a long call's theta genuinely cannot be positive. (Certain American-style or dividend-paying structures can show positive theta in edge cases; that's outside this lesson's scope, which sticks to the plain European non-dividend case exactly as the Black-Scholes lesson does.)

The key insight — theta pays for gamma: strip the formula down to a delta-hedged position (the \(rS\Delta\) financing term is removed by construction, since a delta-hedged book has no net delta to finance; the \(rC\) term is a small financing cost on the option's own value, conventionally set aside for intuition since it's second-order next to the convexity term for a reasonably-dated option). What's left is:
\[ \Theta \approx -\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2S^2\Gamma \]
Θ ≈ −½σ²S²Γtheta and gamma are the same convexity, viewed through two different partial derivatives

This is the single most practically important idea in this lesson. A delta-hedged, long-option (long-gamma) position bleeds theta at a rate directly proportional to its own gamma — not as a coincidence, but because the PDE forces them to move together. That bleed is the fair price of convexity: it's what the option holder pays, continuously, for the right to a position whose delta improves automatically as the underlying moves. The other side of that trade — a market-maker who sold the option and is running the mirror-image delta-hedged book — is short gamma and, by the same equation with the opposite sign, long theta: they collect exactly what the long-gamma side pays.

Whether that rent was fairly priced comes down to one comparison: implied volatility (the \(\sigma\) baked into the option's price, and therefore into its theta) versus realized volatility (how much the underlying actually moves while the position is held). A long-gamma, delta-hedged position continuously rebalances — buying dips and selling rallies, in effect — and each rebalance locks in a small trading gain proportional to the squared size of the move, the same \(\sigma^2S^2\) shape that appears in the theta formula above. If realized volatility runs exactly at the level implied \(\sigma\) assumed, those rebalancing gains offset the theta bleed almost exactly on average — the position is a wash, as it should be under a fairly priced no-arbitrage model. If realized volatility comes in higher than implied, the rebalancing gains outpace the theta paid, and gamma wins. If realized volatility comes in lower, the rebalancing gains fall short of what was paid in theta, and the position loses money even though it was never wrong about direction — theta wins.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Theta is rent, not a random tax. You're paying, every single day, for the privilege of owning a position that gains extra when the underlying swings around a lot. If it actually swings around as much as you paid for (or more), the rent was a fair deal. If it stays unusually calm, you paid rent for a wild ride that never happened — and you lose money even though you never had the direction wrong.

Realized vol > implied vol

Gamma-scalping gains from continuous rebalancing exceed the theta paid. The long-gamma, delta-hedged position profits — it correctly paid rent for convexity that turned out to be worth more than the rent charged.

Realized vol < implied vol

Rebalancing gains fall short of the theta bled away. The position loses money on net, even with a perfectly executed hedge and zero directional error — it simply overpaid for convexity that didn't materialize.

3. Why theta accelerates near expiry — the same \(1/\sqrt{\tau}\) shape as gamma

The convexity term in the closed-form theta, \(-S\varphi(d_1)\sigma / (2\sqrt{\tau})\), carries a \(1/\sqrt{\tau}\) in its denominator — the exact same relationship the Gamma Exposure lesson derives for at-the-money gamma itself (\(\Gamma_{\text{ATM}}\approx \varphi(0)/(S\sigma\sqrt{\tau})\)). That's not a coincidence: the delta-hedged identity \(\Theta\approx-\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2S^2\Gamma\) says theta and gamma must scale together, term for term, as \(\tau\) shrinks. Whatever acceleration the previous lesson showed for gamma near expiry, theta inherits the identical shape — it doesn't decay smoothly and linearly as expiry approaches, it accelerates, with the sharpest moves concentrated in the final few days rather than spread evenly across the option's life. The worked example below makes this concrete with the same three checkpoints (30, 7, and 1 day to expiry) the Gamma Exposure lesson used for gamma.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Theta doesn't drain out at a steady drip like a leaky faucet — it drains faster and faster the closer you get to the very end, like a countdown timer that speeds up. The last few days before expiry lose value far more per day than a stretch of days three weeks out, even though it's the exact same number of days ticking by.

4. Weekend theta — a practitioner quirk, not a universal rule

The closed-form theta above treats time as continuous — every calendar day contributes the same fraction of \(\sqrt{\tau}\)'s shrinkage, whether it's a trading day or not. In practice, some models and desks charge theta non-uniformly around a weekend: since two calendar days pass with no trading (and, depending on the underlying, often lower realized volatility over that stretch than an average weekday), some conventions front-load, back-load, or otherwise smooth the theta charged on the Friday before a weekend rather than simply multiplying the daily rate by three. This is a genuine market-practice detail worth knowing exists — but it is a convention, not a single universal rule, and different desks and platforms handle it differently. Treat it as "be aware this exists and can differ" rather than a fixed formula to memorize.

5. Calendar spreads — trading the acceleration differential directly

A calendar spread (also called a horizontal spread) is built specifically to harvest the \(1/\sqrt{\tau}\) acceleration shown above: sell a near-dated option and buy a longer-dated option at the same strike. Both legs have similar delta and similar gamma if the strike is close to at-the-money, but their thetas differ sharply, precisely because the near leg's \(\tau\) is smaller and \(1/\sqrt{\tau}\) is larger there. The near (short) leg decays faster in dollar terms than the far (long) leg loses value from the same passage of time, so as long as the underlying stays reasonably close to the strike, the position's net theta is positive — the spread collects the differential between how fast the two legs are decaying, not a directional bet on the underlying at all. The position's main risk is the mirror image of the same coin: a big enough move away from the strike hurts the near leg's relative gamma disadvantage far more than the collected theta compensates for, which is exactly the same gamma-theta tradeoff from the key insight above, just expressed as a spread between two expiries instead of a single delta-hedged option.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

A calendar spread is like renting out a fast-melting ice cube while holding onto a slow-melting one of your own. You collect rent on the fast-melting one every day, and as long as nothing dramatic happens, your slow-melting one barely shrinks in comparison — so you pocket the difference. The risk is if things move too far from where you set it up, the trade stops being about melting speed and starts being about something you didn't sign up for.

🎯
Real-world trading scenario

Deciding whether the theta bleed is worth paying

A trader is running a long-gamma, delta-hedged book on a single at-the-money FX option seven days from expiry, using the numbers above: implied volatility priced the option at \(\sigma=20\%\), and per the worked example the position bleeds roughly $0.082 per day in theta at this snapshot (per 100 units of notional, in the same convention as the Black-Scholes lesson's dollar pricing). Every day, the trader rebalances the hedge — buying a little on dips, selling a little on rallies — and each rebalance banks a small gain proportional to how much the underlying actually moved that day, squared.

Three days in, the trader checks realized volatility over the life of the position so far: the underlying has been moving noticeably more than a 20%-annualized pace would suggest — call it 27% realized. Per the key insight above, that's the "realized > implied" case: the rebalancing gains from three days of larger-than-priced-for moves have more than kept pace with the roughly $0.24 of theta paid over those three days ($0.082 × 3, ignoring the small further acceleration as \(\tau\) shrinks). The trader's read: implied volatility was priced too low relative to what's actually happening, so continuing to hold the long-gamma position and keep rebalancing is worth the ongoing rent, at least at the current pace of realized moves. Nothing about the trader's directional view on the underlying entered this decision at all — it's a volatility comparison, exactly as the model says it should be.

Had realized volatility instead come in at 14% — quieter than implied — the same math would say the opposite: the rebalancing gains aren't covering the rent being paid, and continuing to hold the position is a bet that the recent quiet won't persist through expiry, not a free lunch. Either way, the decision reduces to one comparison: realized vol against implied vol, with theta as the explicit, quantified price of admission.

Section 03

The OI angle: short gamma and long theta are the same fact

The Gamma Exposure & Dealer Hedging Flows lesson establishes, under the standard simplifying assumption that dealers are net writers of the open interest they hold (\(s_k=-1\) at most strikes), that aggregate dealer gamma exposure is typically negative — "dealers are usually short gamma." That lesson defines, for a strike \(k\), the signed dealer gamma exposure:

\[ \text{DealerGEX}_k = s_k\,\Gamma_k\,\text{OI}_k\,100\,S^2\,(0.01) \]

This lesson's key insight, \(\Theta\approx-\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2S^2\Gamma\), applies just as well to the dealer's own signed position. If the dealer holds a signed gamma of \(s_k\Gamma_k\) per contract at strike \(k\) (the same \(s_k\Gamma_k\) that appears inside DealerGEX above), the same delta-hedged identity gives the dealer's signed theta per contract as \(-\tfrac12\sigma^2S^2(s_k\Gamma_k)\). Aggregating that across the strike's open interest exactly the way DealerGEX aggregates gamma (same \(\text{OI}_k\times100\) scaling) defines an analogous quantity — call it \(\text{ThetaExp}_k\), the dealer's aggregate theta exposure at that strike, in the same delta-hedged-approximation spirit as GEX itself (not a standardized industry term the way GEX is, just the same construction applied to the other Greek):

\[ \text{ThetaExp}_k = s_k\,\Theta_k\,\text{OI}_k\,100 \approx -\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2S^2\big(s_k\Gamma_k\,\text{OI}_k\,100\big) \]

The bracketed term, \(s_k\Gamma_k\,\text{OI}_k\,100\), is exactly the piece that DealerGEX rescales by \(S^2(0.01)\). Solving DealerGEX's own formula for that same bracketed term and substituting it in gives a direct, equation-level bridge between the two quantities — the \(S^2\) cancels entirely, leaving only \(\sigma\):

\[ \text{ThetaExp}_k \;\approx\; -\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2S^2 \cdot \frac{\text{DealerGEX}_k}{0.01\,S^2} \;=\; -50\,\sigma^2 \cdot \text{DealerGEX}_k \]
−50σ²always negative — σ² can't be negative
ThetaExp = (−50σ²)×DealerGEXa negative constant times DealerGEX — the sign always flips

Since \(-50\sigma^2\) is strictly negative for any real \(\sigma\), this equation says the sign of \(\text{ThetaExp}_k\) is always the opposite of the sign of \(\text{DealerGEX}_k\) — not usually, not typically, but structurally, term by term, as a direct consequence of the same delta-hedged approximation used throughout this lesson. Plug in the standard case: \(\text{DealerGEX}_k<0\) (dealers short gamma) forces \(\text{ThetaExp}_k>0\) (dealers long theta). "Dealers are usually short gamma" and "dealers are usually long theta" are not two separate empirical facts about how options markets happen to behave — they are the same fact, read off \(\partial^2V/\partial S^2\) and \(\partial V/\partial t\) of one identical PDE.

Applying this to the toy three-strike book from the previous lesson's worked example (\(\sigma=20\%\), \(\text{TotalDealerGEX}\approx -\$18.51\text{B}\) at the one-week-to-expiry snapshot):

\[ \text{TotalThetaExp} \approx -50(0.20)^2 \times (-\$18.51\text{B}) = -2 \times (-\$18.51\text{B}) = +\$37.02\text{B (per year, illustrative)} \]

Under the same assumptions and the same delta-hedged approximation that produced the gamma number, the dealer book that is short roughly $18.51B of GEX is, at the same instant, long roughly $37.02B of aggregate theta exposure — collecting time decay at that implied rate, for as long as the position and the σ assumption used to build it hold. This is an illustrative extension of the gamma lesson's own toy example, not a claim about any real, currently outstanding book; it inherits every caveat that lesson already flagged about the \(s_k\) sign being an assumption rather than an observed fact, plus this lesson's own caveat that the delta-hedged approximation drops the smaller financing term.

Section 04

How practitioners actually use this

📉

Gamma scalping

A long-gamma, delta-hedged trader rebalances continuously and compares the resulting trading gains to the theta paid — the realized-vs-implied-vol comparison from the key insight above, run as an ongoing, live decision rather than a one-time calculation.

📅

Calendar spreads

Selling the faster-decaying near leg against a longer-dated leg at the same strike harvests the 1/√τ acceleration differential directly, as derived above — a structural theta trade rather than a directional one.

🗓️

Weekend theta conventions

Desks and platforms that charge theta non-uniformly around a weekend are making a practitioner adjustment to the continuous-time model's assumption that every calendar day contributes equally — worth knowing the adjustment exists, without assuming any one specific convention is universal.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where theta gets misread in practice

Theta is not linear — don't extrapolate today's rate across the whole month. The worked example above shows the daily decay rate itself changing sharply as expiry approaches (roughly doubling, then accelerating further). Multiplying today's theta reading by "30 days left" systematically understates how much value is actually lost near the end of that window and overstates it near the beginning.

The weekend-theta convention is not universal. As flagged above, different desks and platforms handle the two non-trading calendar days differently — don't assume a specific smoothing rule without checking how the particular pricing source in front of you actually handles it.

The delta-hedged approximation drops a real term. \(\Theta\approx-\tfrac12\sigma^2S^2\Gamma\) is extremely accurate when the financing term is small relative to the convexity term (as in the worked example above, where it was a near-constant ≈$1/year residual), but it is still an approximation — for a deep in-the-money option with a large \(rC\) or a long time to expiry, that dropped term stops being negligible, and the exact closed-form should be used instead.

Realized-vs-implied is a comparison over the position's remaining life, not a single day's noise. One unusually quiet or unusually wild day doesn't settle whether theta or gamma is "winning" — the comparison in the trading scenario above is only meaningful assessed over a large enough sample of the rebalancing path, the same discipline this project applies to any strategy result more generally: a single data point is not a track record.

Section 06

Self-test

Q1. Starting from the Black-Scholes PDE, derive the expression for Θ, and explain in one sentence why this means theta isn't an "extra" assumption on top of delta and gamma.
The PDE is \(\partial V/\partial t + rS\partial V/\partial S + \tfrac12\sigma^2S^2\partial^2V/\partial S^2 - rV = 0\). Substituting \(\Theta=\partial V/\partial t\), \(\Delta=\partial V/\partial S\), \(\Gamma=\partial^2V/\partial S^2\) gives \(\Theta + \tfrac12\sigma^2S^2 \Gamma + rS\Delta - rC = 0\); isolating \(\Theta\) gives \(\Theta = rC - rS\Delta - \tfrac12\sigma^2S^2\Gamma\). Because this is a pure algebraic rearrangement of the same no-arbitrage PDE that produces delta and gamma, theta is fully determined once \(C\), \(\Delta\) and \(\Gamma\) are known — it requires no separate assumption or estimation of its own.
Q2. State the delta-hedged theta-gamma approximation and explain, in plain terms, what "theta pays for gamma" means.
For a delta-hedged position, \(\Theta\approx-\tfrac12\sigma^2S^2\Gamma\) (dropping the small \(rC-rS\Delta\) financing residual). It means a long-gamma, delta-hedged position's continuous time decay is, to a close approximation, just its own gamma scaled by a constant — the holder is paying, every instant, a rent proportional to how much convexity they're holding. That rent is exactly offset, on average, by the gains from continuously rebalancing the hedge as the underlying moves, if realized volatility matches the implied volatility the rent was priced on. It's "payment for convexity," not an arbitrary tax.
Q3. In the worked example, why did the delta-hedged approximation −½σ²S²Γ leave almost exactly the same residual (≈−$1/year) at all three expiries (30, 7, and 1 day), rather than the residual shrinking along with everything else?
The residual is the dropped financing term, \(rC - rS\Delta\), which for a delta-hedged position is dominated by \(rC\): the risk-free rate times the option's own value. In this worked example's round numbers (\(r=\sigma^2/2\)), \(d_2=0\) exactly at every \(\tau\), which keeps \(N(d_2)=0.5\) fixed and makes the financing term's shape relatively stable across these particular expiries even as the convexity term changes by more than 5× — a feature of this specific parameter choice being highlighted for clarity, not a claim that the residual is always constant in general.
Q4. Why does theta accelerate as expiry approaches, and why is this the same underlying mathematical fact as gamma's own acceleration shown in the previous lesson?
The closed-form theta's convexity term contains \(1/\sqrt{\tau}\) in its denominator, the identical relationship the Gamma Exposure lesson derives for at-the-money gamma. This isn't a separate coincidence — the delta-hedged identity \(\Theta\approx-\tfrac12\sigma^2S^2\Gamma\) forces theta to inherit whatever shape gamma has, since gamma is the only variable quantity on the right-hand side (\(\sigma\) and \(S\) are held fixed). Because \(\Gamma\propto1/\sqrt{\tau}\), and that relationship blows up faster than linearly as \(\tau\to0\), the same number of remaining days matters far more in the final week than three weeks out — exactly the shape shown numerically in the worked example (roughly 2.0× then 2.6× stepwise increases going 30→7→1 DTE).
Q5. Explain how a calendar spread profits from time decay, using the 1/√τ relationship explicitly.
A calendar spread sells a near-dated option and buys a longer-dated option at the same strike. Because theta's magnitude scales with \(1/\sqrt{\tau}\), the near leg's smaller \(\tau\) gives it a substantially larger daily theta than the far leg's larger \(\tau\) gives its own decay — the two legs are not decaying at comparable rates even though they share a strike. As long as the underlying stays reasonably close to that strike, the position collects the faster-decaying near leg's theta while paying the far leg's much slower decay, netting a positive theta position built entirely from the acceleration differential rather than from any directional view.
Q6. Derive why "dealers are typically net short gamma" and "dealers are typically net long theta" are the same fact rather than two separate empirical observations.
Applying the delta-hedged identity to a dealer's own signed position at strike \(k\) gives \(\text{ThetaExp}_k\approx-\tfrac12\sigma^2S^2 (s_k\Gamma_k\,\text{OI}_k\,100)\), and substituting the same bracketed term from the previous lesson's \(\text{DealerGEX}_k = s_k\Gamma_k\, \text{OI}_k\,100\,S^2(0.01)\) formula gives \(\text{ThetaExp}_k\approx -50\sigma^2\times\text{DealerGEX}_k\). Since \(-50\sigma^2\) is always strictly negative, this equation forces \(\text{ThetaExp}_k\) and \(\text{DealerGEX}_k\) to always carry opposite signs — not usually, always, as a direct algebraic consequence. Plugging in the standard assumption that \(\text{DealerGEX}_k\) is typically negative (dealers short gamma) therefore forces \(\text{ThetaExp}_k\) to be typically positive (dealers long theta) — the two statements are the same PDE fact, read off \(\partial^2V/\partial S^2\) versus \(\partial V/\partial t\), not two independent empirical claims about how options markets happen to behave.
