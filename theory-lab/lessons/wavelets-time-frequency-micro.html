Wavelets: Seeing When, Not Just Which

The previous lesson in this series, Fourier Analysis & the Frequency Domain, built a tool that answers "which cycles does this series contain" — but only as one average taken over the entire sample. This lesson picks up exactly where that one left off: swapping the endless sine wave at the heart of Fourier analysis for a small, localized "wavelet" that gets shifted and stretched across the series, trading away some of Fourier's precision to get back something it threw away entirely — a picture of when each frequency actually shows up.

Section 1

One spectrum, computed once, for the whole series

A periodogram averages frequency content over every time point before it ever reports a number.

Σ

One number per frequency

A periodogram reports total energy at each frequency, computed once across the entire sample — there's no second reading, no "how did this change."

∞

No timestamp attached

The sine and cosine waves underneath a Fourier transform never turn off — they're defined for all time, so nothing about the result is anchored to a particular moment.

⇄

Two very different stories, one spectrum

A short cycle and a long cycle running side by side the whole time, versus a short cycle for the first half and a long cycle for the second — a periodogram can barely tell these apart.

⚠

Non-stationary markets get blurred

A market that's calm for months and then suddenly choppy is exactly the case a single global spectrum handles worst — the calm and the chaos get averaged into one number per frequency.

This isn't a bug in any particular implementation — it's what "averaging over the whole sample" necessarily means. A trader who mistook "this cycle ran the entire session" for "this cycle appeared only in the last twenty minutes" would be reading the same periodogram two very different, and very consequential, ways.
Section 2

Same series, two tools that disagree on what it shows

A market that's calm in the first half and choppy in the second. Toggle between how each tool reports it.

Both bumps are reported as simply present — evenly, as if each held true for the whole session. A market that was choppy the entire time and one that turned choppy only in the second half can print an almost identical spectrum.
Exactly where each pattern sits along the time axis is now directly visible — the shift from calm to choppy shows up as a shift in which band lights up, not as two bumps with no address.

Nothing about the underlying data changed between these two panels — only the question each tool is built to answer. Fourier asks "which frequencies, averaged over everything." A time-frequency view asks "which frequencies, and roughly when," which is a genuinely different, two-dimensional question.

Section 3

A wavelet is a wave that turns itself off

∿

Fourier's basis never stops

The sine and cosine waves underneath a Fourier transform are defined for all time — they never turn on or off, never localize to any particular stretch of the series.

◗

A wavelet is a short, localized ripple

It's built the opposite way: a small oscillating waveform that's concentrated in a short span and decays to near-zero outside it — "wave" because it oscillates, "-let" because it's small.

↔

Shifted in time (parameter b)

The same "mother wavelet" shape gets slid along the series — b marks where it's currently centered, a genuinely new parameter Fourier's f never needed.

⇕

Stretched or squeezed (parameter a)

Scale a stretches the wavelet to catch slow, low-frequency patterns, or compresses it to catch fast, high-frequency ones — a is the wavelet world's stand-in for frequency.

Slide the shifted, scaled wavelet ψ((t−b)/a) across the series, multiply pointwise, and sum — exactly how the Fourier transform was computed in the previous lesson, except the answer now depends on two things, scale and location, instead of frequency alone. That's W(a,b): how strongly that particular size-and-place pattern matches the series.

Handed two descriptions of the same song, a Fourier-style one lists every note that appears anywhere and how loud it is on average; a wavelet-style one is closer to sheet music — the same notes, placed in time, showing exactly when each one plays. The tool trades away some of Fourier's very precise frequency resolution to get that timing back — there's no version of this that gives you both for free.

A small scale squeezes the same basic shape into a tight, fast ripple — a good match for a sudden spike or a burst of high-frequency noise. A large scale stretches it into a slow, broad hump — a good match for a gradual trend. Every scale in between checks the series for a pattern of that particular size, at every position b along it.

Section 5

Turning a scale into a period you can actually read

Scale isn't itself a frequency — it's an inverse-ish stand-in for one. Given a wavelet's base frequency, scale converts to an approximate frequency and period.

Blank, non-numeric, or zero-and-negative scale all show "—" rather than a broken number — a is a stretch factor, and a wavelet can't be stretched by nothing.

This is deliberately the simplest possible version of the relationship — real wavelet software (Morlet in particular) uses a somewhat more careful formula tying a wavelet's own center frequency to scale — but the shape of the idea is exactly this: larger scale → lower approximate frequency → longer approximate period, and vice versa.

Section 6

The output is a picture, not a spectrum

Time along one axis, scale (inverse frequency) along the other — each cell shows how strongly that size-of-pattern matched the series at that moment.

This scalogram is the direct visual analogue of the previous lesson's periodogram — except it plots \(|W(a,b)|^2\) as a 2-D image, scale against time, instead of collapsing everything into one static curve. Reading it top-to-bottom answers "which size of pattern"; reading it left-to-right answers "when." A single periodogram of this exact series would report both the slow trend and the high-frequency noise as simply present — with no way to say the trend faded out right as the noise took over.

Section 7

Worked example: pinpointing a single shock exactly

Eight toy daily returns (%), mostly small, with one large shock in the middle: [0.10, −0.10, 0.05, −0.05, 3.00, −0.05, 0.05, −0.10]

One-level Haar detail coefficients

A one-level Haar transform pairs up consecutive points and, per pair, computes a half-difference — how much the pair disagrees with its own average.

d₁ = 0.100
d₂ = 0.050
d₃ = 1.525
d₄ = 0.075
d₃ is roughly 15–30× the size of every other detail coefficient — and its position in the list identifies exactly which pair of time steps (4 and 5) the shock sits in.
A Fourier transform of the same 8 points would still register the shock — a sudden jump is broadband, touching every frequency a little — but it would spread that energy across every frequency bin with no way to say which of the 8 time steps it came from.
Section 8

Where this actually earns its keep on a desk

A researcher wants to know whether EURUSD's intraday cycle character shortens right after a scheduled ECB announcement, and for how long.

Fourier's answer

A single periodogram over the whole session reports whichever cycle lengths were present somewhere that day — with no way to tell whether the shorter cycle ran the entire session or was concentrated in the twenty minutes right after the release.

Wavelet's answer

A scalogram of the same return series shows scale (cycle length) against time-of-day directly — the shortening, if it's real, shows up as a visible narrow band right at the announcement, fading back to the pre-event pattern afterward.

Where this needs qualifying

A scalogram showing an interesting pattern around a known event is a genuinely useful descriptive picture of market behavior — it is not, by itself, a validated trading signal. Turning "the scalogram shows a pattern around news events" into an actual rule still needs the same honest out-of-sample bar as anything else in this curriculum.

Section 9

Where a scalogram misleads

⇌

Time and frequency resolution always trade off

A wavelet narrow enough to pinpoint exactly when something happened is necessarily spread across many frequencies, and vice versa — no wavelet choice escapes this, closely related to the Heisenberg uncertainty principle.

◧

The cone of influence

Near the very start and end of a finite sample, a wavelet is partly hanging off the edge of the available data — readings there should be trusted far less than ones safely in the middle.

⚖

Mother wavelet choice is a real decision

Haar (flat steps) localizes sharp jumps precisely but resolves nearby frequencies poorly; Morlet (a tapered oscillation) resolves frequencies cleanly but blurs an abrupt event's exact timing. Neither is "the" correct default.

🔍

Hot spots can be look-elsewhere noise

A scalogram has many time-scale cells — scanning all of them for a bright patch is the same multiple-comparisons problem as scanning many lags or many strategies for a "significant" result.

Section 10

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

What does a periodogram fail to tell you that a scalogram shows?

A periodogram already answers "which frequencies." What it can't answer is "when" — it collapses every time point together before ever looking at frequency content.
Question 2

In W(a,b), what does a large scale (a) roughly correspond to?

Stretching the mother wavelet (large a) makes it oscillate slowly, matching long, low-frequency patterns. b, not a, is the time-location parameter.
Question 3

In the worked Haar example, why was d₃ so much larger than d₁, d₂, and d₄?

A detail coefficient is a half-difference within a pair. Every other pair holds two similarly small values; the shock pair's huge internal disagreement is exactly what makes d₃ stand out — and its position identifies the shock's timing.
Question 4

Why is choosing between a Haar and a Morlet mother wavelet a real, consequential decision?

Haar is well matched to sharp, instantaneous jumps; Morlet is well matched to smoothly evolving cycles. The right choice depends on the feature being hunted for — it's a modeling decision, not a neutral default.
Section 11

What to carry forward

01

A periodogram gives one averaged spectrum for the whole series — no timing, and no way to distinguish "always present" from "present somewhere."

02

A wavelet is a small, localized ripple — shifted (b) and scaled (a) across the series — in place of an endless sine wave.

03

Scale is the wavelet world's stand-in for frequency: large a stretches it (slow, low-frequency match), small a compresses it (fast, high-frequency match).

04

The output, a scalogram, is a 2-D time-frequency map, not a 1-D spectrum — time on one axis, scale/frequency on the other.

05

Nothing escapes the resolution trade-off: sharper timing always costs some frequency precision, and vice versa.

06

A scalogram is a descriptive lens on how cyclical behavior evolves — a real-looking pattern still needs an honest out-of-sample test before it's a signal.

What this now lets you do

Recognise when a series' frequency content is genuinely changing over time rather than trusting one flat, averaged spectrum. Read a scalogram's two axes correctly — time and scale — instead of mistaking it for a fancier periodogram. Know that a mother wavelet's shape (Haar vs. Morlet) is itself a choice, matched to whether you're hunting for a sharp jump or a smoothly evolving cycle.
