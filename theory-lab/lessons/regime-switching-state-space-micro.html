Regime-Switching State Space Models, Fusing Two Hidden States

Two earlier lessons each hid one thing: the Hidden Markov Model lesson hid a discrete regime label, and the Kalman filter lesson hid a continuous drifting quantity. Real systems often hide both at once — a market that is either "trending" or "mean-reverting," and, within whichever regime is currently active, a slowly drifting continuous level. This lesson fuses the two, hits the exponential wall that fusing them naively creates, and covers the practical fix — Kim's filter — that keeps the computation tractable.

Section 1

Two guessing games, nested inside each other

This lesson is a direct sequel to Hidden Markov Models & Regime Detection and Kalman Filter & Adaptive Relationships — read both first if you haven't; this one builds nothing new, it fuses what they already built.

▦

HMM alone — discrete only

Tracked a hidden regime — trending vs. choppy — with observations drawn straight from a per-regime Gaussian. No continuous drift lived inside a regime.

◉

Kalman filter alone — continuous only

Tracked a continuously drifting hidden state — a hedge ratio — but assumed one single, fixed set of dynamics held for the entire series.

⇄

Fused — both, feeding each other

Which set of Kalman dynamics is "active" is itself a hidden discrete regime, evolving by a Markov chain — exactly the HMM's machinery, one layer up.

Picture the HMM's "which weather regime is it" guessing game and the Kalman filter's "where exactly is the boat" tracking game running simultaneously, each sharpening the other. In calm weather you trust the boat to drift slowly and predictably; in a storm you expect it to move erratically. But which regime you believe you're in also depends on how well each regime's implied drift matches the radar pings you've actually been receiving. That circularity is exactly what makes this model powerful — and exactly what makes it hard to compute exactly.
Section 2

Same recursion, every coefficient now regime-indexed

Reuse both prior lessons' notation directly. Compare this line-by-line with the plain Kalman filter's x_t = x_{t-1} + w_t.

The fused model
x_t = A_(S_t) x_(t-1) + w_t
·
w_t ~ N(0, Q_(S_t))
y_t = H_(S_t) x_t + v_t
·
v_t ~ N(0, R_(S_t))
Only one thing changed versus the plain Kalman filter: every coefficient now carries a subscript S_t — which regime is active right now. The regime itself, S_t ∈ {1,…,N}, evolves by the same transition matrix A from the HMM lesson (A_ij = P(S_t=j | S_(t-1)=i), each row summing to 1).
A

A_(S_t), H_(S_t)

The state/observation coefficients — a different linear relationship allowed per regime.

Q

Q_(S_t), R_(S_t)

The process/observation variances — a calm regime might pair small Q with small R; a crisis regime, both large.

N

N complete parameter sets

One full (A_k, Q_k, H_k, R_k) per regime k — the model switches which set governs the state according to the hidden Markov chain.

If the regime were observed, this would be trivial

Each of the 2 branches must itself split over the 2 possible values of S_2 — because the correct estimate of x_2 genuinely differs depending on which regime path actually occurred. N² = 4.

N³ = 8, and it keeps doubling every step: N^T after T steps. A mere 30 steps at N=2 already implies over a billion regime paths — hopeless to track exactly.
N² branches
collapse
N branches
Kim's filter: after propagating forward to N² regime-pair estimates each step, collapse the branches sharing the same current regime back into one Gaussian per regime — via moment matching. The running total never exceeds N, no matter how large T grows.
This is the exact same "exponentially many hidden paths" problem the HMM lesson's forward algorithm sidestepped for a purely discrete state — here a continuous mean/variance rides along inside each branch, so there's no single running sum to reuse; something has to be collapsed instead.

Section 5

Stickier regimes let belief lock in; fast-switching ones cap it

Same story as five "turbulent-looking" observations in a row, run through a two-regime Bayes+Markov update. Drag the slider to change how sticky the regimes are (the transition matrix's diagonal).

Try it
Regime stickiness — P(stay in same regime)s = 0.85
After 5 straight turbulent-looking observations (each with a 3× likelihood ratio favoring "turbulent"), P(turbulent) reaches 91.9%.

At low stickiness, each step's transition mixing drags belief back toward 50/50 regardless of the evidence, capping how confident the model can ever become. At high stickiness, consecutive evidence compounds almost freely — belief climbs toward certainty. This is the same diagonal-vs-off-diagonal trade-off the HMM lesson introduced, now visibly shaping how fast the fused model's regime belief can move.

Section 7

Collapsing two disagreeing branches costs something real

The worked example from the full lesson: two regime-conditional Kalman estimates that both landed in regime "calm," collapsed into one Gaussian.

Two branches, one collapse
stayed calm: x̂=1.30, P=0.004, weight 0.75
switched to calm: x̂=1.10, P=0.010, weight 0.25
collapsed: x̂=1.250, P=0.0130
0.0130 is larger than either individual branch's own variance (0.004 and 0.010) — even though it's built from a weighted average of two smaller numbers.

The collapsed variance is exactly the law of total variance: the weighted average of each branch's own variance (0.75×0.004 + 0.25×0.010 = 0.0055) plus the weighted variance of the branches' means around the new collapsed mean (0.75×(1.25−1.30)² + 0.25×(1.25−1.10)² = 0.0075). That second term only exists because the two branches disagreed on the mean by 0.20 — merging opinions that disagree is never free. Had they agreed closely, the second term would shrink toward zero and the collapse would cost almost nothing.

Section 8

What this is for: a hedge ratio that behaves differently by regime

A carry-trade hedge ratio, plausibly stable in calm periods and erratic during a risk-off unwind.

Calm carry regime
  • Small process variance Q — the hedge ratio drifts slowly and predictably
  • Tight observation noise R — much like the Kalman filter lesson's original worked example
  • The linear relationship itself stays roughly stable
Risk-off unwind regime
  • Large Q — the hedge ratio moves fast and erratically as carry trades close in a rush
  • Noisier R, and the relationship itself can temporarily weaken
  • Which regime is active is inferred, not hand-set by a volatility threshold
Illustration only

This is presented purely to show what the fused model is for — it is explicitly not a claim that this specific setup has been built, backtested, or shown to outperform a plain Kalman filter or a hand-set regime rule in this repo. It also multiplies the Kalman filter lesson's own Q/R-tuning caution across every regime at once, rather than resolving it.

Section 9

Where the fusion gets over-trusted

≈

Kim's filter is an approximation, not exact

It loses real information whenever regime-conditional branches genuinely disagree — exactly what the worked example's inflated collapsed variance showed.

#

An explosion of free parameters

Each of N regimes needs its own Q, R, possibly its own A and H, on top of the N×N transition matrix — a large parameter count relative to most sample sizes.

⏮

The same lookahead trap as the smoother and the HMM

Smoothed regime probabilities and smoothed continuous-state estimates use future data and must never substitute for a live, forward-only filtered estimate.

⚖

More general isn't automatically better

A model that can represent more possibilities is automatically a more overfittable one — added complexity has to earn its place with real out-of-sample evidence.

Section 10

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

What, specifically, is allowed to depend on the hidden regime S_t?

Q_(S_t), R_(S_t), and potentially A_(S_t)/H_(S_t) are each indexed by the currently active regime. The predict-then-update recursion itself is unchanged from the plain Kalman filter.
Question 2

Why can't the filter just look up which regime is active and run an ordinary Kalman filter?

If S_t were observed, this would reduce to exactly that simple lookup. The entire difficulty comes from inferring the regime and the continuous state simultaneously, each from data shaped by the other.
Question 3

After T steps, how many regime paths does exact filtering need to track?

Every branch splits into N new branches at every step — multiplying the count, not adding to it — so 1 branch becomes N after step 1, N² after step 2, and N^T after T steps.
Question 4

In the worked example, why was the collapsed variance (0.0130) larger than both branches' own variances (0.004 and 0.010)?

Collapsed variance = weighted average of the branches' own variances (0.0055) + weighted variance of their means around the collapsed mean (0.0075). That second term only appears because the branches disagreed — it vanishes if they agree.
Question 5

Is a fused regime-switching state-space model built or tested anywhere in this repo?

regime_classifier_mtf.py uses an HMM alone (discrete regime only); beta_estimator.py runs a Kalman filter alone (continuous state only, no regime dependence). This lesson is a concept lesson describing what fusing them would look like.
Section 11

What to carry forward

01

A regime-switching state-space model fuses the HMM's discrete hidden regime with the Kalman filter's continuous hidden state — the continuous dynamics' own parameters (Q, R, possibly A/H) depend on which regime is currently active.

02

Exact filtering is intractable — the number of regime-conditional branches multiplies by N every step, reaching N^T after T steps, hopeless beyond a handful of steps.

03

Kim's filter (1994) collapses N² branches back to N every step via moment matching, keeping the computation linear in T instead of exponential.

04

The collapse's cost is exactly the law of total variance's second term — new uncertainty manufactured whenever the branches being merged actually disagreed.

05

Regime stickiness controls how fast belief can move — sticky regimes let evidence compound toward confidence; fast-switching regimes cap belief near a permanent ceiling.

06

This is the most parameter-heavy tool in its category, its standard estimation method is itself an approximation, and it remains a concept lesson — not built or tested anywhere in this repo.

What this now lets you do

Recognize when a real system plausibly needs both a discrete regime and a continuous drifting state, rather than forcing one tool to do the other's job. Know that exact fusion is computationally impossible beyond a handful of steps, and that Kim's filter is the standard practical fix — an approximation with a real, quantifiable cost whenever branches disagree, not a free lunch. And know to weigh that cost, plus the parameter explosion, against a simpler incumbent before reaching for this model at all.
