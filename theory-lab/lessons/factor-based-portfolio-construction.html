Factor-Based Portfolio Construction

Instead of estimating how every asset co-moves with every other asset directly, express each one's return as a handful of shared exposures — then build a portfolio by choosing how much of each exposure you actually want.

CONCEPT — theory only, factors named here are not validated on this repo's OOS data ~16 min read Prereqs: PCA & Factor Regimes, Alpha vs. Beta Separation
TL;DR
  • A factor model expresses each asset's return as \(r_i = \sum_k \beta_{ik} f_k + \varepsilon_i\) — a handful of shared factor exposures \(\beta_{ik}\) plus idiosyncratic noise — instead of estimating every pairwise covariance directly, which needs \(N(N-1)/2\) numbers that only grows worse as the universe gets larger.
  • The single-factor version of this is already covered in depth: the market-model regression from the Alpha vs. Beta Separation lesson is exactly \(K=1\) factor (the market itself). This lesson generalizes to \(K>1\) named factors and to building a portfolio from the exposures, not just measuring them after the fact.
  • Named FX/macro factor families — carry, momentum/trend, value — each come with real academic literature behind them (cited below), but citing that literature is not the same as this repo having validated any of them on its own out-of-sample data.
  • Once exposures are estimated, portfolio-level factor exposure is just the weighted sum of asset-level exposures — so hitting a target profile ("long carry, neutral momentum") is a small linear system: solve for weights such that the weighted exposures match the target.
  • Worked example: a 2-asset, 2-factor system solved exactly for the weights that deliver a stated target exposure, with zero free parameters beyond the target itself.
Section 01

Why this matters

The PCA & Factor Regimes lesson showed that a basket of correlated instruments is often really a small number of common factors wearing many costumes — and that PCA finds those factors statistically, purely from the correlation structure of the data, with no economic story attached to what a given component actually represents. The Alpha vs. Beta Separation lesson showed the single-factor special case from the other direction: regress a return stream against one specific, named factor (the market) to split it into a slope (beta, cheap and hedgeable) and an intercept (alpha, the part actually worth paying for).

Factor-based portfolio construction sits between those two ideas and extends both of them. Instead of PCA's statistically-discovered, unnamed factors, it uses a small number of named, economically motivated factors — carry, momentum, value are the standard FX/macro trio. And instead of stopping at measuring one asset's exposure to one factor (Alpha vs. Beta's market-model regression), it asks the next question: given every asset's exposure to every named factor, what weights across the basket deliver the overall factor profile you actually want? That's a fundamentally different, cheaper parameter problem than estimating a full covariance matrix directly, and it's the idea developed step by step below.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Describe a fruit basket by ripeness and sweetness, not fruit by fruit

Imagine describing every possible pair of fruits in a basket by how similar they are to each other — apple-to-orange, apple-to-banana, orange-to-banana, and so on for every pair — an approach that gets unmanageable fast as the basket grows, and tells you nothing about why two fruits happen to be similar. The alternative: describe each fruit along a small number of shared, meaningful dimensions — how sweet, how ripe, how acidic — and any two fruits' similarity falls straight out of comparing those few numbers, with a story attached to why. A factor model does exactly this for asset returns: instead of one number per pair of assets (an unstructured, ever-growing pile of pairwise covariances), it uses a handful of per-asset numbers — how much carry exposure, how much momentum exposure, how much value exposure — from which every pairwise relationship can be reconstructed, cheaply, and with an economic story behind each dimension.

Section 02

The math, step by step

1

The factor model — return as a sum of shared exposures

Generalize the Alpha vs. Beta Separation lesson's single-factor market model to \(K\) named factors:

\[ r_i = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \beta_{ik}\, f_k + \varepsilon_i \]
r_iasset i's return
β_ikasset i's exposure (loading) to factor k
f_kfactor k's own return/realization that period
ε_iidiosyncratic return — whatever the named factors don't explain

Setting \(K=1\) and \(f_1 = r_m - r_f\) (the market's excess return) recovers exactly the Alpha vs. Beta Separation lesson's single-index model — that lesson is this one's \(K=1\) special case, not a different idea.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Instead of describing every person in a room by how they relate to every other person individually, you describe each person by a few shared traits — how tall, how loud, how funny — and any two people's relationship falls right out of comparing those traits. A factor model does the same thing for an asset's return: instead of one number for every possible pair of assets, use a handful of shared "traits" (carry, momentum, value) per asset.

2

Why this is a parameter-count win

Estimating a full covariance matrix directly for \(N\) assets needs \(N(N-1)/2\) distinct pairwise covariances (plus \(N\) variances) — for a 30-instrument FX universe, 435 numbers, every one of them a noisy sample estimate. A \(K\)-factor model instead needs only \(N \times K\) loadings, a \(K\times K\) factor covariance matrix, and \(N\) idiosyncratic variances:

\[ \Sigma = B\, F\, B^\top + D \]
Bthe N×K loadings matrix — row i is asset i's exposure to every factor
Fthe K×K factor covariance matrix — how the factors themselves co-move
DN×N diagonal matrix of idiosyncratic variances — assumed uncorrelated across assets once factors are removed

With \(N=30\) and \(K=3\) (carry, momentum, value), that's \(30\times3=90\) loadings, \(3\times4/2=6\) factor covariance terms, and 30 idiosyncratic variances — 126 numbers total, versus 435 for the direct approach, and the ones that remain (loadings, factor covariances) are typically far more stable to estimate than 435 individually noisy pairwise covariances, because every asset's loading estimate is pooling information from the same underlying factor return series.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Keeping track of how every guest at a huge party gets along with every other guest is a nightmare that explodes as more guests show up. But if you just note each guest's few defining traits, you can guess how any two get along from those traits alone — way fewer numbers to keep track of, and they're more reliable too, since they're built from broad, shared patterns rather than one noisy guess per pair.

3

Portfolio-level exposure — a weighted sum

Once every asset's factor loadings are known, a portfolio's exposure to any factor is simply the weighted sum of its constituents' loadings to that factor:

\[ B_p = w^\top B, \qquad \text{i.e. } B_{p,k} = \sum_i w_i\, \beta_{ik} \]

This is the same weighted-sum logic as portfolio expected return in the Markowitz lesson (\(E[R_p]=w^\top\mu\)) — nothing more exotic than a dot product, just applied to factor loadings instead of expected returns.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

If each ingredient in a recipe has a "spiciness" rating, the spiciness of the whole dish is just how much of each ingredient you use, multiplied by its own spiciness, added up. A portfolio's exposure to a factor works exactly the same way — multiply each asset's amount held by its own factor rating and add it all up.

4

Solving for a target exposure profile

Turn Step 3 around: instead of computing a portfolio's exposure from its weights, specify the exposure you want — a target vector \(q\) (e.g. "carry exposure +1.0, momentum exposure 0.0") — and solve for the weights that deliver it:

\[ B^\top w = q \]
B^Tthe K×N transposed loadings matrix — one row per factor
wthe unknown weight vector being solved for
qthe target exposure profile — one entry per factor

With exactly as many assets as factors (\(N=K\)) and a non-singular \(B^\top\), this is a square linear system with one exact solution — the worked example below solves precisely this case. With more assets than factors (\(N>K\), the realistic case), the system is underdetermined — many weight vectors hit the same target exposure — and the remaining freedom is typically used to add a second objective on top (minimize idiosyncratic risk, cap position sizes, or hold the smallest total notional that still hits the target), turning Step 4 into a small constrained optimization rather than a single linear solve.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

This flips the previous idea around: instead of asking "how spicy is this dish," you start from "I want a dish that's exactly this spicy and exactly this salty" and work backward to figure out how much of each ingredient to use. With exactly as many ingredients as flavors you're targeting, there's usually one exact answer; with more ingredients than flavors, many different recipes hit the same taste, and you need some other rule to pick just one.

Worked example

Solving the 2×2 system by hand

Two factors — carry (\(C\)) and momentum (\(M\)) — and two assets: AUDUSD with loadings \((C_1, M_1) = (1.0, 0.2)\), USDJPY with loadings \((C_2, M_2) = (-0.8, 0.6)\). Target profile: long carry at exactly \(+1.0\), momentum-neutral (\(0.0\)). Solving \(B^\top w = q\):

\[ \begin{cases} 1.0\,w_1 - 0.8\,w_2 = 1.0 \\ 0.2\,w_1 + 0.6\,w_2 = 0.0 \end{cases} \]

From the second equation, \(w_1 = -3w_2\). Substituting into the first: \(-3w_2 - 0.8w_2 = 1.0 \Rightarrow -3.8w_2 = 1.0 \Rightarrow w_2 = -0.263\), so \(w_1 = 0.789\). Checking both equations: carry \(=0.789(1.0) + (-0.263)(-0.8) = 0.789+0.211=1.000\) ✓; momentum \(=0.789(0.2)+(-0.263)(0.6)=0.158-0.158=0.000\) ✓.

The target ("long carry, neutral momentum") is delivered by going long AUDUSD at 78.9% of notional and short USDJPY at 26.3% of notional — the short USDJPY leg exists specifically to cancel out the momentum exposure AUDUSD's own carry position would otherwise bring along with it, since AUDUSD isn't a pure carry instrument on its own (it carries a small positive momentum loading too).

🎯
Real-world trading scenario

Building a "long carry, neutral momentum" sleeve across five pairs

A macro desk wants a specific factor profile for its FX sleeve this quarter: meaningful long exposure to the carry factor (betting the historical high-minus-low-interest-rate-differential premium persists), but explicitly no directional bet on momentum/trend, because the desk has a separate momentum book already carrying that risk and doesn't want to double up. With loadings estimated for five candidate pairs on both factors, the construction problem is exactly Step 4 generalized to \(N=5>K=2\): infinitely many weight combinations hit the target carry-long, momentum-neutral profile exactly, so the desk adds a second, practical objective — say, minimize each pair's remaining idiosyncratic risk, or cap any single pair's weight — to pick one specific solution out of that whole family.

The mechanical exposure-matching part of this is exactly the linear algebra above; the part that actually determines whether the sleeve makes money is whether carry and momentum, as estimated for these five specific pairs over this specific period, are real, persistent factors and not noise — a separate, harder question the next section is honest about.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

The three factor families named in the TL;DR are each backed by real, peer-reviewed FX/macro literature — stated here as the literature's finding, explicitly not as something this repo has re-tested and confirmed on its own out-of-sample data:

💰

Carry

Sort currencies by interest-rate differential; historically, high-rate currencies have offered a premium over low-rate ones, compensated (the literature argues) by exposure to global FX volatility risk — Menkhoff, Sarno, Schmeling & Schrimpf (2012).

📈

Momentum / trend

Currencies (and other assets) that have recently trended tend to keep trending over intermediate horizons — one of the "everywhere" asset classes in Asness, Moskowitz & Pedersen (2013)'s cross-asset momentum study.

⚖️

Value

Currencies that look cheap relative to a real-exchange-rate-deviation anchor tend to outperform over long horizons — the same framing the Purchasing Power Parity lesson develops in depth, and the value leg of Asness, Moskowitz & Pedersen (2013)'s "everywhere" study.

Real multi-factor FX portfolios at systematic macro funds are typically built exactly along the Step 4 path: estimate loadings for a broad instrument universe against each named factor, then solve (or optimize) for the weights that deliver a chosen combined exposure profile, often layering a risk-based sizing rule (like the previous two lessons' risk parity or hierarchical risk parity) on top of the factor-tilted weights rather than using raw factor exposure alone as the final sizing rule. Citing this literature is not the same claim as this repo having validated carry, momentum, or value on its own instrument universe and time period — the academic record for these factors is real and substantial, but whether they hold up, net of realistic costs, on this project's own data is a separate, untested question.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where factor-based construction goes wrong in practice

Loadings are estimated, not known. Every \(\beta_{ik}\) in this lesson's equations is itself a regression estimate with its own standard error — exactly the same caveat the Alpha vs. Beta Separation lesson raises about a single beta estimate, just now multiplied across every asset and every factor. A target-hitting weight solve built on noisy loadings delivers the target exposure only as accurately as those loadings were actually estimated.

Factor definitions are not standardized across providers. "Momentum" or "value," computed with a different lookback window, universe, or normalization, can produce meaningfully different factor return series and loadings — there is no single, universally agreed construction, unlike, say, a market-cap-weighted equity index.

Crowding and factor decay. A factor's historical premium reflects the compensation investors demanded for a risk (or the inefficiency they exploited) at the time it was documented; as more capital chases the same factor, the premium can compress — exactly the kind of drift this whole site's in-sample-vs-OOS discipline exists to catch, and exactly why this lesson refuses to claim these factors are validated here.

The underdetermined case hides an implicit second objective. When \(N>K\), "solve for the weights hitting the target" is really "pick one solution from an entire family that all hit the target equally well" — whatever tiebreaker gets used (minimum notional, minimum idiosyncratic risk, position caps) is itself a real modeling choice that changes the resulting portfolio, not a neutral default.

Section 04

Key Takeaways

✓A factor model trades pairwise covariances for shared exposures. \(r_i = \sum_k \beta_{ik}f_k + \varepsilon_i\) needs \(N\times K\) loadings instead of \(N(N-1)/2\) pairwise covariances — a real parameter-count win that grows with the universe size.
✓The single-factor market model is this lesson's \(K=1\) special case. Alpha vs. Beta Separation already covers it in depth; this lesson generalizes to named, multi-factor exposures.
✓Portfolio-level exposure is a weighted sum, \(B_p = w^\top B\). Hitting a target exposure profile is solving \(B^\top w = q\) — a small linear system when \(N=K\), a constrained optimization when \(N>K\).
⚠Citing carry/momentum/value literature is not validating them here. Menkhoff et al. (2012) and Asness, Moskowitz & Pedersen (2013) are real, peer-reviewed findings — not evidence that these factors work on this repo's own instruments and time period.
⚠Loadings are estimates, not facts. A target-hitting weight solve is only as good as the \(\beta_{ik}\) estimates feeding it, each with its own standard error.
✗Not built or validated in this repo. No factor loadings are estimated, and no factor-tilted portfolio has been backtested, here.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. Why does a K-factor model need far fewer parameters than estimating a full covariance matrix directly, once the universe is large?
Direct covariance estimation needs one number per pair of assets, \(N(N-1)/2\), which grows roughly with the square of the universe size. A K-factor model instead needs \(N\times K\) loadings, a \(K\times K\) factor covariance matrix, and N idiosyncratic variances — growing linearly in N for fixed K. For a 30-asset universe and 3 factors, that's 126 numbers instead of 435, and the gap widens further as N grows.
Q2. How does this lesson's factor model relate to the Alpha vs. Beta Separation lesson's market-model regression?
They are the same equation at different values of K. Setting K=1 and letting the single factor f_1 be the market's excess return recovers exactly \(r_i - r_f = \alpha_i + \beta_i(r_m-r_f) + \varepsilon_i\) — the market-model regression is this lesson's one-factor special case, not a separate framework.
Q3. Given two assets and two factors, why does solving for a target exposure profile have exactly one solution, while five assets and two factors has infinitely many?
With N=K, the system B^T w = q is square — as many equations as unknowns — and, provided B^T is non-singular, has exactly one solution, the same way a 2x2 system of linear equations pins down exactly one (x,y). With N=5 and K=2, there are only 2 equations but 5 unknown weights, so the system is underdetermined: an entire 3-dimensional family of weight vectors all satisfy the same 2 target-exposure equations, and picking one specific member of that family requires an additional criterion beyond just hitting the target.
Q4. In the worked example, why is USDJPY given a short (negative) weight rather than being left out of the portfolio entirely?
AUDUSD alone, at whatever size needed to hit +1.0 carry exposure, would also bring along an unwanted positive momentum exposure (its 0.2 momentum loading, scaled by that same large weight). The short USDJPY position is sized specifically to cancel that unwanted momentum exposure — USDJPY's own momentum loading (0.6) times its solved weight (-0.263) offsets AUDUSD's momentum contribution exactly, which is why momentum exposure comes out to 0.000 in the check, while carry still nets to the full target of 1.000.
Q5. Why is "carry, momentum, and value are real, cited factors" not the same claim as "this repo's strategies have carry, momentum, or value edge"?
The cited papers document these factors' historical performance on the specific universes, time periods, and construction methodologies each paper used — real, peer-reviewed findings about those datasets. Whether the same factors, built from this repo's own instrument universe with its own realistic costs, would show the same premium over the same or a different period is an entirely separate empirical question that has not been tested here. Literature citation establishes that an idea is worth testing, not that the test has already been run and passed.
Q6. Why does the underdetermined case (more assets than factors) still require a real modeling decision, even after the target exposure is fully specified?
Because specifying the target exposure only pins down which combination of factor exposures the portfolio must deliver — it says nothing about how to choose among the many different weight vectors that all deliver that same combination equally well. Whatever secondary rule breaks that tie (minimizing idiosyncratic risk, capping individual position sizes, minimizing total notional) materially changes which specific portfolio gets built, so it's a real, consequential modeling choice, not a neutral technical detail that "solving the system" already settled.
