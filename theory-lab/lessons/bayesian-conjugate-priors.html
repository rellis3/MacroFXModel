Conjugate Priors & Bayesian Updating in Practice

Bayes' theorem tells you how to combine a prior belief with new evidence. Conjugate priors are the special case where that combination has a clean, closed-form answer — so you can actually watch a belief narrow, update after update, without numerical machinery.

CONCEPT — explained here, not a claim this repo has built or validated it ~16 min read Prereqs: Bayesian Inference & Updating
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • A prior is conjugate to a likelihood when the posterior comes out of the same distribution family as the prior — no numerical integration required, just new parameter values.
  • Beta-Binomial: prior \(\theta \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)\), observe \(s\) successes in \(n\) trials, posterior is exactly \(\text{Beta}(\alpha+s,\ \beta+n-s)\) — the update is just addition.
  • Normal-Normal (known variance): prior \(\mu \sim N(\mu_0,\sigma_0^2)\), the posterior mean is a precision-weighted average of the prior mean and the sample mean, and posterior precision is just prior precision plus data precision.
  • Worked example: starting from a weak belief that "trend days" happen 40% of the time, 10 observed days (3 trend days) then 30 more (9 trend days) narrow the belief's standard deviation from 0.20 → 0.12 → 0.07 — visualized live in the chart below.
  • Conjugacy is a mathematical convenience, not a law of nature — most real-world likelihoods have no conjugate prior at all, which is exactly why the next lesson in this trio covers MCMC.
  • How it reached trading: conjugate priors (named by Raiffa & Schlaifer, 1961) give closed-form updates — the algebra inside Black–Litterman and the Kalman filter, and the fastest way to track a live hit rate or mean.
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

I want to know how often we really get a trend day. Not a call for tomorrow. The long-run rate.

Then your belief isn't one number. It's a curve over every possible rate, from zero to a hundred percent. It's higher where you think the rate probably sits.

Updating a whole curve every day sounds like a lot of work.

In general it is. You'd need numerical integration or simulation. But there's a shortcut. Pick the right family of curves for your starting belief, and the updated belief lands in the same family. Only two numbers change. That's a conjugate prior.

Which family for a rate?

The Beta. It lives between zero and one, and it has two settings. Think of them as pretend counts: trend days you imagine you've already seen, and quiet days you imagine you've already seen.

So what do I start with?

Say two pretend trend days and three pretend quiet ones. That centres you at forty percent, but only weakly. Your spread is about twenty points either way.

Then I watch ten real days, and three are trend days.

You add. Two plus three makes five trend days. Three plus seven makes ten quiet ones. Your best guess is five out of fifteen, one third. And the spread falls to about twelve points.

That's it? Just adding?

Just adding. Watch thirty more days with nine trend days, and you're at fourteen and thirty-one. Your guess is about thirty-one percent and the spread is down to about seven points. The curve moved toward the data and got much narrower.

Why did my starting guess fade so fast?

Because it was only worth five pretend days. Forty real days outweigh five pretend ones. Start with a hundred pretend days and the same data would barely move you. How heavy your prior is, is a choice you should state out loud.

And for an average return rather than a rate?

Same idea with the bell curve. You weight your prior and the data by how sure each one is. Sureness, which we call precision, simply adds. So the combined belief is always tighter than either source alone.

Where does that show up in markets?

The Black-Litterman blend of views and market prices is exactly this case. So is the update step of the Kalman filter, run once per bar.

What's the catch?

Two. Real days aren't independent: trend days cluster, so the curve narrows faster than it should. And a narrow curve only describes the sample you saw. It doesn't promise the future looks the same.

One line?

Pick a starting curve that matches your data, and updating a belief becomes adding up counts.

Section 01

Why this matters

The Bayesian Inference & Updating lesson derived Bayes' theorem and showed, with a single flagged-day example, that a prior and a likelihood combine into a posterior. What it didn't show is how you'd actually compute that posterior when the "belief" isn't a single number like "20% chance of a trend day" but an entire distribution over a range of possible values — for instance, "I'm not sure if the true trend-day rate is 15%, 25%, or 35%, but I have some idea it's somewhere in that neighborhood." Representing and updating a whole distribution, one observation at a time, is what the rest of applied Bayesian statistics is built on.

In general, combining an arbitrary prior distribution with an arbitrary likelihood produces a posterior with no simple closed form — you'd need numerical integration or simulation to even evaluate it (that's the subject of the next lesson, MCMC: Metropolis-Hastings & Gibbs Sampling). Conjugate priors are the handful of special cases where the prior and likelihood are matched so that the posterior lands in exactly the same distribution family as the prior — only the parameters change. That makes the update an algebra problem instead of a numerical-methods problem, and it's why every textbook introduction to Bayesian updating starts here: it's the version you can compute by hand, watch narrow update after update, and build intuition from before reaching for simulation-based methods.

💡
Plain-English intuition

A belief as a shape, not a single number

Instead of saying "I believe there's a 30% chance today is a trend day" (one number), imagine you say "I believe the true underlying rate of trend days is somewhere around 30%, but I'm genuinely unsure whether it's 20% or 40%." That fuzzy belief is a whole distribution — a curve that's higher near 30% and lower out at the edges. A conjugate prior is a convenient family of curves (the Beta distribution, for a rate between 0 and 1) that has a special property: when you feed it real data through the likelihood, the resulting updated curve is still a member of the same family — just narrower and shifted toward wherever the data actually pointed. You never have to redraw the whole shape from scratch; you just update two numbers that describe it, and the curve tightens around the truth as more days go by.

How it reached trading

Updating a belief every trade, in closed form

Conjugate updating · Raiffa & Schlaifer, 1961; in markets through Black–Litterman (1990–92) and the Kalman filter

A desk that wants a live estimate of a strategy's win rate, a mean return or a volatility can't run a numerical integration after every fill. Raiffa and Schlaifer, at Harvard Business School, named and catalogued "natural conjugate" priors in 1961 so that business decisions could be updated by hand: the posterior lands in the same family, and updating is arithmetic on a couple of numbers.

That arithmetic is what made Bayesian updating practical in markets. The Black–Litterman posterior is the normal-prior, normal-evidence conjugate case, which is why it has a closed form. The Kalman filter's update step, used for time-varying hedge ratios and betas, is the same Gaussian conjugate algebra run once per bar.

On a desk today, conjugate pairs are the fast first answer: Beta-Binomial for a hit rate or the frequency of a trend day, Normal-Normal for a drifting mean, Gamma-type priors for a variance. When a model steps outside these families, MCMC takes over.

Section 02

The math, step by step

Watch the formula build
Where Beta(α+s, β+n−s) comes from, one term at a time
\[ f(\theta) \propto \color{#fbbf24}{\theta^{\alpha-1}(1-\theta)^{\beta-1}} \]

Start with the prior kernel. \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) behave like pretend counts of successes and failures you have already "seen".

\[ f(\theta \mid \text{data}) \propto \theta^{\alpha-1}(1-\theta)^{\beta-1} \times \color{#fbbf24}{\theta^{s}(1-\theta)^{n-s}} \]

Multiply by the Binomial likelihood kernel: \(s\) real successes and \(n-s\) real failures. Constants that do not involve \(\theta\) drop out.

\[ f(\theta \mid \text{data}) \propto \theta^{\color{#fbbf24}{\alpha+s}-1}(1-\theta)^{\color{#fbbf24}{\beta+n-s}-1} \]

Same bases, so the exponents add. Real counts land on top of the pretend counts.

\[ \theta \mid \text{data} \sim \color{#10b981}{\text{Beta}(\alpha+s,\ \beta+n-s)} \]

That is the kernel of a Beta, so the posterior is a Beta. From \(\text{Beta}(2,3)\) with 3 trend days in 10, you get \(\text{Beta}(5,10)\), mean \(5/15 = 0.333\).

Beta-Binomial conjugacy

Let \(\theta\) be an unknown rate between 0 and 1 — the true long-run fraction of days that are trend days, for instance. The Beta distribution is a natural prior for a rate, because it's defined exactly on the interval \([0,1]\) and can take on almost any shape (flat, peaked, skewed) depending on two parameters \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\):

\[ f(\theta;\alpha,\beta) = \frac{\theta^{\alpha-1}(1-\theta)^{\beta-1}}{B(\alpha,\beta)} \]
θ^(α-1)(1-θ)^(β-1)the "kernel" — the only part that depends on θ
B(α,β)the normalizing constant, so the curve integrates to 1 over [0,1]

You can read \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) as pseudo-counts: \(\alpha\) behaves like "successes I've already imagined seeing" and \(\beta\) like "failures I've already imagined seeing," before any real data arrives. A Beta(2,2) prior is a mild, symmetric belief centered at 0.5; a Beta(2,3) prior is a mild belief centered slightly below 0.5.

Now suppose you actually observe \(n\) independent trials (days) and count \(s\) successes (trend days) and \(n-s\) failures. The Binomial likelihood for a fixed true rate \(\theta\) is:

\[ L(\theta) = \binom{n}{s}\theta^{s}(1-\theta)^{n-s} \]
C(n,s)number of ways to arrange s successes among n trials — doesn't depend on θ
θ^s(1-θ)^(n-s)the part that actually depends on θ

Bayes' theorem says the posterior is proportional to prior times likelihood. The normalizing constants (\(B(\alpha,\beta)\) and \(\binom{n}{s}\)) don't involve \(\theta\), so they can be dropped and restored at the end — all that matters is what happens to the \(\theta\)-dependent kernels when you multiply them:

\[ f(\theta \mid \text{data}) \propto \underbrace{\theta^{\alpha-1}(1-\theta)^{\beta-1}}_{\text{prior kernel}} \times \underbrace{\theta^{s}(1-\theta)^{n-s}}_{\text{likelihood kernel}} = \theta^{\alpha+s-1}(1-\theta)^{\beta+(n-s)-1} \]
θ^(α+s-1)exponents on θ simply add: (α-1)+s
(1-θ)^(β+n-s-1)exponents on (1-θ) simply add: (β-1)+(n-s)

That last expression is exactly the kernel of a \(\text{Beta}(\alpha+s,\ \beta+n-s)\) distribution. Since a Beta distribution is completely determined by its kernel (there's only one normalizing constant that makes any given kernel integrate to 1), this proves the posterior is \(\text{Beta}(\alpha+s,\ \beta+n-s)\) — no numerical integration was ever needed. This is what "conjugate" means: the prior family (Beta) and the posterior family (also Beta) are the same, for this particular likelihood (Binomial).

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

A conjugate prior is a shortcut: pick the right kind of starting guess, and updating it after new evidence becomes plain addition instead of a whole fresh calculation. For Beta-Binomial, picture \(\alpha\) as a jar already holding some pretend "yes" tokens and \(\beta\) as a jar holding some pretend "no" tokens, from before you saw any real data. Every real success or failure you observe, you just drop one more matching token into the right jar — the new totals are your new belief, no rebuilding required.

The posterior mean falls out immediately from the standard Beta-mean formula, \(a/(a+b)\), applied to the new parameters:

\[ E[\theta \mid \text{data}] = \frac{\alpha+s}{\alpha+\beta+n} \]
α+βthe prior's "weight," in units of pseudo-observations
nthe real data's weight, in units of actual observations

Read this as a weighted average: the posterior mean sits between the prior mean \(\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)\) and the raw sample proportion \(s/n\), with the weights being how many pseudo-observations the prior is worth (\(\alpha+\beta\)) versus how many real observations you actually collected (\(n\)). A prior built from only 2-3 pseudo-observations gets swamped quickly by real data; a prior built to feel like 200 pseudo-observations takes a lot of real data to move. The posterior variance shrinks as data accumulates:

\[ \text{Var}[\theta \mid \text{data}] = \frac{(\alpha+s)(\beta+n-s)}{(\alpha+\beta+n)^2(\alpha+\beta+n+1)} \]

Notice the denominator grows roughly with \(n^3\) while the numerator grows roughly with \(n^2\) — so the variance shrinks toward zero as more data arrives, which is exactly the "narrowing belief" the chart below shows happening live.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Two things happen together as more days come in: your best guess drifts toward whatever the real data is actually showing, and you get more confident in that guess (the curve gets taller and skinnier). It's like guessing a new coworker's age — with zero information your guess might be spread thinly across 25 to 45, but after a few conversations you both narrow in on a number and feel a lot surer of it, even if your central guess only moves a little.

Normal-Normal conjugacy (known variance)

The same idea applies to an unknown mean instead of an unknown rate. Suppose your prior belief about some parameter \(\mu\) (say, a strategy's true average daily return) is itself normal, with a known variance \(\sigma^2\) for the data-generating process:

\[ \mu \sim N(\mu_0, \sigma_0^2), \qquad x_1,\dots,x_n \mid \mu \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2) \text{ i.i.d.} \]
μ₀, σ₀²prior mean and prior variance — how sure you are before any data
σ²the (known, assumed) variance of each individual observation

It's cleanest to work in precision — the reciprocal of variance, \(\tau = 1/\sigma^2\) — because precision, unlike variance, simply adds when you combine independent sources of information. The prior contributes precision \(\tau_0 = 1/\sigma_0^2\); the data (via its sample mean \(\bar{x}\), whose own sampling variance is \(\sigma^2/n\)) contributes precision \(\tau_{\text{data}} = n/\sigma^2\). The posterior distribution of \(\mu\) is then, again, exactly normal:

\[ \mu \mid x_1,\dots,x_n \;\sim\; N\!\left(\mu_n,\ \sigma_n^2\right), \qquad \mu_n = \frac{\tau_0\,\mu_0 + \tau_{\text{data}}\,\bar{x}}{\tau_0+\tau_{\text{data}}}, \qquad \sigma_n^2 = \frac{1}{\tau_0+\tau_{\text{data}}} \]
μₙposterior mean — a precision-weighted average of prior mean and sample mean
σₙ²posterior variance — the reciprocal of total precision, always smaller than either source alone

In words: posterior precision is just prior precision plus data precision — information always accumulates, never disappears — and the posterior mean is a precision-weighted average of what you believed before and what the data says, where a more precise (lower-variance) source automatically gets more weight without you having to decide that by hand.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Precision is just "how sure am I," turned into a number — the more sure you are, the bigger the number. If two friends both guess how many jellybeans are in a jar, and one of them is a jar-guessing expert (very precise) while the other is just eyeballing it (not very precise), the sensible combined guess should lean much more heavily toward the expert's number. Precision-weighted averaging does exactly that automatically: the more confident source gets more say, without anyone having to decide that by hand.

A quick numeric check: suppose a prior belief about a strategy's true daily drift is \(\mu_0=0\%\) with \(\sigma_0=0.5\%\) (so \(\tau_0 = 1/0.25 = 4\)), and \(n=20\) days are observed with sample mean \(\bar{x}=0.15\%\), assuming a known per-day volatility \(\sigma=1.0\%\) (so \(\tau_{\text{data}} = 20/1 = 20\)). Then \(\mu_n = (4\times 0 + 20\times 0.15)/24 = 3/24 = 0.125\%\) — pulled most of the way toward the sample mean because the data's precision (20) dwarfs the prior's precision (4), but not all the way, because the prior still contributes a little pull toward zero. The posterior variance is \(\sigma_n^2 = 1/24 \approx 0.0417\), so \(\sigma_n \approx 0.204\%\) — narrower than either \(\sigma_0=0.5\%\) or the data's own standard error of the mean, \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}=0.224\%\), because it combines both sources.

Beta(1,1) (flat over [0,1]) still asserts that every rate from 0% to 100% is equally plausible beforehand — itself a strong claim in most real settings, where you'd never seriously entertain a 95% trend-day rate. Worked example

Updating a belief about the trend-day base rate

Start with a mildly informative prior, \(\text{Beta}(2,3)\) — mean \(2/5=0.40\), representing "I lean toward thinking trend days are a bit less than half of all days, but I'm not confident." Its variance is \(\frac{2\times 3}{5^2\times 6}=\frac{6}{150}=0.04\), so its standard deviation is \(\sqrt{0.04}=0.20\) — a wide, uncertain belief.

Round 1 — 10 days observed, 3 are trend days. Posterior is \(\text{Beta}(2+3,\ 3+7)=\text{Beta}(5,10)\). New mean: \(5/15=0.333\). New variance: \(\frac{5\times 10}{15^2\times 16}=\frac{50}{3600}=0.01389\), so standard deviation \(\sqrt{0.01389}=0.118\) — already less than half as wide as before.

Round 2 — 30 more days observed, 9 more are trend days (cumulative: 40 days, 12 trend days). Treat the round-1 posterior as round-2's prior and update again — or equivalently, since Beta-Binomial updating doesn't care whether the data arrives in one batch or many, just add all the counts to the original prior at once: \(\text{Beta}(2+12,\ 3+28)=\text{Beta}(14,31)\). New mean: \(14/45=0.311\) — much closer to the data's own 30% sample rate. New variance: \(\frac{14\times 31}{45^2\times 46}=\frac{434}{93150}=0.00466\), standard deviation \(\sqrt{0.00466}=0.068\).

The belief's standard deviation went \(0.20 \to 0.118 \to 0.068\) — it more than halved, then nearly halved again, while the mean drifted from an assumed 0.40 down to an observed-consistent 0.311. That is the entire lesson in three numbers: the posterior mean moves toward the data, and the posterior spread shrinks, automatically, with no separate "confidence" calculation bolted on — the width of the distribution is the confidence statement.

Real-world scenario

A trader tracking how often a level actually holds

A discretionary trader keeps a private log: "when price touches a prior-day high with volume confirmation, does it hold as resistance?" They start with an honest, weakly-informed prior — say \(\text{Beta}(3,3)\), a 50/50 guess with only mild conviction — because they don't yet have a real track record for this exact setup. After 15 occurrences (9 held, 6 didn't), their belief becomes \(\text{Beta}(12,9)\), mean \(12/21\approx 0.571\), noticeably tighter than the starting guess. After 60 more occurrences accumulate over the following months (38 held, 22 didn't), the belief becomes \(\text{Beta}(50,31)\), mean \(50/81\approx 0.617\), and — because the variance formula's denominator grows roughly with the cube of the total count — the belief is now dramatically narrower than after the first 15 observations, even though the mean barely moved. This is the honest, quantified version of "I'm getting more confident in this setup the more times I see it work" — a feeling every discretionary trader has, made precise instead of vibes-based. It says nothing about whether \(0.617\) is a good enough hit rate to trade profitably after costs — that's a separate question this framework doesn't answer on its own.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

Conjugate Beta-Binomial and Normal-Normal updating show up anywhere a rate or a mean needs to be tracked and refined as small batches of new evidence arrive, especially when a fast, closed-form answer matters more than modeling every last real-world wrinkle:

🖱️

Click-through / conversion-rate testing

Beta-Binomial is the standard closed-form model behind "Bayesian A/B testing" tools — each variant's true conversion rate gets its own Beta posterior, updated live as visits and conversions accumulate.

⚙️

Reliability & quality control

Manufacturing defect rates and hardware failure rates are natural Beta-Binomial problems — a small early sample gives a wide posterior; as production volume grows, the estimate of the true defect rate tightens.

📈

Rolling estimates that must stay fast

Normal-Normal updating is the conceptual seed behind the Kalman Filter lesson's recursive mean/variance tracking — the same precision-weighted-average logic, extended to a value that drifts over time instead of staying fixed.

The common thread is speed and interpretability over generality: a conjugate update is one line of arithmetic, requires no simulation, and every intermediate quantity (the pseudo-counts, the precision) has a plain-English meaning. The cost is that conjugacy only exists for a handful of matched prior/likelihood pairs — the moment a real problem needs, say, a mixture of behaviors, a bounded-but-skewed variance, or a likelihood with no algebraic closed form, conjugacy breaks and you need the simulation-based machinery covered in the next lesson, MCMC: Metropolis-Hastings & Gibbs Sampling.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where conjugate updating quietly goes wrong

Treating i.i.d. as automatic. Both derivations above assume each new observation is independent of the others given \(\theta\) (or \(\mu\)). Consecutive trading days are frequently not independent — volatility clusters, and one trend day raises the chance the next day is also a trend day. Feeding autocorrelated data into a plain Beta-Binomial update overstates how quickly the posterior should narrow.

Forgetting the "known variance" assumption in Normal-Normal. The clean closed form above required \(\sigma^2\) to be a known constant. If \(\sigma^2\) is itself unknown and must be estimated from the same data (the realistic case almost always), the conjugate pair is Normal-Inverse-Gamma instead, and the posterior for \(\mu\) alone becomes a (fatter-tailed) Student-\(t\), not a Normal — using the Normal formula anyway understates uncertainty.

A tight posterior is not validation. A narrow Beta posterior after 200 observations says "I'm confident about what happened in this sample" — it says nothing about whether the sampled period was representative of the future, or whether the underlying process is even stationary. Conjugate updating narrows a belief about the past; it doesn't certify the future will look the same.

Picking a prior after peeking at the data. Choosing \(\alpha,\beta\) (or \(\mu_0,\sigma_0^2\)) to make the posterior land somewhere you already wanted is p-hacking wearing a Bayesian costume. State the prior and its justification (a real historical base rate, ideally) before looking at the evidence being used to update it.

Scenario drill · an illustrative trading log

Fifteen occurrences in, and the setup looks good

You log one setup: price touches the prior day's high with volume confirmation, and you note whether it holds as resistance. You started with an honest, mild \(\text{Beta}(3,3)\) prior. After 15 occurrences, 9 held, so your belief is \(\text{Beta}(12,9)\), mean about 0.571. This morning the setup appears again. What do you do with the estimate?

  1. Treat 57% as the true hold rate and size the trade as if it were known.
    \(\text{Beta}(12,9)\) still has a spread of about 0.11. A true rate anywhere from the high 40s to the high 60s fits what you have seen. The width of the curve is the confidence, and right now it is wide.
  2. Trade it small, add today's result to the counts, and let the curve narrow before you size up.
    This is what conjugate updating is for. Each occurrence is one more count, and the spread shrinks with every one. You size to the belief you have, not the one you hope to have.
  3. Switch to a heavier, more optimistic prior, now that you can see the setup works.
    Choosing the prior after peeking at the data is p-hacking in a Bayesian costume. The prior should be stated before the evidence that updates it.
  4. Ignore the log until you have 100 occurrences.
    Safe, but it wastes what you already know. The posterior tells you exactly how unsure you are at 15; you do not need a magic sample size to use it.

What to take from it. This is an illustrative situation built on the lesson's own trader log, not a historical event. In the lesson, 60 more occurrences (38 held) take the belief to \(\text{Beta}(50,31)\): mean about 0.617, with the spread down from about 0.11 to about 0.05. The mean barely moved; the confidence did. Even then, a hit rate says nothing about profit after costs.

Section 04

Key Takeaways

✓Conjugate = same family in, same family out. Beta prior + Binomial likelihood → Beta posterior; Normal prior + Normal likelihood (known variance) → Normal posterior. No numerical integration needed.
✓Beta-Binomial update is just addition: posterior is Beta(α+successes, β+failures) — the prior's pseudo-counts and the real counts simply combine.
✓Normal-Normal update is a precision-weighted average: posterior precision = prior precision + data precision; posterior mean weights each source by how precise it is.
⚠A "flat" prior is still an assumption. Beta(1,1) asserts every rate is equally plausible beforehand — state and justify the chosen prior rather than treating any default as assumption-free.
⚠Conjugacy is the exception, not the rule. Most real-world likelihoods have no matching conjugate prior at all — that's the entire motivation for MCMC, covered next.
✗A narrow posterior is not the same as a validated edge. It quantifies confidence about the observed sample, not about whether that sample generalizes — the honest OOS discipline this curriculum applies everywhere else still applies here.
ℹHow it reached trading: closed-form updates, every bar. Raiffa & Schlaifer (1961) built them for hand calculation; in markets the same algebra powers Black–Litterman's posterior and the Kalman filter's update step.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. What does it mean for a prior to be "conjugate" to a likelihood?
It means the posterior distribution (prior × likelihood, renormalized) belongs to the same distribution family as the prior itself — only the family's parameters change. For a Beta prior combined with a Binomial likelihood, the posterior is again a Beta distribution, just with updated parameters (α+successes, β+failures). Conjugacy is a property of a specific prior/likelihood pairing, not a general fact about all Bayesian updates.
Q2. If the prior is Beta(4,6) and you observe 8 successes in 20 trials, what is the exact posterior?
Beta(4+8, 6+12) = Beta(12, 18). The posterior mean is 12/30 = 0.40, compared to the prior mean of 4/10 = 0.40 (unchanged here purely by coincidence, since the observed rate 8/20=0.40 matched the prior mean exactly) and the raw sample rate of 8/20 = 0.40.
Q3. In the Normal-Normal case, why is it easier to reason in terms of precision (1/variance) than variance directly?
Because precisions from independent sources of information simply add (posterior precision = prior precision + data precision), while variances combine through a less intuitive reciprocal-sum formula. Working in precision turns "how confident am I" into a quantity that accumulates additively as more independent evidence arrives, which is also why the posterior mean is naturally expressed as a precision-weighted average of the prior mean and the sample mean.
Q4. Why does a prior with a large α+β (e.g. Beta(40,60)) resist being moved by new data more than a prior with a small α+β (e.g. Beta(2,3))?
Because α+β is literally the prior's weight in pseudo-observations in the posterior-mean formula (α+s)/(α+β+n) — a Beta(40,60) prior behaves as if 100 pseudo-observations had already been seen, so a real sample of, say, n=20 only shifts the total count from 100 to 120, a small relative change. A Beta(2,3) prior only "weighs" 5 pseudo-observations, so the same n=20 real observations dominate the total (5 vs 20) and move the posterior mean much further.
Q5. Why doesn't conjugate updating exist for every possible prior/likelihood combination?
Conjugacy requires the algebraic form of the prior's kernel and the likelihood's kernel to multiply into something that is still a recognizable, normalizable distribution kernel from the same family — this only happens for a short, specific list of matched pairs (Beta-Binomial, Normal-Normal, Gamma-Poisson, and a handful of others). Most real likelihoods — especially anything nonlinear or built from a complex model — simply don't produce a tidy algebraic match with any standard prior family, which is exactly why simulation-based methods like MCMC exist: to compute a posterior numerically when no closed form is available.
