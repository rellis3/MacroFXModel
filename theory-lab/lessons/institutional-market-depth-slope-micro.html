Market Depth & Order-Book Slope, What the Book Looks Like Before Anyone Trades It

Every earlier microstructure signal in this Theory Lab looks at trades that already happened, or at changes to the book over time. This deck asks a plainer question: forget what just happened — what does the resting book look like right now? Depth, slope, and depth imbalance answer that from a single snapshot, no trade history required.

The whole idea
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

My screen shows one bid and one offer, one pip apart. Is that the liquidity?

It is the front door. Depth is everything resting behind it: how much size sits at the best price, and at each price further out.

Give me the lesson's book.

Bids of three hundred, two hundred and fifty and four hundred on the first three levels. Offers of two hundred, a hundred and fifty and a hundred and eighty. Add them up as you go and the bids total nine hundred and fifty within three levels. The offers only five hundred and thirty.

And slope?

How fast that running total grows per tick away from the touch. On the bid side it averages two hundred and eighty-seven and a half per tick. On the offer side, a hundred and fifty-seven and a half. The bid side builds depth nearly twice as fast.

So a steep book is good?

Careful, that word cuts both ways. In this convention a bigger slope means a deeper book. But a trader walking the book plots price against size filled, and then a thin book looks steep, because the price jumps fast. Always ask which way someone is measuring.

And the imbalance?

Bid size minus offer size, over the total. At the touch it is three hundred against two hundred: plus zero point two, mildly bid-heavy. Use all three levels and it is about plus zero point two eight. More bid-heavy the deeper you look.

Which one do I use?

Depends on your order. If you trade more than what sits at the touch, the deeper number is the one that bites. The touch number feeds the microprice, which here sits a tenth of a pip above the mid.

Isn't this the same as order flow imbalance?

No. Depth imbalance is a photograph of what is resting now. Order flow imbalance is a flip-book of how the book has been changing. A side can be heavy right now and still be draining fast.

When has depth mattered for real?

The Treasury flash rally of the fifteenth of October 2014. The ten-year yield fell about sixteen basis points and came back within about twelve minutes. The official report on it documented how thin order book depth had become around the event.

And day to day?

Read depth a few levels deep before you size a child order. A thin, shallow side means smaller slices and more patience.

One line?

The quote tells you the price. Depth, slope and imbalance tell you what it will cost to move size through it.

Section 1 · How it reached trading

How much size can the book take right now?

Order-book depth and slope · electronic limit-order books from the 1980s–90s; Næs & Skjeltorp, 2006

How it reached trading

Depth beyond the best quote was invisible until electronic books (Toronto 1977, Paris 1986, London 1997) published the whole queue.

Biais, Hillion & Spatt (1995) and Næs & Skjeltorp (2006) measured its shape; regulators used thinning depth to explain the October 2014 Treasury flash rally.

Section 3

Depth: level-1 vs. cumulative

The touch alone is rarely the whole story once an order is bigger than what's sitting right there.

Two ways to measure "how much is here"
Level-1 depth
=
Q(1) — size at the touch only
Cumulative depth D(n)
=
Q(1) + Q(2) + … + Q(n)
D(n) answers what level-1 depth alone can't: how much size would actually need to be consumed to walk the book out to n ticks from the touch?
Section 4

Order-book slope — two conventions, both real

Depth-vs-distance (this deck's convention)
  • slope = average per-tick growth of D(n) beyond the touch
  • LARGER slope = depth builds up fast = deep, liquid book
  • Næs & Skjeltorp (2006) construction
Walk-the-book (colloquial)
  • Plot price reached vs. quantity consumed — the inverse view
  • STEEP price curve = thin, dangerous book
  • The numerical opposite direction of the first convention
Same book, opposite word

"Steep" means a deep book under one convention and a thin book under the other. Neither usage is wrong — always check which axis a "slope" figure is actually computed against before deciding whether a large number is good news or bad news.

Section 6

Depth imbalance is exactly QI — reused, not reinvented

Same formula, this deck's own notation
DI(1)
=
(Q_b(1) − Q_a(1)) / (Q_b(1) + Q_a(1))
=
QI
Because DI(1) = QI exactly, the OFI deck's own microprice identity plugs straight in: P_micro = M + (S/2)·QI. Nothing new is derived here — DI(1) is fed into a formula that already existed.

What's genuinely new: extending the same ratio to cumulative depth, DI(n) = (D_b(n) − D_a(n)) / (D_b(n) + D_a(n)) — useful once an order is larger than what's resting at the touch alone.

Section 7

Level-1 vs. cumulative — they can disagree

Same toy book throughout: bid 300/250/400 across 3 levels, ask 200/150/180.

Try it
Bid: 60%
Ask: 40%
DI(1) = (300−200)/(300+200) = 0.200 — mildly bid-heavy at the touch alone.
Bid: 64.2%
Ask: 35.8%
DI(3) = (950−530)/(950+530) = 0.284 — MORE bid-heavy than the level-1 reading, because the bid's extra depth further out outpaces the ask's.

Same book, same instant — only how many levels are included changes the answer. This is a real design choice, not a rounding difference.

Section 9

Where this quietly goes wrong

👻

Displayed ≠ committed

Icebergs hide true size; spoofing fabricates size that was never meant to trade. Every formula here takes displayed size at face value.

🧩

FX has no single book

Spot FX is OTC across fragmented ECNs — one venue's slope isn't "the market's" depth. This repo's own OANDA spot venue has no live top-of-book ladder at all.

⏱️

Snapshots go stale in milliseconds

Depth and imbalance can flip entirely on the very next book update — treat either as an instant read, never a forecast.

🔀

Check the slope convention

"Steep" means opposite things under the two conventions from Section 4 — confirm which axis before judging a number.

Section 10

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

What's the difference between level-1 depth and cumulative depth D(n)?

D(n) answers how much size must be consumed to walk the book to n ticks — a question level-1 depth alone can't answer.
Question 2

Under this deck's stated convention, does a LARGER slope value mean a deeper book or a thinner one?

The walk-the-book convention inverts this — a deep book gives a FLAT price curve there. Both usages exist; check which one a source means.
Question 3

How does DI(1) relate to QI from the Order Flow Imbalance deck?

DI(1) = (Q_b−Q_a)/(Q_b+Q_a) = QI exactly, so the OFI deck's microprice identity plugs in unchanged.
Question 4

Can DI(1) and DI(n) for a larger n disagree on the same book, at the same instant?

A book can be more (or less) lopsided a few ticks back than right at the touch — which n you choose is a real, consequential decision.
Question 5

Why can depth imbalance and Order Flow Imbalance point in opposite directions at the same moment?

A book can be bid-heavy right now while that same size has been shrinking over recent updates — a photograph and a flip-book, both true at once.
Question 6

How does order-book slope differ from Kyle's λ?

Related in spirit — both describe how costly size is — but built from entirely different data using entirely different methods.

Scenario drill · 15 October 2014

A large Treasury futures order on a morning when the book is thin

It is the morning of 15 October 2014. Treasuries have been rallying, and when you look past the best bid and offer in the 10-year futures book, the size resting within a few ticks is far below what you normally see: cumulative depth barely grows beyond the touch. You have a large sell order to complete before noon. How do you work it?

  1. Send the whole order as one market order now and be done with it.
    With little resting beyond the touch, a large market order walks through level after level, and each one is further away. A shallow slope is exactly the warning that size will move the price fast.
  2. Read cumulative depth a few levels deep, cut child orders well below what is resting there, and work them patiently.
    This sizes each slice to the liquidity that is actually there rather than to the quote. On a thin day, smaller slices and more time keep each one from walking the book.
  3. The quoted spread is still one tick, so size the slices as you would on a normal day.
    The quote shows only the front door. Depth and slope are what tell you the cost of size, and both are saying this is not a normal day.
  4. Hold the whole order until depth recovers, then trade normally.
    It avoids walking a thin book, but you carry the full position and its price risk while you wait, and you still have a deadline. Patient slicing gets some of it done safely.

What happened. Between about 9:33 and 9:45 a.m. New York time, the 10-year Treasury yield fell roughly 16 basis points and then came back, with no clear news to explain it. The US agencies' joint staff report on the event documented how thin order book depth was around the move. A large order sent into that book would have paid heavily for walking it.

Section 11

What to carry forward

01

Depth (level-1 and cumulative D(n)) and order-book slope describe the SHAPE of the resting book from a single snapshot — no trade history needed at all, unlike every other signal in this Theory Lab.

02

Slope's direction is genuinely convention-dependent: this deck's depth-vs-distance convention says larger = deeper; the walk-the-book convention says the opposite — always check which axis before calling a number "steep."

03

Depth imbalance at level 1 is exactly QI from the Order Flow Imbalance deck, feeding the same microprice identity unchanged — extended here to cumulative depth, DI(n), which can disagree with DI(1).

04

Depth imbalance (a resting snapshot) and OFI (a sequence of book changes) are genuinely different signals that can point opposite ways at the same instant — neither substitutes for the other.

05

Displayed depth can hide real size (icebergs) or fabricate it (spoofing), and FX's fragmented, OTC structure means no single book ever describes "the whole market" anyway.

06

How it reached trading: electronic books made depth visible; desks and regulators now read it as a live liquidity gauge before sizing orders.

What this now lets you do

Read a raw ladder of resting bid/ask sizes and turn it into cumulative depth, a slope estimate under a clearly-stated convention, and a depth imbalance that plugs directly into the microprice — and know exactly how that reading differs from Order Flow Imbalance and from Kyle's λ.
