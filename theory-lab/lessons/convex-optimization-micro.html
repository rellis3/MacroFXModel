Convex Optimization, Or: How to Know You Found the Best Answer

Gradient descent and Newton's method are both very good at finding a point where the slope goes flat — but a flat slope only tells you the search has stopped, not that it stopped somewhere good. Convexity is the one shape-property of a problem that closes that gap completely: on a convex problem, the point where the search stops is provably the best point there is, anywhere. This is why Markowitz portfolio construction is reliably solvable, and exactly what breaks the moment a realistic trading rule gets added.

Section 1

A bowl has one bottom; an egg carton has many

"Convex" is a shape claim, not a calculus trick — and it's checkable just by looking.

🥣

Bowl-shaped, informally

Pick any two points on the curve and draw a straight line ("chord") between them. A convex function's actual curve never pokes up above that chord anywhere in between — it always sits at or below it.

📐

Bowl-shaped, formally

f(θx + (1−θ)y) ≤ θf(x) + (1−θ)f(y) for every θ ∈ [0,1]. The left side is the curve's real value between x and y; the right side is the chord's value at that same point.

🥚

The alternative: an egg carton

A non-convex function can have several separate dips — some local minima, only one of them the true global minimum. Nothing about a flat slope tells you which kind of dip you're standing in.

△

The set of allowed answers matters too

A convex set is one where the straight line between any two allowed points stays entirely inside the set. A convex function minimized over a convex set is the full definition of a convex optimization problem — both halves are required.

Where this needs qualifying

Convexity of the objective alone isn't enough. A perfectly convex function minimized over a non-convex feasible set — exactly what a "hold at most 10 names" rule produces, covered later in this deck — loses the guarantee entirely. Both the function's shape and the shape of the allowed region have to be convex.

Section 2

Drop a ball in each shape and watch where it stops

Same idea, two shapes. Where a marble starts only matters in one of them.

Try it
Wherever the ball starts, it rolls to the exact same bottom. There is only one place it can end up — so "the ball stopped rolling" and "the ball found the lowest point there is" mean the same thing.
Start A rolls into the shallow dip and stops — a flat slope, every appearance of success — and never discovers the deeper dip sitting just past the hump. Which dip you land in depends entirely on where you started.

This is the entire practical stakes of convexity in one picture. Gradient descent and Newton's method can only feel the local slope under wherever they currently stand — exactly like a marble that can't see over the next hill. In the bowl, that's all the information they need. In the egg carton, it isn't nearly enough.

Section 3

Checking convexity without drawing every chord

Testing the chord inequality for every pair of points would take forever. Curvature gives a shortcut.

′′

One dimension: f''(x) ≥ 0

The function is convex exactly when its curvature never bends downward anywhere — always curving upward like a genuine bowl, or momentarily flat, never the wrong way.

H

Many dimensions: the Hessian

Stack every second partial derivative into a matrix H. It's the many-variable generalization of f''(x) — curvature in every direction, at once.

PSD

vᵀHv ≥ 0, every direction v

"Positive semi-definite" (PSD) means the curvature is never negative no matter which direction you look. PSD everywhere ⇔ convex — the operational test used throughout this lesson.

⚡

Sylvester's shortcut

For a small matrix by hand: it's PSD if every leading principal minor (the 1×1, then 2×2, …) is non-negative. The live calculator later in this deck runs exactly this check.

This is not a coincidence waiting to happen later: a Hessian and a covariance matrix are both symmetric matrices being asked the exact same question — "is there any direction in which this matrix produces a negative number?" That question is about to reappear wearing Markowitz's clothing.
Section 4

Watch convexity actually break

Drag the slider to add a wiggle term to a plain bowl. Watch new local minima appear — and the guarantee disappear with them.

Try it
Wiggle amplitude A — f(x) = 0.06x² + A·sin(1.3x)A = 0.00
A = 0: a plain bowl. Exactly 1 local minimum — automatically the global minimum.

At A = 0 this is a pure quadratic bowl: convex, one minimum, no ambiguity. As A grows, the sine term adds curvature that periodically bends the wrong way — the Hessian test from the previous slide starts failing at those points — and new local minima peel off from the single bottom. Once there's more than one, "gradient descent stopped here" no longer implies "this is the best answer anywhere."

Section 5

The one guarantee that makes this all worth it

Why "local minimum" and "global minimum" become the same sentence on a convex function.

local minimum x*
+ convexity ⇒
global minimum

Suppose x* is only a local minimum, and some other point y elsewhere scores strictly better. Convexity forces every point on the straight line from x* toward y — including points arbitrarily close to x* — to score no worse than a straight blend of f(x*) and f(y), which is strictly better than f(x*) itself. That means a point inside x*'s own neighborhood beats x*, contradicting the assumption that x* was a local minimum in the first place. No such y can exist. Every local minimum of a convex function is automatically the global minimum — not confirmed by searching everywhere, but made logically impossible to beat, by the shape of the function alone.

The payoff

For a convex problem, gradient descent or Newton's method finding a point with zero gradient is enough to conclude it found the best point, full stop — no separate search over the rest of the space is ever needed.

Section 6

Why Markowitz's variance term is always convex

The mean-variance objective from this curriculum's Markowitz & Black-Litterman lesson is a convex quadratic — with no extra assumptions needed.

wᵀΣw

Portfolio variance is a quadratic form

Variance in the weight vector w, with covariance matrix Σ sitting in the middle. Its Hessian works out to a constant, 2Σ — the same matrix, regardless of w.

Σ

A valid covariance matrix is always PSD

Built from real variances and covariances of real random variables — and a variance can never be negative, in any direction of combination. That's structural, not assumed.

✓

PSD Hessian ⇒ convex, always

Since 2Σ is PSD everywhere, portfolio variance is convex everywhere, for any real covariance matrix — exactly the Hessian test from two slides ago, applied directly.

⊞

Linear constraints keep the set convex too

Weights summing to 1, a target return, no-shorting box constraints — all linear, so all convexity-preserving. Objective + set: a genuine convex quadratic program.

This is exactly why mean-variance optimization is reliably, provably solvable rather than a heuristic search — the deeper mechanics of the covariance matrix, the weight constraints, and the actual worked minimum-variance portfolio are covered in this curriculum's Markowitz & Black-Litterman lesson.

Grounded against this repo's actual code

The full lesson tags this material "CONCEPT — not built or tested in this repo," and that check still holds: portfolioBacktest/ in this codebase implements a cointegration pairs-trading backtester, with no mean-variance solver, QP library, or covariance-based optimizer anywhere in it. A symmetric matrix is PSD exactly when both eigenvalues are ≥ 0 — equivalently, a ≥ 0, d ≥ 0, and det ≥ 0. If a quadratic form vᵀMv is built from a non-PSD matrix, it is not convex: it curves upward in some directions and downward in others, an egg-carton shape, not a bowl.

Try pushing ρ (correlation) toward 1 by raising b close to √(ad) — the determinant collapses toward zero and the smaller eigenvalue follows it down, right to the boundary between strictly convex and merely flat. Push b past that point and the matrix stops being PSD entirely.

Section 8

The checklist a convex optimum must pass

The KKT conditions — necessary and, for a convex problem, sufficient to certify a true global optimum.

∇L=0

Stationarity

The Lagrangian's gradient vanishes — the direct generalization of "the gradient is zero," now accounting for every constraint's pull on the solution.

✓

Primal feasibility

The candidate point actually satisfies every original constraint. A mathematically convenient point that breaks a constraint isn't a valid answer.

λ≥0

Dual feasibility

Every inequality-constraint multiplier is non-negative — a negative one would mean a constraint is rewarded for being violated.

λg=0

Complementary slackness

Each constraint is either exactly binding (sitting on its boundary) or its multiplier is exactly zero. This is how a solver identifies which constraints actually shape the answer.

For a genuinely convex problem, passing this whole checklist is enough on its own to certify the global optimum — no separate search required. That sufficiency, not just necessity, is itself a special benefit convexity provides and non-convex problems don't get.
Section 9

"Just hold at most 10 names" breaks all of it

One realistic-sounding trading rule, two very different problems underneath.

Position caps — stays convex
  • wᵢ ≥ 0 (no shorting) or wᵢ ≤ 0.1 (position cap)
  • Both are linear constraints — the feasible set stays convex
  • Still a convex QP, solved to guaranteed optimality in well under a second
Cardinality — destroys convexity
  • "At most 10 nonzero weights" — a discrete choice of which 10 of N assets
  • The feasible set becomes a union of many separate flat pieces, not one convex region
  • Local optimum ≠ global optimum guarantee is gone; the problem is combinatorially hard (NP-hard)

The straight line between a point using one subset of 10 assets and a point using a different subset of 10 assets typically passes through weight vectors with more than 10 nonzero entries — outside the allowed set entirely. That single fact is the whole reason cardinality constraints break convexity.

The practical fallout

Practitioners don't abandon the convex core — they layer a heuristic on top: greedy asset selection then a convex re-solve, an ℓ₁-penalty relaxation (the same trick behind LASSO regression), or a mixed-integer solver that handles the discreteness directly but scales far worse and offers weaker optimality guarantees than a convex QP solver ever needs.

Section 10

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Informally, what does it mean for a function to be convex?

f(θx+(1−θ)y) ≤ θf(x)+(1−θ)f(y): the curve's real value between two points never exceeds the chord connecting them — bowl-shaped, not wavy.
Question 2

What is the single most important practical guarantee convexity provides?

A convex function's shape makes a hidden, better answer elsewhere logically impossible — so "the search stopped" and "the search found the best point" mean the same thing.
Question 3

Why is Markowitz's portfolio variance, wᵀΣw, guaranteed convex for any valid covariance matrix Σ?

Variance can never be negative in any combination of assets, which is exactly what "Σ is PSD" means — so the Hessian 2Σ is PSD everywhere and the objective is convex, no extra assumptions needed.
Question 4

A symmetric 2×2 matrix has a negative determinant. What does that tell you?

det = λ₁λ₂. A negative determinant means the eigenvalues have opposite signs, so at least one is negative — curving downward in some direction. Not PSD, not convex.
Question 5

What happens when a "hold at most 10 names" cardinality constraint is added to the Markowitz problem?

The objective stays convex — it's the feasible set that breaks: "at most 10 nonzero weights" is a union of separate flat pieces, not one convex region, and the problem becomes combinatorially hard rather than a smooth convex QP.
Section 11

What to carry forward

01

Convex means bowl-shaped — the chord between any two points on the curve never dips below the curve: f(θx+(1−θ)y) ≤ θf(x)+(1−θ)f(y).

02

The Hessian test operationalizes it: a twice-differentiable function is convex exactly when its Hessian is positive semi-definite everywhere.

03

The payoff: on a convex problem, any local minimum is automatically the global minimum — no hidden better answer can exist elsewhere.

04

Non-convex problems have no such guarantee — multiple local minima can trap a solver in a "false" optimum that looks like success.

05

Markowitz's variance term wᵀΣw is always convex, because a valid covariance matrix Σ is always positive semi-definite — the reason mean-variance optimization is reliably solvable.

06

A realistic-sounding rule (cardinality — "at most 10 names") can destroy convexity entirely, turning a clean convex QP into a combinatorial, NP-hard search.

What this now lets you do

Look at a solver's "converged" result and ask the right next question: was the underlying problem actually convex, or just comfortably stopped? Check a symmetric matrix for positive semi-definiteness by hand — trace, determinant, eigenvalues — to certify a quadratic objective is genuinely a bowl. And recognise, before promising a quick turnaround, when a seemingly small constraint (cardinality, a minimum lot size, an all-or-nothing rule) is about to trade a guaranteed millisecond solve for an overnight heuristic run.
