Duration & Convexity, Two Terms of a Taylor Series

A bond's price is a function of yield — nothing more exotic than that. Duration is what you get from drawing a tangent line to that function at today's yield: a linear estimate of how much price moves per unit of yield change. Convexity is the correction for the fact that the function actually curves, so the tangent line quietly drifts away from the truth the further yield moves. Together they explain why a "duration-only" hedge always leaves a little bit of money on the table — and why that leftover money always favors the bondholder.

Section 1

Price is a function of yield, P(y)

Every bond's price traces one curve as yield moves — lower yield, higher price, and the bend is the whole story.

Because P(y) is a smooth function, calculus applies to it exactly the way it applies to any other smooth function: near any single point, you can approximate the whole curve with the tangent line at that point (a first-order Taylor term), and then correct that approximation with a term that captures how the curve bends away from the tangent (a second-order Taylor term). Everything in this lesson is those two ideas, applied to bond pricing.

This is not a bond-specific trick borrowed from finance — it's the general "linear approximation, then a curvature correction" idea from this curriculum's foundation-mathematics lessons, aimed at one specific, very consequential function.
Section 2

Before duration is a sensitivity number, it's a weighted-average wait: how long, on average, before you get your money back?

Duration ≈ 4.62 years — shorter than the 5-year maturity, because some cash arrives early and pulls the weighted average forward.

Macaulay duration is a genuine weighted average: each cash flow's timing is weighted by its share of the bond's present value, PV(CFt)/P, and those weights always sum to exactly 1. This is the same "weighted average" idea as a sample mean — the data points are payment dates, and the weights are each payment's slice of value instead of 1/n each.

Section 3

Modified duration is the tangent line's slope

Rescale Macaulay duration by one factor of (1+y), and it stops being a timing number and becomes a direct price-sensitivity number.

Where the formula comes from
dP/dy
=
−Dmod × P
Differentiate the pricing formula P = Σ CFt(1+y)−t term by term, and this is exactly what falls out — modified duration is nothing but the price curve's exact slope at the current yield, rescaled to read as a percentage.
Dmod = DMac / (1+y)
→
ΔP/P ≈ −Dmod × Δy

For the 5-year, 4% coupon, $1,000 face bond at 5% YTM (price $956.71): DMac ≈ 4.62 years, so Dmod = 4.62 / 1.05 ≈ 4.40. That single number says: for a small yield move, expect roughly a 4.40% price move in the opposite direction.

This is a first-order Taylor expansion — f(y₀+Δy) ≈ f(y₀) + f′(y₀)Δy — applied to P(y), with f′(y₀) playing the role of −Dmod·P. Duration is that tangent line's slope. Nothing more exotic than that.

Section 5

Convexity is the curvature correction

The second-order Taylor term uses the curve's second derivative — how fast the slope itself is changing.

ΔP/P ≈ −DmodΔy
+
½ × C × (Δy)²
◡

Convexity is always positive, for a plain bond

Every term inside C's defining sum is a positive cash flow times a positive discount factor times a positive t(t+1) weight — so C > 0 always, for any ordinary fixed-coupon bond.

²

The correction can never be negative

½C(Δy)² is one-half times a positive number times a squared quantity — squared quantities are never negative, whichever direction yield actually moved.

✓

For the worked-example bond, C ≈ 24.47

Computed the same way as duration — weight each cash flow's present value by t(t+1) this time instead of plain t, then rescale by P·(1+y)².

Because the correction term is always non-negative, duration alone always underestimates a plain bond's true price after any yield move — up or down. The straight-line tangent is always a lower bound on the truth, and convexity is precisely the missing piece that closes most of the gap.

Section 8

Convexity is always a gift to the bondholder

Shock the same bond by 100bp in each direction. The linear model says the gain and the loss should be identical in size — convexity says otherwise.

Yields fall 100bp (5% → 4%)

Price rises from $956.71 to $1,000.00 — a gain of $43.29.

Yields rise 100bp (5% → 6%)

Price falls from $956.71 to $915.75 — a loss of $40.96.

Same size move, opposite directions — but the gain ($43.29) is bigger than the loss ($40.96). A purely linear (duration-only) model would have predicted an identical $42.10 either way. The extra $1.19 on the upside and the $1.14 of cushion on the downside are both the same convexity term, working in the bondholder's favor regardless of which way yields move.
Where this needs qualifying

This "always a gift" result assumes fixed, contractual cash flows. A callable bond or a mortgage-backed security can have negative convexity, because falling yields trigger prepayment/refinancing that shortens the bond's actual cash-flow schedule right when it would otherwise be gaining the most — breaking the fixed-CF assumption this lesson's formulas rely on.

Section 9

How this actually gets used on a desk

📏

DV01 — duration in dollars

DV01 ≈ Dmod × P × 0.0001 — the dollar gain or loss for a 1 basis-point yield move. Desks size and hedge positions in DV01 terms, not percentages.

⚖

Duration matching / immunization

A pension fund matches the duration of its assets to the duration of its liabilities, so a parallel rate shift moves both sides by roughly the same amount.

💰

Convexity is bought and sold as its own factor

Especially in mortgage-backed securities, where convexity can turn negative, and in options markets, where "long gamma" is the same second-order idea under a different name.

🪢

Barbell vs. bullet

A barbell (very short + very long maturities) can match a bullet's (single intermediate maturity) duration while carrying meaningfully more convexity — a standard relative-value lever.

Both duration and convexity, as derived here, assume every point on the yield curve shifts by the same amount — a parallel shift. A portfolio can be perfectly duration-neutral to a parallel move and still lose money on a curve steepening or flattening, which is exactly why finer, curve-shape-aware tools exist.

Section 10

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Why is a coupon-paying bond's Macaulay duration always shorter than its maturity?

Duration is a weighted average of payment dates. Coupons paid before maturity carry some weight too, pulling that average forward from the final maturity date. Only a zero-coupon bond, with 100% of its value at one date, has duration exactly equal to maturity.
Question 2

In what precise sense is modified duration "the first-order Taylor term" of the bond pricing function?

Differentiating P(y) = Σ CF_t(1+y)^−t term by term gives dP/dy = −D_mod·P exactly. Plugging that into the generic first-order Taylor formula f(y₀+Δy) ≈ f(y₀) + f′(y₀)Δy produces ΔP/P ≈ −D_mod·Δy directly — no separate "duration math" involved.
Question 3

A bond's yield rises by 100bp. Does duration alone overestimate or underestimate the resulting price?

The convexity correction ½C(Δy)² is always non-negative for a plain bond, so the true price is always at or above the duration-only (tangent-line) estimate — in either direction of yield move. Duration-only always underestimates price after any shock.
Question 4

Yields move by the same 100bp in each direction for the same bond. Which is larger — the resulting dollar gain (yields fall) or the resulting dollar loss (yields rise)?

Convexity's correction term is positive regardless of the direction yield moves, so it adds to the gain on a yield decline and cushions the loss on a yield rise — the net effect is a gain strictly bigger than a same-sized loss, exactly the asymmetry a purely linear (duration-only) model misses.
Question 5

Can a bond ever have negative convexity?

This lesson's convexity formula assumes fixed, contractual cash flows. A callable bond or MBS breaks that assumption — falling yields trigger prepayment/refinancing that shortens the actual expected cash-flow schedule, which can flip the price-yield curve concave over some range: negative convexity.
Section 11

What to carry forward

01

Bond price is a function of yield, P(y) — and duration and convexity are nothing more than the first two Taylor terms of that one function, evaluated at today's yield.

02

Modified duration is the tangent line's slope, rescaled to percent: ΔP/P ≈ −Dmod×Δy — accurate for a small move, a guess for a large one.

03

The linear estimate breaks down for larger moves because the true price-yield curve is convex — it bends away from its own tangent line as yield moves further from where duration was measured.

04

Convexity is the second-order Taylor term, ½×C×(Δy)² — always non-negative for a plain bond, so the true curve always sits on or above its tangent line.

05

That's why duration alone always underestimates a plain bond's true price after any yield move, and why a same-sized yield fall produces a bigger dollar gain than a yield rise produces a dollar loss.

06

Desks size and hedge in duration/DV01 terms daily, but trade convexity as its own factor — especially once it turns negative, as it can for callable or mortgage-backed bonds.
