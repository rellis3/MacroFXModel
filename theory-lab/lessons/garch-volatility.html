GARCH & Volatility Clustering

Big moves cluster together even though the direction of the next move stays unpredictable — GARCH is the model that turns "volatility has memory" into a number you can forecast one day ahead.

CONCEPT — one of the volatility estimators used across this project's research ~16 min read Prereqs: Ornstein-Uhlenbeck & Mean Reversion
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • Volatility clusters — big moves (of either sign) follow big moves, and calm follows calm — even though the direction of the next move stays close to unpredictable.
  • GARCH(1,1) forecasts tomorrow's variance as \(\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha\epsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta\sigma_{t-1}^2\): a floor, a reaction to yesterday's squared shock, and inertia from yesterday's own variance level.
  • \(\alpha+\beta\) is the persistence — how slowly a spike decays. This lesson's worked example uses \(\alpha=0.08,\ \beta=0.90\) (persistence 0.98, half-life ≈ 34 days); this project's actual estimator uses \(\alpha=0.06,\ \beta=0.91\) (persistence 0.97, half-life ≈ 23 days).
  • GARCH is one of three sigma estimators this project runs (alongside HV20 and Yang-Zhang), currently applied to the index asset class (NQ) — it isn't a claim that GARCH beats the others OOS, just the estimator this project runs there.
Section 01

Why this matters

Stare at almost any daily return chart for a currency pair, an index, or a commodity and one pattern jumps out even before you compute anything: calm stretches and turbulent stretches come in runs. A 2% daily move doesn't show up in isolation surrounded by 0.2% days — it shows up next to other 1–3% days, clustered around a news shock, a central bank surprise, or a liquidity event. Then, days or weeks later, the market goes quiet again. This is called volatility clustering, and it is one of the most robust empirical regularities in financial time series — documented across essentially every liquid asset class, going back to Mandelbrot's observation in 1963 that "large changes tend to be followed by large changes, of either sign, and small changes tend to be followed by small changes."

That last clause — of either sign — is the key thing that makes this a statement about volatility and not about direction. A big up day and a big down day both raise the odds that tomorrow is also a big day, regardless of which way it goes. So the returns themselves are close to unpredictable from their own past (this is the efficient-markets / random-walk-ish behavior you may already associate with prices), but the size of the returns — the volatility — is highly predictable from its own recent past. That distinction is the whole reason GARCH exists: it is a model of the second moment (variance) built on top of a process whose first moment (mean/direction) is assumed to be roughly unforecastable.

Why does a builder working on this repo need to care? Because every strategy here that expresses a level as "price ± some multiple of σ" — the daily/weekly/20-day forecast bands, the entry-zone confidence engine, the regime classifier's day-type score — is only as good as the σ estimate feeding it. If σ is estimated as a flat trailing average that doesn't know volatility clusters, it will be systematically too narrow right after a shock (when the market is actually still hot) and too wide long after a shock has faded (when the old big day is still sitting in the trailing window, artificially inflating the estimate). GARCH exists to fix exactly this lag.

The whole idea in one line: returns themselves stay close to unpredictable from their own past, but the size of returns — volatility — is highly predictable from its own recent past. GARCH models that second moment, on top of a first moment (direction) assumed to be roughly unforecastable.
💡
Plain-English intuition

Volatility has momentum; returns (mostly) don't

Think of market volatility like weather rather than like a coin flip. A coin flip has no memory — knowing today's flip was heads tells you nothing about tomorrow's. But "how stormy is it" absolutely has memory: a stormy day is followed by an elevated chance of another stormy day, and a long calm spell tends to stay calm for a while. Nobody can predict tomorrow's wind direction from today's, but the intensity of the weather is forecastable in a way that compounds day to day. GARCH treats volatility exactly like that storminess variable: it doesn't try to predict which way price goes tomorrow, only how large the swing is likely to be, and it lets today's storminess directly inform tomorrow's forecast — the way a meteorologist updates a forecast the instant a new storm forms rather than waiting for a 20-day average to catch up.

Section 02

The math, step by step

We need a bit of notation before writing any model down. Let \(P_t\) be the closing price on day \(t\), and define the daily log return \(r_t = \ln(P_t / P_{t-1})\) — the day's percentage move, expressed in log form because log returns are additive across time and (approximately) symmetric for small moves. We split the return into a mean part and a shock part:

\[ r_t = \mu + \epsilon_t \]
μthe small, usually near-zero average daily drift
ε_tthe day's unpredictable shock — the part GARCH models

\(\mu\) is the (usually assumed-small, sometimes assumed-zero) average daily drift, and \(\epsilon_t\) is the day's unpredictable shock — the residual, the part of the return not explained by a constant drift. For a return series that's close to a random walk, \(\mu\) is small enough that people often just treat \(\epsilon_t \approx r_t\) directly. The entire question GARCH answers is: how large do we expect \(\epsilon_t\) to be today, given everything we know up to yesterday? We formalize "how large" as a conditional variance:

\[ \sigma_t^2 = \mathrm{Var}(\epsilon_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}) \]
σ_t²today's conditional variance — a forecast, not a historical average
F_{t-1}everything observable through yesterday's close

Read this as: "\(\sigma_t^2\) is the variance of today's shock, conditional on \(\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\)" — where \(\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\) (a standard notation in time-series stats, the "filtration" or information set) just means "everything observable up through the close of day \(t-1\)." The word conditional is doing all the work here: this is not "the variance of returns over history," it's "the best variance forecast given what happened right up to now" — a number that is allowed to change every single day as new information arrives. Contrast that with a flat historical-volatility window (e.g. an HV20 estimate):

\[ \hat\sigma_{HV20}^2 = \frac{1}{20}\sum_{i=1}^{20} \epsilon_{t-i}^2 \]
1/20equal weight on every one of the last 20 days
Σno concept of "conditional on what just happened"

HV20 gives the last 20 days equal weight — day \(t{-}1\) counts exactly as much as day \(t{-}20\) — and it has no concept of "conditional on what just happened." It also has a well-known artifact: when a big shock day rolls out of the 20-day window (21 days later), the estimate drops abruptly even though nothing new happened in the market that day — it's purely an artifact of the rolling window's edge. This is sometimes called the "ghosting" effect. GARCH replaces the flat window with a model that (a) reacts to new shocks immediately and (b) decays their influence smoothly over time instead of dropping them off a cliff.

Step 1 — ARCH(1): let variance react to the last shock

Robert Engle's original 1982 ARCH model (AutoRegressive Conditional Heteroskedasticity — "heteroskedasticity" just means "non-constant variance") proposed the simplest possible version of this idea: make today's variance a function of yesterday's squared shock.

\[ \sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha\,\epsilon_{t-1}^2 \]
ωthe floor — variance never falls below a quiet-market baseline
α·ε_{t-1}²how strongly one big day pushes tomorrow's forecast up

Squaring is not cosmetic — it's the load-bearing idea. A raw shock \(\epsilon_{t-1}\) can be positive or negative, but "how much variance should this generate" doesn't care about sign: a −2% day and a +2% day should both raise tomorrow's expected variance by the same amount, since both are evidence of an active, turbulent market. Squaring converts "a shock of either sign" into "a magnitude," which is exactly the quantity a variance (itself always non-negative) should respond to. If you didn't square it, a small positive and small negative shock could cancel out and the model would learn nothing about the market's actual turbulence.

For this recursion to always produce a valid (non-negative) variance, the parameters must satisfy \(\omega > 0\) and \(\alpha \ge 0\). \(\omega\) sets a floor — the variance never falls below what a quiet, shock-free market would produce — and \(\alpha\) controls how strongly one big day pushes tomorrow's forecast up.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

ARCH says: "how big was yesterday's move, no matter which direction? Add a chunk of that to my baseline nervousness, and that's my forecast for how big today's move will be." Squaring the shock is just a trick to make "big up day" and "big down day" count the same, since both are equally good evidence the market got jumpy.

Step 2 — GARCH(1,1): let variance also carry its own momentum

ARCH(1) has a limitation: it only remembers yesterday's shock, and it forgets everything about how elevated variance itself has already been running. Tim Bollerslev's 1986 generalization (the "G" in GARCH) fixes this by adding a second input — yesterday's own variance forecast — so the model has memory of its own recent level, not just of the last shock:

\[ \sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha\,\epsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta\,\sigma_{t-1}^2 \]
ω
The floor
Baseline variance, anchors the long-run level
α·ε_{t-1}²
The reaction
How sharply the model responds to yesterday's fresh shock
β·σ_{t-1}²
The inertia
Momentum carried over from wherever variance already was

This is GARCH(1,1) — "(1,1)" meaning one lag of the squared shock and one lag of the variance itself; higher-order GARCH(p,q) variants exist but (1,1) is by far the most widely used in practice and is what this repo implements. Every symbol, defined explicitly:

\(\sigma_t^2\)Today's conditional variance — the model's forecast of how large today's squared shock is expected to be, made using only information available through yesterday's close. \(\sigma_t = \sqrt{\sigma_t^2}\) is the corresponding volatility (in the same units as returns).
\(\epsilon_{t-1}\)Yesterday's realized shock (approximately yesterday's return, \(r_{t-1}\), once the small drift \(\mu\) is netted out). \(\epsilon_{t-1}^2\) is the "news" term — the fresh, single-day evidence of how turbulent the market just was.
\(\sigma_{t-1}^2\)Yesterday's own conditional-variance forecast, i.e. the model applied to itself one step back. This is what gives GARCH its "memory of its own level," distinct from just reacting to the newest shock.
\(\omega\)The constant ("baseline") term, \(\omega>0\). It anchors the recursion so variance always has a positive floor and pins down the long-run average level (derived below).
\(\alpha\)The ARCH coefficient — how much weight goes on yesterday's fresh squared shock. Larger \(\alpha\) means the model reacts more sharply to a single big news day. Typical estimated values for daily FX/equity returns are roughly 0.03–0.12.
\(\beta\)The GARCH coefficient — how much weight carries over from yesterday's variance level itself. Larger \(\beta\) means an elevated-volatility regime is stickier and decays more slowly. Typical estimated values are roughly 0.85–0.95 — usually much larger than \(\alpha\), meaning most of today's forecast is inherited persistence, with the newest shock only nudging it.
\(\alpha+\beta\)The persistence of the process — see below. It governs how quickly a volatility spike decays back toward normal.

Read the recursion as three additive pieces: a baseline floor (\(\omega\)), a reaction to the newest piece of evidence (\(\alpha \epsilon_{t-1}^2\)), and inertia from wherever variance already was (\(\beta \sigma_{t-1}^2\)). That third term is precisely what a flat trailing-window average like HV20 does not have: HV20 re-derives its estimate from scratch out of the raw window every day, with no explicit sense of "the variance process itself has momentum." GARCH's \(\beta\) term encodes that momentum directly into the model.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

GARCH(1,1) is just ARCH with a memory upgrade: instead of only asking "how big was yesterday's move," it also asks "how nervous was I already feeling yesterday," and blends both into today's forecast. That second question is what makes an already-elevated volatility regime stay elevated for a while, rather than the forecast resetting from scratch every single day.

Stationarity: \(\alpha + \beta < 1\)

For this recursion to describe a mean-reverting variance process — one that, absent new shocks, settles down toward a stable long-run level rather than wandering off to infinity or zero — the parameters must satisfy

\[ \alpha + \beta < 1 \]
α+β < 1required for variance to mean-revert instead of exploding

Intuitively: each day, a fraction \(\alpha+\beta\) of "how far variance currently is from its long-run average" carries forward into tomorrow (proof of that exact statement is the persistence derivation two paragraphs down). If \(\alpha+\beta \ge 1\), that fraction never shrinks — a variance shock either persists forever (the borderline \(\alpha+\beta=1\) case, called IGARCH, integrated GARCH) or compounds explosively (\(\alpha+\beta>1\), which is not a valid model — variance forecasts blow up). Any legitimate GARCH(1,1) fit on real return data needs \(\alpha+\beta<1\), and it's a sanity check worth running on any fitted or hand-set parameters before trusting them.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Think of α+β as "how much of today's extra nervousness sticks around tomorrow." If it's less than 1, some of it always fades, so calm eventually returns — the forecast has a home to settle back into. If it were 1 or more, none of it (or more than all of it) ever fades, and the model's forecast would just keep climbing forever, which isn't how real markets behave.

The unconditional (long-run) variance

Given stationarity holds, the recursion has a well-defined long-run average variance — the level the forecast reverts to when there's been no unusual news for a long time. We can derive it directly from the recursion. Take expectations of both sides of \(\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha \epsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta \sigma_{t-1}^2\). Because \(\epsilon_t = \sigma_t z_t\) for a standardized shock \(z_t\) with \(E[z_t^2]=1\), we have \(E[\epsilon_{t-1}^2] = E[\sigma_{t-1}^2]\) — the average squared shock equals the average variance. In a stationary process, \(E[\sigma_t^2]\) and \(E[\sigma_{t-1}^2]\) both equal the same constant, call it \(\bar\sigma^2\):

\[ \bar\sigma^2 = \omega + \alpha\bar\sigma^2 + \beta\bar\sigma^2 \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \bar\sigma^2(1-\alpha-\beta) = \omega \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \bar\sigma^2 = \frac{\omega}{1-\alpha-\beta} \]
σ̄²the long-run variance — what the model reverts to with no new shocks

This is the number the model would forecast on an infinitely quiet day with no recent shocks to react to — the steady-state variance. Every GARCH(1,1) forecast can be read as "the long-run variance, nudged up or down by however far the most recent shock and the most recent variance level were from that long-run value."

Persistence and the volatility "half-life"

Subtract \(\bar\sigma^2\) from both sides of the recursion and use \(\omega = \bar\sigma^2(1-\alpha-\beta)\) to substitute out \(\omega\); a few lines of algebra collapse to:

\[ \sigma_t^2 - \bar\sigma^2 \;=\; \alpha\big(\epsilon_{t-1}^2-\bar\sigma^2\big) + \beta\big(\sigma_{t-1}^2 - \bar\sigma^2\big) \]
σ_t² − σ̄²the excess over the long-run level, decaying at rate α+β

Taking expectations forward (using \(E[\epsilon_{t-1}^2]=E[\sigma_{t-1}^2]\) again) shows that the expected deviation from the long-run level decays geometrically, at rate \(\alpha+\beta\) per day: a deviation today implies an expected deviation of \((\alpha+\beta)\) times as large tomorrow, \((\alpha+\beta)^2\) times as large in two days, and so on. That's why \(\alpha+\beta\) is called the persistence of the process — it's literally the day-over-day decay rate of a volatility shock's excess over normal. From a geometric decay rate, the standard half-life formula falls straight out — the number of days for a shock's excess variance to fall to half its initial size:

\[ n_{1/2} = \frac{\ln(0.5)}{\ln(\alpha+\beta)} \]
n₁/₂days for a shock's excess variance to fall to half its size

When \(\alpha+\beta\) is close to 1 (say 0.97–0.99, common for daily equity/index data), the half-life stretches to weeks — a volatility spike from a single event lingers in the forecast for a long time. When \(\alpha+\beta\) is more moderate (say 0.85–0.90), the half-life shrinks to a handful of days — the model "forgets" a shock quickly. This single number, \(\alpha+\beta\), is usually the first thing worth checking when someone hands you a fitted GARCH model: it tells you, at a glance, how sticky the volatility regime the model is describing actually is.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Volatility "remembers being scared." Persistence (α+β) is basically how good that memory is — close to 1 means it takes weeks to calm back down after a scare, like a jumpy dog that stays on edge long after the loud noise stopped; further from 1 means it settles down again within a few days. Half-life just converts that memory into a number of days: how long until half the extra fear has worn off.

Worked example

Forecasting tomorrow's σ after a shock day, then watching it decay

Take illustrative parameters: \(\alpha = 0.08\), \(\beta = 0.90\), so persistence \(\alpha+\beta = 0.98\), and set \(\omega = 1.0\times10^{-6}\) (variance units, i.e. \((\text{daily return})^2\)). First, the long-run daily volatility this implies:

\[ \bar\sigma^2 = \frac{\omega}{1-\alpha-\beta} = \frac{1.0\times10^{-6}}{0.02} = 5.0\times10^{-5} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \bar\sigma = \sqrt{5.0\times10^{-5}} \approx 0.00707 \approx 0.71\% \]

So on a quiet day with no recent shocks, this model expects roughly a 0.71% daily move — a plausible order of magnitude for a major FX pair. Now suppose yesterday's variance forecast was sitting right at that long-run level, \(\sigma_{t-1}^2 = 5.0\times10^{-5}\), but yesterday's realized shock was a large 2.5% move: \(\epsilon_{t-1} = 0.025 \Rightarrow \epsilon_{t-1}^2 = 0.000625\). Plug into the GARCH(1,1) recursion to get today's forecast:

\[ \sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha\epsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta\sigma_{t-1}^2 = 1.0\times10^{-6} + 0.08(0.000625) + 0.90(5.0\times10^{-5}) \]
\[ \sigma_t^2 = 0.000001 + 0.00005 + 0.000045 = 0.000096 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \sigma_t \approx 0.0098 \approx 0.98\% \]

One 2.5% shock day pushed the next-day forecast from 0.71% up to 0.98% — roughly 38% higher than the long-run level. A flat HV20 estimate, by contrast, would barely move: adding a single 2.5% day into a 20-day equal-weighted average nudges the mean up only modestly, and it would keep contributing that same fixed weight for the next twenty days regardless of what happens next, then vanish abruptly on day 21. GARCH reacted to the shock immediately and in full on day one.

Now trace the decay. With no further shocks, the excess over the long-run variance (\(0.000096 - 0.00005 = 0.000046\)) shrinks by a factor of \(\alpha+\beta = 0.98\) every day. Ten trading days later:

\[ \sigma^2_{t+10} \approx \bar\sigma^2 + 0.000046 \times (0.98)^{10} \approx 5.0\times10^{-5} + 0.0000376 = 0.0000876 \;\Rightarrow\; \sigma_{t+10}\approx 0.94\% \]

Still visibly elevated after two trading weeks. The half-life here is \(n_{1/2} = \ln(0.5)/\ln(0.98) \approx 34\) trading days — about a month and a half — before the excess from that single shock has decayed by half. That's the practical meaning of a persistence near 0.98: one sharp news day leaves a slowly-fading fingerprint on the volatility forecast for a long time, which is exactly the "big moves cluster" stylized fact translated into a number.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

GARCH(1,1) and its many variants are estimated in practice by maximum likelihood — choosing \(\omega,\alpha,\beta\) to make the observed sequence of returns as probable as possible under the model's implied variance path, typically on a rolling or expanding window of historical daily returns, re-fit periodically as new data arrives. A model that reacts to a shock the same day (rather than 20 days later, on a lag, the way a flat trailing window does) is the entire point when a downstream use needs to be honest the day after a surprise, not three weeks after.

In practice GARCH(1,1) is rarely the last word — desks commonly extend it to asymmetric variants (EGARCH, GJR-GARCH) that let a negative shock raise volatility more than a positive shock of the same size (the empirical "leverage effect": bad news tends to spook markets more than equally-sized good news calms them), or swap in range-based estimators (built from the day's High, Low, Open, Close rather than just the close-to-close return) that use more of the day's actual information. ⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where GARCH gets misread or misused

Treating one spike as a permanent regime change. A single large \(\sigma_t\) jump after a shock is exactly what the model is supposed to do — it does not mean the market has permanently repriced; check the persistence \(\alpha+\beta\) to see how fast that spike is expected to fade before reacting to it as a structural shift.

Confusing the conditional forecast with realized truth. \(\sigma_t^2\) is a forecast made with data through \(t{-}1\); it should be evaluated out-of-sample against what actually happened on day \(t\) (e.g. via a proper scoring rule like QLIKE), never treated as ground truth just because it came out of a formula.

Ignoring the stationarity condition when hand-setting or re-parameterizing. If \(\alpha+\beta \ge 1\) slips in (easy to do if parameters are tuned loosely rather than properly estimated), the variance forecast stops mean-reverting and can compound toward absurd values; always sanity-check \(\alpha+\beta<1\) on any parameter set before trusting it.

Small-sample instability. Maximum-likelihood GARCH fits need a reasonably long, ideally regime-varied history to pin down \(\omega,\alpha,\beta\) reliably; fitting on a short or unusually calm/wild window can produce parameters that look fine in-sample and generalize poorly, the same overfitting risk this repo's OOS-split discipline exists to catch generally.

Basic GARCH(1,1) is symmetric. It responds identically to a same-sized up shock and down shock, even though real markets often show a leverage effect (down moves raise volatility more). That's a known simplification, not a bug — just don't assume the base model captures every empirical asymmetry it could; asymmetric variants exist specifically to address this and were left out of the derivation above for clarity.

Section 05

Self-test

Q1. Why does ARCH square the shock, \(\epsilon_{t-1}^2\), instead of just using \(\epsilon_{t-1}\) directly?
Because variance measures magnitude of dispersion, not direction, and it must always be non-negative. A raw shock can be positive or negative; using it unsquared would let a big up-day and a big down-day partially cancel in the running average, which is exactly backwards — both should raise the volatility forecast by a similar amount. Squaring converts sign-carrying shocks into a pure magnitude measure that a variance term can sensibly respond to, and guarantees the contribution to \(\sigma_t^2\) is always \(\ge 0\).
Q2. What does \(\alpha+\beta\) being close to 1 (e.g. 0.97–0.99) imply, and what happens if it reaches or exceeds 1?
\(\alpha+\beta\) is the day-over-day decay rate of a volatility shock's excess over the long-run level — the persistence. Close to 1 means a spike decays very slowly (a long half-life, weeks to months), i.e. volatility regimes are sticky. At exactly 1 (IGARCH) a shock never decays — it permanently shifts the variance level. Above 1 the recursion is no longer mean-reverting at all and variance forecasts can grow without bound; this is not a valid stationary GARCH model and signals a parameter or estimation problem.
Q3. Why can a GARCH forecast for tomorrow diverge sharply from a simple trailing HV20 average right after a big news day?
GARCH reacts to yesterday's shock immediately — the \(\alpha\epsilon_{t-1}^2\) term injects the full new squared shock into the very next forecast. HV20 gives that same shock only 1/20th of the weight in an equal-weighted average, so the visible move in the trailing average is much smaller on day one, then that 1/20th weight sits unchanged for 20 days before disappearing abruptly (the "ghosting" effect) regardless of what the market does next. Right after a shock, GARCH has already moved a lot; HV20 has barely moved and won't fully reflect that day's impact (or stop reflecting it) until its rolling window catches up.
Q4. Derive (in words) why the unconditional variance is \(\omega/(1-\alpha-\beta)\).
In a stationary process, the expected variance today equals the expected variance yesterday — call it \(\bar\sigma^2\). Because the average squared shock equals the average variance (\(E[\epsilon_{t-1}^2]=E[\sigma_{t-1}^2]\)), taking expectations of the recursion gives \(\bar\sigma^2 = \omega + \alpha\bar\sigma^2 + \beta\bar\sigma^2\). Collecting the \(\bar\sigma^2\) terms: \(\bar\sigma^2(1-\alpha-\beta)=\omega\), so \(\bar\sigma^2 = \omega/(1-\alpha-\beta)\) — which is only well-defined (positive and finite) when \(\alpha+\beta<1\), the same stationarity condition from Q2.
Q5. Basic GARCH(1,1) responds the same way to a +2% day as to a −2% day. Is that realistic, and what's the usual fix?
Not fully — empirically, negative shocks (bad news, risk-off moves) tend to raise subsequent volatility more than equally-sized positive shocks, sometimes called the leverage effect. Plain GARCH(1,1) is symmetric in \(\epsilon_{t-1}^2\) and can't capture that asymmetry. Practitioners address it with asymmetric extensions like EGARCH or GJR-GARCH, which add a term that activates specifically when the prior shock was negative. This repo's engine uses plain GARCH(1,1); that's a known simplification, not an oversight to "fix" without a reason to.
Q6. In a typical GARCH(1,1) sigma-series implementation, the first two entries of the output series are set to \(\sqrt{\omega/(1-\alpha-\beta)}\) rather than to zero or to the first observed return. Why?
Because the recursion needs a starting value for \(\sigma_{t-1}^2\) before there is any prior GARCH output to recurse on, and starting at zero (or at one noisy single-day return) would bias the earliest forecasts. The unconditional/long-run variance \(\omega/(1-\alpha-\beta)\) is the model's own best "no information yet" guess — exactly the steady-state level derived in the lesson — so initializing there gives the walk-forward series a sensible, bias-free starting point rather than an arbitrary one.

Further reading
  • Robert F. Engle, "Autoregressive Conditional Heteroskedasticity with Estimates of the Variance of United Kingdom Inflation," Econometrica, 1982 — the original ARCH paper; part of the body of work cited in Engle's 2003 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences.
  • Tim Bollerslev, "Generalized Autoregressive Conditional Heteroskedasticity," Journal of Econometrics, 1986 — introduces the GARCH generalization (the \(\beta\sigma_{t-1}^2\) term) used throughout this lesson.
  • John C. Hull, Options, Futures, and Other Derivatives — the chapter on estimating volatility (EWMA and GARCH(1,1)) is a standard, widely-used practitioner-level treatment with worked numerical examples similar in spirit to the one above.
  • Dan Yang & Qiang Zhang, "Drift-Independent Volatility Estimation Based on High, Low, Open, and Close Prices," Journal of Business, 2000 — the range-based estimator this repo runs as its third sigma series (Yang-Zhang), an OHLC-range alternative to close-to-close GARCH/HV.
