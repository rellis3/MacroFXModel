The Hurst Exponent & Variance Ratio Test

Two different ways to ask the same question of a price series — is it wandering randomly, drifting with momentum, or snapping back toward a mean? — and why the second, less famous method is the one to trust more.

CONCEPT — a feature used elsewhere in this project's entry-bias research ~28 min read Prereqs: Ornstein-Uhlenbeck & Mean Reversion
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • R/S analysis estimates the Hurst exponent \(H\) as the slope of \(\log(R/S)_n\) regressed on \(\log n\) — the rescaled range of a chunk's cumulative mean-deviations, measured across several window lengths.
  • \(H\) partitions into three regimes: \(H<0.5\) is anti-persistent/mean-reverting, \(H=0.5\) is a random walk (range grows as \(\sqrt{n}\)), and \(H>0.5\) is persistent/trending.
  • The variance ratio test (Lo & MacKinlay 1988) asks the same question more rigorously: under a random walk, \(VR(q)=\operatorname{Var}(r_t^{(q)})/(q\cdot\operatorname{Var}(r_t^{(1)}))\) should equal exactly 1, and it comes with an actual standard error/z-statistic instead of an eyeballed regression slope.
  • This isn't just theory here — this project's own entry-bias logic already runs live R/S analysis (lags 2/4/8/16) as one of five entry-bias features, treating \(H<0.45\) as confirming a fade and \(H>0.55\) as confirming a follow, shared across its live entry logic and its backtest engine. The server-side conviction score dropped it in July 2026, after an out-of-sample benchmark across 10 instruments found no usable predictive relationship.
  • Both statistics are biased in small samples and say nothing about direction — they only license which strategy family (fade vs. follow) has a right to work right now.
  • Real-world episode: trend following's fat and lean years. Managed-futures funds finished 2008 up by double digits and had a very strong 2022 as inflation drove months of one-way moves in bonds and the dollar, but much of the 2010s was lean for the same rules. This desk's FX-only trend test was null (trend-following-fx); what validated was persistence of tape speed, not direction (tape-speed-persistence).
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

Everyone tells me to check whether a market is trending or mean-reverting before I pick a strategy. How do you actually measure that?

Picture three people leaving a bar. One wanders at random. One keeps stumbling the same way. One is on a leash held by a friend at the door. The question is which one you are watching.

And you tell them apart by how far they get?

By how their distance grows with time. The random walker's distance grows with the square root of the number of steps. The stumbler gets further, faster. The one on the leash stays closer. The Hurst exponent puts one number on that. A half means random. Above a half means trending. Below a half means mean-reverting.

Where does the name come from?

Harold Hurst, a British hydrologist. He studied the Nile's flood records to size a reservoir, and found that wet years clustered with wet years. His number came out well above a half. Decades later Mandelbrot took the method into markets.

You said there's a second test, and that it's the better one.

The variance ratio, from Lo and MacKinlay in 1988. If daily moves are independent, the wobble of a five-day return should be exactly five times the wobble of one day. Independent wobbles just add up.

So you compare the real five-day wobble with five times the daily one.

Yes. Say the daily variance is about point one three. Five times that is about point six four. If the real five-day figure comes out at point four five, the ratio is about point seven. The days are cancelling each other out. That is mean reversion.

And if it came out at point nine five?

Then the ratio is about one and a half. The days are teaming up. That is trending. And unlike the Hurst slope, this test comes with a proper standard error, so you can ask whether the gap from one is real or just noise.

Does persistence actually show up in real markets?

It comes and goes. Trend followers bet on it. In 2008 the stock market lost about thirty-seven percent, and broad managed-futures indices finished up by double digits. Much of the 2010s was lean for the same rules. Then in 2022 inflation peaked at nine point one percent, the Fed hiked hard, yields rose and the dollar climbed for most of the year, and trend followers had a very strong year.

So if I get a Hurst reading of point five five on the last few weeks, I follow the trend?

Careful. On short samples the classic method is biased towards finding trends, even in pure noise. A reading from a few dozen bars can flip across a half on noise alone. And neither number tells you direction, only whether recent moves have reinforced or undone each other.

So what is it good for?

As a gate. It tells you which family, fade or follow, has a right to work. And near the middle, it tells you to stand aside.

Give me the one-line version.

Watch how the wobble grows with time: faster than the square root means trending, slower means mean-reverting, and a short window is a noisy hint, not a fact.

Section 01

Why this matters

Almost every trading decision in this repo boils down to one binary question asked over and over: at this level, right now, should the system fade the move (bet on reversion) or follow it (bet on continuation)? This project's own day-type classifier answers a version of this with a drift-vs-diffusion score; this lesson covers a different, older, and in some ways more fundamental way to answer it: measure how "persistent" the recent path itself has been, independent of any particular price model. That is what the Hurst exponent and the variance ratio test are for. Neither one predicts direction — both measure a property of the path: does the series have memory that reinforces past moves (trending), memory that undoes past moves (mean-reverting), or no memory at all (random walk)? That property changes which family of strategy — fade or follow — has any right to work in the current regime.

💡
Plain-English intuition

A drunk person's walk vs. a drunk person on a leash

Picture three people leaving a bar. The first is a textbook random walker: each step is an independent coin flip, left or right, with no memory of the last step. Given enough time, they could end up anywhere — their distance from the bar door grows with the square root of the number of steps, and that's it, that's the whole story. The second person is drunk in a way that reinforces itself: stumble left, and the next stumble is more likely to also go left — momentum, overcorrection into overcorrection. They wander further, faster, than pure chance would predict; their distance from the door grows faster than the square root of time. The third person is drunk but tied to a leash held by a very patient friend standing at the door: stumble left, and the leash tension makes the next stumble more likely to go right, back toward center. They stay closer to the door than pure chance would predict; their distance grows slower than the square root of time. The Hurst exponent is a single number, extracted purely from the historical wandering pattern, that tells you which of the three people you are watching — no model of "why," just a measurement of how the distance-from-start has scaled with time in the past.

Section 02

The math, step by step

Watch the formula build
Where the Hurst exponent comes from, one term at a time
\[ \color{#fbbf24}{Y_t = \sum_{i=1}^{t} (X_i - \bar{X})} \]

Take a chunk of \(n\) returns and walk it: start at zero and add each day's deviation from the chunk's own mean. This is the detrended cumulative walk.

\[ R(n) = \color{#fbbf24}{\max_{t} Y_t - \min_{t} Y_t} \]

Measure how far that walk swings: its highest point minus its lowest point. A walk that keeps going one way swings wide; one that keeps coming back swings narrow.

\[ \left(\frac{R}{S}\right)_n = \frac{\max_{t} Y_t - \min_{t} Y_t}{\color{#fbbf24}{S(n)}} \]

Divide by the chunk's own standard deviation. Now a calm chunk and a wild chunk can be compared on the same footing.

\[ \left(\frac{R}{S}\right)_n \approx \color{#fbbf24}{c \cdot n^{H}} \]

Repeat for several window lengths \(n\). Hurst found the rescaled range grows as a power of \(n\). For a random walk that power is one half: the square-root law.

\[ \log\left(\frac{R}{S}\right)_n = \log c + \color{#10b981}{H} \log n \]

Take logs and the power law becomes a straight line. Its slope is the Hurst exponent: below 0.5 mean-reverting, 0.5 random, above 0.5 trending.

We'll build this in three passes: (1) the rescaled-range statistic and how it's used to estimate H, the Hurst exponent; (2) what values of H mean; (3) the variance ratio test, a statistically cleaner way to ask the identical question.

1. Rescaled range (R/S) analysis

Take a series of n observations — say, daily price changes or daily log returns, \(X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n\). The R/S method asks: if I track the cumulative sum of deviations from the mean of this chunk of data, how wide a range does that cumulative path swing through, relative to how volatile the chunk was? Concretely, for a chunk of length n:

\[ \bar{X} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} X_i \]
\[ Y_t = \sum_{i=1}^{t} (X_i - \bar{X}), \quad t = 1, \ldots, n \]
\[ R(n) = \max_{1 \le t \le n} Y_t \;-\; \min_{1 \le t \le n} Y_t \]
\[ S(n) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} (X_i - \bar{X})^2} \]
\[ \left(\frac{R}{S}\right)_n = \frac{R(n)}{S(n)} \]
Y_tdetrended cumulative walk built from the chunk
R(n)that walk's total swing — max minus min
S(n)the chunk's own standard deviation — puts R on a scale-free footing

\(Y_t\) is a "detrended cumulative walk" built from the chunk — start at zero, and at each step add that day's deviation from the chunk's own mean. \(R(n)\) is simply the highest point that walk reaches minus the lowest point it reaches — its total swing. \(S(n)\) is the ordinary (population) standard deviation of the same chunk, used to put the swing on a scale-free footing: a chunk of 1% daily moves and a chunk of 0.1% daily moves should give comparable R/S values once you divide out their own volatility. Hurst's empirical finding, and the reason this procedure exists, is that for a huge range of natural time series (river flood heights, in his original work), \(\left(R/S\right)_n\) scales as a power law in n:

\[ \left(\frac{R}{S}\right)_n \approx c \cdot n^{H} \]

where c is some constant and H is the Hurst exponent, a number typically between 0 and 1. Taking logs turns the power law into a straight line, which is the practical way H is estimated:

\[ \log\left(\frac{R}{S}\right)_n = \log c + H \log n \]

In practice you don't estimate this from one chunk. You split the series into windows of several different lengths n (say 8, 16, 32, 64 bars), compute the average \((R/S)_n\) across all windows of each length, plot \(\log(R/S)_n\) against \(\log n\), and fit a straight line through those points. The slope of that line is your estimate of H. This is exactly what this project's own live Hurst estimator does with its ordinary-least-squares step.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Take a chunk of daily price moves and walk a little dot up or down by each day's move, starting from zero. R/S just measures how far that dot's path swings from its highest point to its lowest point, adjusted for how jumpy the days were. Do that for short chunks and long chunks, plot the results, and the steepness of the resulting line — the Hurst exponent — tells you whether longer stretches of time make the swing grow the "normal" amount, faster than normal (trending), or slower than normal (mean-reverting).

\(c\)a proportionality constant absorbed by the regression intercept; not itself of interest

2. Reading H

H is a statement about how a series' variability scales with the length of the window you measure it over, and it partitions into three regimes:

  • \(H = 0.5\): this is the pure random walk / Brownian motion case. Increments are independent of each other, and the classic result that a random walk's range grows with \(\sqrt{n}\) (not n, not \(n^{0.3}\)) is exactly \(n^{0.5}\) — matching the R/S power law with \(H=0.5\). This is the "no edge to be found in the path shape" baseline.
  • \(H < 0.5\): anti-persistent / mean-reverting. An up-move is, on average, more likely to be followed by a down-move than pure chance would predict. The series' range grows more slowly than \(\sqrt{n}\) — it keeps "coming back." This is the drunk person on the leash. \(H\) near 0 is strongly mean-reverting.
  • \(H > 0.5\): persistent / trending. An up-move is, on average, more likely to be followed by another up-move than pure chance predicts. The range grows faster than \(\sqrt{n}\) — momentum reinforcing itself. \(H\) near 1 is strongly trending (in the limit, a straight line).
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Think of H as a single dial that reports "does this series have a memory, and what kind." Dial at 0.5: no memory, pure coin-flip wandering. Dial pushed above 0.5: a stubborn memory that reinforces itself — an up day makes another up day slightly more likely, so moves compound into trends. Dial pushed below 0.5: a contrarian memory — an up day makes a down day slightly more likely, so moves keep getting undone, which is mean reversion.

This is worth pausing on because it's the same fade/follow question that Ornstein-Uhlenbeck mean reversion and trend-day classification ask from different angles — H is a third, model-free lens on it: it doesn't assume any particular generative process (no OU equation, no drift/diffusion decomposition), it just measures how the empirical range has scaled historically.

Two historical notes worth knowing, because they explain both the name and why this shows up in finance at all. Harold Edwin Hurst was a British hydrologist who spent decades studying the Nile river's flood and drought records while working on dam design for the Aswan reservoir; he needed to know how large a reservoir had to be to smooth out runs of unusually wet or dry years, and found empirically that the Nile's records had \(H\) well above 0.5 (estimates run from about 0.7 to 0.9) — strong long-run persistence, wet years clustering with wet years — very much not a random walk, which had real engineering consequences for how much storage capacity a dam needed. Decades later, Benoit Mandelbrot picked up Hurst's rescaled-range method and applied it to financial time series as part of his broader argument (developed further in fractal geometry and his critique of the standard Gaussian/random-walk finance models) that market returns show long-range dependence and "wild" statistical behavior that a pure random walk understates — this is the direct lineage from a Nile River engineering problem to a line of code in a forex entry-bias feature.

3. The variance ratio test — the cleaner alternative

R/S analysis has real statistical weaknesses (detailed in the pitfalls box below), which motivated Andrew Lo and A. Craig MacKinlay to formalize a more direct, testable version of the exact same underlying question in their 1988 paper. Their insight: a defining, precisely falsifiable property of a true random walk is that the variance of its returns scales exactly linearly with the time horizon. If one-day returns have variance \(\sigma^2\), then non-overlapping q-day returns — being the sum of q independent one-day returns — must have variance exactly \(q\sigma^2\), because variance is additive across independent increments. No stronger assumption than "increments are uncorrelated" is required. So define the variance ratio:

\[ VR(q) = \frac{\operatorname{Var}(r_t^{(q)})}{q \cdot \operatorname{Var}(r_t^{(1)})} \]
r_t^(q)the q-period return, e.g. 5-day
q·Var(r_t^(1))what variance should be under a random walk
VR(q)= 1 under the random-walk null; deviations are directly interpretable

where \(r_t^{(q)}\) is the q-period return (e.g. the 5-day return) and \(r_t^{(1)}\) is the 1-period return (the daily return). Under the random-walk null hypothesis, \(VR(q) = 1\) for every q, exactly. Deviations from 1 are directly interpretable:

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

If daily moves were truly independent coin flips, a 5-day return's "wobble" should be exactly 5 times a single day's wobble — no more, no less, because independent wobbles just add up. The variance ratio is a straightforward "actual wobble ÷ expected wobble" check. Above 1 means the days are teaming up to wobble more than expected (trending); below 1 means the days are canceling each other out more than expected (mean-reverting).

  • \(VR(q) > 1\): q-day variance is larger than the random-walk prediction — returns are positively autocorrelated — trending, the same story as \(H>0.5\).
  • \(VR(q) < 1\): q-day variance is smaller than the random-walk prediction — returns are negatively autocorrelated — mean-reverting, the same story as \(H<0.5\).

Lo & MacKinlay also derived the statistic's asymptotic distribution under the null, which is what makes this "more statistically rigorous" than R/S: you get an actual standard error and a z-statistic for how far \(VR(q)\) is from 1, so you can ask "is this deviation statistically significant, or noise?" rather than eyeballing a log-log regression slope. In practice, with finite samples and possible heteroskedasticity in returns (which real FX and equity returns have — see GARCH & Volatility Clustering, the next lesson in this series), a heteroskedasticity-robust version of the test statistic is preferred over the simple homoskedastic one.

Worked example

A tiny variance-ratio calculation by hand

Suppose we have 10 daily log returns (in percent, for readability): 0.4, -0.2, 0.6, 0.1, -0.5, 0.3, -0.1, 0.5, -0.4, 0.2. Mean is 0.09%, close enough to zero that we'll treat deviations as the returns themselves for this toy example.

Step 1 — variance of 1-day returns. Variance (population formula, using the 10 raw values mean-centered on their mean of 0.09) is \(\operatorname{Var}(r^{(1)}) = 0.1289\) (in \(\%^2\)).

Step 2 — build 5-day returns. Split into two non-overlapping 5-day blocks and sum each block's returns: block 1 = 0.4 - 0.2 + 0.6 + 0.1 - 0.5 = 0.4; block 2 = 0.3 - 0.1 + 0.5 - 0.4 + 0.2 = 0.5. (In a real test you'd want dozens of non-overlapping 5-day blocks, not two — this toy example only has 10 days to work with, so treat it as illustrating the arithmetic, not a real test.)

Step 3 — compare. If daily returns were independent, the variance of a 5-day sum should be \(5 \times \operatorname{Var}(r^{(1)}) = 5 \times 0.1289 = 0.6445\). Suppose the actual sample variance of 5-day block returns, computed from many such blocks in a longer real dataset, comes out to \(0.45\) instead. Then:

\[ VR(5) = \frac{0.45}{0.6445} \approx 0.70 \]

A ratio of 0.70 — meaningfully below 1 — says 5-day variance is smaller than 5× the 1-day variance: the series is damping itself over a 5-day horizon relative to what independence would predict. That's the signature of mean reversion — down days are, on net, being partly offset by up days within the same week more often than chance alone would produce. If instead the 5-day sample variance came out to \(0.95\), giving \(VR(5) = 0.95/0.6445 \approx 1.47\), that would say the opposite: 5-day variance is 47% larger than independence predicts, i.e. daily moves are reinforcing each other within the week — trending. Whether either of these hypothetical numbers is statistically different from 1, rather than sampling noise from a true random walk, is exactly what Lo & MacKinlay's asymptotic standard error answers — with only 10 raw observations behind it, this toy example has nowhere near enough data to make that call.

Section 03

It actually happened: trend following's fat years and lean years

Mandelbrot had a name for the persistence Hurst found in the Nile: the Joseph effect, after the biblical seven fat years followed by seven lean years. Trend following is a strategy that bets on that effect in markets, a bet that \(VR(q) > 1\) at horizons of weeks to months. Its own track record has fat years and lean years too.

It actually happened

2008, the 2010s and 2022: when persistence switched on and off

2008. As the financial crisis deepened, markets moved the same way month after month: equities down, government bond yields down, commodities collapsing in the second half. The S&P 500 lost about 37% including dividends. Broad managed-futures indices, which track funds that mostly follow trends across futures markets, finished the year up by double digits. Moskowitz, Ooi and Pedersen's study of time-series momentum in 58 liquid futures and forwards found that an asset's own past 12-month return tended to predict its next month, and that the strategy did best in extreme markets like this one.

The 2010s. Much of the following decade was a long, lean stretch for the same funds. A common explanation is that with rates pinned near zero and central banks stepping in after sell-offs, moves often reversed before trend rules had built a position. A monthly variance ratio near 1, or below it, is exactly the world in which a trend rule buys late and sells late.

2022. Inflation surged; US CPI peaked at 9.1% year on year in June. The Fed raised rates from near zero, with four 75bp hikes between June and November. Bond yields rose for most of the year, and by late September the dollar had reached its strongest level in about two decades. Trend followers, short bonds and long the dollar, had a very strong year while a conventional stock-and-bond portfolio had one of its worst. The strategy was the same throughout. What changed was whether the market's monthly moves were persistent.

Where the textbook version breaks. Both statistics treat persistence as one fixed property of a series: a single \(H\), a single \(VR(q)\) per horizon. Trend following's history says persistence is episodic. It switches on when one macro force pushes the same way for months, then switches off for years. A full-sample \(H\) can sit near 0.5 while hiding long stretches above and below it, and a rolling estimate only confirms a trend once much of it has already happened. There is a second trap. Persistence in the size of moves (volatility clustering) is far more reliable than persistence in their direction, and the two are easy to mix up.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Trend following is like betting that a rainy week will be followed by another rainy week. Some years the weather really does come in long wet spells and the bet pays well. Other years the weather flips every couple of days and the bet loses a little, again and again. Measuring "how streaky has the weather been" tells you about the past, not which kind of year you are about to get.

The tempting trading claim: when the path looks persistent, follow it. This desk has tested direction-following on FX, and separately tested whether the pace of the tape persists.

Desk test: nothing found trend-following-fx: trend following works on FX. FX-only basket: null, verified not-a-bug with an independent rebuild. Broad multi-asset: modest (~0.3 out of sample), barely beating buy-and-hold. Fading retail positioning is about as null as trend following on FX. A trend chip on an FX card describes the past 20 days, not the next 20.

A slow 15-minute approach is followed by a slow hour, a fast one by a fast hour: next-hour dwell is monotonic in 68 of 68 cells across 17 pairs, and the effect is the same at random prices, so it is a property of the tape. That is persistence in the size of moves. It says nothing about which way the next move goes.

Section 04

How practitioners actually use this

Neither the Hurst exponent nor the variance ratio is, by itself, a trading signal — both are diagnostic / classification tools that sit upstream of a strategy choice. The most common real use is as a regime gate: compute \(H\) or \(VR(q)\) on a rolling window of recent price history, and use the reading to decide which strategy family is even allowed to trade right now — mean-reversion strategies (fading extremes, range trades) are switched on when the reading says anti-persistent, and trend/breakout strategies are switched on when the reading says persistent, with the strategy standing aside (or reducing size) near \(H \approx 0.5\) / \(VR \approx 1\) where the path is statistically indistinguishable from noise and neither family has a demonstrated edge. This is precisely the same "score → choice" selector pattern this repo's own dayTypeScore → selectStrategy brick implements, just computed a different mathematical way. A second common use is diagnostic rather than tactical: some practitioners compute \(H\) across different asset classes or across different multi-year periods purely to characterize how "random-walk-like" a market has been, independent of any specific strategy, as part of general market-structure research (this is closer to Mandelbrot's original use than to a live trading rule).

A crucial caveat that shows up repeatedly in the literature and is worth internalizing before trusting either statistic: both are estimated from a finite, noisy, non-stationary sample of history, and — like every regime read discussed in this series — they say something about the recent past, with no guarantee the same persistence or mean-reversion property holds going forward. A window that measured \(H = 0.35\) last month can measure \(H = 0.55\) this month with no structural change in the market at all, just estimation noise on a short window. Treat a rolling \(H\) or \(VR\) reading as a noisy, decaying prior about the current regime, not a fact about the market's permanent nature.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where R/S and variance-ratio estimates go wrong

The trap: R/S is systematically biased toward finding \(H > 0.5\) on data from a true random walk when the sample is short — and a live feature computed on lags of 2–16 bars over 20–60 daily bars is squarely in that small-sample regime. A single reading of \(H=0.55\) from a short window is much weaker evidence than the same number from thousands of observations.
  • R/S is biased in small samples. The classical R/S estimator is known (from the small-sample expected value of R/S worked out by Anis & Lloyd in 1976, and simulation studies since) to be biased toward finding \(H > 0.5\) even on data generated by a true random walk when the sample is short, because the expected R/S of short windows does not yet follow the asymptotic \(\sqrt{n}\) law. Lo (1991) added a second problem: ordinary short-range autocorrelation also inflates classical R/S. A handful of windows of length 8–16, as used in a live feature computed on maybe 20–60 daily bars, is exactly the small-sample regime where this bias is largest — a reading of \(H = 0.55\) from a short window carries much less evidence than the same number from thousands of observations.
  • Both statistics are sensitive to short-term autocorrelation you didn't intend to measure. Bid-ask bounce, stale/asynchronous quotes, or a single large jump inside the window can inject spurious autocorrelation that has nothing to do with genuine trending or mean-reverting behavior in the underlying price process.
  • Overlapping windows are not independent observations. A variance ratio test computed with overlapping q-day windows (common because it uses more of the data) needs the Lo–MacKinlay overlapping-sample standard error — treating the overlapping q-day returns as if they were independent observations will overstate statistical significance.
  • H near 0.5 does not mean "definitely a random walk." It means the sample gave no strong evidence against a random walk — absence of evidence, not evidence of absence, especially given the small-sample bias above. Likewise a single point estimate of 0.6 without a confidence interval or a significance test says less than it sounds like it says.
  • Neither statistic tells you the sign of the next move. A high \(H\) says "moves have recently reinforced each other," not "the market is going up." It licenses a trend-following approach to whatever direction is already in motion — it does not forecast a direction.
  • Regime instability. Both statistics are computed on rolling historical windows and will drift as the window rolls forward, sometimes flipping sides of 0.5 / 1.0 on estimation noise alone rather than a genuine regime change — see the caveat above.
Scenario drill · 13 July 2022

Inflation at 9.1%, and your Hurst reading just dipped below a half

US CPI for June has just printed at 9.1% year on year. The Fed raised rates by 75bp a month ago. Bond yields have risen for most of the year and the dollar is strong. You run trend rules that trade on horizons of weeks to months, and they are short bonds and long the dollar. Suppose a Hurst reading on the last 30 daily bars has just slipped from 0.58 to 0.47 (a hypothetical reading for this drill). What do you do?

  1. Close the trend book. H is below 0.5, so the market has turned mean-reverting.
    One reading from 30 bars is squarely in the small-sample zone, where R/S is noisy and biased, and can flip across 0.5 on estimation noise alone. It is weak evidence of a regime change, and the rules trade a longer horizon than the window measures.
  2. Keep running the rules at their normal size, and treat the short reading as noise until longer-horizon evidence says otherwise.
    The gate question is about persistence at the horizon the rules trade. A short-window wobble around 0.5 does not answer it, and neither statistic forecasts direction anyway. Sticking to the process avoids trading on noise.
  3. Double the size. Inflation at 9.1% means the trend has to continue.
    Neither H nor the variance ratio says which way the next move goes, and a strong macro story is not a measurement. Doubling up on conviction is how a trend book gets caught by the reversal it cannot see coming.
  4. Trim a little, and wait for a variance ratio on a longer window, with its standard error, before deciding more.
    Asking for a proper test with a standard error is the right instinct. But trimming on one noisy reading still lets noise move your size, which costs you when the persistence is real.

What happened. The Fed raised rates by 75bp three more times, on 27 July, 21 September and 2 November. Yields kept rising for most of the year, and by late September the dollar had reached multi-year highs (the Fed's broad trade-weighted index peaked on 26 September). Trend followers, short bonds and long the dollar, had a very strong year. The 30-bar Hurst reading in the setup was hypothetical; the real lesson is that one short-window reading crossing 0.5 is a noisy hint, not a regime change.

Sources: BLS, Consumer Price Index news release, 13 Jul 2022 (9.1% over 12 months); FOMC statements of 27 Jul, 21 Sep and 2 Nov 2022 (75bp each); Federal Reserve nominal broad dollar index (FRED, DTWEXBGS).

Section 05

Key Takeaways

✓H is the slope of log(R/S)ₙ regressed on log n. \(H<0.5\) is anti-persistent/mean-reverting, \(H=0.5\) is a random walk, \(H>0.5\) is persistent/trending.
✓The variance ratio test asks the identical question more rigorously. Under a random walk \(VR(q)=1\) exactly, and Lo & MacKinlay's asymptotic distribution gives you an actual standard error and z-statistic, not an eyeballed slope.
✓This isn't just theory here. This project's own entry-bias logic already runs live R/S analysis (lags 2/4/8/16) as one of five entry-bias features, shared across its live entry logic and its backtest engine; the server-side conviction score dropped it in July 2026 after it showed no usable out-of-sample relationship.
✓Neither statistic predicts direction. Both only measure whether the recent path has memory that reinforces (trending) or undoes (mean-reverting) past moves — that licenses which strategy family has a right to work, not which way price goes next.
⚠R/S is biased toward finding H>0.5 in small samples — exactly the regime a live feature computed on a few dozen daily bars sits in.
⚠A single H or VR reading is noisy and can flip on estimation noise alone. Treat it as a decaying prior about the current regime, never a fact about the market's permanent nature.
✗Persistence is episodic, and direction persists far less than pace. Trend followers thrived in 2008 and 2022 and struggled through much of the 2010s with the same rules. On this desk, trend following on an FX-only basket was null (broad multi-asset ~0.3 out of sample); what validated was persistence of tape speed (68 of 68 cells), which says nothing about direction.
Section 06

Self-test

Q1. What Hurst exponent would a pure, noiseless sine wave have, versus pure white noise?
A pure sine wave is maximally persistent in the sense that matters to R/S: it has strong, deterministic autocorrelation over any window shorter than its period, so its rescaled range grows very fast with window length and its estimated \(H\) is close to 1 (in the idealized deterministic limit, R/S analysis on a smooth periodic signal tends toward the top of the range, often reported near 1). Pure white noise — independent draws with no memory at all — is the textbook random-walk-increment case, giving \(H \approx 0.5\) when you accumulate it into a walk (note: white noise itself, un-accumulated, isn't quite the R/S setup; R/S is normally applied to a series whose cumulative sum is the object of interest, matching \(H=0.5\) for a driftless random walk built from i.i.d. increments).
Q2. Why is R/S analysis known to be biased in small samples, and what does that mean for a Hurst feature computed on a few dozen daily bars?
The range statistic \(R(n)\) (max minus min of a cumulative walk) has a skewed, heavy-tailed small-sample distribution, and its small-sample expected value (Anis & Lloyd 1976) and later simulation studies show the classical R/S estimator systematically overestimates \(H\) — finds spurious persistence — even on data simulated from a true random walk, when the window is short. A feature computed with lags of only 2–16 bars on a rolling window of daily bars is squarely in this small-sample regime, so an individual reading should be treated as noisier and more biased toward "trending" than the same math would suggest on a long, clean sample — exactly the kind of claim an out-of-sample A/B test, not the formula alone, needs to settle.
Q3. What's the practical advantage of the variance ratio test over Hurst / R-S analysis?
The variance ratio test has a rigorously derived asymptotic distribution under the random-walk null (Lo & MacKinlay 1988), so a computed \(VR(q)\) comes with an actual standard error and z-statistic — you can say "this deviation from 1 is statistically significant at the 5% level" rather than just eyeballing how far a log-log regression slope sits from 0.5. R/S analysis gives you a point estimate of \(H\) with a much less well-behaved (and small-sample-biased) sampling distribution, making formal significance testing on it harder to trust.
Q4. Why does variance scale exactly linearly with horizon under the random-walk null — where does the \(q\sigma^2\) come from?
Variance is additive across independent random variables: if \(r^{(1)}_1, r^{(1)}_2, \ldots, r^{(1)}_q\) are \(q\) independent one-period returns each with variance \(\sigma^2\), then their sum \(r^{(q)} = \sum r^{(1)}_i\) has variance \(\operatorname{Var}(r^{(q)}) = \sum \operatorname{Var}(r^{(1)}_i) = q\sigma^2\), because the covariance cross-terms in the variance-of-a-sum formula are all zero under independence. This is a direct consequence of the random-walk assumption (independent, identically distributed increments), which is exactly why any measured deviation of \(VR(q)\) from 1 is direct evidence against that assumption.
Q5. If a rolling 20-day Hurst reading flips from 0.4 to 0.6 between yesterday and today with no major news, has the market's fundamental character actually changed?
Not necessarily, and often not at all. Both \(H\) and \(VR(q)\) are estimated from a short, overlapping-ish rolling window, and both are known to have meaningful sampling variance on realistic sample sizes — a single new bar entering the window (and an old one leaving it) can shift the estimate noticeably even when the underlying data-generating process hasn't changed. Per the "regime instability" pitfall above, a single-day flip across the 0.5 boundary is much more likely to be estimation noise than a genuine regime shift; it should raise the bar for confirming evidence, not be acted on alone.
Q6. Why is it inappropriate to run R/S or variance-ratio analysis directly on raw price levels instead of returns?
Price levels are generally non-stationary and trend upward or downward over long stretches for reasons (interest rate differentials, secular economic trends) that have nothing to do with short-horizon persistence or reversion in the return-generating process. Feeding raw levels into R/S will pick up that long-run drift and mechanically report high persistence regardless of the actual microstructure of returns. Both techniques are properly applied to returns (or mean-deviations within a window, as the R/S procedure explicitly does), which strips out the trend and isolates the increment-to-increment dependence structure that \(H\) and \(VR(q)\) are meant to measure.
