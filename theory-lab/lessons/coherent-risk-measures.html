Coherent Risk Measures: VaR, CVaR & the Artzner Axioms

Value-at-Risk answers "where's the cutoff?" Expected Shortfall answers "and how bad does it get past there?" — a difference that sounds academic until you combine two portfolios and VaR tells you diversification made things worse.

CONCEPT — explains the theory behind risk-measure choice; not a built or backtested module in this repo ~14 min read Prereqs: none required, though familiarity with a normal/loss distribution helps
TL;DR
  • \(\text{VaR}_p\) is the \(p\)-confidence loss threshold — the loss level you expect to exceed only \((1-p)\) of the time. It marks a point, nothing more.
  • \(\text{CVaR}_p\) (Conditional VaR, a.k.a. Expected Shortfall) is the average loss given you're already past that threshold — it describes the whole tail, not just its edge.
  • Artzner, Delbaen, Eber & Heath (1999) defined four axioms a risk measure must satisfy to be called coherent: monotonicity, sub-additivity, positive homogeneity, and translation invariance.
  • VaR fails exactly one of the four — sub-additivity — and a simple two-position example makes the failure concrete: combining two individually "safe" positions can produce a portfolio VaR that exceeds the sum of the parts.
  • CVaR satisfies all four axioms. That single fact is why post-2008 banking regulation shifted its standard tail-risk measure from VaR toward Expected Shortfall.
  • Both are quantile-based statistics that get shakier the further into the tail you push them — see Extreme Value Theory & Tail Risk for the machinery built to extend them past what a raw sample can support.
Section 01

Why this matters

Every risk limit, capital charge, and "how much could we lose" conversation eventually reduces to a single number computed from a loss distribution. Which number you pick is not a cosmetic choice. A risk measure that behaves sensibly when you look at one position in isolation can behave nonsensically the moment you combine positions — and combining positions is the entire point of running a portfolio instead of a single trade. The specific failure this lesson is built around — Value-at-Risk's inability to guarantee that diversification never looks worse than concentration — is not a hypothetical curiosity. It is the reason risk committees, regulators, and portfolio managers learned to distrust VaR as a single, all-purpose summary statistic, and it is the reason Expected Shortfall exists as the alternative with a mathematically provable good property VaR simply does not have.

💡
Plain-English intuition

A risk measure that ignores everything past the cutoff can be fooled by what's just beyond it

Imagine you're told a bridge is rated to survive "a 1-in-100-year flood." That single fact tells you nothing about what happens if a 1-in-200-year flood arrives instead — maybe the bridge survives with minor damage, maybe it collapses entirely. Two bridges can share the exact same "1-in-100-year" rating while one shrugs off anything worse and the other fails catastrophically the moment the water rises one inch further. VaR is that rating: it tells you the height of the water at which you cross into "bad outcome" territory, and says absolutely nothing about how bad things get once you're past it. CVaR is the more useful engineering question: "given that the flood exceeds the rated level, how high does the water typically actually get?" It's an average over the whole disaster region, not a single line drawn on a wall. That difference — a point versus an average over everything worse than the point — is also exactly why VaR can misbehave when you combine two positions: it only cares about one quantile, so it can't "see" that two individually rare bad events can occasionally happen at the same time, pushing the combined loss profile's own quantile to a worse place than either position's quantile alone. CVaR, being an average over the whole tail, never has this blind spot.

Section 02

The math, step by step

We'll define both risk measures precisely, state the four axioms a "coherent" risk measure must satisfy, then work through exactly why VaR fails one of them while CVaR passes all four.

VaR and CVaR, defined precisely

Let \(L\) be a random variable representing a loss (positive \(L\) means you lost money — this lesson works in loss units throughout, so "bigger is worse" reads naturally). Fix a confidence level \(p\), typically 0.95 or 0.99.

\[ \text{VaR}_p(L) = \inf\{\, \ell : P(L > \ell) \le 1-p \,\} \]
pthe confidence level, e.g. 0.95
ℓthe smallest loss threshold satisfying the condition
1−pthe tail probability you're willing to tolerate exceeding

Read this as: the smallest loss threshold \(\ell\) such that the probability of losing more than \(\ell\) is at most \(1-p\). Equivalently, \(\text{VaR}_{0.95}\) is the 95th percentile of the loss distribution — the loss level you expect to exceed only 5% of the time. VaR answers exactly one question: where is the bad-outcome cutoff. It says nothing about how bad things get once you're past that cutoff.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

VaR is like a weather forecast that only tells you "there's a 5% chance it rains harder than this." It draws one line and says "most days you'll be fine relative to this line." It never tells you whether, on that unlucky 5% day, you get a light drizzle past the line or a full-blown flood — it's completely silent about everything past the cutoff it names.

\[ \text{CVaR}_p(L) = E[\, L \mid L > \text{VaR}_p(L) \,] \]
E[·]the average, not just a single cutoff
L > VaR_p(L)conditioning on already being in the bad tail

Read this as: the average loss, given that you're already in the tail beyond VaR. This is also called Expected Shortfall (ES) — the two names refer to the same quantity in the continuous-distribution case this lesson uses throughout.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

CVaR answers the question the weather forecast skipped: "okay, on the days it does rain harder than that line — on average, how bad does it actually get?" Instead of one dry sentence about a cutoff, it gives you the average severity of every bad day, which is a much more honest picture of what "bad" actually means here.

The four Artzner-Delbaen-Eber-Heath coherence axioms

In their 1999 paper "Coherent Measures of Risk" (Mathematical Finance), Philippe Artzner, Freddy Delbaen, Jean-Marc Eber, and David Heath asked a foundational question: what properties should any sensible risk measure \(\rho\) have, regardless of which specific formula computes it? They proposed four, and called a measure satisfying all four coherent. Stated here for a risk measure \(\rho\) applied to loss random variables \(L\), \(L_1\), \(L_2\):

1

Monotonicity

If \(L_1 \le L_2\) in every possible outcome (position 1 never loses more than position 2, ever), then \(\rho(L_1) \le \rho(L_2)\). A position that can never be worse than another can't be rated riskier than it.

\[ L_1 \le L_2 \ \text{(pathwise)} \implies \rho(L_1) \le \rho(L_2) \]
2

Sub-additivity

Combining two positions can never make the measured risk worse than the sum of their individual risks. Diversification should never look like it makes things worse.

\[ \rho(L_1 + L_2) \le \rho(L_1) + \rho(L_2) \]
3

Positive homogeneity

Scaling a position by a positive factor \(\lambda\) scales its risk by exactly the same factor. Doubling a position doubles its risk — no bonus or penalty just from pure scale.

\[ \rho(\lambda L) = \lambda \, \rho(L), \qquad \lambda \ge 0 \]
4

Translation invariance

Adding a certain (non-random) amount \(c\) to the loss — no uncertainty about it at all — should change the measured risk by exactly \(c\), no more and no less.

\[ \rho(L + c) = \rho(L) + c \]
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

The four axioms are really just four common-sense rules for a fair risk scoreboard. A position that literally can never lose more than another shouldn't be scored as riskier (monotonicity). Two positions put together shouldn't score as riskier than adding up their separate scores (sub-additivity). Doubling a bet should double its score, nothing fancier (positive homogeneity). And bolting on a guaranteed extra loss with zero uncertainty should just add that exact amount to the score, no surprises (translation invariance). A "coherent" measure is simply one that never breaks any of these obviously reasonable rules.

\(L, L_1, L_2\)Random variables representing losses (positive = you lost money) for a position or portfolio.
\(\rho(\cdot)\)A generic risk measure — a function that takes a loss distribution and returns a single number, e.g. VaR or CVaR.
\(p\)The confidence level, e.g. 0.95 or 0.99 — how much of the distribution you want "covered" before the tail begins.
\(1-p\)The tail probability — the chance of exceeding the risk measure's threshold.
\(\text{VaR}_p(L)\)The \(p\)-confidence loss threshold — the \((1-p)\) quantile of the loss distribution.
\(\text{CVaR}_p(L)\)Also called Expected Shortfall (ES) — the average loss conditional on being past \(\text{VaR}_p(L)\).
\(\lambda\)A non-negative scaling factor applied to a position's size.
\(c\)A deterministic (certain, non-random) amount added to a loss — e.g. a fixed cash charge.
\(\inf\{\cdot\}\)"Infimum" — the smallest value in a set satisfying a condition; here, the smallest loss threshold that satisfies the VaR definition.

Why VaR fails sub-additivity — and why CVaR doesn't

VaR actually satisfies three of the four axioms without much trouble — monotonicity, positive homogeneity, and translation invariance all hold for VaR under normal conditions. The one it fails, sub-additivity, is exactly the axiom that matters most for portfolio risk management, because it's the formal statement of "diversification should never look worse than concentration." The reason VaR can fail it is structural, not a numerical fluke: VaR only reports where a single quantile of the loss distribution sits, and throws away all information about the distribution's shape beyond that point. When you combine two positions, the combined distribution's shape near its own quantile can look qualitatively different from either position's shape alone — in particular, two individually-rare bad outcomes can now occur together, shifting probability mass in a way VaR, looking at only one point, has no way to anticipate from the individual VaRs alone. The worked example below makes this completely concrete with two independent defaultable positions. CVaR doesn't have this failure mode because it's built from an average over the entire tail region, not a single point on it — and averages of loss variables are always sub-additive (the expectation of a sum is the sum of the expectations, and conditioning on the combined tail event rather than each position's own separate tail event only ever pulls the combined average down toward, never above, the two separate tail averages). This is also the reason Expected Shortfall is provably convex as a function of portfolio weights, which matters practically too: a convex risk measure can be minimized with standard optimization tools, while VaR's lack of convexity makes it a genuinely harder, sometimes ill-behaved object to optimize directly (a point developed further by Rockafellar & Uryasev's linear-programming formulation of CVaR minimization — see Further Reading).

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Two friends each individually have only a small chance of catching a cold this week, so on their own, neither one looks "risky" by a simple cutoff rule. But the chance that at least one of the two catches a cold is bigger than either friend's own chance alone — and VaR, which only ever looks at one cutoff point, gets caught off guard by that combined possibility. CVaR doesn't have this blind spot because it's already averaging over every bad outcome, including the ones where both friends get sick together.

The single most important fact in this lesson: VaR is not sub-additive. Combining two positions that are each individually "safe" at a given confidence level can produce a combined VaR that is larger than the sum of the two individual VaRs — the mathematical opposite of what diversification is supposed to do. CVaR never has this problem; it satisfies all four coherence axioms.
🧮
Worked example

Two independent defaultable bonds where VaR breaks and CVaR doesn't

Two independent corporate bonds, A and B, each with a 4% probability of default over the horizon. On default, each bond loses its full $100 face value; otherwise it loses $0. Fix the confidence level at \(p = 0.95\) (tail probability \(1-p = 5\%\)).

Individual VaR. For bond A alone: \(P(L_A > 0) = 4\% \le 5\%\), so \(\text{VaR}_{0.95}(L_A) = \$0\) — the 95th-percentile outcome for a single bond is "no default," which costs nothing. The identical logic gives \(\text{VaR}_{0.95}(L_B) = \$0\). Summed: \(\text{VaR}_{0.95}(L_A) + \text{VaR}_{0.95}(L_B) = \$0\).

Combined VaR (independence assumed). The portfolio \(L_A + L_B\) has three possible outcomes: $0 with probability \(0.96^2 = 92.16\%\); $100 with probability \(2 \times 0.04 \times 0.96 = 7.68\%\) (exactly one default); $200 with probability \(0.04^2 = 0.16\%\) (both default). Checking the VaR definition at \(\ell = \$0\): \(P(L>0) = 7.68\% + 0.16\% = 7.84\% > 5\%\), so \(\ell=0\) fails the condition. At \(\ell = \$100\): \(P(L>100) = 0.16\% \le 5\%\), which satisfies it. So \(\text{VaR}_{0.95}(L_A+L_B) = \$100\).

\(\text{VaR}_{0.95}(L_A+L_B) = \$100 > \text{VaR}_{0.95}(L_A) + \text{VaR}_{0.95}(L_B) = \$0\). Sub-additivity is violated — combining the two "individually safe" positions produced measured risk where none existed before.

Now CVaR, same setup. For bond A, \(\text{CVaR}_{0.95}(L_A) = E[L_A \mid L_A > 0] = \$100\) (the only outcome with positive loss is the $100 default). Same for B: \(\text{CVaR}_{0.95}(L_B) = \$100\). Summed: \(\$200\). For the combined portfolio, \(\text{VaR}_{0.95}(L_A+L_B)=\$100\), so \(\text{CVaR}_{0.95}(L_A+L_B) = E[L_A+L_B \mid L_A+L_B > 100]\) — the only outcome exceeding $100 is "both default," which costs exactly $200, so \(\text{CVaR}_{0.95}(L_A+L_B) = \$200\).

\(\text{CVaR}_{0.95}(L_A+L_B) = \$200 \le \text{CVaR}_{0.95}(L_A) + \text{CVaR}_{0.95}(L_B) = \$200\). Sub-additivity holds (with equality at this particular boundary case) — CVaR never reports the combined risk as worse than the sum of the parts.
VaR vs. CVaR: individual sum vs. combined portfolio, from the worked example above
Left pair: VaR of the combined portfolio ($100) exceeds the sum of the individual VaRs ($0) — a concrete sub-additivity violation. Right pair: CVaR of the combined portfolio ($200) exactly equals the sum of the individual CVaRs ($200) — the axiom holds, illustrating why CVaR is coherent and VaR is not.
🎯
Real-world trading scenario

Two market-making desks, each individually inside their VaR limit, breach the combined limit the day they're merged

A multi-strategy shop runs two small, independent market-making books — one in commodity futures, one in single-name credit default swaps — each with its own 95% one-day VaR limit, and each comfortably inside that limit on its own books because each desk's probability of a genuinely bad day (a large inventory loss or a name defaulting) sits just under 5%. Ahead of a cost-cutting reorganization, risk management is asked to combine the two books into a single unified P&L for headcount and capital purposes, and to certify that the combined book still sits inside a single, unified VaR limit set at the sum of the two desks' prior individual limits. Using the exact mechanism in the worked example above — each desk individually has a sub-5% chance of a bad day, but the chance that at least one of the two has a bad day on the same day is higher than either alone — the combined book's VaR comes back above the sum of the two prior limits, even though nothing about either desk's actual risk-taking changed. A risk manager relying only on VaR reads this as "merging the desks made the firm riskier," which is backwards: nothing changed except that VaR's blind spot to tail co-occurrence got exposed by the act of combining. A parallel CVaR calculation on the same combined book does not produce this artifact — it correctly reflects that the two desks' tail risks, while now visible together, didn't actually become more severe than the sum of the parts.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

  • Regulatory capital moved from VaR to Expected Shortfall. The Basel Committee's Fundamental Review of the Trading Book (FRTB) replaced the previous VaR-based market-risk capital framework with one based on Expected Shortfall at a 97.5% confidence level, citing VaR's inability to capture tail severity as a primary motivation.
  • Portfolio optimization prefers a convex risk measure. Because CVaR is convex (a direct consequence of coherence), it can be plugged into standard convex-optimization machinery to find, say, the minimum-CVaR portfolio subject to a return target — a well-posed problem. Minimizing VaR directly is a harder, non-convex problem that can have multiple local optima and behave unstably as the portfolio weights change.
  • Risk aggregation across desks or business lines. Firms that report a single firm-wide risk number built by combining many desks' individual risk numbers need the aggregation itself to behave sensibly — this is precisely the sub-additivity property, and precisely where a naive VaR-only aggregation can silently mislead a risk committee about whether the firm, in aggregate, is more or less diversified than its parts suggest.
  • VaR is still reported everywhere, just not alone. VaR remains simple, cheap to compute, and intuitive to communicate ("a 1-in-20-day loss level"), so it hasn't disappeared from risk reporting — it's typically reported alongside CVaR/Expected Shortfall rather than in place of it, letting a reader see both the threshold and what lies beyond it.
⚠️
Common pitfalls

Ways coherence gets misunderstood or misapplied

  • Assuming VaR is "wrong" rather than "incomplete." VaR isn't computed incorrectly in the example above — the arithmetic is right. The problem is that VaR, by construction, only ever reports one quantile and therefore cannot see co-occurring tail events across positions. It's a genuinely different question from "is the number computed correctly," and conflating the two leads to unproductive arguments about whose VaR calculation has a bug when the real issue is what the statistic is even capable of representing.
  • Forgetting the sub-additivity violation needs tail dependence or asymmetry to bite. If two positions' losses are perfectly correlated, or the loss distribution is smooth and unimodal (like a normal distribution) rather than having a discrete jump like a default, VaR often does behave sub-additively in practice. The violation in the worked example specifically requires the discrete, "mostly fine, occasionally catastrophic" shape typical of default risk, options with jump risk, or other binary/tail-heavy exposures — it is a real and important failure mode, but not one that appears in every possible loss distribution.
  • Treating CVaR as automatically "the truth" because it's coherent. Coherence is a property of how the risk measure behaves under combination and scaling — it says nothing about whether the underlying loss distribution feeding into CVaR was itself estimated well. A CVaR computed from too few tail observations is still an unstable estimate (see Extreme Value Theory & Tail Risk for exactly this problem and the peaks-over-threshold machinery built to address it) — coherence is a mathematical property of the formula, not a guarantee about the quality of the data feeding it.
  • Applying the axioms outside their stated assumptions. The Artzner et al. framework is stated for a fixed, known probability space and (in the original formulation) a coherent measure defined over a set of "acceptable" future net worths; extending the exact same intuitions to, say, a strategy whose return distribution itself is uncertain (model risk on top of the risk measure) requires additional care the basic four axioms don't by themselves provide.
Section 04

Key Takeaways

✓VaR marks a threshold; CVaR averages the tail beyond it. That structural difference is the source of everything else in this lesson.
✓Coherence is four precise axioms. Monotonicity, sub-additivity, positive homogeneity, and translation invariance — Artzner, Delbaen, Eber & Heath (1999).
✗VaR fails sub-additivity. Two individually "safe" defaultable positions can combine into a portfolio whose VaR exceeds the sum of the individual VaRs — the worked example shows $0+$0 → $100. This is not a hypothetical edge case; it's the textbook counterexample for exactly this reason.
✓CVaR is coherent — it satisfies all four axioms. The same worked example shows CVaR holding sub-additivity exactly ($200 ≤ $200), which is why bank regulation moved toward Expected Shortfall after 2008.
⚠Coherence is about aggregation behavior, not estimation quality. A coherent measure can still be a badly estimated one if the underlying tail data is thin — coherence and estimation accuracy are separate concerns.
⚠This lesson is conceptual. It explains the theory behind why risk-measure choice matters; it is not a claim that any specific VaR/CVaR implementation is built or validated anywhere in this project.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. What, precisely, does \(\text{VaR}_{0.99}(L)\) tell you, and what does it explicitly NOT tell you?
It tells you the smallest loss threshold \(\ell\) such that the probability of losing more than \(\ell\) is at most 1%, i.e. the 99th percentile of the loss distribution. It explicitly does not tell you anything about how bad losses get once you're past that threshold — two distributions with identical \(\text{VaR}_{0.99}\) can have completely different behavior in the tail beyond it, one barely exceeding it and another running many multiples past it.
Q2. State the sub-additivity axiom in your own words, and explain why it corresponds to "diversification should never look like it makes things worse."
Sub-additivity requires \(\rho(L_1+L_2) \le \rho(L_1) + \rho(L_2)\) — the risk of the combined position can never exceed the sum of the risks of the two positions taken separately. Combining positions is the mathematical operation underlying portfolio diversification, so a risk measure that violates this can report a diversified portfolio as riskier than holding the pieces apart, which contradicts the entire economic rationale for diversifying in the first place.
Q3. In the two-bond worked example, why is \(\text{VaR}_{0.95}\) of each individual bond exactly $0, and why does that change once the two bonds are combined?
Each bond individually defaults with only 4% probability, which is below the 5% tail probability implied by \(p=0.95\) — so the 95th-percentile outcome for a single bond is "no default," costing $0. Once the two independent bonds are combined, the probability that at least one defaults rises to \(1-0.96^2 = 7.84\%\), which is now above 5% — so the 95th-percentile outcome of the combined portfolio is no longer "no default," it's "at least one default," which costs $100, not $0.
Q4. Why does CVaR not suffer the same sub-additivity failure in that example?
CVaR is an average over the entire tail region, not a single quantile point, so it doesn't depend on exactly where the quantile boundary happens to fall. In the example, \(\text{CVaR}_{0.95}\) of the combined portfolio conditions on the single worst outcome (both bonds default, $200) and simply reports that $200 — which is exactly equal to, never more than, the sum of the two individual CVaRs ($100 + $100). Averaging over the tail, rather than reading off one boundary point, is what prevents the artifact that hit VaR.
Q5. Which three of the four coherence axioms does plain VaR usually satisfy, and which one does it fail?
VaR generally satisfies monotonicity, positive homogeneity, and translation invariance without issue. The axiom it fails is sub-additivity — and that's the one with the most direct economic consequence for portfolio risk management, since it's the formal statement that diversification should never look worse than concentration.
Q6. A colleague says "CVaR is coherent, so it's always the more accurate risk number." What's wrong with that statement?
Coherence is a property of how a risk measure behaves under combination, scaling, and translation — it says nothing about whether the loss distribution feeding into the calculation was itself estimated well. A CVaR computed from a thin tail sample (few extreme observations) can still be an unstable, poorly estimated number even though the CVaR formula itself is coherent. Coherence and estimation quality are separate questions — conflating them overstates what the coherence property actually guarantees.

Further reading
  • Philippe Artzner, Freddy Delbaen, Jean-Marc Eber, and David Heath, "Coherent Measures of Risk," Mathematical Finance, Vol. 9, No. 3, July 1999, pp. 203–228 — the original paper defining the four coherence axioms and proving VaR fails sub-additivity while a class of measures including CVaR satisfies all four.
  • R. Tyrrell Rockafellar and Stanislav Uryasev, "Optimization of Conditional Value-at-Risk," Journal of Risk, Vol. 2, No. 3, 2000, pp. 21–41 — shows CVaR's convexity and gives a linear-programming formulation for minimizing it directly in portfolio optimization.
  • Carlo Acerbi and Dirk Tasche, "On the Coherence of Expected Shortfall," Journal of Banking & Finance, Vol. 26, No. 7, 2002, pp. 1487–1503 — a rigorous proof of Expected Shortfall's coherence properties across general (not just continuous) loss distributions.
  • Basel Committee on Banking Supervision, "Minimum Capital Requirements for Market Risk" (the Fundamental Review of the Trading Book, FRTB), Bank for International Settlements, 2019 — the regulatory framework that replaced VaR with Expected Shortfall as the standard market-risk capital measure.
