Drawdowns, Risk of Ruin, and Why Good Systems Get Abandoned

A system with a real edge still loses eight in a row, still sits underwater for months, and still makes you doubt it at exactly the wrong moment. Here is the arithmetic of the bad stretch — so you can tell "normal" from "broken" before you are inside one.

Section 1

−20% needs +25%. −50% needs +100%. −75% needs +300%. Small holes are nearly symmetric; big ones are not.
Section 2

"1 in 595" is the wrong number

At a 55% win rate, the chance a run of 8 losses starts on this trade is 0.45⁸ ≈ 1 in 595. But you don't take one trade — you take hundreds, and every one of them is a possible starting point.

Section 4

The worst hole only gets deeper

Maximum drawdown is a running maximum — it can only stay the same or grow. Same 55% edge, 2% risk per trade, 2,000 simulated traders:

Tradesmedian max DD90th pctended below start
10015.0%24.8%19.4%
30021.2%31.2%5.3%
1,00029.6%41.0%0.1%

The longer you trade, the surer you are to be up — and the deeper the worst hole you'll have sat in. It scales with volatility relative to edge: a backtest's single max drawdown is one draw, and live trading usually beats it.

Recency, loss aversion and performance-chasing all push the same way. Morningstar's "Mind the Gap" studies keep finding the average investor earns less than the funds they hold — money arrives after good runs and leaves after bad ones.

Section 8 · It actually happened

Value's lost decade, ~2007–2020

Buying cheap stocks over expensive ones is one of the most-studied premiums in finance. Measured by the standard academic value factor, it then suffered the longest, deepest drawdown in its recorded history — sharpest in 2018–2020 as growth and tech kept winning.

"Value is dead" became a common refrain; managers saw years of outflows and some closed. From November 2020 — the month of the first effective vaccine results — value rebounded sharply through 2021 and 2022. Those who left near the bottom took the loss and missed the recovery.

Not a promise

This doesn't prove every drawdown recovers. It shows that a long, deep drawdown is exactly what a real premium can look like from the inside.

Section 9 · It actually happened

March 2000: Tiger closes near the turn

Julian Robertson's Tiger Management, one of the great hedge funds of the 1980s and 90s, refused to chase internet stocks, lagged badly through the late-90s boom, and suffered heavy losses and redemptions.

Late March 2000

Robertson announces he is closing the funds and returning outside money, saying in effect he no longer understands the market.

What followed

The Nasdaq had peaked earlier that month. The bubble deflated, and the cheap, unloved stocks Tiger favoured did far better over the following years.

Section 10 · The desk's verdict

What was tested here

Not tested here"A deep drawdown means the edge is gone." Not testable from a drawdown alone — an edge and no edge produce drawdowns that look alike for hundreds of trades. Test the live record against the tested one instead.
Desk test: held upThe desk's one fully-audited strategy (2-year yield-spread FX sleeve): ~109 out-of-sample trades, 63% win, PF 2.2. This lesson's maths: a run of 4 losses has a 73.5% chance over 109 trades, a run of 5 a 37.7% chance. Even a validated edge should expect four or five in a row. Backtest evidence; not forward-proven.
Desk test: nothing foundThe flip side: trend following on FX. FX-only basket null, confirmed with an independent rebuild; multi-asset only modest (~0.3 out of sample). Some drawdowns are no edge at all — and only a test tells you which.
Section 11 · Your exercise

Write your drawdown rules before you need them

1

Your streak

Put your win rate and yearly trade count into the calculator. Write down the run length that's more likely than not this year.

2

Your normal drawdown

Simulate your settings; write down the 90th-percentile max drawdown. Shallower than that is normal.

3

Size to it

Can't live with that number? Cut risk per trade now. Stay at or under half Kelly.

4

Define "broken"

A statistical rule, e.g. live win rate 2.5 SDs below tested after 100+ trades. Only that rule switches the system off.

Section 12

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

You're down 40% from the peak. What gain gets you back?

d/(1−d) = 0.4/0.6 = 66.7%. The 60% that's left has to grow by two-thirds.
Question 2

At 55% wins, how likely is a run of 8+ losses somewhere in 300 trades?

1 in 595 is for one starting trade. Counted over all starting points, it's 24.0% — and more likely than not by ~750 trades.
Question 3

Same 55% edge. Which change most raises the chance of being cut in half?

Size drives ruin: touching −50% in 300 trades goes from 0.1% at 2% risk to 42.1% at 10% (full Kelly).
Question 4

What's the right evidence that a system's edge has gone?

Drawdowns look the same with and without an edge. Only a pre-stated test of the live record against the tested one can separate them.
Section 13

What to carry forward

01

Recovery needs d/(1−d): −50% needs +100%. Keep drawdowns small.

02

Compute streaks, don't feel them. At 55%, 8 in a row is 24% over 300 trades.

03

Max drawdown grows with time and with volatility relative to edge. Live will usually beat the backtest's worst.

04

Bet size decides ruin. Stay at or below half Kelly.

05

Untested here: "a drawdown means the edge is gone". Tested null: FX trend following. Decide on rules written in advance — in both directions.
