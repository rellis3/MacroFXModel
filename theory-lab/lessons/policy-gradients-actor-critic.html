Policy Gradients & Actor-Critic Methods

How to optimize a policy directly — including a continuous one, like a position size — by raising the probability of actions that turned out better than expected, and why this being a powerful optimizer says nothing about whether the thing being optimized is any good.

CONCEPT — explained here, not built or tested anywhere in this repo ~23 min read Prereqs: Q-Learning & Temporal-Difference Methods
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • Q-learning needs a \(\max_a\) over actions at every step — fine for a handful of discrete choices, awkward or impossible for a continuous action like a position size. Policy gradient methods sidestep this by directly parameterizing and optimizing a policy \(\pi_\theta(a|s)\).
  • The policy gradient theorem gives the exact gradient of expected return with respect to the policy's parameters: \(\nabla J(\theta) = E[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a|s)\cdot Q^\pi(s,a)]\) — this is the REINFORCE / score-function estimator.
  • The intuition is one sentence: raise the probability of actions that led to higher-than-expected return, lower the probability of ones that led to lower-than-expected return.
  • Actor-critic methods use a learned value function (the "critic") to build an advantage estimate \(A(s,a)=Q(s,a)-V(s)\), which replaces the raw, noisy return in the policy gradient — the actor is the policy being updated by that gradient; the critic is what makes the gradient estimate far less noisy.
  • The TD error from the previous lesson, \(r+\gamma V(s')-V(s)\), is a one-step estimate of the advantage — the two lessons converge on the same quantity from different directions.
  • Mandatory honest framing: RL is a general-purpose optimizer, not a trading strategy. It will optimize whatever reward and environment you hand it — including a poorly specified reward or an unrealistic simulator — and "solve" it beautifully while still failing live. This lesson explains a method, not an edge.
  • How it reached trading: Moody & Saffell (1998–2001) trained trading rules by gradient ascent on a Sharpe-style reward, and Buehler et al.'s "Deep hedging" (2019) used the same direct approach to hedge with costs.
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

Q-learning scores every action and picks the best. But my real decision is a size. It's a dial, not a menu.

That's exactly where it struggles. Picking the best out of infinitely many sizes at every step is awkward. So policy gradients skip it. They learn the rule that outputs the size directly.

How do you improve a rule without scoring every option?

One sentence. Make the actions that turned out better than expected more likely, and the ones that turned out worse less likely. That's the whole policy gradient theorem.

Better than expected according to whom?

The critic. It's a second model that learns what this situation usually delivers. The actor is the policy that picks the action. The critic gives it a fair yardstick.

Give me a number.

One state, two choices, small or large, fifty fifty to start. The critic expects a return of one. You try large and get two. That's one better than expected, so the advantage is plus one.

And the nudge?

With a step size of a tenth, large goes from fifty percent to about fifty two and a half. Small drops to forty seven and a half. One good surprise, one small shift.

Why not just reward big returns?

Because in a great state every action pays. Comparing to the critic strips out the part every action shares, so only the choice that genuinely beat the usual gets pushed. Same direction on average, far less noise.

Has this been used on real money?

Moody and Saffell trained trading rules this way from 1998 to 2001, on a Sharpe style reward net of costs. And in 2019 a J.P. Morgan and E T H Zurich team published deep hedging, which learns hedge ratios with transaction costs included.

So what's the catch?

It's an optimizer, not an edge. Give it raw profit with no penalty for risk and it will learn to take tail risk, because nothing said not to. Give it a simulator with free fills and it will exploit them beautifully.

So the one-line version?

Push up what beat expectations, push down what didn't, and put the risk into the reward, because the method will find every gap you leave.

Section 01

Why this matters

Both previous lessons in this sequence solved for the best action by taking a \(\max\) over a small, discrete set of choices — Stay versus Open, Hold versus Close. Real position sizing isn't a menu of three options; it's a continuous number, and computing \(\max_a Q(s,a)\) over a continuous \(a\) is, in general, its own hard optimization problem nested inside every single decision step. Policy gradient methods avoid that nested optimization entirely by parameterizing the policy itself — a function that outputs an action (or a distribution over actions) directly from the state — and improving those parameters with gradient ascent on expected return, the same general machinery used to train almost every other model in this curriculum.

This lesson closes the loop on the whole sequence: lesson 1 defined what "good" means for a state or an action (the value function); lesson 2 showed how to learn those values from experience without knowing the model; this lesson shows how to use a value estimate to directly improve a policy, including over a continuous action space — and then states plainly, in the honest section below, why none of that machinery is itself an edge.

💡
Plain-English intuition

"Raise the probability of what worked" — and nothing more mysterious than that

Picture a policy as a slot machine with a dial: turning the dial changes how likely the machine is to pick each available action. Start with the dial in a neutral position — every action equally likely. Pull the lever once, observe which action got picked and what return followed. If that return was better than what you generally expect from this situation, turn the dial slightly toward that action — make it a bit more likely next time. If it was worse than expected, turn the dial slightly away from it. Repeat this over and over, and the dial gradually settles on whichever actions have actually been paying off.

That's the entire idea behind the policy gradient theorem — it's just that instead of a physical dial, the "dial" is a set of numerical parameters \(\theta\) feeding into a probability distribution over actions, and "turn it toward what worked" is precise calculus: nudge \(\theta\) in the direction that increases the log-probability of the action taken, scaled by how much better (or worse) than expected that action's outcome was. No action ever needs to be the provably single best one at any given moment — the policy just needs to keep shifting, gradually and correctly, toward whatever has been working.

How it reached trading

Choosing a position size, not picking from a menu

Policy gradients · Moody & Saffell's direct reinforcement (1998–2001); deep hedging, Buehler et al. (2019)

Value-based methods score a short list of actions. Real trading decisions are continuous: how much to hold, what hedge ratio to run, how fast to trade. Instead of valuing every possible size, a policy gradient parameterises the rule that outputs the size and pushes its parameters uphill on the objective. The method came from 1980s and 1990s control and machine-learning research.

John Moody and Matthew Saffell brought this "direct reinforcement" to trading in papers from 1998 to 2001. Their trader's position was a smooth function of recent returns, and its parameters were trained by gradient ascent on a running Sharpe ratio net of costs, which they found more robust than Q-learning. In 2019 Hans Buehler, Lukas Gonon, Josef Teichmann and Ben Wood, from J.P. Morgan and ETH Zurich, published "Deep hedging". A neural network chose the hedge at each step and was trained on simulated paths to minimise a risk measure of hedged P&L, with transaction costs included. It showed how to hedge where Black–Scholes delta has no answer, such as with costs or with no perfect hedge available.

On desks the ideas now appear in hedging exotic books under costs, in execution-schedule research and in allocation experiments with actor-critic methods such as PPO. In every case the reward has to carry the risk, because the optimiser will exploit any gap in it.

Section 02

The math, step by step

Watch the formula build
The actor-critic gradient, one term at a time
\[ J(\theta) = \color{#fbbf24}{E_{\pi_\theta}\big[\,G_0\,\big]} \]

Start with the goal: the expected total return when actions come from the policy \(\pi_\theta\). We want to push \(\theta\) uphill on it.

\[ \nabla_\theta J(\theta) = E_{\pi_\theta}\Big[\,\color{#fbbf24}{\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a|s)}\,\Big] \]

The score function: the direction in \(\theta\) that makes the sampled action more likely. On its own it averages to zero, so it needs a weight.

\[ \nabla_\theta J(\theta) = E_{\pi_\theta}\Big[\,\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a|s)\cdot \color{#fbbf24}{Q^{\pi_\theta}(s,a)}\,\Big] \]

Weight it by how good the action was. This is REINFORCE: unbiased, but noisy, because the weight carries a whole trajectory of luck.

\[ \nabla_\theta J(\theta) = E_{\pi_\theta}\Big[\,\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a|s)\cdot \big(Q^{\pi}(s,a) \color{#fbbf24}{{}- V^{\pi}(s)}\big)\,\Big] \]

Subtract the critic's baseline, what this state usually delivers. The expectation does not change, but the noise drops.

\[ \color{#10b981}{\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = E\big[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a|s)\cdot A^\pi(s,a)\big]} \]

That difference is the advantage. In the worked example \(A = 2 - 1 = +1\), and one step of size 0.1 lifts \(\pi(a_2)\) from 0.5 to about 0.525.

Setting up the objective. Parameterize a policy by \(\theta\) — for a discrete action set, \(\pi_\theta(a|s)\) is commonly a softmax over a set of learned scores; for a continuous action like a position size, it's commonly a Gaussian whose mean and (log) standard deviation are both output by \(\theta\). Either way, define the objective as the expected return under that policy:

\[ J(\theta) = E_{\pi_\theta}\big[\,G_0\,\big], \qquad G_0 = \sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^t r_t \]
J(θ)the thing we want to maximize — expected total discounted return, under policy π_θ
G₀the actual discounted return from a single played-out trajectory

The obstacle is that \(J(\theta)\) is an expectation over an entire distribution of trajectories, and that distribution itself depends on \(\theta\) (a different policy visits different states with different probabilities) — so it isn't obvious how to differentiate \(J(\theta)\) with respect to \(\theta\) at all without also having to differentiate through the environment's own transition probabilities, which are unknown (exactly the gap the previous lesson's model-free framing was built to avoid re-introducing).

The score-function trick. The policy gradient theorem resolves this with a clever algebraic identity that avoids ever needing to differentiate the environment. Start from the identity \(\nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(a|s) = \pi_\theta(a|s)\,\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a|s)\) (which follows directly from the chain rule applied to \(\log\), since \(\nabla_\theta \log f(\theta) = \nabla_\theta f(\theta)/f(\theta)\)). This lets a gradient of a probability be rewritten as that same probability times a gradient of its log — and because the result is again multiplied by \(\pi_\theta(a|s)\), it can be folded back inside an expectation over actions sampled from \(\pi_\theta\) itself, which is exactly what makes it usable with sampled experience instead of an exact sum over every possible action.

\[ \nabla_\theta J(\theta) = E_{\pi_\theta}\Big[\,\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a|s)\cdot Q^{\pi_\theta}(s,a)\,\Big] \]
∇J(θ)the gradient we actually want — which direction to move θ to increase expected return
∇log π_θ(a|s)the "score function" — which direction in θ-space makes this specific action more likely
Q^π(s,a)how good that action actually was — the weight applied to the score direction
∇log π_θ(a|s)
The direction
Which way to nudge θ to make this exact action more likely next time
Q^π(s,a)
The weight
How much better (or worse) than nothing this action's outcome actually was
∇J(θ)
The result
Average that weighted direction over experience — the gradient ascent step

Read the whole expression exactly as the intuition box described: for every sampled action, compute the direction in parameter space that would have made that specific action more likely (\(\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a|s)\)), then scale that direction by how good the action actually turned out to be (\(Q^\pi(s,a)\)). Average this over many sampled state-action pairs and you get an unbiased estimate of the true gradient of expected return — this is the REINFORCE algorithm (Williams, 1992), also called the score-function estimator. Crucially, this gradient never required differentiating the environment or computing a \(\max_a\) over actions at all — it only ever needs to evaluate the policy's own log-probability and its gradient, both of which are known exactly, since \(\theta\) is the modeler's own choice of parameterization.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

REINFORCE is a simple rule: after you try something and see how it goes, ask "which direction would I need to nudge my habits to make me more likely to do that again?" — and then nudge in that direction, harder if the result was really good, softer if it was just okay, backward if it was bad. Do this over and over across many tries, and your habits drift toward whatever's been working, without ever needing to peek inside the "environment" that produced the outcome.

Why this handles continuous actions for free. For a Gaussian policy over a continuous action (mean \(\mu_\theta(s)\), std \(\sigma_\theta(s)\)), \(\log \pi_\theta(a|s)\) and its gradient with respect to \(\theta\) are both simple closed-form expressions — no \(\max\) over an infinite action set ever appears anywhere in the method. This is the direct answer to the motivating problem: value-based methods need to search over actions to act (and, for Q-learning specifically, to bootstrap); policy gradient methods only ever need to evaluate and differentiate their own chosen probability distribution.

Actor-critic — reducing variance with a learned baseline. Raw REINFORCE, using the actual sampled return \(G_t\) (or the true \(Q^\pi(s,a)\), in practice always itself estimated) as the weight, works but is noisy — the weight is an entire trajectory's worth of accumulated randomness. The fix follows a general and useful fact about this gradient estimator: subtracting any quantity that doesn't depend on the action \(a\) from the weight leaves the gradient's expectation unchanged (because \(E_{a\sim\pi_\theta}[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a|s)] = 0\) — the log-probability gradient averages to zero over the policy's own action distribution), while it can dramatically reduce the estimator's variance if the subtracted quantity is well-chosen. The natural choice is the state's own value, \(V^\pi(s)\), giving the advantage function:

\[ A^\pi(s,a) = Q^\pi(s,a) - V^\pi(s) \]
A^π(s,a)the advantage — how much better this specific action is than the state's average action
Q^π(s,a)value of this specific action
V^π(s)the baseline — average value of the state, over the policy's own action distribution

The policy gradient becomes \(\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = E[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a|s)\cdot A^\pi(s,a)]\), which says something sharper than the raw-return version: don't just reward actions that led to a good outcome in absolute terms — reward actions that did better than this state's own average. A state that's generally excellent (every action pays off) shouldn't inflate every one of its actions' gradient weight equally; only the actions that beat that state's own baseline should get pushed up. This is precisely why subtracting a baseline reduces variance without introducing bias: it removes the part of the signal that's common to every action in a given state (and therefore uninformative about which action to prefer), leaving only the part that actually discriminates between actions.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine grading a student's test score not against zero, but against how well the class usually does on that particular test. A "good" score on an easy test and the same score on a hard test shouldn't count the same — what actually tells you something is how much better or worse than the usual result this particular answer was. The advantage does exactly that for actions: it compares an action's outcome against what this state usually delivers, so only the genuinely above- or below-average choices push the policy around.

In practice \(V^\pi(s)\) is rarely known exactly, so it's learned alongside the policy by its own separate function — the critic — typically trained with exactly the TD(0) update from the previous lesson: \(V(s) \leftarrow V(s) + \alpha[r+\gamma V(s') - V(s)]\). The actor is the policy \(\pi_\theta\) itself, updated by the policy gradient using the critic's advantage estimate as the weight. This is where the two previous lessons meet:

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Actor-critic is two roles working together. The actor is the one actually making decisions — placing the trade, choosing the position size. The critic is more like a coach standing on the sidelines, constantly updating an opinion of "how good is this situation, roughly?" so the actor has something honest to measure each individual decision against, instead of just reacting to raw, noisy outcomes.

The TD error and the advantage are, to a first approximation, the same quantity. A single-sample estimate of the advantage is \(A(s,a) \approx r+\gamma V(s') - V(s)\) — exactly the TD error \(\delta\) from the previous lesson. TD learning names this quantity "how wrong my value estimate was"; actor-critic uses the identical number as "how much better than expected this action's result was." Two lessons, one number, two uses.

Worked example

One REINFORCE update on a two-action softmax policy

Simplify to a single state with two actions — "small position" (\(a_1\)) and "large position" (\(a_2\)) — so the update can be worked by hand without a full trajectory. Use a softmax policy with logits \(\theta_1,\theta_2\), starting at \(\theta_1=\theta_2=0\), which gives \(\pi(a_1)=\pi(a_2)=0.5\). Suppose the critic currently estimates this state's baseline value at \(V(s)=1\), the agent samples \(a_2\), and observes a return of \(R=2\).

1

Compute the advantage

\[ A = R - V(s) = 2 - 1 = +1 \]

The large position beat this state's own expectation by one full unit of return.

2

Compute the score function for a softmax policy

For a softmax, \(\nabla_{\theta_i}\log\pi(a_2|s) = \mathbb{1}[i=2] - \pi(a_i)\). With \(\pi(a_1)=\pi(a_2)=0.5\): \(\nabla_{\theta_2}\log\pi(a_2)=1-0.5=0.5\) and \(\nabla_{\theta_1}\log\pi(a_2)=0-0.5=-0.5\).

3

Apply the update, α = 0.1

\[ \theta_2 \leftarrow 0 + 0.1(0.5)(1) = 0.05, \qquad \theta_1 \leftarrow 0 + 0.1(-0.5)(1) = -0.05 \]
4

Read off the new policy

\[ \pi(a_2) = \frac{e^{0.05}}{e^{0.05}+e^{-0.05}} \approx 0.525 \]

One update, from one better-than-expected sample, moved the probability of choosing the large position from 50% to about 52.5%. Exactly the intuition-box story, made numeric: the action that beat expectation got a little more likely; the one not taken got a little less likely.

🎯
Real-world trading scenario

A continuous position-sizing policy, in the same language as this lesson

Picture a policy that outputs a continuous position size directly — say, a Gaussian with mean \(\mu_\theta(s)\) (the "center" size the policy leans toward given the current state) and some spread \(\sigma_\theta(s)\) (how much it randomizes around that center to keep exploring). Each trade samples a size from that distribution, observes a return, and updates \(\theta\) using the exact same actor-critic machinery above: the critic estimates this state's baseline value, the advantage compares the realized outcome to that baseline, and the actor's parameters shift the distribution's mean toward sizes that have been outperforming the baseline and away from ones that haven't. Framed this way, position sizing and entry timing aren't two separate problems bolted together — they're both just actions inside the same policy, which is precisely why this framing is attractive on paper. Whether it's attractive in practice depends entirely on the honest section immediately below.

RL is a general optimization framework, not a trading strategy — keep those two claims separate. Everything in this three-lesson sequence is genuinely powerful machinery for optimizing a policy against whatever reward function and environment model you give it. It has no opinion about whether that reward function is well-specified or whether that environment model resembles the real market. A reward defined as raw PnL with no penalty for risk will happily learn a policy that takes on catastrophic tail exposure, because nothing in the reward said not to. A simulated environment without realistic transaction costs, market impact, or that allows any form of look-ahead will get "solved" beautifully — the optimizer did exactly what it was asked, and what it was asked for was wrong. This is not a caveat about a specific weak implementation; it is the structural nature of what an optimizer is. State it plainly: reinforcement learning is a method for solving a decision problem you've already specified correctly. It cannot supply the missing edge, the missing risk penalty, or the missing realism in the environment it was handed, and none of the above lessons should be read as evidence that a specific RL trading system has, or will have, real edge.
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Think of RL as an incredibly obedient genie: whatever wish (reward) you phrase, it will grant exactly and only that wish, as literally as possible, with zero judgment about whether the wish was a good idea. Ask it to maximize profit with no mention of risk, and it will happily find you the riskiest possible way to make money look great in a test environment — not because it misunderstood, but because it did precisely what it was asked, and nothing more.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

Modern policy gradient methods rarely use vanilla REINFORCE or a single fixed critic directly — Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO, Schulman et al. 2017) and Trust Region Policy Optimization (TRPO) constrain each update to avoid moving the policy too far in one step (a large single gradient step on a policy can catastrophically change the policy and the data distribution used to estimate the next gradient, an instability problem the raw theorem above doesn't address on its own), and Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE, Schulman et al. 2016) blends multi-step returns with the critic's baseline to trade off bias and variance in the advantage estimate more carefully than a single TD step. All of them are elaborations on the exact same two ideas in this lesson: a score-function gradient, and a learned baseline to cut its variance.

Outside of games and robotics, this exact machinery underlies RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback), one of the standard methods used to align large language models with human preferences — a reward model trained from human comparisons stands in for the environment's reward function, and a policy gradient (commonly PPO) updates the language model's own output distribution against it. The core lesson — the method optimizes whatever reward it's given, correctly and faithfully, with no ability to notice that the reward itself might be a flawed proxy for what's actually wanted — is exactly the same caution that applies to a trading reward function, described in the honest section above.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where policy gradient and actor-critic methods quietly go wrong

Reward misspecification is invisible until it's too late. A reward that rewards raw return with no drawdown or tail-risk penalty will train a policy that looks excellent on the training environment's own terms, right up until it encounters the tail event the reward never penalized. This is the single most important, most repeated failure mode in applied RL, and it is exactly why the honest section above is mandatory rather than optional in this lesson.

An unrealistic simulated environment is optimized just as eagerly as a realistic one. No transaction costs, no market impact, or any form of look-ahead in a backtest-as-environment will get exploited by a powerful enough optimizer precisely because it's profitable within that environment — the same "free fills are not honest" discipline this curriculum applies to ordinary backtests applies with even more force here, since a policy gradient method will actively search out and amplify whatever unrealistic edge the simulator accidentally offers.

High variance and instability in the raw gradient estimator. Even with a critic-based baseline, policy gradient estimates remain noisier than value-based methods in many settings, and a single overly large update can move the policy into a bad region it struggles to recover from — precisely the instability problem PPO and TRPO exist to constrain.

Scenario drill · an illustrative backtest review

Your sizing agent looks brilliant. What do you check first?

A colleague trained an actor-critic agent to size a futures position. The reward is raw daily P&L. The environment is a historical simulator that fills every order at the mid price with no costs. In the backtest the agent's Sharpe ratio is far above anything your team has run. The agent tends to sit near maximum size and rarely cuts. What do you do?

  1. Deploy at small size. The optimizer clearly found something real.
    The optimizer found the best policy for the problem it was given. That problem had no risk penalty and free fills, so it has no reason to avoid tail risk or trading costs. Small size does not fix a wrong objective.
  2. Fix the problem before trusting the answer: add costs and impact to the simulator, put a drawdown or tail-risk penalty in the reward, then retrain and test walk-forward.
    This is the lesson's central warning. Reinforcement learning solves the decision problem you specified. If the reward lacks a risk penalty and the simulator lacks realism, the agent exploits both, and the backtest measures those gaps rather than an edge.
  3. Switch to PPO so updates are more stable, then retrain.
    PPO does constrain how far each update moves the policy, which helps with instability. But it optimizes the same flawed reward in the same unrealistic simulator just as faithfully, only more smoothly.
  4. Add more training episodes so the critic's baseline becomes more accurate.
    A better critic reduces the noise in the gradient. It does nothing about what the gradient points toward. More training on the wrong objective gives a more confident version of the wrong policy.

What this shows. This is an illustrative situation, not a real episode. Its lesson is the one the page states plainly: a policy gradient is an optimizer, not an edge. Published uses that worked, such as Moody and Saffell's Sharpe-style reward net of costs and deep hedging's costs-included risk objective, put the costs and the risk inside the problem first.

Section 04

Key Takeaways

✓Policy gradients optimize \(\pi_\theta\) directly, avoiding the \(\max_a\) that value-based methods need. This is what makes continuous action spaces — like a position size — tractable without a nested optimization at every step.
✓\(\nabla J(\theta)=E[\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a|s)\cdot Q^\pi(s,a)]\) is the whole idea: raise the probability of actions that led to higher-than-expected return, in exact proportion to how much better than expected they were.
✓Subtracting a state-dependent baseline never changes the gradient's expectation, only its variance. The advantage \(A(s,a)=Q(s,a)-V(s)\) is the natural, variance-reducing choice.
✓The TD error from the previous lesson is a one-step estimate of the advantage. Actor-critic methods are, in substance, TD learning's critic feeding a policy-gradient actor.
✗None of this is a trading edge — it's an optimizer. RL will faithfully optimize a poorly specified reward or an unrealistic simulated environment and "solve" it beautifully while failing live. State this every time this machinery is proposed for a real strategy, not just once in a lesson.
⚠Raw policy gradient estimates are noisy and updates can be unstable. Production methods (PPO, TRPO, GAE) exist specifically to constrain how far a single update is allowed to move the policy.
•On a desk, this is the tool for continuous decisions. It was used for position sizing by Moody & Saffell and for hedge ratios by Buehler et al.'s deep hedging (2019). It is only as good as the risk built into its reward.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. Why do value-based methods like Q-learning become awkward for a continuous action space, and how does a policy gradient method avoid that problem?
Q-learning's update, and acting greedily from a learned Q-function, both require computing \(\max_a Q(s,a)\) — for a continuous action space this is itself a nested optimization problem that has to be solved at every single decision step, with no closed form in general. A policy gradient method never computes this max at all: it directly parameterizes a distribution over actions (e.g. a Gaussian for a continuous action) and only ever needs to evaluate that distribution's own log-probability and its gradient with respect to the parameters — both available in closed form for common choices of policy, with no search over actions required.
Q2. In plain English, what does the policy gradient theorem actually tell you to do?
For every sampled state-action pair, nudge the policy's parameters in whichever direction would have made that specific action more likely, scaled by how good that action's outcome actually was (or, with a baseline, how much better than the state's own average it was). Good outcomes get their action's probability raised; poor outcomes get theirs lowered — averaged over many samples, this produces an unbiased estimate of the true gradient of expected return.
Q3. Why does subtracting the state value V(s) from Q(s,a) reduce the variance of the policy gradient estimator without introducing bias?
Because the expected value of the score function itself, averaged over actions sampled from the current policy, is exactly zero — subtracting any quantity that doesn't depend on the action being taken (like V(s), which only depends on the state) leaves the gradient's expectation completely unchanged. What it does change is the spread of the estimator: V(s) captures the part of the return that's common to every action in that state and therefore uninformative about which action to prefer, so removing it leaves only the action-discriminating signal — the advantage — which is a much less noisy quantity to average over samples.
Q4. What is the concrete link between the TD error from the Q-learning lesson and the advantage function used in actor-critic methods?
A one-step, sampled estimate of the advantage is A(s,a) ≈ r + γV(s') − V(s) — which is exactly the TD error. The TD-learning lesson names this quantity "how wrong the current value estimate turned out to be, given one real sample"; actor-critic uses the identical computed number as "how much better than this state's own baseline the action's outcome was." They are the same expression serving two purposes across the two lessons.
Q5. Why is it wrong to treat "we successfully trained a policy that maximizes reward in our simulated trading environment" as evidence the strategy has real edge?
A policy-gradient method optimizes exactly and only what it is given — the specified reward function inside the specified environment model — with no ability to notice that either might be a flawed stand-in for real trading. A reward with no risk penalty will train a policy that takes on tail risk the reward never punished; an environment with no realistic costs, slippage, or market impact will get exploited for whatever unrealistic edge it happens to offer, precisely because the optimizer is doing its job faithfully. Successfully optimizing a simulated objective is evidence the method works as an optimizer; it is not, by itself, evidence that the underlying reward and environment were specified correctly, which is the actual determinant of whether the result means anything live.
