Does the Edge Survive Costs?

A backtest can prove an effect is real and still describe a strategy that loses money. This lesson turns a gross edge into a net one — spread, slippage, commission, turnover — and ends with a break-even cost and a yes/no you can act on.

CONCEPT ~20 min read Prereqs: Normal distribution; Sharpe ratios (helpful)
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • Net edge = gross edge − cost per round trip. The cost is the full spread, slippage on both sides, commission and financing. The gross edge per trade is your break-even cost: pay more than that and the strategy loses.
  • Significance is not size. A t-statistic grows with \(\sqrt{n}\); the edge per trade does not. With 3,000 trades, a 0.9-pip edge is "highly significant" (t = 3.3) — and a 1.3-pip cost turns it into a loser.
  • Turnover multiplies cost. Cost drag on the annual Sharpe ratio is \((c/\sigma)\sqrt{N}\): the more often you trade, the more of the edge the market keeps.
  • Demand a margin, not a pass. Ask for gross edge ≥ 2× assumed cost, net t ≥ 2, and still positive at double the cost. Real costs are almost always worse than the backtest assumed.
  • The desk's own record agrees: with spread/ATR above 0.15, zero of 30 strategy × timeframe cells were profitable (held up); a VWAP-extension fade was positive gross and 3–8× under cost (nothing found).
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

I've found an intraday fade on EUR/USD. Three thousand trades out of sample, and the edge is highly significant.

How big is it per trade?

Nine tenths of a pip, on average.

And what does one round trip cost you?

The spread is only four tenths of a pip.

You cross the whole spread, but that's not all. Slippage of two tenths on the way in and two tenths on the way out. Commission of five dollars a lot, which is half a pip. Add it up: one point three pips a trade.

So I make nine tenths and pay one point three.

You lose four tenths of a pip on every trade, about four hundred pips a year at a thousand trades. With great statistical confidence.

But the edge is real. The test said so.

It is real. That's the trap. Significance grows with the square root of the number of trades. The edge per trade doesn't grow at all. A huge sample can make a tiny edge look certain. Only its size, against the cost, tells you whether it pays.

So what number should I look at first?

The gross edge is your break-even cost. Here that's nine tenths of a pip. Can you trade this for less than that, every time, bad fills included? Then ask for a margin: edge at least twice the cost, and still positive if costs double.

What if I trade less often?

Often that's the fix. Keep only the strongest fifth of setups and the edge per trade can rise to three pips against the same one point three of cost. A slow USD/JPY swing makes forty pips a trade and pays three. Same cost per trip, much bigger moves.

Has this tripped up real money?

Value Line's stock rankings did well on paper for decades. Its real fund, trading the same rankings, earned far less. André Perold used that gap in nineteen eighty-eight to define implementation shortfall: the rankings weren't wrong, but the paper portfolio never paid to trade.

The one-line version?

Real is not the same as tradeable: compare the edge per trade with the full round-trip cost, and demand room to spare.

Section 01

Why this matters

The earlier lessons in this category turned an analysis into a plan (From a View to a Trade Plan), a probability and a payoff (Thinking in Expected Value) and one combined view (Combining Signals That Disagree). All of that is gross: it describes what the market does, not what you keep. Between the two sit the spread you cross, the price you actually get, the commission, and the cost of holding the position overnight.

Costs are the most common reason a sound piece of research never becomes a trade. They are also the easiest thing to get wrong quietly: a backtest that assumes the quoted spread at a quiet hour, fills at the bar's close and no commission will flatter every fast strategy. This lesson gives you the arithmetic, a calculator that produces a verdict, and a card to fill in before any idea goes live. It connects to execution algorithms and market impact (how costs are minimised), the Roll spread estimator (how to measure them), and multiple testing (why a t of 2 is often not enough in the first place).

💡
Plain-English intuition

A shop that sells at a profit and still goes bust

Picture a stall that buys apples for 10p and sells them for 11p. On paper it makes 1p an apple, every time, without fail — a perfectly real, provable margin. Now add the rent, the card-machine fee and the apples that bruise. If those come to 1.3p an apple, the stall loses money on every sale, and selling more apples makes the loss bigger, not smaller.

A trading edge is the 1p. Costs are the rent. The question is never "is there a margin?" but "is the margin bigger than everything it costs to collect it, with room to spare?" And because the rent is paid per apple, a strategy that trades a lot needs a much bigger margin per trade than one that trades rarely.

How it reached trading

Paper returns versus money returns

Implementation shortfall: André Perold, 1988 · optimal execution: Almgren & Chriss, 2000 · anomalies after costs: Novy-Marx & Velikov, 2016

For a long time a backtest or a model portfolio was judged on its paper returns: buy at the price on the screen, sell at the price on the screen. Anyone who ran real money knew the two never matched, but there was no standard way to measure the gap.

André Perold's 1988 paper gave it a name and a measurement: the implementation shortfall, the difference between the paper portfolio's return and the real one's, broken into spread, market impact, delay and trades that were never filled. Robert Almgren and Neil Chriss (2000) turned the trade-off between trading fast (more impact) and slow (more risk) into an optimisation that execution desks still use. And Robert Novy-Marx and Mihail Velikov (2016) put realistic costs onto dozens of published stock-market anomalies, showing that whether an effect survives depends heavily on how often the strategy has to trade.

On a desk today, transaction cost analysis is a routine report: every fill is compared with the price at the moment of decision, and research is expected to show results after costs, not before.

The math, step by step

Watch the formula build
From gross edge to what you keep, one term at a time
\[ c = \color{#fbbf24}{s} \]

One round trip crosses the whole bid–ask spread: the ask on the way in, the bid on the way out. In the worked example, 0.4 pips.

\[ c = s + \color{#fbbf24}{2\,\ell} \]

Slippage is paid twice, once on each side. At 0.2 pips a side, that adds 0.4.

\[ c = s + 2\,\ell + \color{#fbbf24}{m + f} \]

Then commission and financing. A $5 round-trip commission is 0.5 pips; flat by the close, no financing. Total \(c = 1.3\) pips.

\[ \color{#10b981}{\mu_{\text{net}} = \mu - c = 0.9 - 1.3 = -0.4} \]

Take the cost off the gross edge. The gross edge of 0.9 pips is the break-even cost \(c^{*}\): pay more than that per round trip and the strategy loses, however significant it is.

1. What one round trip costs

A round trip is opening and closing one position. Work in the unit the strategy is measured in — pips for FX, ticks or index points for futures, basis points for a portfolio. The cost of one round trip is:

\[ c = s + 2\,\ell + m + f \]
sthe bid–ask spread: you buy at the ask and sell at the bid, so one full spread per round trip
2ℓslippage on each side: the gap between the price you expected and the price you got
mcommission for the round trip, converted into pips
ffinancing (overnight swap or carry) and any other per-trade cost

Two traps hide in that line. The spread you pay is the spread when you trade — around data releases, at the roll, or in thin hours it can be several times the headline quote. And slippage is paid twice, on the way in and on the way out; stops in particular tend to fill worse than their level, because they trigger when the market is moving against you.

2. Net edge and the break-even cost

Let \(\mu\) be the average gross profit per trade from your backtest. What you keep is

\[ \mu_{\text{net}} = \mu - c, \qquad c^{*} = \mu, \qquad \rho = \frac{\mu}{c} \]
μ
Gross edge
Average profit per trade before any cost
c*
Break-even cost
The most you can pay per round trip and still make nothing: exactly μ
ρ = μ / c
Edge-to-cost ratio
Below 1 it loses; near 1 it is fragile; 2 or more leaves room for costs to be worse than assumed

The break-even cost is the single most useful number in this lesson. It turns "is it profitable?" into a question you can check against a broker's price list: "can I trade this for less than \(c^{*}\) pips a round trip, every time, including the bad fills?"

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

You win 9 sweets every 10 games on average, but each game costs 13 sweets to enter. You can win lots of games and still end up with fewer sweets. The most you could pay to play and not lose is 9 sweets — that is the break-even price.

3. Real versus tradeable: significance is not size

Suppose the trades' profits have standard deviation \(\sigma\) and you have \(n\) of them. The usual test of "is the average really above zero?" is the t-statistic, and the same formula with the net edge tells you whether the profit survives:

\[ t = \frac{\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}, \qquad t_{\text{net}} = \frac{\mu - c}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}, \qquad d = \frac{\mu}{\sigma} \]
σ/√nthe standard error: how much the average would wobble between samples
thow many standard errors the average sits above zero — evidence the effect is real
t_netthe same, after costs — evidence the strategy makes money
deffect size: edge per trade in units of the trade's own noise; does not grow with n

Look at what \(n\) does. Quadruple the number of trades and \(t\) doubles, while the edge per trade \(d\) stays exactly where it was. A huge sample can make a microscopic edge "significant at the 0.1% level". Significance answers is it there?; only the size, compared with the cost, answers is it worth having? Economists Stephen Ziliak and Deirdre McCloskey wrote a whole book on how often the two get confused.

The trap: a t-statistic of 3 on a strategy with 3,000 trades says the edge is very probably not zero. It says nothing about whether it is bigger than 1.3 pips. Those are different questions, and only the second one pays.

4. Turnover: why fast strategies need fat edges

If you take \(N\) trades a year and they are roughly independent, the annual Sharpe ratio before and after costs is approximately

\[ \text{SR}_{\text{gross}} \approx \frac{\mu}{\sigma}\sqrt{N}, \qquad \text{SR}_{\text{net}} \approx \frac{\mu - c}{\sigma}\sqrt{N}, \qquad \text{drag} = \frac{c}{\sigma}\sqrt{N} \]
Ntrades per year — turnover
√Nmore trades average out noise, so Sharpe grows with √N — but so does the cost drag
draghow much annual Sharpe the costs take away

The cost drag scales with \(\sqrt{N}\) just like the gross Sharpe, so turnover never "averages away" cost. What changes with the holding period is the ratio \(\rho\): a trade held for a day can capture a move of tens of pips while paying the same one-and-a-bit pips of cost as a trade held for five minutes. This is why short-horizon strategies die first. The same idea underlies the spread-to-range rule practitioners use:

\[ g = \frac{s}{\text{ATR}} \]
sthe spread you pay
ATRaverage true range of a bar on the timeframe you trade — the size of a typical move
gthe share of a typical move the spread eats before you start

The desk's own test put the line at 0.15 (see the verdict below). It is a necessary check, not a sufficient one — as the worked example shows, a strategy can pass it and still lose to its total cost.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

If every bus ride costs £1 and you go one stop, the ride is expensive for how far you went. If you ride to the end of the line, the same £1 takes you much further. Fast trading is lots of one-stop rides.

Section 04

Worked example: one signal, three ways to trade it

🧮
Worked example

A "significant" EUR/USD fade that cannot pay its way

1

The gross result

An intraday EUR/USD fade, three years out of sample: \(n = 3{,}000\) trades (about 1,000 a year), average gross profit \(\mu = 0.9\) pips, standard deviation \(\sigma = 15\) pips per trade.

Standard error \(15/\sqrt{3000} = 0.274\) pips, so \(t = 0.9/0.274 = 3.29\) — a two-sided p-value of about 0.001. Effect size \(d = 0.9/15 = 0.06\): tiny, but very probably real.

2

The cost of one round trip

Spread 0.4 pips; slippage 0.2 pips each side, so 0.4; commission $5 a standard lot round trip, which at $10 a pip is 0.5 pips; no overnight financing (flat by the close).

\(c = 0.4 + 2(0.2) + 0.5 + 0 = 1.3\) pips.

3

Net edge, break-even, Sharpe

\(\mu_{\text{net}} = 0.9 - 1.3 = -0.4\) pips a trade. Break-even cost \(c^{*} = 0.9\) pips; \(\rho = 0.9/1.3 = 0.69\). Net \(t = -0.4/0.274 = -1.46\).

Per year: 900 pips gross, 1,300 pips of cost, −400 pips net (−$4,000 per standard lot). Annual Sharpe \(0.06\sqrt{1000} = 1.90\) gross becomes \(-0.0267\sqrt{1000} = -0.84\) net — a cost drag of \((1.3/15)\sqrt{1000} = 2.74\).

4

The spread-to-range check

If a 5-minute bar's ATR is about 4 pips, \(g = 0.4/4 = 0.10\) — inside the 0.15 line. The gate passes and the strategy still loses: the gate looks at the spread alone, and here commission and slippage are two-thirds of the cost.

5

The usable output

Verdict: statistically real, economically dead. Do not trade it. To break even you need all-in costs under 0.9 pips (31% cheaper); to have a 2× margin you need 0.45 pips or less. Neither is credible for a retail-sized account crossing the spread.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

You find a game you win a tiny bit more often than you lose. But every game has an entry fee. Play once and you barely notice; play a thousand times and the fees add up to more than you won.

It actually happened

Value Line: paper versus reality

For decades the investment research firm Value Line ranked stocks into groups by expected performance, and its top-ranked group did well on paper — well enough to become one of the classic puzzles for believers in efficient markets. Value Line also ran a real fund that traded on its own rankings.

In "The Implementation Shortfall: Paper versus Reality" (1988), André Perold used the gap between the paper record and the fund's real returns as his motivating example: the real fund earned far less than the paper portfolio it was built to follow. The rankings were not wrong; the paper portfolio simply never paid to trade. Spreads, market impact, the delay between signal and order, and trades that were never filled took most of the difference. Perold's measure of that gap — implementation shortfall — is now the standard yardstick for execution.

It actually happened

2016: a cost bill for the anomaly zoo

By the 2010s academic finance had published hundreds of patterns that seemed to predict stock returns. Robert Novy-Marx and Mihail Velikov's "A Taxonomy of Anomalies and Their Trading Costs" (Review of Financial Studies, 2016) estimated realistic trading costs for dozens of them. The dividing line was turnover: strategies that rebalance slowly mostly kept a meaningful part of their premium after costs, while many that need frequent rebalancing lost most or all of it. They also showed that simple ways of trading less — such as a wider gap between the signal level that triggers a buy and the level that triggers a sale — rescued part of what costs took. The effect being "real" in the data was never the whole question.

What the desk found when it tested the claims

Desk test: held up "When the spread is a large fraction of the day's range, no short-horizon edge survives it." Spread/ATR above 0.15: zero of 30 strategy × timeframe cells profitable. Cost outruns edge as the timeframe speeds up; the crossover is the same across strategies. This is the \(g = s/\text{ATR}\) check in the calculator — a first filter to run before any new intraday idea, though as the worked example shows, passing it does not make a strategy profitable.
Desk test: nothing found "Price stretched far from VWAP snaps back — fade the extension." Positive gross across timeframes, but 3–8× under cost; the edge decays as fast as the cost does as the timeframe shortens. In this lesson's terms, \(\rho\) between about 0.13 and 0.33: a gross effect that cannot be traded. It survived as a risk overlay on some strategies, not as an entry.
Desk test: nothing found The double-cost stress in practice. A daily volatility-band fade on 25 FX pairs and gold, pre-registered with costs on: the vol-targeted book (485 trades) made a Sharpe of 0.14 ± 0.30, below a random-entry control's 95th percentile of 0.40, and 0.05 at 2× cost. Whatever was there was not distinguishable from random entries before costs were doubled, and almost nothing was left after.
Section 07

How practitioners actually use this

📒

Costs in the backtest, from day one

Research is run net of costs from the start, with spreads that vary by hour and event, not a single average. A gross-only result is treated as unfinished.

✖️2

The double-cost test

A strategy that dies when costs double is fragile. Real costs drift up — wider spreads in stress, bigger size, worse fills than the backtest's bar close.

🧾

Transaction cost analysis

Every fill is compared with the decision price. Realised cost per trade is tracked and fed back into the break-even calculation (see Execution Algorithms).

🐢

Trade less, on purpose

Wider rebalance bands, signal thresholds, and holding periods long enough for the move to dwarf the spread. Cutting turnover is often the cheapest edge improvement available.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where cost thinking goes wrong

Counting half the spread. A round trip crosses the whole spread — half on the way in, half on the way out. Slippage, likewise, is paid on both sides.

Using the quiet-hour quote. Strategies that trade on news, at the open or at the roll pay the spread of those moments, which can be many times the headline figure.

Filling at the bar close. A backtest that enters at the close of the signal bar assumes a price you could not have had. Enter on the next bar's open, plus slippage.

Treating significance as size. A huge \(n\) makes any non-zero edge significant. Compare the edge per trade with the cost per trade, not the p-value with 0.05.

Ignoring financing. A position held overnight pays or earns the swap. On a slow carry-negative trade it can be the biggest cost of all.

Rescuing a dead strategy by filtering on the results. Keeping "only the best setups" chosen after the fact raises the gross edge on paper and nowhere else. Fix the filter first, then test it on fresh data.

Scenario drill · an illustrative research review

A "highly significant" fade lands on your desk

A colleague brings you an intraday EUR/USD fade: 3,000 trades over three years out of sample, about 1,000 a year, average gross profit 0.9 pips, standard deviation 15 pips. The t-statistic is 3.29. All-in cost per round trip, measured honestly, is 1.3 pips. They want to start trading it on Monday. What do you say?

  1. Go ahead. A t of 3.29 means the edge is real.
    Real, yes, and smaller than its cost. At 0.9 pips gross and 1.3 pips a round trip, every trade loses 0.4 pips on average, about 400 pips a year. More trades make the loss more certain, not smaller.
  2. Not as it is. Its break-even cost is 0.9 pips, under the 1.3 it pays. Rework it to trade less on bigger moves, fix any filter in advance, and test it again on fresh data.
    This is the cost-check card: compare the edge per trade with the full round-trip cost, then look for a version with room to spare. A filter chosen after seeing the results has to be confirmed on data it was not chosen on.
  3. Trade it, but with a cheaper broker to cut commission.
    Cutting cost is the right instinct, but even free commission leaves 0.1 pips a trade, a net t near 0.37, and a loss if costs double. It is marginal at best, not a strategy.
  4. Trade it at double the size to make the small edge worth the effort.
    Size multiplies the net edge, and the net edge is negative. Bigger size also tends to mean worse fills, which raises the cost further.

The point. This drill is illustrative: it uses the lesson's worked example, not a real fund. The same pattern is documented in the real world. Perold's 1988 paper showed Value Line's real fund earning far less than its paper portfolio, and Novy-Marx and Velikov (2016) found that many high-turnover stock anomalies lost most or all of their premium to realistic trading costs.

Section 08

Key Takeaways

✓Cost per round trip is \(s + 2\ell + m + f\). The full spread, slippage on both sides, commission, financing — at the hour you actually trade.
✓The gross edge is your break-even cost. Pay more per round trip and you lose. Ask for \(\mu/c \ge 2\).
✓Significance is not size. t grows with \(\sqrt{n}\); the edge per trade does not. A t of 3.3 on a 0.9-pip edge still loses to a 1.3-pip cost.
✓Turnover is where cost bites. Cost drag on Sharpe is \((c/\sigma)\sqrt{N}\); slower trades capture bigger moves for the same cost.
✓Desk test held up: spread/ATR above 0.15 left zero of 30 strategy × timeframe cells profitable. Run it first — it is necessary, not sufficient.
✗Desk tests found nothing tradeable in a VWAP-extension fade (positive gross, 3–8× under cost) or a daily band fade (Sharpe 0.14, 0.05 at 2× cost). That is a verdict on those tests, not on mean reversion as an idea.
Section 09

Self-test

Q1. Spread 0.6 pips, slippage 0.3 pips a side, commission 0.7 pips a round trip, no financing. What is the cost per round trip?
\(c = 0.6 + 2(0.3) + 0.7 + 0 = 1.9\) pips. The slippage counts twice: once on entry, once on exit.
Q2. A strategy averages 2.5 pips gross a trade. What is its break-even cost, and what is the most you should be paying if you want a 2× margin?
Break-even \(c^{*} = \mu = 2.5\) pips. A 2× margin (\(\rho = \mu/c \ge 2\)) means \(c \le 1.25\) pips.
Q3. A backtest of 10,000 trades shows a gross edge of 0.3 pips with σ = 10 pips. Is the edge real? Is it tradeable at 1 pip a round trip?
Standard error \(10/\sqrt{10000} = 0.1\), so \(t = 3.0\): very probably real. Net edge \(0.3 - 1 = -0.7\) pips, net \(t = -7\): very certainly a loser. Real and tradeable are different questions.
Q4. You quadruple the number of trades in your sample at the same average edge. What happens to t and to the effect size d?
\(t = \mu\sqrt{n}/\sigma\) doubles (\(\sqrt{4} = 2\)). The effect size \(d = \mu/\sigma\) does not change at all. More data makes you surer of the edge's size; it does not make the edge bigger.
Q5. Two strategies each have μ/σ = 0.05 per trade and c/σ = 0.03. One trades 2,500 times a year, the other 100. What is each one's annual net Sharpe and cost drag?
Net per-trade ratio \(0.05 - 0.03 = 0.02\). Fast: \(0.02\sqrt{2500} = 1.0\) net, drag \(0.03 \times 50 = 1.5\). Slow: \(0.02 \times 10 = 0.2\) net, drag 0.3. Same ratios, so the faster one has the better Sharpe here — turnover helps when the edge per trade already beats the cost. The catch in practice is that faster trades usually have a smaller edge per trade for the same cost, so \(\rho\) falls as you speed up.
Q6. A strategy passes the spread/ATR ≤ 0.15 gate. Can you conclude it survives costs?
No. The gate looks at the spread alone against the typical move. Commission, slippage on both sides and financing are not in it, and the edge itself may be a small share of the ATR. In the worked example \(g = 0.10\) passed while the total cost was 1.44 times the gross edge. Passing the gate means the idea is not dead on arrival; only the net edge says it is alive.
