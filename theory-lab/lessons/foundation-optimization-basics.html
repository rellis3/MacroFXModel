Optimization Basics: Unconstrained & Constrained

How calculus finds the "best" value of something — with no restrictions, and then with a restriction bolted on via Lagrange multipliers, the exact machinery behind portfolio optimization.

CONCEPT — not built or tested in this repo ~24 min read Prereqs: Partial Derivatives & Multivariable Calculus
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • An unconstrained optimum satisfies the first-order condition — the derivative (or gradient) is zero — and the second-order condition checks whether that flat point is a genuine minimum, a maximum, or a saddle.
  • Constrained optimization — finding the best value of \(f(x)\) while requiring some condition \(g(x)=k\) to hold exactly — is solved with the Lagrangian, \(\mathcal{L} = f(x) - \lambda\,(g(x)-k)\), whose own first-order conditions recover the constraint alongside a new, principled equation.
  • The Lagrange-multiplier intuition: at the constrained optimum, the objective's gradient and the constraint's gradient point along the exact same line — if they didn't, you could nudge along the constraint and still improve the objective, so that point couldn't have been optimal.
  • KKT conditions are the version of this same logic for inequality constraints (≤ or ≥ instead of an exact =) — name-checked here, fully treated in a later Numerical Methods lesson.
  • Markowitz portfolio optimization — minimizing a portfolio's variance subject to hitting a target expected return — is exactly this lesson's Lagrange-multiplier problem, worked fully by hand here with two made-up but plausible assets.
  • How it reached trading: Harry Markowitz (1952) turned portfolio choice into this lesson's constrained problem, minimum variance for a target return, and constrained optimisers have sized portfolios and hedges ever since.

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

People keep asking me for the best portfolio. Best how, though?

That is the right question. Best always means making one number as big or as small as possible. Lowest risk, say. Sometimes there are no rules. Usually there is a rule that must hold.

Start with no rules.

Picture walking up a hill with your eyes closed. While the ground still tilts up under your feet, you are not at the top. The top is the one spot where the ground feels flat in every direction. In maths, the slope is zero there.

So flat means best?

Flat means a candidate. Flat ground could be the top of a hill, the bottom of a valley, or a mountain pass that dips one way and rises the other, like a horse saddle. You check which way the ground curves to tell them apart.

Fine. Now add a rule.

Put a fence on the hill. You may only walk along the fence. The real summit may not even be on it. The best spot on the fence is where walking further along it, either way, stops helping.

How do you find that spot?

At that spot, the uphill direction of the hill points straight across the fence. If it leaned along the fence at all, you could take one more step and still climb. A Lagrange multiplier is the trick that turns that idea into equations you can solve.

Give me numbers.

Two assets. One expects eight percent a year, with twenty percent volatility. The other expects four percent, with ten percent volatility. They only loosely move together. The rule: the portfolio must expect six percent.

Six is halfway between four and eight.

So you hold half and half. The portfolio's volatility comes out at about twelve and a half percent. That is below the fifteen percent you would get by averaging the two, because they do not move in lockstep. That is diversification, for free.

Where did this idea come from?

Harry Markowitz, in nineteen fifty-two. He framed choosing a portfolio as exactly this problem: the lowest risk for a target return, with weights that add up to one. It earned him a share of the Nobel memorial prize in economics in nineteen ninety.

And the multiplier itself? Is it just bookkeeping?

No, it is the price of the rule. It tells you how much extra risk you must accept for one more unit of target return. Push the target higher and each extra unit costs more than the last.

Any catch?

The maths is exact, but only for the numbers you feed it. Expected returns are estimates from history, and the answer is very sensitive to small errors in them.

So the one-line version?

Best means the slope is flat. A rule means the best point on the fence. And the multiplier tells you what the fence costs.

Section 01

Why this matters

"Find the best \(x\)" is one of the most common questions in quantitative finance, and it always turns out to mean one of two more precise things. Sometimes it means "find the \(x\) that makes \(f(x)\) as large (or small) as possible, with no restrictions at all" — an unconstrained problem. More often, in practice, it means "find the best \(x\) subject to some condition that must hold exactly" — a constrained problem: the best portfolio weights that hit a target return, the best-fit line subject to passing through a fixed point, the optimal trade size subject to a fixed risk budget. This lesson builds both cases from the ground up, using only the gradient and Hessian machinery from the previous lesson, and ends at the exact piece of math underneath Markowitz portfolio theory — one of the most widely used ideas in this curriculum's later portfolio-construction material.

Plain-English intuition

The top of a hill has no uphill direction left — and a fence changes where "the top" even is

Imagine standing somewhere on a hilly landscape and being asked to walk to the highest point. As long as there's an uphill direction available to you, you haven't found the top yet — by definition, you could still improve. The genuine summit is recognizable by exactly one property: every direction from there is flat or downhill. There is no direction left that still goes up. That's the entire content of the first-order condition for an unconstrained optimum: a flat gradient means no direction improves things any further, which is exactly what "optimal" should mean, with nothing else restricting where you're allowed to stand.

Now put a fence around part of the landscape — you're only allowed to stand somewhere along a specific path (a ridge line, say), not anywhere on the whole hillside. The unrestricted summit might not even be on that path at all. The best point on the path is instead the spot where walking further along the path, in either direction, stops helping — even though, if you could step off the path, you might still be able to climb higher. At that best-on-the-path point, a striking geometric fact holds: the "uphill" direction of the landscape and the direction the path is heading both point along the exact same line (they're either the same direction or exact opposites). If they didn't — if the path still had a component heading uphill — you could walk a little further along the path and gain height, so you wouldn't have been at the best point on the path yet. That alignment of two different gradients — the landscape's and the path's — is the entire geometric idea behind a Lagrange multiplier, made precise below.

How it reached trading

How much of each asset to hold

Constrained optimisation · Markowitz, 1952 — Black & Litterman, early 1990s

Before the 1950s investors knew diversification helped but had no way to say how much. Investment advice valued each security on its own; nothing traded risk off against return across a whole portfolio.

In 1952 Harry Markowitz, then a graduate student at Chicago, framed the choice as a constrained optimisation in "Portfolio Selection": minimise portfolio variance subject to a target expected return and weights that sum to one. Lagrange multipliers, devised in 1788 for constrained mechanics, solve exactly that. The work earned Markowitz a share of the 1990 Nobel Memorial Prize, and in the early 1990s Fischer Black and Robert Litterman at Goldman Sachs extended it to blend market-implied returns with a manager's views.

Constrained optimisers now sit behind portfolio construction, risk budgets, hedge sizing under limits and execution schedules that trade cost against risk. The multiplier has a desk meaning of its own: the shadow price, how much the objective improves if a constraint is loosened by one unit.

Section 02

The math, step by step

Watch the formula build
Where the Lagrange condition f′(x*) = λg′(x*) comes from, one term at a time
\[ \mathcal{L}(x, \lambda) = \color{#fbbf24}{f(x)} \]

Start with the objective: the thing you want as large or as small as possible. With no fence, you would stop where its slope is zero, \(f'(x^*) = 0\).

\[ \mathcal{L}(x, \lambda) = f(x) - \lambda\,\big(\color{#fbbf24}{g(x) - k}\big) \]

Add the fence. The rule \(g(x) = k\) is rewritten as \(g(x) - k\), which is exactly zero when the rule holds.

\[ \mathcal{L}(x, \lambda) = f(x) - \color{#fbbf24}{\lambda}\,\big(g(x) - k\big) \]

Scale the fence by a new unknown, the multiplier \(\lambda\). Now treat \(\mathcal{L}\) as an ordinary function of two inputs and look for its flat point.

\[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \lambda} = \color{#fbbf24}{-\big(g(x) - k\big) = 0} \]

Flat in the \(\lambda\) direction hands the rule straight back: \(g(x) = k\). You can only stand on the fence.

\[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial x} = f'(x) - \lambda g'(x) = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \color{#10b981}{f'(x^*) = \lambda\, g'(x^*)} \]

Flat in the \(x\) direction says the two gradients are parallel at the best point on the fence. \(\lambda\) is the scale between them, and its size is the price of the fence.

Unconstrained optimization: the first-order condition

For a single-variable function \(f(x)\), the point where \(f\) is locally largest or smallest — with no restriction on \(x\) at all — must be a point where the tangent line is perfectly flat, i.e. where the derivative is exactly zero. This is the first-order condition (FOC):

\[ f'(x^*) = 0 \]
x*the candidate optimal point — a "stationary point"
f'(x*) = 0no uphill or downhill direction left at x* — the flat-tangent condition

For a multivariable function, the identical idea generalizes directly using the gradient from the previous lesson: every partial derivative must be zero at once, i.e. the entire gradient vector must vanish:

\[ \nabla f(x^*) = 0 \]
∇f(x*) = 0every partial derivative is zero — no direction, in any variable, still improves f

A point satisfying the first-order condition is called a stationary point — but "stationary" is deliberately not the same word as "optimal." A flat tangent line (or a flat gradient) is equally consistent with a minimum, a maximum, or — in more than one dimension — a saddle point. The first-order condition narrows down the candidates; it does not, by itself, tell you which kind of candidate you've found.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine walking up a hill with your eyes closed. As long as you can still feel the ground tilting upward under your feet, you know you haven't reached the top yet. The very top is the one spot where the ground feels completely flat in every direction — that flatness is exactly what the first-order condition is checking for.

The second-order condition: telling a minimum from a maximum from a saddle

For a single-variable function, the second derivative \(f''(x^*)\) resolves the ambiguity, using exactly the curvature idea from the Taylor-series lesson: a positive second derivative means the function curves upward at \(x^*\) (a bowl shape — a genuine local minimum); a negative second derivative means it curves downward (a genuine local maximum); a second derivative of exactly zero is inconclusive and needs a higher-order check.

\[ f''(x^*) > 0 \Rightarrow \text{local minimum} \qquad f''(x^*) < 0 \Rightarrow \text{local maximum} \]
f''(x*) > 0curves upward like a bowl — a genuine minimum
f''(x*) < 0curves downward like a dome — a genuine maximum

In more than one variable, the previous lesson's Hessian matrix plays exactly this role: check its eigenvalues at the stationary point. All positive means the function curves upward in every direction (a genuine minimum); all negative means every direction curves downward (a genuine maximum); a mix of positive and negative eigenvalues means the function curves upward in some directions and downward in others — a saddle point, which the first-order condition alone cannot distinguish from a true optimum. If any eigenvalue is exactly zero (and none has the opposite sign), the second-order test is inconclusive, just like \(f''(x^*)=0\) in one variable. This Hessian-eigenvalue check is precisely what a later Numerical Methods lesson's "convex optimization" material formalizes and builds algorithms around — a function whose Hessian has all-positive eigenvalues everywhere (not just at one point) is called convex, and for a convex function, any stationary point found is guaranteed to be the true global minimum, not just a local one.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Flat ground can mean three different things: the top of a hill (a maximum), the bottom of a valley (a minimum), or the middle of a mountain pass that dips down one way and rises up the other, like a horse saddle. The second derivative is what tells these apart — it checks which way the ground curves right where you're standing.

Constrained optimization: the Lagrangian

Now add a restriction: find the \(x\) that optimizes \(f(x)\), but only among the \(x\) that satisfy some constraint \(g(x) = k\) exactly (a fixed target value \(k\)). The unconstrained first-order condition, \(\nabla f = 0\), is generally the wrong thing to check now — the unconstrained optimum may not even satisfy the constraint at all, and even where it's feasible, moving along the constraint might still allow improvement.

The method of Lagrange multipliers solves this by building a new function, the Lagrangian \(\mathcal{L}\), that folds the constraint into the objective using a new variable \(\lambda\) (the "Lagrange multiplier"):

\[ \mathcal{L}(x, \lambda) = f(x) - \lambda\,\big(g(x) - k\big) \]
f(x)the original objective — what you actually want to optimize
g(x) − kthe constraint, rewritten so it equals zero exactly when satisfied
λthe Lagrange multiplier — a new, to-be-solved-for variable

Now take the unconstrained first-order condition of \(\mathcal{L}\), but with respect to both \(x\) and \(\lambda\) at once — treating \(\mathcal{L}\) as an ordinary multivariable function with one extra input, \(\lambda\):

\[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial x} = f'(x) - \lambda g'(x) = 0 \qquad \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \lambda} = -(g(x) - k) = 0 \]
∂L/∂x = 0gives f'(x) = λg'(x) — the parallel-gradients condition, explained below
∂L/∂λ = 0gives back g(x) = k exactly — the original constraint, recovered automatically

Two things worth noticing about this construction, because they're easy to take on faith without seeing why they work: first, differentiating \(\mathcal{L}\) with respect to the new variable \(\lambda\) hands back the original constraint exactly — the Lagrangian is built precisely so that this happens, guaranteeing feasibility is enforced automatically by the same first-order-condition machinery used everywhere else in this lesson, rather than needing to be checked separately. Second, differentiating with respect to \(x\) gives \(f'(x) = \lambda g'(x)\) — read plainly, the objective's gradient and the constraint's gradient must be parallel (scalar multiples of one another, with \(\lambda\) as the scaling factor) at the constrained optimum. This is exactly the fence/ridge-line intuition above, made algebraic: if the two gradients weren't parallel, the objective's gradient would have some leftover component running along the constraint, and moving a little further along the constraint in that direction would still improve \(f\) — meaning the point couldn't have been optimal yet.

A brief word on KKT conditions

Everything above assumes the constraint is an exact equality, \(g(x)=k\). Many real problems instead have an inequality constraint — "risk must be at most some budget," "a weight must be at least zero (no short-selling)." The generalization of Lagrange multipliers to that case is called the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions. In plain language, KKT conditions add one new idea on top of everything above: an inequality constraint is either "active" (binding exactly, behaving just like the equality case above) or "slack" (not binding at all, in which case its multiplier is simply zero and it drops out of the problem entirely) — and the conditions specify exactly how to tell which case you're in. The full mechanics are deferred to a later Numerical Methods lesson in this curriculum; the one thing worth carrying forward from here is that KKT conditions are not a different idea from Lagrange multipliers, they are the same parallel-gradients logic, extended to handle constraints that don't have to bind exactly.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Sometimes a rule isn't "you must walk exactly on this line" but "you're allowed to be anywhere up to this limit" — like a height limit on an amusement park ride. Sometimes you bump right up against that limit, and sometimes the best spot doesn't even come close to it. KKT conditions are just the rules for figuring out which of those two cases you're in.

Worked example

Minimum-variance portfolio for a target return — solved with Lagrange multipliers

Two assets, made-up but plausible: Asset 1 has expected return \(\mu_1 = 8\%\) and volatility \(\sigma_1 = 20\%\); Asset 2 has expected return \(\mu_2 = 4\%\) and volatility \(\sigma_2 = 10\%\); their returns have correlation \(\rho = 0.30\). Find the portfolio weights \(w_1, w_2\) (with \(w_1 + w_2 = 1\)) that achieve a target expected return of \(6\%\) with the smallest possible variance.

1

Write down the objective and the constraints

Portfolio variance, with \(w_2 = 1 - w_1\) substituted in so there's only one free weight, \(w_1\):

\[ \sigma_p^2(w_1) = w_1^2\sigma_1^2 + (1-w_1)^2\sigma_2^2 + 2w_1(1-w_1)\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2 \]

and the return constraint, \(w_1 \mu_1 + (1-w_1)\mu_2 = 6\%\).

2

Solve the return constraint for \(w_1\) directly

Because there's only one free weight after substituting \(w_2=1-w_1\), the return constraint alone pins down \(w_1\) exactly — no Lagrange multiplier is even needed for this simplified two-asset, one-target-return version (the multiplier becomes essential once a third asset, or a second constraint like "weights sum to 1" treated separately, is added — shown in step 4):

\[ w_1(8) + (1-w_1)(4) = 6 \ \Longrightarrow\ 4 + 4w_1 = 6 \ \Longrightarrow\ w_1 = 0.5,\quad w_2 = 0.5 \]
3

Compute the resulting minimum variance

With \(\sigma_1=0.20\), \(\sigma_2=0.10\), \(\rho=0.30\), \(w_1=w_2=0.5\):

\[ \sigma_p^2 = (0.5)^2(0.20)^2 + (0.5)^2(0.10)^2 + 2(0.5)(0.5)(0.30)(0.20)(0.10) \]
\[ = 0.25(0.04) + 0.25(0.01) + 0.5(0.30)(0.02) = 0.01 + 0.0025 + 0.003 = 0.0155 \]

So \(\sigma_p = \sqrt{0.0155} \approx 12.4\%\) — a portfolio volatility below Asset 1's own 20% and even below a naive average of the two (15%), because the correlation is well below 1 and diversification genuinely helps.

4

Where the Lagrange multiplier actually earns its keep

With exactly two assets and exactly one return target, the return equation alone already pins down the one free weight, so \(\lambda\) drops out of the arithmetic — but the full Lagrangian machinery is what justifies solving it that way, and becomes indispensable the moment there's more than one free weight (three or more assets) plus a return-target constraint plus the separate "weights sum to 1" constraint simultaneously. Setting up \(\mathcal{L} = \sigma_p^2(w) - \lambda_1(\mu^\top w - 6\%) - \lambda_2(\mathbf{1}^\top w - 1)\) and solving \(\nabla_w \mathcal{L} = 0\) alongside both constraints is exactly the general \(N\)-asset Markowitz problem — the two-asset case above is the smallest possible instance of it, worked by hand so every step is checkable, before the full \(N\)-asset linear-algebra machinery (a later portfolio-theory lesson) is needed.

Real-world trading scenario

Markowitz portfolio construction is this lesson's Lagrangian, at institutional scale

A portfolio manager overseeing dozens of positions faces exactly the constrained-optimization structure built above, just with many more assets: minimize the portfolio's overall variance, \(w^\top \Sigma w\) (where \(\Sigma\) is the full covariance matrix — the Deeper Foundations linear algebra lesson's central object), subject to hitting a specific target expected return, \(w^\top \mu = \text{target}\), and subject to the weights summing to 1 (fully invested, no cash left over). This is precisely the Lagrangian problem from step 4 of the worked example above, just with \(N\) weights instead of one free weight and two constraints folded in via two separate multipliers instead of one.

The Lagrange multiplier attached to the return constraint has a genuine economic meaning a portfolio manager cares about directly: it measures, at the margin, how much extra portfolio variance must be accepted to push the target return up by one more unit — literally the "price," in variance terms, of demanding a higher return. This is not an abstract mathematical side effect: if the efficient frontier (the curve of best possible risk-return trade-offs) is drawn as minimum variance against target return, \(\lambda\) is exactly its slope, \(d\sigma_p^2/d(\text{target})\), at the target being solved for. That slope rises as the target is pushed higher above the minimum-variance portfolio's own return, so each extra unit of return costs more variance. In the usual picture, with risk on the horizontal axis and return on the vertical, the same fact shows up the other way round: the frontier gets flatter as you move up it.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

Nobody solves a Lagrangian by hand for a 50-asset portfolio — a numerical optimizer (a quadratic program, in Markowitz's case, since the objective is a quadratic function of the weights) does the algebra. What survives into daily practitioner use is the vocabulary and the intuition, not the hand-derivation:

📈

The efficient frontier

Solving the Lagrangian problem above for many different target returns at once traces out the entire efficient frontier — one Lagrange multiplier value per point on the curve.

💰

Shadow prices

A Lagrange multiplier's value is routinely read as a "shadow price" — how much the optimal objective would improve per unit of relaxing a binding constraint (a risk budget, a leverage cap, a sector exposure limit).

🧮

Numerical solvers, not hand algebra

Real portfolio optimizers, risk-parity solvers, and calibration routines use numerical quadratic/convex-programming software that implements KKT conditions internally — the hand-derivation here is what to check the output against conceptually, not a step performed live.

🚧

Binding vs. non-binding constraints

Practitioners routinely check which constraints in a large optimization are actually "binding" (a nonzero multiplier) versus slack (multiplier zero, not actually limiting anything) — exactly the KKT active/inactive distinction name-checked above.

The habit worth carrying forward: whenever a portfolio, risk, or calibration problem is described as finding the "best" something "subject to" some condition, that's a signal the Lagrangian/KKT machinery is running underneath, whether or not the report ever uses the word "Lagrangian" — and any reported "marginal cost" or "shadow price" of a constraint is, structurally, exactly the multiplier \(\lambda\) built on this page.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where this goes wrong in practice

Treating a stationary point of the Lagrangian as automatically optimal. Just as with the unconstrained case, \(\nabla\mathcal{L}=0\) identifies a candidate, not a guarantee — without checking a second-order condition (or confirming the problem is convex), a Lagrangian stationary point could be a saddle rather than a genuine constrained optimum.

Forgetting an inequality constraint can be non-binding. A common mistake when moving from this lesson's exact-equality case to a real problem with inequality (KKT) constraints is assuming every constraint is active. A "risk must be at most X" constraint is often slack at the optimum (the true best portfolio doesn't even need the full risk budget) — forcing it to bind exactly, when it needn't, produces a worse, needlessly constrained solution.

Over-trusting an in-sample Markowitz solution. The minimum-variance-for-a-target-return machinery is exact given the inputs \(\mu\), \(\Sigma\) — but those inputs are themselves estimates from historical data, and Markowitz optimization is well known to be highly sensitive to small errors in the estimated expected returns \(\mu\) especially. A mathematically optimal solution to the wrong (mis-estimated) inputs is not the same thing as a good real portfolio — a distinct problem from anything covered in this lesson's pure optimization mechanics.

Reading the Lagrange multiplier's sign backwards. The sign convention in \(\mathcal{L} = f(x) - \lambda(g(x)-k)\) is a choice (some texts write \(+\lambda\) instead) — the multiplier's numerical value and its interpretation as a shadow price depend on which sign convention was used to set up the problem, so comparing a \(\lambda\) from one source against another without checking the sign convention each used is a subtle, common error.

Scenario drill · an illustrative Monday

The optimiser says: buy more than you have

You run the two-asset portfolio from this lesson. Asset 1 expects 8% with 20% volatility, Asset 2 expects 4% with 10%, and their correlation is 0.3. Your client now wants a 10% target return. You type 10 into the calculator above. It says: put 150% of the money in Asset 1 and sell short 50% of Asset 2. Volatility comes out at 28.9%. What do you do?

  1. Put it on. The maths is exact, so the answer is optimal.
    It is exact for the inputs, not for the world. A 10% target is above the best asset's own 8%, so the only way there is borrowing plus a short sale, with more risk than either asset alone. And the 8% is only an estimate: if it is a little too high, the whole plan rests on a number that is not there.
  2. Show the client that 10% needs leverage, a short and 28.9% risk, and offer a lower target or a no-short rule.
    This reads the optimiser honestly. A no-short rule is an inequality fence: it caps Asset 1 at 100%, so the most this pair can deliver is 8% at 20% risk. Making the fence visible lets the client choose the trade-off with open eyes.
  3. Cap Asset 1 at 100% and quietly deliver 8%.
    Safer, and the cap is a sensible rule. But it misses the target without saying so. The client should know that 10% cannot be reached without leverage.
  4. Lower the correlation estimate until the risk looks acceptable.
    That bends the input to get the answer you want. The optimiser will obey, and the real risk will not change at all.

What to take from it. This is an illustrative situation built on the lesson's own two assets, not a historical event. The numbers check out in the calculator above: a 10% target gives \(w_1 = 1.500\), a short-position warning and \(\sigma_p = 28.90\%\). A constrained optimiser always obeys its fences and its inputs. Your job is to check both.

Section 04

Key Takeaways

✓An unconstrained optimum has a zero derivative (or gradient). The first-order condition, \(f'(x^*)=0\) or \(\nabla f(x^*)=0\), says no direction still improves the objective.
✓The second-order condition tells minimum from maximum from saddle. The sign of \(f''(x^*)\), or the Hessian's eigenvalue signs in several variables, resolves what the first-order condition alone leaves ambiguous.
✓Lagrange multipliers fold a constraint into the objective. \(\mathcal{L}=f(x)-\lambda(g(x)-k)\); its own first-order conditions recover the constraint automatically and force the objective's and constraint's gradients to be parallel at the optimum.
✓Markowitz portfolio optimization is exactly this Lagrangian problem. Minimizing portfolio variance subject to a target-return constraint (and a weights-sum-to-1 constraint) is the direct \(N\)-asset generalization of the two-asset worked example.
✓A Lagrange multiplier is a shadow price. Its value measures how much the optimal objective would change per unit change in the constraint's target — a genuinely useful economic number, not just algebra bookkeeping.
⚠KKT conditions (inequality constraints) are only name-checked here, not fully derived. A binding vs. slack distinction — new relative to this page's exact-equality case — is deferred to a later Numerical Methods lesson.
⚠A mathematically exact optimum is only as good as its inputs. Markowitz's clean algebra is well known to be highly sensitive to estimation error in expected returns — a distinct, practical concern from the pure optimization mechanics covered here.
ℹMarkowitz made portfolio choice a constrained optimisation. His 1952 problem, lowest variance for a target return, is a Lagrangian; the multiplier is the price of the constraint.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. Why isn't a zero derivative, \(f'(x^*)=0\), enough by itself to conclude \(x^*\) is a minimum?
A zero derivative only says the function is momentarily flat at \(x^*\) — the tangent line has no slope there. That flatness is equally consistent with a local minimum (curving upward on both sides), a local maximum (curving downward on both sides), or, in more than one variable, a saddle point (curving upward in some directions and downward in others). Distinguishing which one actually holds requires the second-order condition — the sign of \(f''(x^*)\) in one variable, or the signs of the Hessian's eigenvalues in several.
Q2. In plain language, why must the objective's gradient and the constraint's gradient be parallel at a constrained optimum?
If the two gradients were not parallel, the objective's gradient would have some non-zero component running along the direction of the constraint itself (the "ridge line" from the intuition box). That would mean you could move a small step further along the constraint — staying feasible the whole time — and still increase (or decrease, whichever is being optimized) the objective, which would contradict the point being optimal in the first place. Only when the objective's gradient has no leftover component along the constraint — i.e. the two gradients point along the exact same line — is there no feasible direction left that still improves things.
Q3. In the two-asset minimum-variance worked example, why did the Lagrange multiplier not actually need to be computed?
Because with exactly two assets constrained to sum to 1 (\(w_2=1-w_1\)) and exactly one additional return-target constraint, there was only one genuinely free variable (\(w_1\)) and one equation relating it to the target return — that single equation alone was enough to solve for \(w_1\) directly, with no optimization needed at all in this restricted two-asset case. The Lagrangian machinery becomes indispensable once there are more free weights than constraints (three or more assets), where minimizing variance subject to the constraints genuinely requires balancing a trade-off rather than just solving a determined system of equations.
Q4. What is the practical difference between an "active" (binding) and a "slack" (non-binding) inequality constraint in a KKT-type problem?
A binding (active) constraint holds with exact equality at the optimum — for example, a risk budget that is fully used up — and its Lagrange multiplier is generally nonzero, carrying a genuine shadow price. A slack (non-binding) constraint holds with room to spare at the optimum — the true best solution simply doesn't need to push up against that limit — and its multiplier is exactly zero, meaning the constraint has no marginal effect on the optimal objective at all. A common practical mistake is assuming every stated constraint must be binding, when in reality the optimizer may find a better solution well inside some of them.
Q5. Why does the Lagrange multiplier on a Markowitz return-target constraint have an economic meaning, rather than just being a bookkeeping variable?
The multiplier's value equals the rate at which the optimal (minimum) portfolio variance changes as the return target is nudged up by a small amount — in other words, it is exactly the marginal "cost," in variance terms, of demanding one more unit of expected return. That is precisely the slope of the efficient frontier at the target return being solved for, when the frontier is drawn as minimum variance against target return, which is a real, economically meaningful quantity a portfolio manager cares about directly (how much more risk must be accepted to chase a higher return target), not an artifact of the algebra used to solve the constrained problem.

Tucker's 1951 paper "Nonlinear Programming" — the two independent origins of what are now jointly called the KKT conditions, the inequality-constraint generalization named but not fully derived on this page.
