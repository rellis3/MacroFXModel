Lesson 2 · Options in Practice

Five Ways to Trade Options, or How Each One Fails

"I trade options" describes almost nothing. People buy direction, sell premium, trade volatility, hedge and trade one option against another. Same contract, five different bets, five different ways to lose. This guide shows what each one is really betting on, how often it wins, and what edge it needs. Educational, not advice: options you buy can lose the whole premium, and options you sell can lose far more than you collect.

The whole idea
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

People keep telling me options are dangerous. But they are just contracts. Where does the danger actually come from?

From how you use them. There are five common ways. You can buy for direction, sell premium, trade volatility, hedge, or trade one option against another. Same contract, five different bets.

Take the first two. A stock is at one hundred, and the market expects a normal swing of about six percent over the next month.

Buying a call struck at one hundred and five costs about sixty-four cents. It only pays if the stock finishes above one hundred and five, plus the premium. That happens about sixteen times in a hundred. When it does, the average win is about three dollars twenty-six, roughly four and a half times the average loss.

A low win rate, with big wins. And selling?

Sell a put struck at ninety-five and you collect fifty-seven cents. You keep it about eighty-three times in a hundred. But the average win is forty-six cents and the average loss is two dollars eighty-two. The loss is six times the win.

So the seller looks like a genius for months.

Until the month that matters. And here is the key point. If volatility is priced fairly, both traders average the same result before costs, which is zero. A high win rate and a low win rate are two ways of slicing the same average.

Then how does anyone make money?

Only by holding a view that differs from the price, and being right. A seller needs implied volatility to run above what is later realised. A buyer needs a bigger move than the price assumes. Without that, costs turn it into a small, steady loss.

Do costs really matter that much?

Researchers who studied retail option traders, in the Netherlands and in the United States, found they lose money on average. The American study found they favour cheap weekly options, with an average bid-ask spread of twelve point six percent. Paying that on every trade is a heavy toll.

What about the other three styles?

Volatility traders bet on how far the price travels, not which way, and fail when the move is already in the price. Hedgers pay to cap a risk they already hold, so their option usually expires unused. That is the point. Relative-value traders bet that two options are mispriced against each other, and fail when the gap widens before it closes.

Has anyone sold insurance at scale and survived?

Berkshire Hathaway sold about thirty-seven billion dollars of long-dated index puts and collected four point nine billion. At the end of two thousand and eight it valued them as a ten billion dollar liability, a five point one billion dollar paper loss. But the contracts could only be settled at the end, and only a small share needed collateral, so nobody could force it to sell.

So a style can survive, with the right funding.

Right. Before you trade any of the five, finish the sentence: I get paid because. Then know exactly how you lose, and whether you can hold through it.

Section 1

One instrument, five bets

A stock at 100, 30 days, implied volatility 20%, costs of 10 cents a leg. Figures include those costs.

🎯

1. Buy direction

Betting on direction, size and timing at once. The 105 call wins 15.8% of the time; the average win is 4.5 times the average loss.

🧾

2. Sell premium

Betting on a quieter market than priced. The 95 put wins 82.9%, but one loss ($2.82) is worth 6.1 wins ($0.46).

🌊

3. Trade volatility

Betting on how far, not which way. The unhedged 100 straddle wins 40.5%, average win $3.21 against average loss $2.52.

🛡️

4. Hedge

Betting on nothing: paying to cap a risk you hold. 100 shares plus the 95 put cap the loss at $5.67 for $0.57. Win rate is the wrong measure.

⚖️

5. Relative value

Betting that one option is rich against another. Short 30-day, long 90-day call: wins 42.7%, best case $1.36, worst case $1.87.

Same contracts, same fair prices. What changes is the shape of the bet, and with it how you lose.

Same view, different style

You expect 108. The call makes $2.26 a share, the put makes only its $0.47 credit. At 103 (up 3%) the call loses $0.74 and the put wins $0.47. At 90 the call loses $0.74 and the put loses $4.53. A view on direction does not choose the style.

Section 5

Volatility, hedging and relative value

🌊

Trade volatility

Edge is vega times the gap between your volatility forecast and the price: ν·(σ you − σ market) − c. A move that is already priced in pays nothing. Professionals delta-hedge so direction drops out.

🛡️

Hedge

The option usually expires unused. Judge it by the floor it buys and its price. It fails when it hedges the wrong exposure, is bought after the shock at peak prices, or is dropped after a long bleed.

⚖️

Relative value

One option rich against another: term structure, skew, strike against strike. Needs a view of fair value, tight spreads and funding to wait. It fails when the gap widens before it closes.

🔀

Styles blend

A covered call mixes hedging and selling premium. A collar sells upside to pay for downside cover. Name the main bet, then the second bet you did not mean to take.

Section 6

Who tends to use which

Tendencies, not rules. Most participants use more than one style.

🙋

Retail traders

Mostly buying calls and puts (style 1) and covered calls, cash-secured puts and simple credit spreads (style 2).

🏦

Dealers and market makers

Quote both sides, earn the spread, then hedge the delta and vega they are left with. Their book is the other side of everyone else's trade.

📈

Funds

Volatility and relative-value funds run styles 3 and 5, delta-hedged and risk-sized. Macro funds buy options for convexity and sell them for income.

🏭

Corporates, managers, insurers

Mostly hedging (style 4): currency and commodity exposure, protective puts and collars on holdings.

Section 7

What the retail data say

Two peer-reviewed studies

Bauer, Cosemans and Eichholtz (2009), Journal of Banking & Finance. Most individual investors lost substantially on their option positions, much more than on equities. The authors point to poor timing and high trading costs, and find gambling and entertainment motives matter far more than hedging.

United States, payment for order flow

Bryzgalova, Pavlova and Sikorskaya (2023), Journal of Finance. Retail investors favoured cheaper, weekly options, with an average bid-ask spread of 12.6%, and lost money on average.

For scale: 12.6% of the $0.64 call is about 8 cents of spread. A round trip across it costs that whole width, so the lab's 10 cents per leg is realistic for such options. These are averages in particular samples. They do not say every retail trader loses.

Section 8 · It actually happened

Berkshire Hathaway's index puts, 2008

It actually happened

By the end of 2008 Berkshire had sold put options on the S&P 500, FTSE 100, Euro Stoxx 50 and Nikkei 225, expiring in 15 and 20 years (first on 9 September 2019, last on 24 January 2028). Maximum payout $37.1 billion; premiums received $4.9 billion. Neither side could settle early, and only a small percentage of its derivative contracts called for collateral. At year-end it valued the puts at a $10 billion liability, a reported mark-to-market loss of $5.1 billion with nothing yet paid. Source: Berkshire Hathaway chairman's letter for 2008.

Section 9

Which style fits your edge

Start from what you can actually forecast.

🎯

Direction, size and a window

Buy direction. You must beat the break-even and the time decay. Size it so losing the whole premium is acceptable.

🧾

Implied usually above realised

Sell premium, defined risk first. Size by the worst case, not the margin, and expect rare large losses.

🌊

A better forecast of move size

Trade volatility, delta-hedged. The forecast must beat implied out of sample, with tight execution.

🛡️

A risk you already hold

Hedge. You need a reason, a budget and a fair price. Judge it by what it protects.

⚖️

One option rich against another

Relative value. Tight spreads on every leg, funding to wait, and a view of fair value.

∅

Cannot finish "I get paid because…"

None of the five. Not trading is a position. Without an edge every style averages minus its costs.

Section 10

Where each style quietly goes wrong

🏆

Reading win rate as skill

A seller's high win rate is the shape of the bet. A long winning run is what the shape predicts.

🧭

Choosing from the view, not the price

"I am bullish, so I buy a call" skips how big, by when, and what the market already assumes.

🧱

Defined risk is not small risk

A capped loss limits the worst case. It does not make a bad edge good.

🪙

Ignoring costs

Costs are certain and an edge is not. With wide spreads and several legs they can exceed a small edge.

🔀

Style drift

Selling "to fund the buying" changes the risk without changing the limits. Write down the bet before you trade.

📏

Sizing from margin

Margin is a funding number, not a risk number. Size by the worst case you can name.

Section 11

Quick check

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

A short put wins 83% of the time but averages a loss. Why?

At a fair price p·W = (1 − p)·L. The put wins $0.46 and loses $2.82 on average, so 0.829 × 0.46 − 0.171 × 2.82 = −$0.10 after costs.
Question 2

If implied volatility equals realised, what does each style average before costs?

A fairly priced option's average payoff equals its price. Costs then take a toll on every leg, so each style averages minus its costs.
Question 3

Why is win rate the wrong measure for a hedge?

You buy a hedge to cap a risk you already hold. 100 shares plus the 95 put cap the loss at $5.67 a share for a premium of $0.57.
Question 4

What kept Berkshire's short index puts from forcing it out?

A $5.1 billion paper loss at the end of 2008 did not force it to sell. A retail seller facing daily margin can be closed out at the worst moment.
Question 5

Realised volatility comes in at 25% against 20% implied. Who is paid?

With costs of 10 cents a leg the straddle buyer averages +$0.94 and the call buyer +$0.34, while the put seller averages −$0.50.
Your call
Scenario drill · an illustrative earnings week

Everyone expects a big move. Which side do you take?

This is a hypothetical situation, not a real company, with round numbers chosen to keep the arithmetic clear. A stock is at $100 and reports results tomorrow after the close. The nearest at-the-money straddle costs $6.40, so the market is pricing a move of roughly 6% either way. You also expect a big move, perhaps 6%. You have no view on direction, and costs are $0.10 per option leg. What do you do?

  1. Buy the straddle. A big move is coming.
    The price already contains your forecast. If the move is 6% as priced, the straddle returns about what it cost before costs, and the 20 cents of costs on two legs makes the average result negative. You need the move to be larger than 6.4% plus costs, and you have no reason to think it will be.
  2. Sell the straddle. The price is high, so collect it.
    A price that matches a fair forecast is not high. If the move is 6% you roughly break even before costs. A 15% move would lose about $8.60 against the $6.40 collected, with no cap on how large it can be, and you have no reason to think the move will be smaller than priced.
  3. Pass, unless you can say why you expect a move bigger or smaller than 6.4%.
    Edge is your forecast against the price. Where the two agree there is none, and costs make the average trade a loss on either side. A trade is worth considering only when you have a reason for a different number, and then you size it for the case where you are wrong.
  4. Buy a small straddle for the fun of it, sized so that losing the premium is trivial.
    Defensible as a bet you treat as entertainment, with the loss capped at an amount you do not mind. It is not an edge: the expected result is minus the costs. The research on retail option traders found entertainment was a bigger motive than hedging, and it found large average losses.

This is a hypothetical, so there is no outcome to report. The arithmetic is the point. If the typical move really is the priced 6.4%, a straddle buyer's average result is zero before costs and −$0.20 after them, and the seller's is also −$0.20. Both sides lose the toll to the market maker. Someone can only do better than that with a forecast that differs from the price and is right.

Section 12

What to carry forward

01

There are five styles: buy direction, sell premium, trade volatility, hedge and relative value. Same contracts, different bets, different ways to lose.

02

Win rate and payoff shape are one fact: at fair prices p·W = (1 − p)·L. A high win rate means a large average loss.

03

Edge = V(you) − P − c: your forecast against the price, minus costs. For a volatility view it is vega times the volatility gap.

04

Retail option traders have lost money on average in two peer-reviewed studies; timing, costs and wide spreads are among the reasons.

05

It actually happened, Berkshire 2008: $37.1bn of 15 and 20 year index puts, $4.9bn collected, a $5.1bn paper loss, no forced selling. Funding, not style, decided whether it could hold on.

06

Before any trade, finish the sentence "I get paid because…", name how you lose, and check you can hold through it.

What this now lets you do

Look at any options trade and say which of the five bets it really is, how often it should win and how big the average win and loss are, what must be true for it to pay, and how it fails. That is the question set the rest of this series builds on.
