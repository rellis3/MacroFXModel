Lesson 21 · Applied Statistics & Validation

ARIMA & VAR, Beyond One Lag, One Series

AR(1) told you a series pulls back toward one mean, remembering only yesterday. Real data rarely cooperates that cleanly — a shock can keep echoing forward after the level has already reverted, a raw price level needs to be tamed before "mean reversion" even applies, and in FX and macro almost nothing moves in isolation. ARIMA and VAR are the two directions that single-series mean reversion generalizes in: more memory inside one series, or more series feeding back on each other.

The whole idea
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

My simple model says a series gets pulled back toward its mean. Why would I need anything fancier?

Because that model has two blind spots. It only remembers yesterday. And it only knows about one series.

Start with the memory.

ARIMA gives it more. First, more lags of the level: today can depend on yesterday, the day before, and so on. That's the autoregressive part. Second, a memory of past surprises rather than past levels. A shock can keep echoing for a bar or two after the price has settled. That's the moving-average part.

And the I in the middle?

It stands for integrated. Most raw prices trend, so there's no fixed mean to pull back to. You difference first: you model daily changes, not levels. For most daily financial series, once is enough.

How do I pick how many lags?

You read them off the data. Plot the partial autocorrelations of the changes. If they spike at lags one and two and then drop to nothing, while the plain autocorrelations fade away slowly, that's two autoregressive lags. You write down ARIMA two, one, zero.

And then I'm done?

Not yet. Check the leftovers. If a Ljung-Box test still finds a pattern in the residuals, say at lag three, you go round again. Identify, estimate, check, revise. That loop is the Box-Jenkins method.

Now the second blind spot. I want to know whether the German and US yield spread leads euro-dollar.

That's two series, so you need a vector autoregression. Each series is regressed on its own past and on the other one's past. In the simplest case that's four coefficients, fitted together.

Why not just regress euro-dollar on yesterday's spread?

Because that only looks for one arrow. The VAR checks both directions at once: spread to currency, and currency to spread.

And if the spread's past does help forecast the currency?

Then the spread "Granger-causes" the currency. Careful with that word. It means the spread's history helps the forecast. It doesn't mean the spread makes the currency move. A hidden common driver can produce the same result.

Anything that should keep me humble?

Two things. The number of coefficients grows with the square of the number of series. Five series with four lags is about a hundred coefficients. And in 1983 Meese and Rogoff showed that exchange-rate models, VARs included, didn't beat a random walk out of sample.

So the one-line version?

Difference first, read the lags off the data, model the series together, and make your forecast beat a random walk before you believe it.

Section 1 · How it reached trading

Beating the random walk — or finding out you can't

Box & Jenkins (1970); Sims (1980); Meese & Rogoff (1983).

How it reached trading

Box and Jenkins' 1970 recipe gave forecasters a disciplined model for one series. Sims' 1980 VARs let a few variables — rates, inflation, a currency — explain each other, and became standard at central banks.

Meese and Rogoff (1983) set the FX bar: out of sample, exchange-rate models, VARs included, did not beat a random walk. ARIMA is now the benchmark; VARs map how a shock spreads.

Section 2

AR(1) grows a longer memory: AR(p)

AR(1) only looks back one step: Xₜ = c + φXₜ₋₁ + εₜ. The natural next move is to let today depend on several past values at once, each with its own weight.

Xₜ = c + φ₁Xₜ₋₁ + φ₂Xₜ₋₂ + ⋯ + φₚXₜ₋ₚ + εₜ
Xₜ

Today's value

The thing being modeled — a price, a rate, a spread, once it's already stationary.

φᵢ

Weight on lag i

How much of the value from i periods ago still explains today, after accounting for every lag in between — not a raw correlation.

p

How far back to look

The order of the model — how many past lags actually enter the equation.

εₜ

This period's shock

The white-noise surprise the model can't explain — same role as dWₜ in the OU lesson, in discrete time.

You don't choose p by eyeballing raw autocorrelations — you use the partial autocorrelation function (PACF) instead, because it strips out what the intervening lags already explain. The AR(1) signature you already know — one PACF spike at lag 1, then a sharp drop, alongside an ACF that decays smoothly — generalizes directly: for AR(p), the PACF shows significant spikes at lags 1 through p and then cuts off, while the ACF keeps decaying gradually well past lag p.

Why the PACF, not the ACF

A raw autocorrelation at lag 3 mixes together "lag 3 truly matters" and "lag 3 correlates with lag 2, which correlates with lag 1, which correlates with today." The PACF answers the sharper question — does lag 3 add anything once lags 1 and 2 are already in the model — which is exactly what deciding p requires.

Section 3

A different kind of memory: MA(q)

AR(p) only ever uses past levels of X. Some series carry a second, different memory — for past shocks, not past positions.

Xₜ = εₜ + θ₁εₜ₋₁ + ⋯ + θqεₜ₋q
AR term — memory of levels

"Today depends on where X was i days ago" — the rubber-band-to-a-post mechanism from the OU lesson. A price that sits persistently away from fair value and slowly closes that gap over many days is AR-like behavior.

MA term — memory of shocks

"Today depends on how large the surprise was j days ago" — an echo that can persist even after the level itself has already fully reverted. A news shock that fully passes through price by the next bar, but whose noisy aftereffects (a bid/ask imbalance, a liquidity gap slowly refilling) nudge returns for one or two more bars, is MA-like behavior.

The identification signature flips relative to AR: an MA(q) process has an ACF that cuts off sharply after lag q (spikes at 1 through q, then ~zero), while its PACF decays gradually instead. Real series can carry both mechanisms at once — which is exactly the motivation for combining them rather than picking only one.

Section 5

Blending both memories: ARMA(p,q)

Xₜ = c + Σ φᵢXₜ₋ᵢ + εₜ + Σ θⱼεₜ₋ⱼ
AR

Σ φᵢXₜ₋ᵢ

The autoregressive piece — memory of past levels.

MA

Σ θⱼεₜ₋ⱼ

The moving-average piece — memory of past shocks.

ARMA blends both in one equation. The practical reason to allow mixed models at all, rather than always fitting a pure AR or a pure MA, is parsimony: a series that would need a high-order pure AR(10) or MA(10) to fit well is often described just as accurately by a low-order ARMA(1,1) — fewer parameters estimated from the same amount of data, and fewer free parameters is directly less overfitting risk.

Four canonical shapes cover the identification step: PACF cutoff with ACF decay is pure AR(p). ACF cutoff with PACF decay is pure MA(q). Both decaying gradually is a mixed ARMA(p,q). Neither cutting off at all almost always means the series still isn't stationary — differencing hasn't happened yet.
Section 6

Why raw price levels need differencing first — the "I"

Everything so far assumes X is already stationary. Most raw financial levels fail that outright — no fixed mean to revert to, exactly the φ ≥ 1 boundary the OU lesson flags as "the model doesn't apply here."

Push φ toward 1 and the blue path stops snapping back toward zero — it starts to look like the undifferenced red line above. That's not a coincidence: φ = 1 on a series is exactly the "no fixed mean" case, which is precisely why differencing has to happen before this AR(1) machinery is even valid.
The trap

Reading ACF/PACF shapes off a raw, non-stationary price level — before checking stationarity or differencing — produces a meaningless, slowly-decaying ACF at every lag, not a real cutoff pattern. Always difference (or run a unit-root test like Augmented Dickey-Fuller) first. Most daily financial series need only d = 1; d = 2 is uncommon and usually a sign something's off in the transformation, not a genuine property of the data.

Section 7

ARIMA(p,d,q) and the Box-Jenkins loop

Once the differenced series passes its stationarity check, an ordinary ARMA(p,q) gets fit to that series. Put together, that's ARIMA.

ΔᵈXₜ = c + Σ φᵢΔᵈXₜ₋ᵢ + εₜ + Σ θⱼεₜ₋ⱼ
p

AR order

How many past levels enter — read off the PACF cutoff.

d

Differencing order

How many times to difference until stationary — from a unit-root check.

q

MA order

How many past shocks enter — read off the ACF cutoff.

This is the Box-Jenkins methodology — an iterative recipe, not a single fit. Identify: difference until stationary, pattern-match ACF/PACF to propose p and q. Estimate: fit the candidate model's coefficients. Check: the residuals should look like white noise — a Ljung-Box test checks this formally. If structure remains, go back to step one and revise. For example: a series needing one difference whose PACF shows spikes at both lag 1 and lag 2 before cutting off suggests ARIMA(2,1,0) — but if diagnostic checking then flags leftover autocorrelation at lag 3, that's the model telling you it isn't done, and you extend or add an MA term rather than accepting the first candidate.

This is the AR(1) half of an ARIMA(1,1,1) fit on the differenced series: ΔX̂ₜ₊₁ = c + φ·ΔXₜ. A full ARIMA(1,1,1) forecast also adds θ times the last residual — left out here so the AR mechanism stands on its own — and requires |φ| < 1 to be a stationary, mean-reverting fit at all.

Section 9

VAR: when series feed back on each other

Everything above still describes one series' own history. VAR widens the question: does each series' own past, and every other series' past, help forecast it?

Xₜ = a₁₁Xₜ₋₁ + a₁₂Yₜ₋₁ + ε₁ₜ
Yₜ = a₂₁Xₜ₋₁ + a₂₂Yₜ₋₁ + ε₂ₜ

a₁₁ and a₂₂ are each series' own AR(1) coefficient. a₁₂ is the cross-effect of Y's past on X; a₂₁ is the cross-effect of X's past on Y. All four are estimated jointly from the same system — which is exactly what lets a VAR reveal bidirectional feedback that a single "Y on lagged X" regression, by construction, can never see. It was only ever set up to look for one arrow.

Section 10

Granger causality — narrower than the name suggests

The classic diagnostic built on a fitted VAR: does X's history significantly improve forecasting Y, beyond Y's own past?

Concretely: compare a regression of Y on only its own lags against one that also adds X's lags, and run an F-test on whether adding X improves the fit by a statistically significant amount. If it does, X "Granger-causes" Y. That is a strictly statistical, predictive claim — not a claim of true, structural, mechanistic causation.

Roosters crowing right before sunrise "Granger-cause" the sunrise by this narrow definition, even though everyone knows the rooster isn't actually causing the sun to rise — the Earth's rotation drives both on its own schedule.
Where this needs qualifying

A third, unobserved common driver — a shared macro-regime shock hitting both series with slightly different timing — can produce a clean "X Granger-causes Y" result with no direct causal link between them at all. Treat a Granger result as "X's history has genuine forecasting content for Y," never as "X makes Y happen."

Section 11

Where this family of models goes wrong in practice

⚠

Spurious regression

Fitting AR/VAR-family models to raw, trending levels can produce impressively high R² and significant-looking coefficients that are pure statistical mirage. The "I" step exists precisely to prevent this — skipping it is the most common way this family of models breaks before estimation even starts.

k²

VAR's parameter count grows fast

A VAR's coefficients grow roughly as k² × p (k series, p lags) — a VAR(4) across 5 series already needs on the order of 100 coefficients. Adding a series "just in case" squares the parameter growth, not adds to it linearly — a fast route to overfitting on a few thousand daily bars.

≈

Cointegrated series need a VECM

Two series that are each non-stationary individually but move together long-run lose that relationship if you naively difference both before fitting a plain VAR. A Vector Error-Correction Model keeps the long-run equilibrium as an explicit term instead of differencing it away.

↻

Regime dependence

A lead-lag relationship estimated over one regime isn't guaranteed to hold in the next. A single full-sample coefficient or Granger result can hide a regime-dependent reversal a pooled test would never surface.

ARIMA is the standard classical baseline for single-series forecasting — the model to beat before crediting a fancier approach with adding anything. VAR's home turf is macroeconomics, where it displaced older simultaneous-equation models built on hard-to-defend structural assumptions; Christopher Sims's 1980 case for letting an unrestricted VAR reveal dynamic relationships from the data directly is part of the work behind his 2011 Nobel Memorial Prize.

Section 12

Quick check

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

What's the practical difference between an AR term and an MA term?

AR is a memory of where X has been. MA is a memory of how large recent surprises were — an echo that can persist even after the level itself has already reverted. Real series can carry both, which is why ARMA combines them.
Question 2

Why does ARIMA need a differencing step ("I") that plain ARMA doesn't?

A raw price level has no fixed mean to revert to — the φ ≥ 1 boundary case. Differencing removes that trend so the ARMA machinery becomes valid on what's left. You check stationarity (ACF decay, or a unit-root test) rather than guessing d.
Question 3

A differenced series' PACF shows spikes at lags 1 and 2, then cuts off; its ACF decays smoothly. What order does that suggest?

PACF-cutoff-at-p / ACF-decays is the AR(p) signature — here p=2. One difference was already applied (d=1), and there's no ACF-cuts-off-sharply evidence for an MA term, so q=0.
Question 4

In a two-variable VAR(1), a₁₂ (Y's past → X) comes back near zero while a₂₁ (X's past → Y) is large and significant. What does that mean?

a₂₁ large means X's past meaningfully improves forecasting Y. a₁₂ near zero means Y's past adds nothing for forecasting X. Together that's a one-directional lead-lag relationship, not a two-way feedback loop.
Question 5

Why isn't "X Granger-causes Y" the same claim as "X causes Y"?

Granger causality only means lagged X significantly improves forecasting Y beyond Y's own history. A shared, unobserved shock hitting both series with slightly different timing can produce exactly that signature with no direct causal link at all.
Your call
Scenario drill · an illustrative lead-lag result

The spread Granger-causes EUR/USD. Now what?

You fit a VAR(2) to daily changes in the 2-year DE–US yield spread and daily EUR/USD log returns, both stationary. You test both directions. Two days of lagged spread changes significantly improve the EUR/USD forecast; lagged EUR/USD returns do not help forecast the spread. What do you do with this?

  1. Conclude that the spread drives EUR/USD, and trade it at full size on the full-sample result.
    Granger causality is a forecasting claim, not a mechanism: a common driver could produce the same result. And a full-sample fit can hide a regime where the link reverses. Nothing here has been tested out of sample.
  2. Treat the spread as a candidate leading indicator with a two-day window, then test that timing out of sample, across regimes, against a random-walk benchmark.
    That uses what the VAR found, a direction and a horizon, without over-reading it. The out-of-sample test is where the claim earns trust, and in FX the random walk is the bar to beat.
  3. Refit the VAR on raw yield and price levels to get a stronger fit.
    Fitting trending levels invites spurious regression: impressive fit statistics and significant-looking coefficients that are a statistical mirage.
  4. Use the spread only as a same-day confirmation check.
    Cautious, and it will not blow up. But it throws away the one useful thing the test found: that the spread's effect shows up over the following two days.

What it means. This is an illustrative result, the same hypothetical one the lesson uses, not a documented fit. The documented fact that frames it: Meese and Rogoff (1983) found that exchange-rate models, VARs included, did not beat a random walk out of sample. A one-directional Granger result is a reason to test a timing rule, not proof that one works.

Section 13

What to carry forward

01

ARIMA generalizes AR(1)/OU two ways — more lags of the level (AR(p)), a second memory for past shocks (MA(q)), and differencing (the "I") to make a non-stationary series stationary first.

02

The order (p,d,q) is read off the data, never guessed — the Box-Jenkins loop of identify, estimate, and diagnostic-check (Ljung-Box) revises the model until the residuals look like white noise.

03

VAR is the orthogonal generalization — instead of widening one series' own lags, it widens how many series are modeled jointly, so each variable's forecast can depend on every variable's lags, itself included.

04

A single-equation regression can only see one arrow — a VAR estimates both cross-effects jointly, revealing bidirectional feedback a "Y on lagged X" regression never could.

05

Granger causality is a statistical claim, not a structural one — "X's lags improve forecasting Y," never "X makes Y happen."

06

Single-series ARIMA is already live in this repo's alert pipeline; a multi-series VAR/Granger fit on the yield-spread/FX question is still a research candidate, not a shipped result.

07

How it reached trading: Box–Jenkins and Sims' VARs became central-bank and macro-desk workhorses; Meese & Rogoff (1983) showed the random walk is the FX bar to beat.

What this now lets you do

Read an ACF/PACF pair and identify whether a series looks AR, MA, mixed, or still non-stationary. Explain why a raw price level needs differencing before AR(1)-style reasoning applies at all. Recognize when a research question — like whether the yield spread leads FX — actually needs a VAR with Granger causality testing, rather than a single-equation regression that can only look one direction.
