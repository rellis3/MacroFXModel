Combining Signals That Disagree

Three indicators say long, two say short. The vote says long. The honest answer may be "no view". Here is how to turn five readings into one number — without counting the same opinion three times.

Section 1

From "weigh it up in your head" to a formula

Grinold (1989); Grinold & Kahn (1995).

How it reached trading

Grinold's fundamental law of active management (1989): a strategy's quality grows with each forecast's skill (IC) times the square root of the number of independent bets. Ten correlated signals are not ten bets.

Grinold and Kahn's Active Portfolio Management made it a recipe: score every signal, forecast = volatility × IC × score, combine with weights that respect correlation.

Section 2

Five friends, one newspaper

Three friends say "rain" — but all three read the same paper. That's one opinion said three times. The friend with the barometer and the one who looked at the sky are each telling you something new.

Counting votes

Three trend indicators + two others. "3 to 2, go long." The trend idea gets counted three times.

Counting opinions

Group near-copies into families, weight each family by its tested record, then see what's left.

Section 4

Combine by regression, not by vote

weights w = R⁻¹ · IC · forecast F = Σ wᵢ·sᵢ

sᵢ = each signal's score in its own standard deviations; IC = its correlation with the next return; R = how the signals correlate with each other. R⁻¹ is what stops near-copies being counted twice.

Section 5

Weight by track record — then shrink it

keep B = τ² / (τ² + 1/n) of the measured IC

A correlation measured on n observations has a standard error of about 1/√n. With 100 trades that's 0.10 — bigger than a good signal's true IC. τ = 0.05 is how widely true ICs vary.

50 observations keep 11%, 400 keep half, none keep nothing. An untested indicator gets zero weight, however good its chart looks.

Section 7

"No view" is an output

z = F / IC_comb · expected move = σ·F · P(right) = Φ(|F| / √(1 − IC_comb²))

1

|z| under 1

The signals cancel, or today's reading is ordinary for this set.

2

Move under 2× cost

The edge doesn't survive trading it (lesson 4).

3

Combined IC ≈ 0

Nothing on the screen has a record. Loud is not the same as tested.

Even an excellent combined IC of 0.10 at a two-standard-deviation reading gives a 58% chance of the right direction. Small edges are the normal case.

Section 9 · It actually happened

August 2007: many signals, one position

In the week of 6 August 2007, many quantitative equity funds — each running its own blend of value, momentum and quality signals — took unusually large losses in a few days, while the broad market moved comparatively little.

Khandani and Lo's study argued the funds held far more overlapping positions than anyone knew. A fast unwind by one or more large players pushed prices against everyone holding the same trades. A sharp partial rebound followed within days.

Same pattern, 1998

LTCM's many "separate" relative-value trades all widened together after Russia's default. In September 1998 the New York Fed organised a recapitalisation of about $3.6 billion by a group of banks. Diversification that is counted, not measured, disappears when you need it.

Section 10 · The desk's verdict

What was tested here

Desk test: nothing found"When three macro domains agree, direction follows." Liquidity, curve and credit all +1 or all −1, 39 and 38 episodes 2009–2026: no forward edge on EUR/USD, the S&P 500 or gold. The one cell that looked real (EUR/USD −0.616% over 20 days) pointed the same way after negative confluence too — drift, not prediction.
Desk test: held upTwo genuinely different signals, combined. 2-year and 10-year yield-spread legs: 15.7% trade overlap, 0.300 return correlation. Combined Sharpe 1.15 beat each leg (1.02, 0.81) — close to the formula's (S₁+S₂)/√(2(1+ρ)) ≈ 1.13. Backtest evidence; not forward-proven.

Section 12

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Four momentum indicators, average correlation 0.7. How many independent signals?

4 / (1 + 3 × 0.7) = 4 / 3.1 = 1.29. The ceiling for any number of them is 1/0.7 ≈ 1.43.
Question 2

A new indicator shows IC 0.12 over 100 trades (τ = 0.05). What IC do you use?

B = 0.0025 / (0.0025 + 0.01) = 0.2, so 0.12 × 0.2 = 0.024. The noise in 100 trades (~0.10) is nearly as big as the measurement.
Question 3

In the EUR/USD example, why does ignoring correlation say "long"?

Each trend signal keeps its full 0.020 weight, so the family adds 0.074 against the short side's 0.043. Corrected, the three share one signal's worth and the rates signal wins.
Question 4

The desk found three-domain macro agreement didn't predict direction. So…

The desk's test found no edge in that agreement. Its two yield-spread legs, each with a record and correlated only 0.30, did combine into a better book.
Section 13

What to carry forward

01

Correlated signals are one vote: N_eff = N/(1+(N−1)ρ). Count families, not indicators.

02

Combine by regression (w = R⁻¹·IC), not by vote.

03

Shrink every record by its length; untested means weight zero.

04

Write the no-view rule before you look. "No view" is an answer.

05

Desk test: macro agreement alone found nothing; two different, tested spread legs combined well.
