Cornish-Fisher Expansion

The textbook Gaussian VaR formula quietly assumes returns are symmetric and normally-tailed — the Cornish-Fisher expansion adjusts the quantile itself for skew and fat tails, without ever refitting the whole distribution.

CONCEPT — explains a quantile-adjustment technique; not built or used anywhere in this repo ~13 min read Prereqs: Coherent Risk Measures: VaR, CVaR & the Artzner Axioms (this lesson builds directly on its VaR definition)
TL;DR
  • The standard Gaussian VaR formula, \(\text{VaR}_\alpha = -\mu - z_\alpha \sigma\), assumes returns are normally distributed — but real return series usually have negative skewness \(S\) and positive excess kurtosis \(K\) (fatter tails than a bell curve).
  • The Cornish-Fisher expansion (Cornish & Fisher, 1938) replaces the plain normal quantile \(z_\alpha\) with an adjusted quantile \(z_{CF}\) that corrects for \(S\) and \(K\), then plugs \(z_{CF}\) into the same VaR formula in place of \(z_\alpha\).
  • For a realistic negative-skew, fat-tailed return series (\(S=-0.5\), \(K=3\)), the worked example below shows the CF-adjusted 95% VaR coming out about 4.7% larger than the plain Gaussian VaR — a meaningfully more conservative number, computed from the exact same \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\).
  • The adjustment only touches the quantile \(z_\alpha \to z_{CF}\); it never requires refitting a whole new distribution to the data, which is why it's cheap to bolt onto an existing Gaussian VaR pipeline.
  • Honest limitation: for sufficiently extreme skew/kurtosis combinations, the Cornish-Fisher transform stops producing a valid, monotonically increasing quantile function — it is a useful correction within a bounded range of inputs, not a universal fix for non-normality.
Section 01

Why this matters

Coherent Risk Measures defines \(\text{VaR}_p(L)\) precisely as a quantile of the loss distribution and doesn't assume any particular shape for that distribution. In practice, though, the single most common way people actually compute a VaR number is to assume returns are normally distributed and read the quantile straight off a standard normal table. That's a convenient shortcut, and it's also a specific, checkable assumption — one that fails for almost every real financial return series. Daily FX, equity, and rates returns are not symmetric (a currency can gap much further on a surprise devaluation than it typically drifts on an ordinary day) and they are not thin-tailed (large moves happen more often than a bell curve predicts). A VaR number computed as if neither of those things were true is quietly understating the loss threshold exactly where it matters most — the tail. The Cornish-Fisher expansion is the standard fix that doesn't require throwing out the Gaussian formula and starting over: it corrects the one number that's wrong, the quantile itself.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Same ruler, moved mark

Imagine a tailor who always marks a hem 1.645 inches up from the floor because that's the right number for someone with perfectly average, symmetric proportions. Most customers aren't perfectly average — some lean noticeably more one way, some have unusually long or short limbs relative to their height — and for those customers, using the "average person" mark produces a hem that's subtly wrong, always in the same direction their body deviates from average. The Cornish-Fisher expansion is the tailor learning to nudge that 1.645-inch mark based on two easily measured facts about the specific customer: how lopsided they are (skewness) and how much more extreme their proportions are than typical (kurtosis). The tape measure, the hem formula, none of that changes — only the one number being plugged into it moves, and it moves in a direction and amount that depends on exactly how this customer differs from the idealized average one. That's precisely what Cornish-Fisher does to a VaR quantile: it keeps the same formula, and nudges \(z_\alpha\) into \(z_{CF}\) using the return series's actual skewness and kurtosis instead of assuming they're both zero, as a pure normal distribution would.

Section 02

The math, step by step

Recall: the Gaussian VaR formula

Coherent Risk Measures already covers what VaR means and why it's a defensible (if incomplete) risk statistic — this lesson doesn't re-derive that. What it adds is the most common way VaR actually gets computed in practice: assume a return \(R\) is normally distributed with mean \(\mu\) and standard deviation \(\sigma\), and read the loss threshold straight off the normal distribution's quantile function.

\[ \text{VaR}_\alpha = -\mu - z_\alpha \sigma \]
μthe mean of the return series
σthe standard deviation of the return series
αthe tail probability, e.g. 0.05 for a 95%-confidence VaR
z_αthe standard normal quantile at tail probability α, e.g. \(z_{0.05} \approx -1.645\)

Read this as: the loss threshold equals minus the mean return, minus \(z_\alpha\) standard deviations — and because \(z_\alpha\) is negative for a small \(\alpha\) (it's on the left tail of the return distribution), subtracting it adds a positive amount to the loss threshold, exactly as it should. This formula is exact if returns really are normally distributed. The entire rest of this lesson is about what to do when they aren't.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

The plain Gaussian formula is like guessing how bad your worst day of the year will be using only "on average, how good or bad are my days" and "how much do my days usually wobble." That works fine if your days are all shaped the same, boringly symmetric way. It stops working the moment your bad days are a very different shape than your good days — which, for most real markets, they are.

Real returns have skew and fat tails

Two extra moments describe how a real return distribution departs from the normal shape the formula above assumes:

\(S\)Skewness — the third standardized moment. \(S=0\) is symmetric (normal). \(S<0\) (common for equity/FX returns) means a longer, fatter left (loss) tail than the right tail.
\(K\)Excess kurtosis — the fourth standardized moment, minus 3 (the normal distribution's own kurtosis), so \(K=0\) means normal-tailed. \(K>0\) ("leptokurtic") means more extreme observations in both tails than a normal distribution produces — the well-known "fat tails" of financial returns.

A return series with \(S<0\) and \(K>0\) — negative skew and fat tails — is the typical shape for daily equity and FX returns: crashes are sharper and more frequent than rallies, and both extremes happen more often than a bell curve predicts. Plugging \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) alone into the Gaussian VaR formula throws this shape information away entirely; \(S\) and \(K\) never appear in it.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Skewness is "which direction are the surprises lopsided in" — negative skewness means the nasty surprises tend to be bigger than the pleasant ones, even if pleasant surprises happen more often. Kurtosis is "how often do genuinely extreme days happen, in either direction, compared to what a nice smooth bell curve would predict." Real markets are typically lopsided toward nasty surprises and have more extreme days than a bell curve expects — both facts a plain average-and-wobble summary completely ignores.

The Cornish-Fisher adjusted quantile

Cornish and Fisher's 1938 result gives an adjusted quantile \(z_{CF}\) — built from the plain normal quantile \(z_\alpha\) plus correction terms in \(S\) and \(K\) — that is used in place of \(z_\alpha\) in the exact same VaR formula above:

\[ z_{CF} = z_\alpha + \frac{(z_\alpha^2-1)S}{6} + \frac{(z_\alpha^3-3z_\alpha)K}{24} - \frac{(2z_\alpha^3-5z_\alpha)S^2}{36} \]
z_αthe starting point — the plain Gaussian quantile
Sskewness — feeds the two skew-related correction terms
Kexcess kurtosis — feeds the fat-tail correction term
z_CFthe adjusted quantile — substitute this for z_α in the VaR formula
z_α
The baseline
What you'd use if returns really were normal — S=0, K=0 collapses everything below to zero
(z_α²−1)S/6 − (2z_α³−5z_α)S²/36
Skew correction
Pushes the quantile further into the tail when losses are more extreme than gains (S<0)
(z_α³−3z_α)K/24
Kurtosis correction
Pushes the quantile further out when the distribution has fatter tails than normal (K>0)

Every term besides \(z_\alpha\) vanishes when \(S=0\) and \(K=0\) — so a perfectly normal return series gives \(z_{CF}=z_\alpha\) exactly, and the Cornish-Fisher VaR reduces back to the plain Gaussian VaR. The adjustment is purely a correction for how far the actual distribution's shape departs from normal; it adds nothing when there's no departure to correct for.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Going back to the tailor: the Cornish-Fisher formula is the exact recipe for how much to nudge that hem mark, based on measuring exactly how lopsided and how extreme this particular customer's proportions are. If the customer happens to be perfectly average in every way, the recipe correctly says "nudge it by zero" — you get the original mark back untouched.

\(\mu, \sigma\)Mean and standard deviation of the return series — unchanged from the plain Gaussian formula.
\(\alpha\)The tail probability defining the confidence level, e.g. \(\alpha=0.05\) for a 95%-confidence VaR.
\(z_\alpha\)The \(\alpha\)-quantile of the standard normal distribution — negative for \(\alpha<0.5\), e.g. \(z_{0.05}\approx-1.645\).
\(S\)Skewness of the return series — the third standardized moment.
\(K\)Excess kurtosis of the return series — the fourth standardized moment minus 3.
\(z_{CF}\)The Cornish-Fisher adjusted quantile — substituted for \(z_\alpha\) in the VaR formula to get a skew/kurtosis-aware loss threshold.

Worked example

Plain Gaussian VaR vs. Cornish-Fisher VaR, walked arithmetically

A daily return series has \(\mu = 0\%\), \(\sigma = 1.20\%\), skewness \(S=-0.5\), and excess kurtosis \(K=3\) — a realistic negative-skew, fat-tailed shape for a daily FX or equity return series. Compute the 95%-confidence VaR (\(\alpha=0.05\), \(z_{0.05}=-1.645\)) both ways.

1

Plain Gaussian VaR

Plug \(z_\alpha=-1.645\) straight into the VaR formula:

\[ \text{VaR}_{0.05} = -0\% - (-1.645)(1.20\%) = 1.974\% \]
2

The skew correction terms

With \(z_\alpha=-1.645\): \(z_\alpha^2-1 = 1.706025\), so the first skew term is \((1.706025)(-0.5)/6 = -0.14217\). And \(2z_\alpha^3-5z_\alpha = -0.67782\), so the second skew term is \(-(-0.67782)(0.25)/36 = +0.00471\).

3

The kurtosis correction term

\(z_\alpha^3-3z_\alpha = 0.48359\), so the kurtosis term is \((0.48359)(3)/24 = 0.06045\).

4

Sum for z_CF, then recompute VaR

\[ z_{CF} = -1.645 - 0.14217 + 0.06045 + 0.00471 \approx -1.7220 \]
\[ \text{VaR}_{0.05}^{CF} = -0\% - (-1.7220)(1.20\%) = 2.066\% \]
2.066% vs. 1.974% — the Cornish-Fisher-adjusted VaR is roughly 4.7% larger than the plain Gaussian VaR, computed from the exact same \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\). The entire difference comes from the quantile shift \(z_\alpha \to z_{CF}\); nothing else in the formula changed. (Setting \(\mu=0\) here keeps the arithmetic clean — a nonzero mean shifts both VaR numbers by the identical amount and doesn't change this comparison at all.)
🎯
Real-world trading scenario

A risk desk's daily VaR limit understates true tail risk on a carry book

A desk running short-volatility FX carry trades reports a daily 99% VaR limit computed the standard way: historical \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) plugged into the plain Gaussian formula. The strategy's return profile is exactly the shape carry trades are known for — small, steady gains most days (positive carry) punctuated by occasional sharp drawdowns when a funding currency spikes (a classic negative-skew, fat-tailed profile, since the strategy's own construction caps the upside per period while leaving the downside comparatively open). Reporting VaR the plain Gaussian way is, in effect, pretending the desk's returns look like a coin flip around a fair average — the exact opposite of the strategy's actual return shape. A risk manager who recomputes the same VaR limit using the desk's own historical skewness and kurtosis in the Cornish-Fisher adjustment gets a materially larger, more honest number for how bad a genuinely bad day could be — without needing to build or fit an entirely new distributional model, just by feeding two already-computable sample statistics into the adjusted quantile formula above.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

  • A drop-in upgrade to an existing Gaussian VaR pipeline. Because the correction only changes \(z_\alpha \to z_{CF}\) and leaves the rest of the formula untouched, it's a small, cheap change to bolt onto a VaR calculation that's already built around the normal-quantile approach — no need to refit a new parametric distribution or rebuild the pipeline around historical simulation.
  • A standard tool in risk-management and portfolio software. Cornish-Fisher-adjusted VaR is a documented, commonly offered option in commercial risk platforms and appears throughout the quantitative risk literature (see Favre & Galeano, 2002, in Further Reading) specifically because it gives a fast, moment-based way to account for non-normality without a full distributional refit.
  • Only as good as the sample moments feeding it. Skewness and especially kurtosis are notoriously noisy to estimate from a limited sample — a short or unstable return history can produce an \(S\) and \(K\) that don't reflect the distribution's true shape, and the CF adjustment inherits whatever estimation error is baked into those two numbers.
  • A stepping stone toward, not a replacement for, full tail modeling. Cornish-Fisher is a local correction around the Gaussian quantile — for genuinely extreme tail estimation, practitioners often turn to purpose-built tail machinery instead (see Further Reading for pointers on where that conversation continues).
⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where Cornish-Fisher gets over-trusted

  • The expansion can become non-monotonic for extreme skew/kurtosis — this is a real, well-documented limitation, not an edge case to hand-wave away. The whole point of a quantile function is that it increases as the confidence level increases — a 99% VaR should never come out smaller than a 95% VaR on the same distribution. For sufficiently large \(|S|\) and \(K\), the Cornish-Fisher transform can violate exactly that property, producing an implied quantile function that is no longer monotonically increasing in \(\alpha\). When this happens, the "adjusted VaR" the formula spits out is not a valid quantile at all — it's an artifact of the polynomial correction overshooting, and using it uncritically can silently produce a nonsensical risk number. This failure mode is formally characterized in the literature (see Jaschke, 2002, in Further Reading) — Cornish-Fisher is a useful correction within a bounded range of \((S,K)\) inputs, not a universal fix for non-normality.
    🧒 Explain like I'm 5

    Imagine the tailor's nudge-the-hem rule works great for normal amounts of lopsidedness, but if you feed it an absurdly extreme customer, the formula can get confused and tell you to make the "worse" hem mark end up higher than the "better" one — nonsense a real ruler would never produce. That's exactly what can happen here: for extreme enough skew or kurtosis, the adjustment can output a 99%-confidence loss number that's smaller than the 95%-confidence one, which is obviously wrong and a sign the formula has been pushed past where it's trustworthy.

  • Treating "more conservative" as automatically "more correct." The worked example above shows CF-VaR coming out larger than Gaussian VaR for negative skew and positive excess kurtosis — but the direction and size of the adjustment depend entirely on the sign and magnitude of \(S\) and \(K\) that were fed in. A badly estimated \(S\) or \(K\) (see below) can push \(z_{CF}\) the wrong way just as easily as the right one.
  • Feeding it noisy sample skewness/kurtosis from a short history. \(S\) and especially \(K\) are higher-order moments, and higher-order sample moments converge far more slowly and noisily than the mean or variance. A CF adjustment computed from, say, 60 days of returns can be dominated by one or two extreme observations rather than reflecting the distribution's true shape.
  • Forgetting this is still a quantile-based statistic. Everything the coherent-risk-measures lesson says about VaR's blind spot past the threshold still applies here — Cornish-Fisher adjusts where the quantile sits, it does not turn VaR into an average-of-the-tail measure like CVaR.
Section 04

Key Takeaways

✓Cornish-Fisher adjusts the quantile, not the formula. \(z_\alpha \to z_{CF}\) is the entire change — the VaR formula \(-\mu-z\sigma\) stays exactly the same.
✓The correction vanishes for a truly normal series. S=0, K=0 collapses every correction term to zero, so \(z_{CF}=z_\alpha\) exactly.
✓The worked example is concrete: 2.066% vs. 1.974%. A realistic S=−0.5, K=3 return series produces a CF-adjusted 95% VaR about 4.7% larger than the plain Gaussian number, from identical μ and σ.
✗The expansion can become non-monotonic for extreme skew/kurtosis. Outside a bounded range of (S,K), the "adjusted quantile" it produces is not a valid quantile function at all — a documented limitation, not a universal fix.
⚠It's only as good as the sample S and K feeding it. Higher-order moments are noisy in short samples — a badly estimated skew or kurtosis can push the adjustment the wrong way.
⚠This lesson is conceptual. It explains the technique precisely; it is not a claim that Cornish-Fisher-adjusted VaR is computed or used anywhere in this project.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. What single quantity does the Cornish-Fisher expansion adjust, and what stays exactly the same as the plain Gaussian VaR formula?
It adjusts only the quantile — \(z_\alpha\) becomes \(z_{CF}\). The surrounding formula, \(\text{VaR}_\alpha = -\mu - z\sigma\), is completely unchanged; \(z_{CF}\) is simply substituted for \(z_\alpha\) inside it.
Q2. Why does z_CF equal z_α exactly when a return series is perfectly normal?
A perfectly normal series has skewness \(S=0\) and excess kurtosis \(K=0\). Every correction term in the Cornish-Fisher formula is multiplied by \(S\), \(K\), or \(S^2\), so with both equal to zero, all three correction terms vanish and \(z_{CF}\) reduces to \(z_\alpha\) with nothing added or subtracted.
Q3. In the worked example (S=−0.5, K=3), is z_CF more negative or less negative than z_α, and why does that make the resulting VaR larger?
\(z_{CF}\approx-1.722\) is more negative than \(z_\alpha=-1.645\). Because the VaR formula subtracts \(z\) times \(\sigma\), a more negative \(z\) subtracts a more negative quantity, which adds more to the VaR — so a more negative \(z_{CF}\) directly produces a larger (more conservative) VaR number, exactly as the arithmetic in the worked example shows (2.066% vs. 1.974%).
Q4. What, precisely, goes wrong with the Cornish-Fisher expansion for sufficiently extreme skew/kurtosis?
The implied quantile function can stop being monotonically increasing in the confidence level \(\alpha\) — meaning the "adjusted VaR" at a higher confidence level (e.g. 99%) can come out smaller than at a lower one (e.g. 95%) on the same distribution, which is nonsensical for a genuine quantile. This is a known, documented limitation (Jaschke, 2002) — the technique is valid only within a bounded region of skew/kurtosis inputs, not universally.
Q5. Why is the Cornish-Fisher adjustment only as reliable as the sample skewness and kurtosis fed into it?
\(S\) and \(K\) are higher-order (third and fourth) standardized moments, and higher-order sample moments converge much more slowly and noisily than the mean or standard deviation, especially in short samples. A handful of extreme observations can dominate a sample's estimated skewness or kurtosis, meaning a CF adjustment built on a short or unstable history can reflect estimation noise rather than the distribution's true shape.
Q6. A colleague says "Cornish-Fisher VaR is always more conservative than Gaussian VaR, so it's always the safer number to use." What's wrong with that statement?
Whether \(z_{CF}\) is more or less negative than \(z_\alpha\) — i.e. whether the adjustment makes VaR larger or smaller — depends entirely on the sign and size of the sample \(S\) and \(K\) that go into it, not on some built-in conservative bias in the formula itself. A badly estimated or genuinely positively-skewed, thin-tailed series could push \(z_{CF}\) toward a smaller VaR than the Gaussian number. The direction of the adjustment is data-dependent, not automatic.
