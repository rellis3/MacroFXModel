Primer 1 · Math Primer

Descriptive Statistics, the Vocabulary Every Later Lesson Assumes

Every lesson after this one in this series uses σ, variance, and "standard deviations away from the mean" without re-explaining any of it. Ornstein-Uhlenbeck's stationary variance, GARCH's forecast of σ itself, every Bollinger Band and VaR statement and "is this move unusual" filter — all of it is built from five ideas: mean, variance, standard deviation, z-scores, and the bell curve. This is where they get built, from nothing.

Section 1

One number can't describe a week

Two very different weeks of daily returns can share the exact same mean.

A week of five calm +0.1%-ish days averages the same as a week of +5%, −4%, +3%, −3%, +0.1% days, if the numbers are picked right — even though the second week was obviously wilder. The mean answers "where is the center?" and throws away everything about how far the data strays from it.

What the mean alone can't tell you

Two datasets can be identical on their mean and completely different in how predictable they are. A calm, tight series and a volatile, unpredictable one need a second number — one that captures typical spread, not just center — before they can be told apart. That's the entire job of standard deviation, built up over the next few slides.

Section 2

Divide by n−1 = 5: variance = 2.0 (%)². Squaring makes every term positive, same as absolute value — but it also penalizes big deviations disproportionately more than small ones (2× the deviation ⇒ 4× the contribution), and the square function is smooth everywhere. That's why variance won.

A deviation of 1% contributes 1 to the sum of squares; a deviation of 5% contributes 25 — twenty-five times more, not five times more. That "rare-big-moves-count-a-lot" behavior is exactly the property risk measurement wants: one 5% loss can be catastrophic in a way that five separate 1% days aren't.

Section 3

Population variance vs. sample variance

Two versions of the same formula. Mixing them up is one of the most common statistics mistakes.

Population, σ²

σ² = (1/n) Σ (xᵢ − μ)². Valid only if you have every value in the entire population — every trading day that will ever occur — and know the true mean μ exactly. Impossible in practice.

Sample, s²

s² = (1/(n−1)) Σ (xᵢ − x̄)². Used whenever you have a sample (the last 100 trading days, say) and only an estimate x̄ of the true mean — nearly always the finance case.

The sample mean x̄ isn't the true μ — it's estimated from the same data you're measuring spread around, and by construction x̄ is the number that makes the sum of squared deviations as small as possible for this particular sample. Measuring spread around a self-optimized center instead of the real one always understates the true variance a little. Dividing by the smaller number n−1 instead of n — Bessel's correction — inflates the result just enough to cancel that understatement, on average.

The degrees-of-freedom version of the same idea

Once x̄ is computed from n data points, only n−1 of the deviations (xᵢ − x̄) are free to vary — the last one is always forced, because all n deviations must sum to exactly zero. One piece of independent information — one degree of freedom — was "used up" computing x̄ from the data itself. The gap matters most on small samples: at n=5, dividing by 4 instead of 5 is a 25% difference; at n=1000, it's a rounding error.

Section 4

Standard deviation: undoing the squaring

Variance is in squared units — "percent squared" isn't a number anyone can picture. Taking the square root undoes the squaring and returns to the original units: s = √s².

Try it — recompute mean, variance and s live

Starts on the worked example. Edit any day's return.

Mean, x̄ = 1.0000%
Sum of squared deviations = 10.0000
Sample variance, s² = sumsq ÷ (n−1) = 2.0000 (%)²
Standard deviation, s = √s² = 1.4142%
A standard deviation of "1.41% per day" is directly interpretable, the way "2 percent-squared" never is. This is the σ referenced everywhere else in this series.
Section 5

The normal distribution: a shape built from two numbers

The bell curve is a specific, symmetric shape defined entirely by its mean μ (where the peak sits) and its standard deviation σ (how wide the bell is).

⬤

The peak — a fixed scaling constant

f(x) = 1/(σ√2π) · e^−(x−μ)²/2σ². The leading fraction doesn't shape the curve at all — it's chosen so the total area underneath equals exactly 1, as it must for any probability distribution.

⌒

The exponential — what creates the bell

At x = μ the exponent is 0, so e⁰ = 1 — the peak. Because (x−μ) is squared, direction doesn't matter: the curve is symmetric around μ.

↘

Fast-decaying tails

As x moves away from μ, (x−μ)² grows fast, driving e^(large negative number) toward zero quickly. That's why extreme values are rare rather than gently uncommon.

This shape shows up constantly for a deep reason — the Central Limit Theorem, which (informally) says adding up many small, independent random effects tends to look approximately normal, whatever the individual effects looked like. For this page, treat it simply as the reference shape "typical, well-behaved noise" gets compared against.

In words: about 68% of a normal distribution's values fall within 1 standard deviation of the mean; about 95% within 2; about 99.7% — nearly all of it — within 3. A value with |z| > 2 is already in roughly the rarest 5% of outcomes if the data really is normal, and |z| > 3 is roughly the rarest 0.3%.

Section 7

Because a z-score is unitless, it lets you compare a currency pair's daily return to a completely different instrument's return, or to its own weekly return, on the exact same scale — precisely why z-scores are the standard tool behind "is this move unusual" gates and filters.

Section 8

Is today's move normal, or a tail event?

This is exactly the question a z-score answers on a trading desk. Illustrative numbers, not a fitted historical figure.

Eyeballing volatility
  • "A 1.9% move — that feels big" with no reference point
  • The same raw move means something totally different on a calm instrument than a wild one
  • No consistent threshold — "big" quietly redefines itself instrument to instrument
  • Doesn't scale: can't compare today's move to last month's, or to a different pair's
Rigorous z-scoring
  • Typical daily σ ≈ 0.6%. Today's move is +1.9%.
  • z = (1.9 − 0) / 0.6 ≈ 3.17 — beyond the ±3σ band
  • Same rule, every instrument, every timeframe — a consistent "unusual" threshold
  • |z| > 3 is roughly the rarest 0.3% under normality — a genuine tail-event flag, not a hunch
"Is today's move within normal noise, or a real signal?" is exactly the question z-scores answer on a desk. A 2-sigma daily loss is shorthand for "roughly a 1-in-40-ish day event, if returns were normal" — a precise, comparable statement that "that felt like a big move" never gives you.
Section 9

How practitioners actually use this

1

Pick a rolling window N

The last 20 or 100 observations — long enough to estimate x̄ and s reliably, short enough to track a changing regime.

2

Recompute the mean and s over that window

Slide the window forward one observation at a time — that's what makes it "rolling," not a single fixed-sample statistic.

3

Score the newest observation

z = (x_new − x̄) / s, using the just-computed rolling mean and standard deviation.

4

Threshold consistently

"Only act when |z| > 2" adapts as volatility changes, instead of a fixed raw-price cutoff that goes stale.

This is the statistical backbone of Bollinger Bands (price bands drawn at ±2σ from a moving average — literally a z-score gate drawn on a chart) and of Value-at-Risk-style risk statements. Standard deviation applied to returns is the most common starting definition of "volatility" in finance — it's what σ means throughout this series before any GARCH refinement is layered on top.

Where this needs qualifying

Real FX and equity returns are well documented to have fatter tails than a true normal distribution — extreme moves happen meaningfully more often than the 68/95/99.7 rule predicts. And standard deviation is not standard error: standard deviation measures spread in the data itself, while standard error measures the uncertainty in an estimate like the mean, and shrinks as the sample grows. Use a z-score gate as a consistent, comparable filter — not as a literal probability guarantee.

Section 10

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Why does averaging the raw deviations xᵢ − x̄ always give exactly zero?

Σ(xᵢ − x̄) = 0 for any dataset, always — that's the trap that makes raw average deviation useless as a spread measure, no matter how the data is shaped.
Question 2

Why does sample variance divide by n−1 instead of n?

Bessel's correction: x̄ minimizes the sum of squared deviations for its own sample, so measuring spread around it (instead of the true μ) understates variance. Dividing by n−1 corrects for it — and the gap is largest exactly when n is small.
Question 3

Roughly what percentile does a z-score of 2 correspond to, for normally distributed data?

About 95% of a normal distribution falls within ±2σ, leaving about 5% split across both tails — roughly 2.3% above z=2. That's within the ±2σ band's edge but beyond the ±1σ band.
Question 4

A currency pair's typical daily σ is 0.6%. Today it moved +1.9%. What does that imply?

z = 1.9 / 0.6 ≈ 3.17. |z| > 3 is roughly the rarest 0.3% of outcomes under normality — a move well worth flagging, not one to eyeball away.
Question 5

Two datasets share the same mean but dataset A has a larger standard deviation than dataset B. What can you conclude?

Mean and standard deviation are a two-number summary of center and spread — not a statement about shape (normal vs. fat-tailed, skewed vs. symmetric), quality, or any relationship between two separate datasets.
Section 11

What to carry forward

01

The mean gives a center; it throws away everything about spread — that's the gap standard deviation exists to fill.

02

Averaging raw deviations from the mean always gives zero, algebraically — square them (variance) instead, which also penalizes big moves disproportionately more than small ones.

03

Divide a sample's squared deviations by n−1, not n — Bessel's correction, because the mean used was already estimated from that same data.

04

Standard deviation is variance's square root, back in the original units — this is the σ referenced everywhere else in this series.

05

z = (x − μ) / σ turns any value into a unitless "how many sigmas away" number — the same ruler for any instrument, any timeframe.

06

The 68/95/99.7 rule turns a z-score into a percentile — but real returns are fatter-tailed than normal, so extreme z-scores show up more often than the rule predicts.

What this now lets you do

Compute a mean, variance, and standard deviation from raw returns by hand. Turn any value into a z-score, and read a z-score back into "how unusual is this, really." Use the 68/95/99.7 rule to judge whether today's move is ordinary noise or a genuine tail event. Recognize σ, z, and rolling z-scores the instant they resurface — under GARCH, Bollinger Bands, VaR, or any other name — later in this series.
