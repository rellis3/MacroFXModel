Existence & Uniqueness of SDE Solutions

Every SDE lesson so far in this curriculum — Ornstein-Uhlenbeck, Black-Scholes's GBM, Heston — quietly assumes a solution exists and is the only one. That assumption isn't automatic. This lesson states the precise conditions under which it holds, and shows a coefficient choice where it doesn't.

CONCEPT — the well-posedness question every SDE in this curriculum has been assuming ~16 min read Prereqs: Itô's Lemma
TL;DR
  • Writing down an SDE \(dX_t=\mu(X_t,t)dt+\sigma(X_t,t)dW_t\) doesn't automatically mean a process satisfying it exists, or that it's the only one — that has to be proven, coefficient by coefficient.
  • The standard existence-and-uniqueness theorem is the SDE analogue of Picard-Lindelöf for ordinary differential equations: a global Lipschitz condition plus a linear growth condition on \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) together guarantee a unique strong solution.
  • Geometric Brownian motion's coefficients, \(\mu(x)=\mu x\) and \(\sigma(x)=\sigma x\), satisfy both conditions cleanly on any bounded region — but the honest full argument on all of \(\mathbb{R}\) needs slightly more care than a naive "it's globally Lipschitz" claim, because the Lipschitz constant these coefficients produce grows with the size of the region.
  • A coefficient that violates the Lipschitz condition — \(\sigma(x)=\sigma\sqrt{|x|}\), the square-root diffusion behind the CIR short-rate model — needs a separate, weaker set of conditions (Yamada-Watanabe, 1971) to guarantee existence and uniqueness at all.
  • Violating the growth condition instead is worse than a technical inconvenience: a deterministic illustration, \(dX/dt=X^2\), shows a solution that exists only up to a finite time and then diverges — there is no solution at all beyond that point, for any method.
Section 01

Why this matters

ito-lemma.html derives the calculus needed to manipulate an SDE once you have one — but it starts from the premise that \(X_t\) already exists as a well-defined random process satisfying \(dX_t=\mu(X_t,t)dt+\sigma(X_t,t)dW_t\). Every SDE-based lesson in this curriculum inherits that premise silently: Ornstein-Uhlenbeck writes down \(dX_t=\theta(\mu-X_t)dt+\sigma\,dW_t\) and moves straight to solving it; Black-Scholes's geometric Brownian motion does the same; Heston writes down two coupled SDEs and never asks whether the pair is well-posed. In every one of those cases the assumption happens to be safe, because the coefficients involved are well-behaved. But it is not automatically safe for an arbitrary choice of \(\mu(x,t)\) and \(\sigma(x,t)\), and the difference between a well-posed SDE and an ill-posed one is not a footnote — it is the difference between a model that has one answer and a model that has no answer, or infinitely many, at the exact moment you try to compute with it.

This lesson is the formal version of a question worth asking before trusting any new SDE specification, including one you might invent yourself for a new pricing or forecasting idea: given this particular \(\mu(x,t)\) and \(\sigma(x,t)\), is there actually a unique process that satisfies the equation? The answer, for the two most common coefficient families in quantitative finance, turns out to be reassuring but not trivial — and the one family where it genuinely breaks (the square-root diffusion behind short-rate models) is exactly why short-rate-models-vasicek-cir-hull-white.html, later in this sequence, needs a different existence argument than the one built here.

💡
Plain-English intuition

The same question ordinary differential equations already had to answer

Long before stochastic calculus existed, ordinary differential equations (ODEs) ran into exactly this problem. Write down \(dx/dt = f(x)\) with some starting point \(x(0)=x_0\), and it is tempting to assume a curve \(x(t)\) tracing out the solution simply exists, for all time, and that it's the only curve doing so. Neither is automatic. The classical fix is the Picard-Lindelöf theorem: if \(f\) is Lipschitz continuous (its slope is bounded — it can't wiggle arbitrarily fast) and doesn't grow too explosively as \(x\) gets large, a unique solution exists, at least locally in time. Without those conditions, two very different things can go wrong, and it's worth keeping them separate in your head because they're genuinely different failures:

Non-uniqueness — a rough enough \(f\) can let more than one curve pass validly through the same starting point, so "the" solution isn't well defined; the textbook example is \(dx/dt = \sqrt{|x|}\) with \(x(0)=0\), which is solved both by \(x(t)\equiv 0\) forever and by \(x(t) = t^2/4\) peeling away from zero — both satisfy the equation, and nothing in the equation itself picks one. Finite-time blow-up — a fast-growing enough \(f\) can make the solution race off to infinity before any fixed time horizon is reached, so there is no solution defined on the interval you wanted at all; the chart below shows this happening explicitly. An SDE has both of these same two failure modes, plus a third source of trouble that has no ODE counterpart: the diffusion term \(\sigma(X_t,t)\,dW_t\) can itself be badly behaved even when the drift \(\mu\) is perfectly tame. The existence-and-uniqueness theorem below is the direct stochastic-calculus analogue of Picard-Lindelöf, adapted to control both terms at once.

Section 02

The math, step by step

Start from the general form every SDE in this curriculum is a special case of:

\[ dX_t = \mu(X_t,t)\,dt + \sigma(X_t,t)\,dW_t, \qquad X_0 = x_0 \]
μ(X_t,t)the drift — how the process moves on average, possibly depending on its own current level and on time
σ(X_t,t)the diffusion coefficient — how much randomness is injected, again possibly level- and time-dependent
x₀a fixed, known starting value

ito-lemma.html works entirely with the special case \(\mu(X_t,t)=\mu\) and \(\sigma(X_t,t)=\sigma\), constants — the simplest possible coefficients, for which existence and uniqueness is never in doubt. The general form above allows \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) to be functions of the current level \(X_t\) (and, in principle, of time \(t\)) — which is exactly what OU's mean-reverting drift, GBM's level-proportional coefficients, and CIR's square-root diffusion all are. The question this lesson answers: for which functions \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) does this equation actually pin down a unique process \(X_t\)?

1. The existence-and-uniqueness theorem

The standard result (see Øksendal, Stochastic Differential Equations, Theorem 5.2.1, or Karatzas & Shreve, Brownian Motion and Stochastic Calculus, for the fully rigorous statement and proof) requires two conditions on the coefficients, holding for all \(x,y\) and all \(t\) in the time horizon of interest:

|μ(x,t)−μ(y,t)| + |σ(x,t)−σ(y,t)| ≤ K|x−y|
Global Lipschitz condition
The coefficients can't change output faster than a fixed multiple K of the change in input — rules out the "two solutions from one starting point" failure
|μ(x,t)| + |σ(x,t)| ≤ K(1+|x|)
Linear growth condition
Neither coefficient is allowed to grow faster than linearly in x — rules out the "solution races to infinity before time T" failure

Given both conditions, with the same constant \(K\) working for both, a unique strong solution \(X_t\) exists on \([0,T]\) for any fixed time horizon \(T\). "Strong" here means the solution is constructed explicitly as a function of the given Brownian path \(W_t\) (as opposed to a "weak" solution, which only needs some Brownian motion on some probability space to work — a distinction that matters for the numerical-schemes lesson next in this sequence, but not for the existence question itself). The proof strategy mirrors Picard-Lindelöf almost exactly: construct a sequence of successive approximations (Picard iteration) and use the Lipschitz condition to show they form a Cauchy sequence that converges to a genuine solution; the linear growth condition is what stops that limit from escaping to infinity before reaching \(T\).

\(\mu(x,t)\), \(\sigma(x,t)\)The drift and diffusion coefficients, written as functions of the current level \(x\) and time \(t\) — the general form every specific SDE (OU, GBM, CIR, ...) plugs a particular function into.
\(K\)A single fixed constant that bounds both the Lipschitz condition and the linear-growth condition simultaneously — the theorem needs one \(K\) working for both, not two different ones.
\(|\mu(x,t)-\mu(y,t)|\)How much the drift's output changes between two different current levels \(x\) and \(y\) — the Lipschitz condition caps this at \(K\) times the gap between \(x\) and \(y\) themselves.
Strong solutionA solution \(X_t\) built as an explicit (measurable) function of the specific Brownian path \(W_t\) you were handed — the practical notion of "solution" used everywhere in this curriculum's simulations.
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

The Lipschitz condition is like saying a hill can't be infinitely steep — a ball rolling down it can slide fast, but it can never suddenly teleport to a totally different spot. That rules out two different solutions branching off from the same starting point. The linear growth condition is a different promise: the hill can't get steeper and steeper without any limit as you go further out, the way a car's engine can't have literally unlimited horsepower. Without that second promise, the ball could accelerate away to infinity before you even finish watching it.

2. GBM's coefficients: Lipschitz and linear growth, with an honest caveat

Geometric Brownian motion — the process behind black-scholes.html and every other lognormal-price model in this curriculum — has coefficients \(\mu(x) = \mu x\) and \(\sigma(x) = \sigma x\), where the Greek \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) on the right are fixed constants (the drift and volatility parameters) and \(x\) is the current level. Both are linear functions of \(x\), so both conditions are easy to check directly:

\[ |\mu x - \mu y| + |\sigma x - \sigma y| = (|\mu|+|\sigma|)\,|x-y| \]

That is exactly the Lipschitz form, with constant \(K=|\mu|+|\sigma|\) — a single fixed number, the same for every pair \(x,y\), anywhere on the real line. Linear growth is equally direct: \(|\mu x|+|\sigma x| = (|\mu|+|\sigma|)|x| \le (|\mu|+|\sigma|)(1+|x|)\), the same \(K\) again. Both conditions hold with room to spare — which is the honest reason GBM is the field's default starting point: its well-posedness is essentially free.

The caveat worth stating precisely, not glossing over: the Lipschitz constant \(K=|\mu|+|\sigma|\) above is exact and region-independent for GBM specifically because its coefficients are exactly linear — the same \(K\) works whether \(x\) ranges over \([1,100]\) or the whole real line. That is a special property of linear coefficients, not a general feature of "growth-condition-satisfying" processes. For coefficients that are only locally Lipschitz (well-behaved on every bounded region, with a Lipschitz constant that can still grow as the region widens) the textbook-clean global argument above doesn't directly apply, and the honest proof of existence on all of \(\mathbb{R}\) needs a second ingredient: a non-explosion argument (showing the process doesn't escape to infinity in finite time even though the local Lipschitz constant is unbounded globally), on top of the local Lipschitz property. GBM's coefficients are simple enough that this technicality resolves cleanly — the process provably never explodes in finite time — but stating "GBM's coefficients are globally Lipschitz, done" without this caveat skips a real step that a fully rigorous treatment (see Øksendal, Chapter 5) does not.
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

GBM's coefficients are just "some fixed percentage of the current level" — like a savings account that grows by exactly the same percentage rate no matter how big the balance gets. That kind of same-percentage-every-time relationship is about as gentle and predictable as a slope can be, which is exactly why it sails through both conditions with room to spare. The honest caveat is just: "gentle everywhere I checked" isn't quite the same as "gentle absolutely everywhere," and a fully rigorous proof still has to double-check the process doesn't secretly explode way out at the far edges.

3. A coefficient that fails: the square-root diffusion

Contrast GBM's linear \(\sigma(x)=\sigma x\) with the square-root diffusion coefficient that defines the CIR short-rate model (built in full in short-rate-models-vasicek-cir-hull-white.html, later in this sequence):

\[ \sigma(x) = \sigma\sqrt{|x|} \]

Check the Lipschitz condition at \(x=0\), \(y=\epsilon\) for a small \(\epsilon>0\):

\[ \frac{|\sigma(\epsilon)-\sigma(0)|}{|\epsilon-0|} = \frac{\sigma\sqrt{\epsilon}}{\epsilon} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{\epsilon}} \longrightarrow \infty \text{ as } \epsilon\to 0 \]
σ/√εthe local "slope" of √x blows up as ε→0 — no finite K can bound it near x=0

The square-root function has infinite slope at the origin — its graph is vertical there — so no finite Lipschitz constant \(K\) can bound the ratio for every pair of points near \(x=0\). The square-root diffusion genuinely fails the Lipschitz condition, right at the boundary value that matters most for a short rate (zero). This is not a technicality to wave away: the standard existence-and-uniqueness theorem from step 1 simply does not apply to this coefficient, and a separate, weaker set of conditions is needed instead — the Yamada-Watanabe conditions (Yamada & Watanabe, 1971), which replace the Lipschitz requirement with a weaker Hölder-type condition tailored to exactly this square-root behaviour, and which is what actually justifies that the CIR process is well-defined. short-rate-models-vasicek-cir-hull-white.html picks this thread back up when it introduces CIR properly, alongside the separate Feller condition that governs whether the resulting process stays strictly positive.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Near zero, the square-root function is almost vertical — like a cliff edge rather than a gentle hill. A tiny nudge in \(x\) near zero produces a huge jump in \(\sqrt{x}\), and that ratio just keeps getting worse the closer you get to zero. No single "speed limit" \(K\) can describe a slope that's heading toward straight-up-and-down, so this coefficient genuinely breaks the Lipschitz rule right where it matters most for an interest rate: the boundary at zero.

What the linear-growth condition is actually protecting against
Linear-growth diffusion (GBM, μ=0.08, σ=0.20) — stays bounded Superlinear drift, \(dX/dt=X^2\), X(0)=1 — races to infinity as t→1
The blue path is a single simulated GBM realization, whose coefficients satisfy linear growth exactly as derived in step 2 — note it stays within a narrow band near its starting value across the entire time axis, exactly as the theorem's growth bound predicts. The red curve is the exact closed-form solution of the deterministic ODE \(dX/dt=X^2\), \(X(0)=1\), which is \(X(t)=1/(1-t)\) — its right-hand side grows quadratically, violating linear growth, and the solution genuinely does not exist past \(t=1\): it isn't merely hard to simulate there, no solution of the equation is defined beyond that point at all. This is the deterministic (drift-only) version of exactly the failure the growth condition in step 1 rules out for the full stochastic case.
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine a car whose engine gets more powerful the faster it's already going — not just faster, but faster at an accelerating rate. It doesn't just speed off into the distance eventually; it can hit an actual wall of infinite speed at some exact, finite moment, and after that moment there's genuinely nowhere for the story to continue. That's what violating the growth condition means: not "hard to simulate," but "the road itself stops existing" past a specific point in time.

Worked example

Checking both conditions numerically for a specific GBM specification

Take \(\mu=0.08\), \(\sigma=0.20\) (an 8% annual drift, 20% annual volatility — plausible parameters for an equity index). The Lipschitz constant is exact and region-independent for these linear coefficients:

\[ K = |\mu| + |\sigma| = 0.08 + 0.20 = 0.28 \]

Check linear growth at a specific point, \(x=100\):

\[ |\mu x| + |\sigma x| = |0.08 \times 100| + |0.20 \times 100| = 8 + 20 = 28 \]

Compare against the bound \(K(1+|x|) = 0.28 \times (1+100) = 28.28\). \(28 \le 28.28\) — the condition holds, with a small margin coming entirely from the "\(+1\)" in \(K(1+|x|)\) (at large \(x\) the two sides converge, since \(K\cdot x\) and \(K(1+x)\) differ by a fixed constant \(K\) that matters less and less relative to \(Kx\)). Both conditions hold with the identical constant \(K=0.28\), for every \(x\) — exactly what the theorem in step 1 requires, and precisely why no special care is needed before simulating a GBM specification like this one.

Real-world trading scenario

A quant proposes a new local-volatility specification

Picture a desk considering a bespoke local-volatility model for an FX pair, where the diffusion coefficient is specified as \(\sigma(x) = \sigma_0\,x^{1.5}\) instead of GBM's plain \(\sigma_0 x\) — a modeling choice made to fit an observed volatility smile more aggressively at high spot levels. Before wiring this into a pricing engine and trusting its Monte Carlo output, the honest first question is exactly the one this lesson poses: is this coefficient well-posed at all? \(x^{1.5}\) still satisfies a local Lipschitz condition away from zero (its derivative, \(1.5x^{0.5}\), is finite and bounded on any region excluding the origin), but it grows faster than linearly as \(x\to\infty\) — violating the linear growth condition outright. That doesn't necessarily mean the model is unusable, but it does mean the textbook existence-and-uniqueness guarantee from step 1 does not cover it, and a real non-explosion check (does the process actually reach infinity in finite time with positive probability, given the specific parameters in play) is a genuine, separate piece of due diligence before the model is trusted — not a formality to skip because "it looks like GBM with an extra exponent."

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

Almost nobody proves existence and uniqueness from scratch before pricing a trade — in practice, the field has settled on a short list of coefficient families (GBM, OU/Vasicek, CIR, and their well-studied extensions like Heston and SABR) whose well-posedness was established once, in the academic literature, decades ago, and every desk simply cites that result. Where this actually surfaces as live due diligence:

🧪

Vetting a genuinely new coefficient specification

Any time a model departs from the standard families — a custom local-volatility surface, a bespoke jump-diffusion drift — checking Lipschitz and growth conditions (or citing a paper that already has) is the first honest gate before trusting simulated output.

📉

Diagnosing a numerical scheme that "blows up"

If a Monte Carlo simulation of an SDE regularly produces NaNs or diverging paths, the first question is whether the underlying coefficients even satisfy a growth condition — sometimes the numerical scheme isn't the bug, the specification is.

📐

Choosing between competing model families

Part of why CIR (not a naive square-root-of-anything model) became the standard positivity-preserving short-rate process is that its specific coefficients were the ones a rigorous existence argument (Yamada-Watanabe) was actually worked out for.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where this gets misapplied

Treating "the simulation ran without crashing" as proof the SDE is well-posed. A numerical scheme with a fixed, coarse time step can silently step over a genuine finite-time blow-up or a non-uniqueness issue and still produce a plausible-looking path — the well-posedness question is about the true continuous-time equation, not about whether a particular discretization happened to behave on a particular run.

Assuming "locally Lipschitz" is as good as "globally Lipschitz." A coefficient can be perfectly smooth (locally Lipschitz) everywhere and still fail to have a global solution, if it grows fast enough that the solution can escape to infinity in finite time — local smoothness alone never rules out blow-up; the growth condition is what does that, separately.

Confusing the Lipschitz condition's failure with "the model must be wrong." CIR's square-root diffusion fails the Lipschitz condition and is nonetheless one of the most widely used, well-validated short-rate specifications in the industry — the Yamada-Watanabe conditions were developed precisely because plenty of genuinely useful models have exactly this kind of non-Lipschitz behaviour at a boundary. A failed Lipschitz check means "use the more careful theorem," not "abandon the model."

Forgetting that the theorem needs one constant K for both conditions. It's possible to construct coefficients that are Lipschitz with one constant and satisfy linear growth with a completely different, unrelated constant — the theorem as classically stated wants both bounded by the same \(K\), which is easy to arrange in practice (take the larger of the two) but easy to miss when checking the conditions separately.

Section 04

Key Takeaways

✓An SDE isn't automatically well-posed just because you wrote it down. Existence and uniqueness of a solution require the coefficients to satisfy specific conditions — they aren't free.
✓Global Lipschitz + linear growth ⟹ unique strong solution. The SDE analogue of Picard-Lindelöf; Lipschitz rules out non-uniqueness, linear growth rules out finite-time blow-up.
✓GBM's linear coefficients satisfy both conditions cleanly — with an exact, region-independent Lipschitz constant K=|μ|+|σ| — which is the honest reason it's the field's default starting point.
⚠The "GBM is globally Lipschitz" claim needs a caveat on all of ℝ. The fully rigorous argument pairs local Lipschitz behaviour with a separate non-explosion check, not a single global-K claim asserted without qualification.
⚠The CIR-style square-root diffusion σ(x)=σ√|x| fails Lipschitz at x=0. It needs the weaker Yamada-Watanabe conditions instead — a failed Lipschitz check doesn't mean the model is broken, it means a different theorem applies.
✗Violating linear growth can mean no solution exists at all past some finite time — not a simulation inconvenience. dX/dt=X² with X(0)=1 has no solution whatsoever past t=1, for any method.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. State the two conditions the existence-and-uniqueness theorem requires, and which specific failure mode each one rules out.
The global Lipschitz condition, \(|\mu(x,t)-\mu(y,t)|+|\sigma(x,t)-\sigma(y,t)|\le K|x-y|\), rules out non-uniqueness — it prevents the coefficients from being rough enough that two different valid solutions could pass through the same starting point. The linear growth condition, \(|\mu(x,t)|+|\sigma(x,t)|\le K(1+|x|)\), rules out finite-time blow-up — it prevents the solution from being able to race off to infinity before a fixed time horizon \(T\) is reached. Both need to hold with the same constant \(K\) for the theorem to guarantee a unique strong solution.
Q2. Why does GBM's Lipschitz constant come out to a single fixed number, K=|μ|+|σ|, rather than a number that depends on the region of x being considered?
Because GBM's coefficients, μ(x)=μx and σ(x)=σx, are exactly linear in x. For a linear function, the ratio |f(x)-f(y)|/|x-y| is a constant (the slope) for every pair x,y — it doesn't matter how far apart x and y are, or how large they are. That's a special consequence of linearity; a coefficient with any curvature (like x^1.5 or √x) would generally have a Lipschitz "constant" that changes depending on which region of x you're checking, or that fails to be bounded at all somewhere in the domain.
Q3. A coefficient σ(x)=σ√|x| is proposed for a new short-rate model. Does it satisfy the global Lipschitz condition? Show why or why not.
No. Checking the ratio near x=0, between x=0 and y=ε for small ε>0: |σ(ε)-σ(0)|/|ε-0| = σ√ε/ε = σ/√ε, which diverges to infinity as ε→0. No finite constant K can bound this ratio for every pair of points near the origin, so the global Lipschitz condition fails there. This is exactly the CIR square-root diffusion, and it's why CIR needs the separate Yamada-Watanabe existence conditions rather than the standard theorem.
Q4. Explain, using dX/dt=X², X(0)=1 as the example, what "violating the linear growth condition can mean no solution exists at all" actually means in practice.
The exact solution of dX/dt=X² with X(0)=1 is X(t)=1/(1-t), which is well-defined and finite for every t in [0,1), but has no defined value at t=1 or beyond — it isn't that X(t) becomes "very large," it genuinely diverges to infinity at that specific finite time, and the differential equation has no solution curve extending past it. This isn't a numerical artifact of a particular simulation method; it's a property of the true continuous-time equation, caused by the right-hand side, X², growing faster than linearly in X — exactly the growth condition the existence theorem requires to rule this out.
Q5. A Lipschitz check on a proposed local-volatility coefficient fails. Does that mean the model should be abandoned?
Not necessarily. CIR's square-root diffusion fails the standard global Lipschitz condition and is still one of the most widely used and validated short-rate models in the industry. A failed Lipschitz check means the standard existence-and-uniqueness theorem from step 1 doesn't directly apply — it signals that a different, more careful argument (like the Yamada-Watanabe conditions for square-root-type diffusions) is needed to establish whether a unique solution exists, not that the model is automatically broken or unusable.
