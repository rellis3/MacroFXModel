Same Greeks, Different Risk

A short put and a short call carry the same signs on gamma, theta and vega — yet one can lose at most the strike and the other can lose without limit. The Greeks are local slopes; the payoff is the whole road, cliff included.

CONCEPT — explained here; desk verdicts quoted where tested ~16 min read Prereqs: The Four Option Positions, Theta Decay, Gamma Exposure
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • Short put and short call are both short gamma, long theta, short vega. At the same strike their gamma and vega are not just the same sign — they are the same number.
  • Their worst cases are nothing alike. Short put: strike − premium (97.80 on a 100 strike). Short call: unlimited.
  • The reason is put-call parity: short call = short put + short forward. The forward has no gamma, no vega and almost no theta, so the Greeks can't see it — but it is a straight line with no floor, and it carries the whole unlimited tail.
  • Greeks are a Taylor expansion around today's price. For a ±50 move on a 30-day ATM option, the Greek estimate says −113 and −111; the true losses are −47.8 and −47.6. Local slopes are not a map of the tails.
  • Far in the money the short call's gamma and vega go to zero — the Greeks fall silent exactly where the loss is largest.
  • Desk verdict on the dealer-level version ("short gamma amplifies"): range-only, Nasdaq-only, and the reliable state is long gamma being quiet. Direction from gamma is falsified, and it does not carry to FX.
Section 01

Why this matters

The Four Option Positions put short put and short call on the same diagonal of the 2×2: both sellers, both collecting premium, both with the same three Greek signs. A reference card that only carries signs makes them look like the same risk. They are not, and no Greek on the card tells you which one you are holding. Since size is set by the worst case, this is the gap that matters most for anyone who actually sells options.

It also matters one level up. A dealer book that has sold a lot of options is "short gamma" — the aggregated version of this lesson, and the mechanism behind every GEX number on a screen (see Gamma Exposure & Dealer Hedging Flows). "Short gamma" is a statement about the local slope of the book. It says nothing about whether the book's tail is bounded, and as Section 04 shows, the desk's own test of what dealer gamma predicts lands much more narrowly than the folk version.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Two cars with the same speedometer reading, on different roads

Greeks are like a car's dashboard: speed (delta), how fast speed is changing (gamma), fuel burned per hour (theta). Two cars can show identical dashboards at this instant. One is on a road that ends at a wall a known distance away — it can only travel so far. The other is on a road that ends at a cliff edge with no bottom. The dashboard describes right now. It cannot tell you what is at the end of the road.

A short put is the first car: the underlying can fall only to zero, so the loss stops at the strike minus the premium. A short call is the second: there is no ceiling on price, so there is no floor on the loss. Same dashboard, different roads.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

You and your friend each promise something for £2. You promise to buy a toy for £100 if your friend wants to sell it — the worst that can happen is the toy becomes worthless and you lose about £98. Your friend promises to sell a toy for £100 if someone wants to buy it — but they don't own one, and if toys suddenly cost £1,000 they have to buy one at that price and hand it over. Same £2, same kind of promise, very different worst day.

Section 02

The math, step by step

1. The payoffs at expiry

With strike \(K\), premiums \(p_P\) (put) and \(p_C\) (call), and \(S_T \ge 0\) the price at expiry:

\[ \text{Short put: } p_P - \max(K - S_T, 0) \;\ge\; p_P - K \qquad \text{Short call: } p_C - \max(S_T - K, 0) \;\to\; -\infty \]
p_P − Kthe short put's floor, hit at S_T = 0 — a price cannot go below zero
−∞the short call has no floor: S_T has no ceiling

2. Put-call parity: the two shorts differ by a forward

Rearranging parity, \(C - P = S - Ke^{-r\tau}\), and negating both sides:

\[ \underbrace{-C}_{\text{short call}} = \underbrace{-P}_{\text{short put}} \;-\; \underbrace{\big(S - Ke^{-r\tau}\big)}_{\text{short forward}} \]
−P
The convexity
Short gamma, short vega, long theta — and a bounded tail
−(S − Ke−rτ)
The straight line
Zero gamma, zero vega, tiny theta — and an unbounded tail
−C
The sum
Same convexity Greeks as the short put; delta lower by exactly 1

Differentiate each piece. The forward \(S - Ke^{-r\tau}\) has delta 1, gamma 0, vega 0, and theta \(-rKe^{-r\tau}\) (a financing drip). So:

\[ \Gamma_{\text{sc}} = \Gamma_{\text{sp}},\quad \nu_{\text{sc}} = \nu_{\text{sp}},\quad \Delta_{\text{sc}} = \Delta_{\text{sp}} - 1,\quad \Theta_{\text{sc}} = \Theta_{\text{sp}} + rKe^{-r\tau} \]
Γ, ν equalnot merely the same sign — the same number
Δ − 1the forward shifts direction by one full unit
+rKe⁻ʳᵗa small financing term, per year

Every Greek that describes curvature, time and volatility is identical between the two. The whole difference in their tails lives inside a straight line — and a straight line is the one shape whose risk the second-order Greeks literally cannot register.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

A short call is a short put with a "bet the price goes down" glued on. That glued-on bet is a perfectly straight ramp — no bends — and the bend-detectors (gamma, vega) can't see straight ramps at all. But the ramp goes up forever, and that's where the never-ending loss comes from.

3. Greeks are a Taylor expansion — local by construction

The Greeks are the coefficients of a Taylor expansion of the position's value around today's price, time and volatility:

\[ \delta V \approx \Delta\,\delta S + \tfrac12\Gamma\,(\delta S)^2 + \Theta\,\delta t + \nu\,\delta\sigma \]
Δ δSfirst-order: the tangent line
½Γ(δS)²second-order: the bend, assumed constant
Θ δt, ν δσtime and volatility, holding everything else fixed

It is accurate for small \(\delta S\). Gamma itself changes as price moves (that is what speed and the other second-order Greeks measure), so for a big move the quadratic overshoots or undershoots the real curve. And as a short call goes deep in the money its gamma and vega shrink towards zero: the Greeks report a calm, nearly linear position at exactly the moment its loss is largest and still growing one-for-one with price.

Worked example

Two shorts, 30 days, strike 100 — local twins, global strangers

\(S = K = 100\), \(\sigma = 20\%\), \(r = 2\%\), \(\tau = 30/365\). Sell one put for 2.20 and one call for 2.37.

1

The Greeks today

Short put: Δ = +0.477, Γ = −0.0695, Θ = +0.0353/day, ν = −0.114 per vol point.
Short call: Δ = −0.523, Γ = −0.0695, Θ = +0.0408/day, ν = −0.114 per vol point.
Gamma and vega identical. Deltas differ by exactly 1.000. Thetas differ by 0.0055/day = \(rKe^{-r\tau}/365\). ✓ parity.

2

A ±50 move, Greeks vs reality

Taylor estimate, short call, +50: \(-0.523(50) - \tfrac12(0.0695)(50^2) = -26.1 - 86.9 = -113.0\).
Taylor estimate, short put, −50: \(+0.477(-50) - 86.9 = -110.7\).
True losses (repriced with 30 days left): −47.80 and −47.63. The quadratic overshoots by more than double, because gamma collapses once the option is deep in the money. Locally the two shorts are mirror images; the Taylor estimate can't separate them.

3

A 100-point move — where they part

Fall to 0: short put loses 97.80 at expiry — and that is the most it can ever lose.
Rise to 200: short call loses 97.80 — and at 300 it loses 197.80, at 500 it loses 397.80. Nothing in step 1's Greeks predicted this; the difference is the short forward from Section 02, a straight line the second-order Greeks cannot see.

Section 03

When the call side had no ceiling

Unbounded loss forces a particular sequence of actions on whoever is short the upside — whether short a call or short the stock itself, since below the strike a deep-ITM short call behaves exactly like short stock. Each step is something an actor is forced to do:

ShockNews shrinks the shares actually available to trade, or a buying wave hits
→
Call writers / dealersDelta of the short call heads toward −1 (that's gamma); to stay hedged they must buy the underlying into the rise
→
Short sellersMargin calls grow with price; lenders can recall borrowed shares; they must buy to close
→
The marketForced buyers meet a thin supply of sellers; price rises further
→
OutcomeLoss grows with no floor until positions are closed — at the worst prices
It actually happened

October 2008: Volkswagen, briefly the world's most valuable company

On Sunday 26 October 2008, Porsche disclosed that it controlled about 74% of Volkswagen's ordinary shares — roughly 43% held outright and most of the rest through cash-settled options. The state of Lower Saxony held about another 20%. That left only a few percent of the shares freely tradable, while a large number of shares had been sold short by funds betting the price would fall.

The short sellers needed to buy shares back, and there were far too few to go round. Over the next two trading days VW's share price rose roughly fivefold, trading above €1,000 intraday on 28 October and making Volkswagen, for a brief moment, the most valuable listed company in the world by market capitalisation. The squeeze eased after Porsche said it would release some of its position to provide liquidity. For anyone short the upside — short shares, or short calls — there had been no level at which the loss was contractually capped.

January 2021 replayed the pattern in GameStop. A wave of retail buying, much of it through short-dated call options, met heavy short interest; the share price multiplied many times within weeks. Melvin Capital, a fund that had been short the stock, needed an emergency capital infusion of about $2.75 billion from Citadel and Point72, and several brokers restricted buying in the stock on 28 January. Neither episode involved anyone being wrong about delta on a Monday morning. Both were about a payoff with no ceiling.

🎯
Real-world trading scenario

Two "equivalent" premium-selling trades on a risk report

A risk report shows two positions on the same index, each selling one-month at-the-money options for about 2% of notional: one sells puts, one sells calls. The Greek columns read the same — short gamma, long theta, short vega, the same gamma and vega figures, deltas that differ by one. A manager who sizes both by the Greek report allocates them the same risk.

The stress column tells a different story. "Index −50%": the put seller loses about 48% of notional and no more can be lost below zero. "Index +50%": the call seller loses about the same — and "+100%", "+200%" keep adding a full point of loss for every point of rally. The honest sizing rule is the one the Greek report cannot carry: size each short by its stressed loss, not its local Greeks, and recognise that a short call's stress scenario has to be chosen, because the contract itself never supplies a worst case.

Section 04

The dealer-level version: what "short gamma" does and doesn't predict

Aggregate every option a dealer has sold and you get the book-level gamma that Gamma Exposure builds into GEX. The folk reading follows straight from this lesson's chain: when dealers are short gamma, their hedging buys rallies and sells dips, amplifying moves; when long gamma, they lean against moves and damp them. People then often take a further step — "short gamma means a breakout is coming", "long gamma means price will mean-revert to a level". This lesson has a precise warning for that step: gamma is a local slope. It speaks to how much hedging flow a move of a given size triggers, not to which way the next move goes.

That is exactly where the desk's own test landed:

Desk test: held up (range only) Tested here (pre-registered, 793 days of Nasdaq futures): next-day realised range against a same-trailing-vol Brownian baseline is +0.176 higher on short-gamma days (p 0.0008), rising to +0.315 when matched on trailing vol. Positive in 4 of 4 specifications and 6 of 6 usable years; FOMC/CPI/NFP/opex days contribute nothing extra (+0.306 vs +0.306). The asymmetry runs the other way from the folk version: short-gamma days sit at a range ratio of about 1.02 — essentially ordinary — while long-gamma days sit near 0.85. The reliable state is "long gamma is quiet", not "short gamma is wild".
Desk test: no direction found Gamma carries no directional content. The adjacent wall-magnet direction claim was falsified separately at 48.8% out-of-sample against a 49.9% placebo. And the range effect does not generalise to FX — it was tested there and does not carry.

Read alongside this lesson, that result is close to what the mathematics would lead you to expect. Gamma measures how sharply hedging needs change as price moves, so it is plausible it says something about how far price travels. It has no term for which way — exactly as a short put and a short call share a gamma figure while pointing in opposite directions.

Section 05

How practitioners actually use this

🧨

Stress tables beside Greek tables

Risk reports carry scenario P&L at large shocks (±10%, ±25%, vol up 20 points) next to the Greeks, because the Greeks are a Taylor expansion and stress P&L is a full reprice.

🛡️

Capping the uncapped side

Call sellers commonly buy a further-out call (a call spread) or hold the underlying (a covered call), turning an unbounded tail into a bounded one at the cost of some premium.

📐

Higher-order Greeks for the bend

Speed, vanna and volga track how gamma and vega themselves move — the next layer of the Taylor expansion (see Second-Order Greeks). They improve the local picture; they still don't replace a full reprice.

📊

GEX as a range read

On this desk's evidence, dealer gamma belongs in a statement about expected range on the Nasdaq — "quieter than a vol-matched baseline" when long — and never in a statement about direction.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where same-signs gets mistaken for same-risk

"Same Greeks, same risk." Short put and short call at one strike have identical gamma and vega. Their worst cases are 97.80 and unlimited. Greeks are slopes at a point, not a description of the tail.

"Gamma near zero means the position is safe." A deep in-the-money short call has gamma and vega near zero and is losing one-for-one with every rally. Low gamma means linear, not small.

"The Taylor estimate is conservative." In the worked example it overstated a 50-point loss by more than double; in other configurations (a jump through a strike from just outside it) it understates. It is not reliably either.

"Bounded means small." The short put's loss is bounded at 97.80 for 2.20 collected — about 44 times the premium. Bounded is a property of the shape, not a statement about comfort.

"Short gamma means a breakout is coming." The desk's test found short-gamma days are essentially ordinary in range, long-gamma days are the quiet ones, and gamma carries no direction. On FX it carries nothing.

Key Takeaways

✓Same signs, even same numbers. Short put and short call at one strike share gamma and vega exactly; theta differs by \(rKe^{-r\tau}\); delta by exactly 1.
✓Short call = short put + short forward. The forward is a straight line — invisible to gamma and vega — and it carries the entire unbounded tail.
✓Greeks are local; payoffs are global. Size shorts by stressed loss, not by Greek columns.
✓Validated on the Nasdaq: dealer gamma says something about range. +0.176 (p 0.0008) wider on short-gamma days vs a Brownian baseline, +0.315 vol-matched, 793 days — with long gamma (≈0.85) the informative, quiet state.
⚠Low gamma is not low risk. Deep in the money, the short call's Greeks go quiet while its loss runs one-for-one.
✗Falsified: gamma as a direction signal. The adjacent wall-magnet direction test scored 48.8% out-of-sample against a 49.9% placebo.
✗Does not carry to FX. The range result is Nasdaq-only; on FX, say nothing from GEX.
Section 07

Self-test

Q1. Name the three Greeks whose signs a short put and short call share, and the one they don't.
Both are short gamma (Γ < 0), long theta (Θ > 0 in the normal case) and short vega (ν < 0). Delta differs: the short put is positive delta (bullish), the short call negative delta (bearish).
Q2. Use put-call parity to show the two shorts have identical gamma.
\(-C = -P - (S - Ke^{-r\tau})\). The bracket is linear in \(S\), so \(\partial^2/\partial S^2\) of it is zero. Differentiating twice: \(-\Gamma_C = -\Gamma_P\), i.e. the short call and short put have the same gamma. The same argument with \(\partial/\partial\sigma\) gives equal vega.
Q3. K = 100, put premium 2.20, call premium 2.37. What is each short's worst case at expiry?
Short put: \(2.20 - 100 = -97.80\), reached at \(S_T = 0\). Short call: unbounded — at \(S_T = 300\) it is \(2.37 - 200 = -197.63\), and it keeps falling with price.
Q4. Why did the Taylor estimate say −113 for a +50 move on the short call, when the true loss was −47.8?
The estimate \(\Delta\delta S + \tfrac12\Gamma\delta S^2\) assumes gamma stays at its at-the-money value (0.0695) across the whole move. In reality gamma collapses as the call goes deep in the money and delta saturates at −1, so the true loss is roughly linear past the strike. The Greeks are a local expansion; a 50-point move on a 30-day option is not local.
Q5. What did the desk find when it tested "dealer short gamma amplifies moves"?
On 793 days of Nasdaq futures, short-gamma days had +0.176 wider next-day range than a Brownian baseline (p 0.0008), +0.315 vol-matched — a real range effect. But short-gamma days are essentially ordinary (≈1.02); the informative state is long gamma being quiet (≈0.85). Gamma carries no direction (the wall-magnet direction test: 48.8% vs a 49.9% placebo), and the effect does not carry to FX.
Q6. In the VW squeeze, which of the four option positions was exposed in the same way as the short sellers, and why?
The short call. Deep in the money, a short call's delta approaches −1 and it behaves like short stock: every euro of rally is a euro of loss, with no contractual cap. Short put holders, by contrast, would have been exposed only to a fall, which is bounded at zero.
