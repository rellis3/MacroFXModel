FX Fixings & the WM/Reuters 4pm Fix

One exchange rate, stamped at 4pm in London, that trillions of dollars of funds and indices are valued at. How it is calculated, how banks fill the orders that target it, why month-end moves it, and how it was rigged until 2013.

CONCEPT — how the benchmark works, not a trading signal ~18 min read Prereqs: Execution Algorithms, Market Microstructure
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • A fix is one published exchange rate for one moment of the day. The one that matters most is the WM/Reuters 4pm London closing rate (renamed WM/Refinitiv, and since late 2023 just "WMR", run by an LSEG company). Index providers, fund valuations and custodians use it, so big orders are timed to it.
  • Since 15 February 2015 the 4pm rate is the median of one-second snapshots of trades and best quotes over a five-minute window, 15:57:30 to 16:02:30 London time. Before that the window was one minute, 15:59:30 to 16:00:30.
  • A median is hard to push. To drag it beyond where the market would have printed anyway, you have to hold the price there for close to half the window: about 30 seconds before 2015, about 150 seconds now.
  • Banks take fixing orders: a promise to deal at a rate nobody knows yet. They net buyers against sellers and trade the rest around the window. That promise, and the private knowledge of the flow that comes with it, is where the conflict of interest lives.
  • Month-end and quarter-end: hedged international funds top up their currency hedges at the fix. The rule of thumb: the currency of the stock market that rose most gets sold. On the lesson's illustrative numbers, US stocks up 4% against foreign stocks up 1% means about $20bn of net dollar selling.
  • Real-world episode: on 12 November 2014 UK, US and Swiss regulators fined six banks about $4.3bn combined for FX failings, including attempts to manipulate the 4pm fix through chat rooms. In May 2015 four banks pleaded guilty in the US. The window was widened, and the FX Global Code (2017) now spells out what a bank filling a fixing order may and may not do.
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

Every month-end someone tells me to watch the four o'clock fix. What actually is it?

It's one exchange rate, published for four in the afternoon London time. Index providers value their indices at it. Fund administrators strike prices at it. Custodians convert at it. So a lot of money wants to trade at exactly that rate.

How do they work out one number for the whole market?

Every second for five minutes, from two and a half minutes before four to two and a half minutes after, they take a snapshot of trades and best prices on the main platforms. Then they take the median. Since February twenty fifteen it's been five minutes. Before that it was one.

Why the median and not the average?

Because one huge trade can drag an average, but it barely moves a median. To shift a median you have to keep the price where you want it for close to half the window. With one minute, that was about thirty seconds of pushing. With five minutes, it's about two and a half minutes. Much harder, much more expensive.

And the banks?

A fund says, buy me five hundred million euros at the fix. The bank promises a rate it doesn't know yet. If another client is selling two hundred million, the bank nets them and only has to buy three hundred million in the market. On three hundred million euros, every pip is thirty thousand dollars.

So where's the temptation?

The bank knows the flow before anyone else. If it buys early, cheap, and its own buying lifts the fix, it sells to the client at a higher rate and keeps the gap. Two pips on that order is sixty thousand dollars, paid by the client.

And that's what the scandal was.

Partly. Regulators found traders at different banks in chat groups, sharing what their clients were doing and trying to move the fixes together. On the twelfth of November twenty fourteen, British, American and Swiss regulators fined six banks about four point three billion dollars in total. The next May, four banks pleaded guilty in the United States.

What about month-end?

Funds that own foreign stocks and hedge the currency have to top up the hedge when the stocks rise. Say foreign investors hold six hundred billion dollars of hedged American shares and the market is up four percent. They sell twenty four billion more dollars. If Americans hold four hundred billion of hedged foreign shares that rose one percent, they buy four billion back. Net, about twenty billion dollars to sell.

So I just sell dollars into every month-end?

No. Everyone runs the same sum, so some of it is traded before the fix and priced in. The size estimate is rough. And research finds the dollar tends to firm into the big fixes and give it back afterwards, by a couple of basis points on a normal day. Use the sign as a warning, not a free trade.

The one-line version?

The fix is a five-minute median that the whole buy side marks to. Know when it is, expect a crowd and a jump, and never trade on what you know about someone else's order.

Section 01

Why this matters

Foreign exchange has no closing auction and no single exchange. It trades around the clock, across dozens of platforms and thousands of bilateral relationships, so "the price of the euro" at any moment depends on who you ask. That is fine for a trader, who only needs a price to trade at. It is a real problem for anyone who has to value something: an index made of shares in twenty currencies, a fund that strikes its net asset value once a day, a custodian converting dividends, an auditor checking a balance sheet. They all need one agreed number, at one agreed time, that nobody can argue about later.

A fixing, or fix, is that number. The administrator publishes it for a stated moment, from a published method. WM/Reuters started publishing its rates in 1994, for exactly this reason: before there was an agreed close, different funds were using different rates and their reported returns could not be compared. The 4pm London closing rate became the standard. The administrator says major index providers, including MSCI, FTSE Russell and S&P, use its rates.

For a trader the consequence is simple. If thousands of funds are valued at the 4pm rate, then thousands of funds want to trade at the 4pm rate, because trading at the rate you are valued at is the only way to have no tracking error. So the minutes around 4pm London are when a large share of the day's benchmark-linked flow goes through, all at once, and at month-end and quarter-end there is much more of it. You need to know when that happens, what it does to price and liquidity, and where the legal line runs for anyone handling those orders.

📈

Index providers

An index of shares in many currencies needs one FX rate per currency per day to be converted into one base currency. A published fix gives every user the same number.

🧺

Passive funds and ETFs

A fund tracking that index is valued at the same rate. Trading its FX at the fix removes tracking error from currency, which is why the FSB found passive funds the most likely to use it.

🏦

Custodians and fund administrators

They convert income, subscriptions and redemptions, and strike net asset values. An independent rate is easy to verify for clients and auditors.

🔁

Asset managers and corporates

Mandate transfers between managers, hedge roll-overs and month-end rebalancing are often done at the fix. The FSB noted that transfers between managers are among the largest fix trades of all.

💡
Plain-English intuition

A class photo, taken at four o'clock

Think of the fix as a class photo. The market never stands still, but at 4pm London the photographer takes one picture, and that picture goes in every yearbook: every index, every fund report, every custody statement. So everyone who will be judged by the photo wants to be standing in it. That is why trading crowds into the few minutes around 4pm.

Two problems follow. First, a crowd that all wants to buy at once moves the price, so the photo shows the crowd as much as the market. Second, the people organising the crowd, the banks taking the orders, know where everyone is going to stand before the photo is taken. Until 2013 some of them used that knowledge, and the rules changed: the shutter now stays open for five minutes instead of one, and the photo is the middle of all the frames, so a few people jumping in front of the lens no longer changes the picture much.

Section 02

The math, step by step

Watch the formula build
How long you must hold the price to move a median fix, one term at a time
\[ F = \operatorname{median}\{\color{#fbbf24}{x_1, x_2, \dots, x_N}\} \]

The fix is the median of one sample per second across the window. Since 2015 the window is five minutes, so \(N = 301\) samples; before 2015 it was one minute, \(N = 61\).

\[ \color{#fbbf24}{k} + (N-k)\,q \;\ge\; \tfrac{N}{2} \]

To drag the median up to a target, at least half the samples must sit at or above it. You control \(k\) of them by holding the price there; of the other \(N-k\), a share \(q\) would have landed there anyway.

\[ q = \color{#fbbf24}{1 - \Phi\!\left(\tfrac{\Delta}{\sigma_w}\right)} \]

If clean prices in the window scatter around the true level with spread \(\sigma_w\), the chance that one lands at least \(\Delta\) above it is one minus the normal curve's area up to \(\Delta/\sigma_w\).

\[ \color{#10b981}{T_{\text{hold}} = N\,\frac{\tfrac12 - q}{1-q}} \]

Solve for \(k\): the seconds you must hold the price at the target. Push one spread's worth (\(\Delta = \sigma_w\)) and this is about 41% of the window: 25 seconds of a 61-second window, 122 seconds of a 301-second one. Push far past the natural range and it tends to half the window.

What the administrator actually does. For the liquid "trade currencies" (about two dozen, including all of the G10), WMR takes a snapshot every second during the window from the main electronic trading platforms, such as LSEG's Matching and EBS. It prefers executed trades and falls back to the best bid and offer when trades are too thin. It takes the median of those samples. For the much larger set of less-traded currencies it samples quotes less often, every 15 seconds, over its own window. The FSB's 2014 report describes the quote-based version as the average of the median bid and the median offer:

\[ F = \frac{\operatorname{median}(\text{bid}_s) + \operatorname{median}(\text{offer}_s)}{2} \]
Fthe published fix (mid rate)
bidₛ, offerₛthe best bid and offer captured in snapshot s
medianthe middle value once all snapshots are sorted

The window is centred on the hour. From 15 February 2015 it runs from 15:57:30 to 16:02:30, two and a half minutes either side of 4pm London time. Before that it ran from 15:59:30 to 16:00:30. Note that it is London clock time: 4pm in London is 11am in New York for almost all of the year, except for a few weeks around March and the end of October, when the two countries change their clocks on different dates.

Why a median, not an average. Suppose a trader slams in one trade far away from the market. An average moves by the size of that outlier divided by the number of samples:

\[ \Delta\bar{x} = \frac{x_{\text{out}} - \bar{x}}{N} \]
Δx̄how far the average moves
x_outthe price of the one off-market trade
Nthe number of samples in the window

So a far enough outlier can move an average as much as you like. A median ignores how far the outlier is. It only cares how many samples sit above and below the middle. One wild print shifts the median by at most one place in the sorted list. The FSB put it plainly: to be certain of influencing the median, extra trades "would have to be such as to hold the whole market close to the desired outcome throughout a large part of the fixing window."

Making "a large part of the window" exact. Call the level the market would have fixed at without you \(m\), and your target \(m + \Delta\). The median is at or above the target when at least half of the \(N\) samples are. You hold the price at the target for \(k\) seconds. The other \(N-k\) seconds are ordinary market prices, and a share \(q\) of them happen to land at or above the target anyway. So you need

\[ k + (N-k)\,q \;\ge\; \frac{N}{2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{k}{N} \;\ge\; \frac{\tfrac12 - q}{1-q} \]
kseconds you hold the price at the target
qshare of ordinary samples that land at or above the target anyway
N/2half the samples: what the median needs

In a simple model where ordinary prices in the window scatter normally around \(m\) with spread \(\sigma_w\), the share above the target is \(q = 1 - \Phi(\Delta/\sigma_w)\), where \(\Phi\) is the area under the standard normal curve. Put the two together:

\[ T_{\text{hold}} = N\,\frac{\tfrac12 - q}{1-q}, \qquad q = 1-\Phi\!\left(\frac{\Delta}{\sigma_w}\right) \]
T_holdseconds of the window you must control
Δhow far above the clean fix you want it, in pips
σ_whow widely ordinary prices scatter in the window, in pips
Φstandard normal cumulative probability

The cost of pushing
Seconds you must hold the price against everyone else. More seconds means more money spent and a longer, more visible footprint.

Two facts fall out. First, \(T_{\text{hold}}\) is proportional to \(N\): at the same push, the five-minute window makes a would-be manipulator hold the price about five times as long. Second, as the target moves far outside the normal scatter, \(q \to 0\) and \(T_{\text{hold}} \to N/2\). The limit was about 30 seconds of pushing before 2015 and about 150 seconds after. The model is deliberately simple: it ignores that holding a price also drags the seconds after it, and that real samples are not independent. But it captures why the reform made the fix harder to move, and why collusion was so valuable before it: pooling several banks' orders made it far easier to dominate half a one-minute window.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Line up 301 kids by height and pick the one in the middle. If one giant joins the line, the middle kid barely changes. To change who is in the middle, you need to swap in about 150 tall kids. The old line only had 61 kids, so about 30 swaps did it. That is why making the window five times longer made the fix much harder to cheat.

Month-end: where the predictable flow comes from

Most days the orders at the fix roughly cancel. The FSB found that the net imbalance at the fix has no obvious day-to-day direction, with one exception: it tends to be larger at month-ends and quarter-ends. The reason is hedge rebalancing. Many international equity funds own foreign shares but hedge away the currency, usually with forwards that they adjust once a month, at the month-end fix.

Take a European fund holding US shares with the dollar hedged. Over the month, US shares rise 4%. The fund now owns 4% more dollars' worth of stock than its hedge covers, so at month-end it sells that extra 4% of dollars forward. Now take a US fund holding hedged European shares that rose 1% in euros. It sells 1% more euros, which means it buys dollars. Net those two across the market:

\[ \text{Flow}_{USD} = B\,r_F - A\,r_{US} \]
Flow_USDnet dollar buying (+) or selling (−) from hedge top-ups
A·r_USdollars foreign investors must sell after US stocks rise
B·r_Fdollars US investors must buy after foreign stocks rise

The trader's rule of thumb follows directly: at month-end, the currency of the stock market that went up the most tends to be sold. If US stocks beat the rest of the world, expect dollar selling into the fix; if the rest of the world beat the US, expect dollar buying. Funds that rebalance unhedged index weights push the same way: after US stocks outperform they sell US shares and buy foreign ones, which also means selling dollars. Melvin and Prins (2015) tested exactly this channel and found that equity gains over the month predict that market's currency weakening before the month-end 4pm fix. Bank research desks publish month-end estimates built on the same idea.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

You promised to keep an umbrella exactly as big as your sandcastle. Your sandcastle grew, so at the end of the month you have to buy a bigger umbrella. Every fund with a bigger sandcastle buys umbrellas on the same afternoon, and umbrellas get pricier for a few minutes.

Worked example

Filling a fixing order, honestly and otherwise (illustrative numbers)

An index fund needs to buy €500m against dollars at today's 4pm fix. Another client of the same bank needs to sell €200m at the fix. The bank accepts both orders: it promises to deal with each client at the fix rate, plus an agreed, disclosed fee. The bank does not know that rate yet.

Step 1, net. The two orders partly cancel. The bank can cross €200m of the fund's purchase against the seller internally, at the fix, so only the residual has to go to the market:

\[ \text{€500m} - \text{€200m} = \text{€300m to buy} \]
€500mclient buy order at the fix
€200mclient sell order at the fix, crossed internally
€300mthe residual the bank must buy in the market

Step 2, size the risk. In EUR/USD a pip is 0.0001 dollars per euro, so on €300m every pip of difference between the bank's buying price and the fix is

\[ 300{,}000{,}000 \times 0.0001 = \$30{,}000 \text{ per pip} \]
0.0001one pip in EUR/USD
$30,000the bank's profit or loss per pip of mismatch

Step 3a, the honest fill. The bank buys the €300m spread through the window, because buying evenly while the median is being measured is the closest it can get to paying the median. Say it averages 1.10012 and the fix prints 1.10010. It sold to the client at 1.10010 but paid 1.10012: a 0.2-pip shortfall, or \(0.00002 \times 300{,}000{,}000 = \$6{,}000\), which the agreed fee is meant to cover. The bank was paid for carrying risk, not for moving the price.

Step 3b, the conflicted fill. Instead the bank buys the whole €300m between 15:50 and 15:57, before the window, averaging 1.09990. Its own buying lifts the market, and the fix prints 1.10010. It still sells to the client at the fix, so it keeps

\[ (1.10010 - 1.09990) \times 300{,}000{,}000 = \$60{,}000 \]
1.09990bank's average buying price, before the window
1.10010the fix the client is charged
$60,000the bank's gain: 2 pips paid by its own client

The two pips the client overpaid exist partly because the bank bought first. That is the conflict at the heart of fixing orders, and why the FX Global Code's examples of unacceptable practice include buying more than the client's interest just before the window "with the intent of inflating or deflating the price against the Client." Trading before the window is not banned in itself: the same principle allows working an order before, during or after the window "so long as not to intentionally negatively impact the market price and outcome to the Client." The line is intent, and what the bank does with what it knows.

The trap: "my order, my information". A client's fixing order is the client's information, not the bank's trading signal. Sharing it with traders at other banks, trading extra size in front of it, or timing your own book around it are exactly the behaviours regulators fined in 2014 and 2015. The FSB's recommendations say market-makers should not share information about their positions or flows with each other beyond what a transaction needs.

Worked example

A month-end estimate, start to finish (illustrative numbers)

It is the last business day of the month. Month to date, US equities are up 4% and foreign equities are up 1% in their own currencies. Suppose foreign investors hold \(A = \$600\)bn of US equities with the dollar hedged, and US investors hold \(B = \$400\)bn of foreign equities with the currency hedged. These holdings are made up for the example; the real ones are not published in this form.

\[ A\,r_{US} = 600 \times 0.04 = \$24\text{bn of dollars to sell} \]
A = $600bnhedged US equities owned abroad
r_US = 4%US equity return this month
\[ B\,r_F = 400 \times 0.01 = \$4\text{bn of dollars to buy} \]
B = $400bnhedged foreign equities owned by US investors
r_F = 1%foreign equity return in local currency
\[ \text{Flow}_{USD} = 4 - 24 = -\$20\text{bn} \]
−$20bnnet dollar selling from hedge top-ups

If about half of that is executed at the fix itself (the rest spread over the preceding days or done some other way), the fix sees roughly $10bn of net dollar selling. Read the answer as a direction and an order of magnitude: the dollar is more likely to be offered than bid into 4pm. The size depends on holdings, hedge ratios and timing that nobody outside the funds knows precisely.

Real-world trading scenario (illustrative)

Long USD/JPY into a quarter-end, with US stocks well ahead

It is the last business day of a quarter. US equities have rallied hard over the quarter while Japanese and European equities are flat. You are long USD/JPY from a macro view, with a stop 25 pips below the market, and it is 15:40 in London. The month-end arithmetic points one way: hedgers abroad must sell dollars to top up their hedges, and some of it will hit the 4pm fix. The window will be busy, spreads tight, and the price can jump in the first 30 seconds.

Three sensible moves. One: decide before 15:57:30 whether you want to carry the position through the window at all; a stop sitting a few pips away is a stop placed in the busiest five minutes of the day. Two: if you hold, widen the stop to something the fix can plausibly print through, or cut size so the wider stop costs the same. Three: do not chase the move after 16:02:30. The forced flow is finished once the window closes, and research on the major fixes finds the dollar's pre-fix move tends to reverse afterwards rather than continue.

What you should not do: try to "front-run the fix" with size you would not otherwise trade. The expected edge is a few basis points on a normal day, the estimate of the flow is rough, everyone else has the same estimate, and part of the flow trades in the days before.

Section 03

It actually happened: the FX fixing scandal, 2013–2015

Every piece of this lesson's mechanics shows up in the scandal: the one-minute window, the netting of fixing orders, the private knowledge of client flow, and the median that a group could dominate when it pooled its orders.

It actually happened

Chat rooms, a one-minute window, and $4.3 billion in one day

In June 2013 Bloomberg News reported, citing dealers with knowledge of the practice, that traders at some of the biggest banks had been pushing through trades before and during the 60-second window to move the WM/Reuters rates, and colluding with counterparts to improve their odds. Regulators in the UK, the US, Switzerland and elsewhere opened investigations. The Financial Stability Board set up a group to review the benchmarks, and its final report on 30 September 2014 recommended widening the window, transparent pricing of fixing orders, separate handling of them inside banks, and no sharing of positions or flows between market-makers.

On 12 November 2014 the regulators acted together. The UK's Financial Conduct Authority fined Citibank, HSBC, JPMorgan Chase, RBS and UBS a combined £1,114,918,000 for failings in G10 spot FX between January 2008 and October 2013. It found that traders formed tight groups with names such as "the players", "the 3 musketeers" and "the A-team", shared confidential information about client orders, and used it to try to manipulate the 4pm WM/Reuters and the 1:15pm (London time) ECB fixes and to trigger clients' stop-loss orders. The US Commodity Futures Trading Commission fined the same five banks $1.475bn (Citibank and JPMorgan $310m each, RBS and UBS $290m each, HSBC $275m). The US Office of the Comptroller of the Currency fined Bank of America $250m, Citibank $350m and JPMorgan $350m. Switzerland's FINMA ordered UBS to pay CHF 134m. Six banks, about $4.3bn in one day.

Barclays had pulled out of that settlement. On 20 May 2015 Citicorp, JPMorgan, Barclays and RBS agreed to plead guilty in the US to conspiring to manipulate the price of dollars and euros, with criminal fines of $925m, $550m, $650m and $395m. The Justice Department said euro-dollar traders who called themselves "The Cartel" had coordinated trading around the ECB and WM/Reuters fixes and agreed to hold back bids or offers so as not to move the rate against each other's positions. The same day the Federal Reserve fined six banks more than $1.8bn. In between, on 15 February 2015, the 4pm window was widened from one minute to five.

Individual prosecutions went less well for the authorities. Three of the traders in that chat room were charged in the US in January 2017 and acquitted by a jury in Manhattan on 26 October 2018. The lasting changes were to the market's plumbing: the five-minute median, separate fix desks, transparent fees, and the FX Global Code, first published in May 2017, whose Principle 10 sets out what a bank handling a fixing order may and may not do.

⚠️
Where the textbook version breaks

A median protects against one big trader, not against a coordinated crowd

The median argument in Section 02 assumes the "ordinary" samples are independent of whoever is pushing. Collusion breaks exactly that assumption: if several banks trade the same way, or hold back the other way, the ordinary samples stop being ordinary and \(q\) rises in the pusher's favour. A wider window raises the cost, but the deeper fix was behavioural: separate desks, recorded procedures, no sharing of client flow, and transparent fees so banks are paid for risk rather than for where the fix prints. The DOJ's description of "The Cartel" holding back bids and offers is the median's weak spot in plain sight.

Section 04

How practitioners actually use this

The other fixes on the clock. The 4pm London rate is the biggest, but not the only one, and research finds the same pattern around all the major ones.

🇬🇧

WMR 4pm London

The closing spot rate for 150+ currencies, from a five-minute median. WMR also publishes intraday rates through the day (half-hourly for the main currencies), a 2pm CET fix and a 12 noon New York fix.

🇪🇺

ECB reference rates

Set by a daily concertation between central banks, now normally around 14:10 CET (it was 14:15 CET at the time of the scandal) and, since 1 July 2016, published at around 16:00 CET. The ECB says they are for information only and strongly discourages using them for transactions.

🇯🇵

Tokyo 9:55 fix

Each Japanese bank sets its own rate for customers from market prices at about 9:55am Tokyo time. Payment days ending in 5 or 0 ("gotobi") and month-ends bring heavier importer dollar demand, and the dollar tends to firm into 9:55.

🔄

The round-the-clock pattern

Krohn, Mueller and Whelan (2024) find the dollar tends to rise into the Tokyo, ECB and London fixes and fall back afterwards: roughly 2.5 basis points each way per day across nine major currencies, 1999 to 2019.

How banks fill fixing orders today. The FSB declined to ban trading at a yet-to-be-determined price, because clients need it. Instead the sell side now typically does four things. It prices the service openly, with a disclosed fee or spread rather than an "at mid, free" promise that pushed dealers to earn their money from the price. It runs fixing orders through separate, documented processes, often on a dedicated desk or algorithm. It nets client interest internally and executes only the residual. And it works that residual through the calculation window, sometimes starting earlier, in a way it can show was meant to track the fix rather than move it. The FSB also encouraged industry netting facilities, where orders to trade at the fix are matched against each other before anything reaches the market.

How the buy side uses it. The FSB found a clear split. Passive funds and ETFs favour the fix because it removes currency tracking error. Many active managers deliberately avoid it, noting that the most liquid moment of the day is not the same as the best price, and execute through the day with algorithms instead. Asset managers are expected to do due diligence on which rate and execution method actually serves their clients.

What a trader should do around a fix

  • Keep a fix calendar: 4pm London daily, month-end and quarter-end above all, the ECB rate at 14:10 CET, Tokyo 9:55 (busiest on 5th and 10th days).
  • Estimate the month-end sign from relative equity performance, and treat the size as rough.
  • Decide about stops and size before 15:57:30, not during the window.
  • If you must execute size, choose consciously between the fix (liquid, tight spreads, everyone's price) and an algorithm over a longer period.
  • If you handle client fixing orders, follow your written procedure and the FX Global Code's Principle 10.

What a trader should not do

  • Share, ask for or act on another party's fixing orders, positions or flows.
  • Trade extra size into the window, or just before it, intending to move the print.
  • Treat the pre-fix drift as a free trade: it is a few basis points against your costs, and it reverses.
  • Leave a tight stop sitting in the window on a big month-end and call the fill bad luck.
  • Assume the month-end estimate is all still to come: part of it trades in the days before.
⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where traders get the fix wrong

Confusing the fix with the close. FX has no official close; "the 4pm fix" and "the New York 5pm roll" are different things. Many charting platforms draw daily bars at New York 5pm, so a daily candle and a fund's valuation rate are not the same price, and month-end performance figures in different reports can disagree for that reason alone.

Forgetting the clock changes. The window is 4pm London local time. During the few weeks a year when the UK and US change clocks on different dates, it is not at 11am New York.

Treating the month-end model as a forecast of size. The arithmetic gives a sign and an order of magnitude. Hedge ratios, the share executed at the fix, pre-positioning and other flows (index changes, corporate payments, mandate transfers) all move the real number, and the market prices in some of the estimate before 4pm.

Reading every spike as manipulation. Price moves in the window are mostly the net balance of genuine supply and demand finally meeting. The FSB noted this explicitly. A big print at a quarter-end fix is usually just a big imbalance.

Scenario drill · 12 November 2014

A chat message the day the fines landed

You run the G10 spot desk at a bank. For tomorrow's 4pm fix you hold a client order to buy €400m and another client's order to sell €150m, both at the fix plus an agreed fee. A trader you have known for years at another bank messages you in a chat group: "Big euro buyer at the fix tomorrow? Me too. Pass yours to me and I'll make sure it goes our way." What do you do?

  1. Tell him your net and agree to pool the orders so one of you dominates the window.
    This is the conduct regulators fined: sharing client order information between banks and coordinating to move a fix. With a median, pooling orders is exactly what makes a push possible. It is also how the US criminal case against four banks was built.
  2. Say nothing, but buy an extra €250m of your own just before the window, then sell it back during the window.
    Trading more than the client's interest just before the window to inflate the price against the client is a listed unacceptable practice. Doing it alone instead of with a friend does not change what it is.
  3. Decline to discuss client business, cross the €150m internally and work the €250m residual through the window under your documented procedure.
    This is what the FSB's September 2014 recommendations ask for, and what the FX Global Code later wrote down: no sharing of flows, netting client interest, executing the net amount in a way meant to track the fix, paid by a transparent fee.
  4. Stop accepting fixing orders at all.
    You can't misuse what you don't hold, so it is safe. But clients still need the service, and the FSB chose not to ban fix trading for that reason. Handling the orders properly serves them better than refusing them.

What happened. That day the FCA fined Citibank, HSBC, JPMorgan Chase, RBS and UBS a combined £1.11bn, after finding that traders in groups with names like "the players" and "the A-team" had shared client order information and tried to manipulate the 4pm WM/Reuters and ECB fixes. The CFTC fined the same five banks $1.475bn, the OCC fined Bank of America, Citibank and JPMorgan $950m, and FINMA ordered UBS to pay CHF 134m: about $4.3bn in total. On 20 May 2015 Citicorp, JPMorgan, Barclays and RBS agreed to plead guilty in the US over a euro-dollar chat room whose members called themselves "The Cartel", and the Federal Reserve fined six banks more than $1.8bn.

Section 05

Key Takeaways

✓A fix is one agreed rate for one moment. The WM/Reuters (now WMR) 4pm London closing rate is the one indices, fund valuations and custodians use, so a large share of benchmark-linked FX is timed to it.
✓Since 15 February 2015 it is a five-minute median, 15:57:30 to 16:02:30 London time, of one-second snapshots of trades and best quotes. Before that the window was one minute.
✓Moving a median means owning about half the window. \(T_{\text{hold}} = N(\tfrac12-q)/(1-q)\): roughly 30 seconds of pushing at most before 2015, roughly 150 seconds now. Wider windows make manipulation slower, costlier and more visible.
✓Fixing orders are a promise at an unknown price. Banks net client interest and trade the residual around the window; on €300m, every pip of mismatch is $30,000, which is why pricing and process must be transparent.
✓Month-end sign rule: hedge top-ups sell the currency of the stock market that rose most. \(\text{Flow}_{USD} = B\,r_F - A\,r_{US}\) gives the direction and a rough size.
⚠Know it, don't worship it. The pre-fix drift is a few basis points and tends to reverse; flow estimates are rough and partly priced in. Use the fix calendar to manage stops and execution, not as a stand-alone signal.
✗Client flow is never your signal. Sharing fixing orders and coordinating around the fix cost six banks about $4.3bn on 12 November 2014, led to guilty pleas in May 2015, and is now spelled out as unacceptable in the FX Global Code.
Section 06

Self-test

Q1. Why do index funds want to trade FX at the 4pm London fix even if it is not the cheapest moment of the day?
Because the index, and therefore the fund's benchmark, converts foreign holdings at that same rate. Trading at the rate you are measured against removes currency tracking error: whatever the rate is, the fund and its index use the same one. The FSB found passive funds and ETFs were the most likely users of the fix for exactly this reason, while many active managers avoid it and trade through the day instead.
Q2. Since February 2015, what window and statistic define the WMR 4pm rate for a major currency pair, and what was it before?
A median of one-second snapshots (executed trades, with best bid and offer as a fallback) over five minutes, from 15:57:30 to 16:02:30 London time: 301 samples. Before 15 February 2015 it was one minute, 15:59:30 to 16:00:30: 61 samples. The FSB's September 2014 report recommended the wider window.
Q3. Using the hold-time formula, roughly how many seconds must someone hold EUR/USD one "scatter" (Δ = σ_w) above the clean level to move the median there, in the old and new windows?
With \(\Delta = \sigma_w\), \(q = 1 - \Phi(1) \approx 0.1587\), so the share needed is \((0.5 - 0.1587)/(1 - 0.1587) \approx 0.406\). That is about \(0.406 \times 61 \approx 25\) seconds in the one-minute window and \(0.406 \times 301 \approx 122\) seconds in the five-minute window. For pushes far outside the natural scatter the answer approaches half the window: 30 and 150 seconds.
Q4. A bank holds client fixing orders to buy €500m and sell €200m of EUR/USD. What does it need to trade, and what is a pip worth on that amount?
It can cross €200m internally at the fix and only needs to buy the residual €300m in the market. A pip is 0.0001 dollars per euro, so on €300m it is \(300{,}000{,}000 \times 0.0001 = \$30{,}000\). That is the bank's profit or loss for every pip between its average buying price and the fix, which is why the risk has to be priced openly and the execution designed to track, not move, the fix.
Q5. At month-end, US stocks are up 4% and foreign stocks up 1%. Which way should hedge rebalancing push the dollar at the fix, and why?
Towards dollar selling. Foreign investors holding hedged US shares now own more dollars' worth of stock than their hedges cover, so they sell more dollars forward. US investors holding hedged foreign shares top up by selling a smaller amount of foreign currency, which means buying dollars. With \(A = 600\), \(B = 400\) (in $bn): \(400 \times 1\% - 600 \times 4\% = 4 - 24 = -\$20\)bn. The currency of the market that rose most gets sold.
Q6. What did the 12 November 2014 enforcement actions find, who imposed them, and roughly how much did they total?
The FCA (£1.11bn on Citibank, HSBC, JPMorgan Chase, RBS and UBS), the CFTC ($1.475bn on the same five), the OCC ($950m on Bank of America, Citibank and JPMorgan) and FINMA (CHF 134m from UBS): about $4.3bn across six banks. Regulators found traders had shared confidential client order information in chat groups and attempted to manipulate FX benchmarks including the 4pm WM/Reuters fix. Criminal guilty pleas by Citicorp, JPMorgan, Barclays and RBS followed on 20 May 2015.
