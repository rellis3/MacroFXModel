Obizhaeva-Wang, Footprints in Wet Sand

Kyle's impact never fades because it's information. Almgren-Chriss's temporary impact snaps back the instant you stop trading, with no memory of a moment ago. Obizhaeva-Wang sits in between: a limit-order-book reservoir that heals at a finite resilience rate ρ — and that one assumption alone turns the optimal way to trade a large order from a smooth curve into something lumpier at both ends.

Section 1

Three impact models, one axis: does it decay, and how?

A memory-foam pillow springs back instantly. Wet sand takes real time to smooth back out.

📈

Kyle (1985) — permanent, no decay at all

Δp = λQ: an information-driven repricing that never fades. It isn't a liquidity effect — the market rationally updated its belief, and there's nothing to decay back from.

⚡

Almgren-Chriss's temporary impact — memoryless

Cost ½η·ẋ(t)² at each instant, a pure function of the current trading rate. It reverts the moment the rate drops — no separate state variable, no resilience parameter, because it doesn't need one.

🌊

Obizhaeva-Wang — a genuine half-life

D_t is a real state variable: it jumps up at each trade and decays continuously at rate ρ in between. A trade from a few minutes ago can still be partially displacing the book right now.

Footprints in wet sand, not a memory-foam pillow. The print doesn't vanish the instant your foot lifts — water has to seep back in, which takes real, measurable time. Step in the same spot again too soon and you sink in deeper.
Section 2

Two rules are the entire engine

A decay between trades, a jump at each trade — nothing else drives D_t.

The mechanism
Between trades
→
dD_t = −ρ · D_t · dt
Continuous exponential decay back toward zero, half-life ln(2)/ρ — the book healing itself. Larger ρ means a faster-healing book.
At a trade of size dx
→
D_{t+} = D_{t−} + κ · dx
An instant jump — D_{t−} is whatever the previous trade's impact has already decayed down to; κ is the instantaneous impact-per-unit-traded coefficient.
cost of dx at t
≈
dx · (D_{t−} + ½κ·dx)
A trade pays roughly the leftover concession D_{t−}, plus half of its own instantaneous push — the same "walking the book" triangle used elsewhere in this curriculum.

Section 4

Why the boundaries beat the interior

Almgren-Chriss's optimal path is always smooth. Once impact has real memory, that logic breaks — specifically at the two ends.

t = 0 and t = T — structurally cheap
  • At t=0, D_0 = 0 by construction — no prior impact to fight
  • At t=T, whatever impact this last trade leaves behind never has to be paid for again
  • Neither boundary trade "pays to restart" against its own leftover displacement
The interior — fighting your own footprint
  • An interior trade pushes through leftover D_{t−} from the order's own earlier trading
  • Part of the concession it pays will itself decay away, unused, before the order finishes
  • Money spent moving the price mid-horizon is partly wasted on a push that heals before it can be leveraged again
That asymmetry is the entire intuitive content of the famous result: the cost-minimizing schedule places discrete block trades at t=0 and t=T, with a lighter, smoother rate filling in the interior. It is not an argument for pushing those blocks to their logical extreme — the next slide shows exactly why not.

🐘

All-at-once is worst by far

Every unit pays the full quadratic self-impact of a single 100-unit trade, with no chance for anything to decay first.

📐

C beats B — but only by ~2%

C's boundary trades are bigger, so their own self-impact is higher (16.67 vs. 10.00) — but that's more than offset by a much lower residual/decay-fighting cost (9.25 vs. 16.45).

⚠️

More boundary-loading isn't automatically better

Pushing to the extreme — exactly two 50-unit blocks, nothing in between — costs ≈28.38: worse than C, because a block that large pays too much extra self-impact to be worth the decay savings.

Grepping every .js and .py file for "resilien", "transient impact", and "Obizhaeva" (case-insensitive) turns up nothing modeling a decaying price-impact state.

🗂️

The only "resilient" hits are logging code

bot/main.py's ResilientStreamHandler / ResilientFileHandler are retry-safe logging handlers — "resilient" means "doesn't crash," not "how fast the book heals."

🔗

That lesson's own grounding note is also flagged CONCEPT — not built or tested anywhere in this repo either. Neither impact family is implemented here.

Very few desks solve a full Obizhaeva-Wang-style optimization by hand — but the resilience framing informally justifies why real execution algorithms sometimes "wait out" thin depth, or avoid clustering large child orders too close together in time.
Section 8

Where this model is a stylized approximation

📏

Linear impact, single exponential resilience

Each unit adds exactly κ to D_t, and the book heals along one clean exponential curve. Real books plausibly recover along a mix of timescales a single ρ can't capture.

🎯

ρ is genuinely hard to estimate

Backed out from how fast post-trade price reversals happen — noisier and less standardized than a volatility estimate, and it plausibly varies intraday and by regime.

🧭

Not the Kyle-Obizhaeva Invariance framework

Same coauthor, different paper. Nothing here says how ρ or κ should scale across assets or regimes — that's a different lesson's question entirely.

Section 9

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

What are the two rules that define the impact state D_t?

dD_t = −ρD_t·dt between trades, and D_{t+} = D_{t−} + κ·dx at each trade. Continuous decay plus an instant jump is the entire mechanism.
Question 2

How does Obizhaeva-Wang's transient impact differ from Kyle's impact, in terms of decay?

"Transient" vs. "permanent" is precisely the decays-eventually vs. never-decays-at-all distinction. Kyle's repricing is a belief update the market has no reason to walk back; OW's is a mechanical liquidity-supply effect with a genuine half-life.
Question 3

Almgren-Chriss's temporary impact also "reverts" — so what's actually different about Obizhaeva-Wang?

AC's cost ½η·ẋ(t)² has no separate state variable and no resilience parameter — recovery is built in as immediate. OW's D_t can still be partially displacing the book minutes after the trade that caused it, which is impossible by construction under AC.
Question 4

Why are trades at t=0 and t=T structurally cheaper than trades placed in the interior?

Part of an interior trade's price concession decays away, unused, before the order finishes — money spent moving the price that doesn't help execute the rest of the order any more cheaply. Neither boundary pays that penalty.
Question 5

Does resilience remove the classic impact-vs-timing-risk trade-off from Almgren-Chriss?

A fully worked-out OW execution problem still needs a volatility/risk-aversion term layered on top of the impact side. What resilience changes is the shape that impact side wants: continuous and smooth under AC's memoryless assumption, boundary-loaded under this one.
Section 10

What to carry forward

01

Impact here has a genuine half-life: continuous decay at rate ρ between trades, an instant jump of κ·dx at each one — that decay-plus-jump process is the entire mechanism.

02

Three models, one axis: Kyle — permanent, no decay. Almgren-Chriss — memoryless, instant reversion. Obizhaeva-Wang — genuine finite-speed resilience.

03

The optimal schedule is boundary-loaded, not smooth — but the worked example's ~2% edge over even splitting is a real, modest effect, not a dramatic free lunch.

04

More front/back-loading isn't automatically better: pushing boundary blocks to their extreme cost more in the worked example than a moderate split.

05

Linear impact, pure exponential resilience, and a hard-to-estimate, regime-varying ρ are real limitations — a stylized approximation, not a photograph of the book.

What this now lets you do

Recognize why an execution algorithm might deliberately front-load or back-load large child orders instead of spreading them evenly, and why that logic depends entirely on the book having genuine, finite-speed resilience — not on the memoryless assumption Almgren-Chriss makes.
