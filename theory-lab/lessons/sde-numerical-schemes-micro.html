SDEs 2 · Stochastic Differential Equations

Simulating What You Can't Solve

The last lesson proved an SDE has a genuine, unique solution — it never promised you could write that solution down. This lesson is the direct sequel: it builds the two standard ways to simulate an SDE numerically, step by tiny step, on a computer, and states precisely how much error each way leaves behind. It's the same "take a small step, repeat" idea from Euler's method and Newton-Raphson in this curriculum's Numerical Methods lessons — now with a random shock riding along on every step.

Section 1

Almost nothing worth building has a clean formula

Existence & uniqueness says a solution exists. It says nothing about whether you can write it down.

Textbook SDEs — closed form exists
  • Geometric Brownian Motion (GBM) — Itô's Lemma on ln X reduces it to a plain integral
  • Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) — the linear drift integrates out exactly
  • Handful of others chosen specifically because they're solvable
SDEs actually used in practice — no closed form
  • Heston's stochastic-volatility pair
  • Any local-volatility surface
  • Any jump-diffusion model
  • Almost every short-rate model outside plain Vasicek

GBM and OU aren't representative of "SDEs" — they're the tiny, deliberately convenient sliver that happens to be solvable, which is exactly why every textbook leans on them. The moment a model needs state-dependent volatility, jumps, or more than one correlated factor, the closed form disappears and the only way left to get a number out of it is to simulate the path, one small time step at a time.

Why textbooks lean on GBM and OU

They're the cases where the algebra is clean enough to check a numerical scheme's output against something exact — which is also exactly why this deck (and the full lesson) keeps using GBM for its worked examples: not because it's representative, but because it's the one case where "did the simulation get it right" has a checkable answer.

Section 2

Euler's method, with a random gust added

Ordinary Euler's method (from the Numerical Methods lessons) takes a small straight step along the current slope. Euler-Maruyama adds one thing.

stand at X_n
→
step along drift μ·Δt
+
random shock σ·√Δt·Z
=
X_n+1
↗

The deterministic part is plain Euler

X_n + μ(X_n,t_n)Δt is exactly ordinary Euler's method for dX/dt = μ, applied to the drift alone — nothing new here.

🎲

The addition: a fresh Gaussian shock, every step

σ(X_n,t_n)ΔW_n, where ΔW_n ~ N(0,Δt) — generated in practice as √Δt · Z_n with Z_n an independent standard normal draw.

√

Why √Δt, not Δt

A Brownian increment over a step of size Δt is typically of size √Δt, not Δt — the same scaling fact Itô's Lemma is built from. Halving the step doesn't halve the typical shock; it shrinks it by only √2.

This is the entire recipe: X_n+1 = X_n + μ(X_n,t_n)Δt + σ(X_n,t_n)√Δt·Z_n. Stand at the current point, move along the current drift for one small step, then get pushed by a random gust sized by the current diffusion. Repeat N times and you've traced out one simulated path.

Section 7

Milstein: one extra term, aimed at the error Euler-Maruyama drops

A full Itô-Taylor expansion has a next-order correction term Euler-Maruyama simply discards. Milstein keeps it.

Euler-Maruyama's step
+
½σσ′(ΔW² − Δt)
=
Milstein's step
′

σ′ is the diffusion's own derivative

How fast the diffusion coefficient itself changes as the level X changes — ∂σ/∂X, evaluated at the current point.

🎯

It corrects a specific, named source of bias

Euler-Maruyama implicitly assumes σ stays fixed for the whole step. Milstein's extra term accounts for σ itself moving a little as X moves during that same step.

📈

Only matters when σ depends on the state

If σ is constant (doesn't depend on X at all), σ′ = 0 and the correction term vanishes entirely — Milstein and Euler-Maruyama become identical.

For GBM, σ(X) = σX is linear in X, so σ′(X) = σ — a plain constant, trivial to compute. That's exactly why GBM examples make Milstein look easy: the real cost only shows up once σ is a genuinely awkward function of the state, like an arbitrary local-volatility surface.
Section 8

Which one should you reach for?

Neither scheme is strictly "better" — they trade accuracy against implementation cost differently.

Euler-Maruyama
  • Simpler to implement — only needs μ and σ themselves, no derivative
  • Often good enough, especially when σ is constant or a simple function of the state
  • Converges more slowly per step when σ genuinely depends on the state
  • The default choice in almost every Monte Carlo pricing engine
Milstein
  • Converges faster and more accurately per step for state-dependent diffusion
  • Needs σ′ — the diffusion's derivative — computed or estimated at every step
  • The extra correction term is free (zero) when σ is constant, so there's no downside there
  • Reserved in practice for cases where the extra accuracy is worth the extra derivative
Where this needs qualifying

"Milstein is just strictly more accurate" undersells the real cost: in more than one dimension (correlated SDEs, the very next lesson in this series), the full Milstein scheme needs a Lévy area — an extra stochastic integral that is itself expensive to simulate accurately. That's a genuine reason many multi-asset engines stick with Euler-Maruyama even knowing its per-step accuracy is lower.

What the full lesson says, and what a repo check confirms

The full lesson presents Euler-Maruyama and Milstein as worked mathematical techniques and a hand-computable/Monte-Carlo-checkable example — it does not claim either scheme is already implemented anywhere in this repo's production code. A direct search of this codebase (excluding the Theory Lab lesson pages themselves) turns up no Euler-Maruyama or Milstein simulation code anywhere — no continuous-time SDE step discretization exists in this repo today. Nothing here was found to be stale — this lesson introduces a technique genuinely new to the codebase, not one already sitting in it under a different name.

Section 10

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

What does Euler-Maruyama add to ordinary Euler's method for an ODE?

The deterministic part, X_n + μΔt, is exactly ordinary Euler's method. The entire addition is the random shock term, scaled by the diffusion coefficient and a fresh Gaussian draw each step.
Question 2

Why is the random shock scaled by √Δt rather than Δt?

This is the same scaling fact Itô's Lemma is built from — ΔW_n ~ N(0,Δt), generated in practice as √Δt · Z_n with Z_n a standard normal draw.
Question 3

What extra term does the Milstein scheme add over Euler-Maruyama?

The extra term involves σ′, the diffusion coefficient's derivative with respect to the state — it corrects for σ itself changing during the step, which Euler-Maruyama ignores.
Question 4

For GBM, where σ(X) = σX, what does the Milstein correction term reduce to relative to a general local-volatility model?

σ(X) = σX is linear, so its derivative σ′(X) = σ is just a constant — trivial to compute, which is exactly why GBM worked examples make Milstein look cheap even though a general state-dependent diffusion can make σ′ genuinely costly.
Question 5

When does Milstein's extra correction term vanish entirely, making it identical to Euler-Maruyama?

If σ doesn't depend on X, then σ′ = 0, and the whole correction term ½σσ′(ΔW² − Δt) is zero — there's no distinction left between the two schemes in that case.
Section 11

What to carry forward

01

Existence & uniqueness guarantees a solution exists — it says nothing about whether you can write it down. Almost no SDE worth building has a closed form; GBM and OU are the convenient exceptions, not the rule.

02

Euler-Maruyama is Euler's method plus a Gaussian shock each step: X_n+1 = X_n + μΔt + σ√Δt·Z, with a fresh independent normal draw Z at every step.

03

The √Δt scaling on the shock (not Δt) is the same Brownian-increment fact Itô's Lemma is built from — it's why the random part shrinks more slowly than the deterministic part as Δt shrinks.

04

Milstein adds ½σσ′(ΔW²−Δt), correcting for the diffusion coefficient changing during the step — a genuine pathwise-accuracy improvement, but it needs σ′ to exist and be computable.

05

The correction term is free (zero) when σ is constant — Milstein only earns its keep when the diffusion genuinely depends on the state.

06

Neither scheme is strictly better: Euler-Maruyama is simpler and often adequate; Milstein trades a real implementation cost (σ′, and a Lévy area in multiple dimensions) for faster per-step convergence.

What this now lets you do

Simulate any SDE for which you can write down μ and σ — not just the handful with a closed form — by stepping it forward in small increments on a computer. Choose Euler-Maruyama by default and only reach for Milstein's extra σ′ term when the diffusion genuinely depends on the state and the accuracy is worth the added cost. Recognise that shrinking the time step, not adding more simulated paths, is the lever that fixes discretization error specifically — a distinction the next lesson in this series builds on once multiple correlated SDEs enter the picture at once.
