Factor Models 1 of 5

The Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM)

Markowitz proved there's a mathematically best trade-off between risk and return once you know how every asset moves with every other. CAPM asks the next question: if every investor is smart enough to actually hold that best trade-off, what does that force each individual asset's expected return to be? The answer collapses to a single number — beta — and it's the founding idea every other lesson in this new category either extends or was written to fix.

CONCEPT — CAPM's single-market-portfolio pricing model isn't built here, but this repo's own beta_estimator.py runs the identical rolling-OLS beta regression against macro factor proxies for hedging, not pricing ~17 min read Prereqs: Correlation & Linear Regression (beta is exactly that lesson's OLS slope), Markowitz & Black-Litterman (CAPM is what falls out once everyone holds the same efficient portfolio)
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • CAPM says every asset's expected return is pinned down by exactly one number, its beta: \(E[R_i] = R_f + \beta_i(E[R_m]-R_f)\), where \(\beta_i = \mathrm{Cov}(R_i,R_m)/\mathrm{Var}(R_m)\) — the identical OLS slope formula from the Correlation & Linear Regression primer, just with the market's excess return standing in as "\(X\)".
  • It's the equilibrium consequence of Markowitz's mean-variance framework once every investor shares the same expectations: everyone ends up holding some mix of the risk-free asset and one common risky portfolio, and market-clearing forces that common portfolio to be the market portfolio itself.
  • The Capital Market Line (return vs. total risk σ, only efficient portfolios sit on it) and the Security Market Line (return vs. beta alone, every correctly-priced asset sits on it) are frequently confused but answer different questions — this lesson keeps them explicitly separate.
  • CAPM's assumptions — single period, identical investor expectations, no taxes or costs, unlimited borrowing/lending at the risk-free rate — are all known to be false. That's not a fatal flaw; it's exactly what makes CAPM a clean, tractable baseline rather than a finished answer.
  • Decades of empirical work (Fama & French, 1992, is the canonical citation) found beta alone leaves a large chunk of the actual cross-section of stock returns unexplained — small-cap and "value" stocks have historically earned more than their beta predicts. That specific, well-documented failure is exactly what the next lesson in this category, Fama-French & Carhart, was built to address.
Section 01

Why this matters

This curriculum already has a full lesson on Markowitz mean-variance optimization and its Black-Litterman fix, a lesson on PCA reducing a basket of correlated returns down to a handful of statistical factors, and a lesson on Random Matrix Theory separating genuine factor structure from pure noise in a correlation matrix. What it has never stated, until this lesson, is the single model that historically came first and that every one of those later ideas either builds on or reacts against: the Capital Asset Pricing Model, published independently by William Sharpe (1964) and John Lintner (1965), building directly on Harry Markowitz's 1952 portfolio theory.

The gap is worth naming plainly. Markowitz's framework tells you, given a full vector of expected returns and a covariance matrix, how to build the single best portfolio — but it never says where that expected-return vector should come from in the first place. Black-Litterman's answer was "start from what the market's current weights already imply." CAPM asks the more basic version of the same question, one full step further back: if markets are in equilibrium and everyone is a rational mean-variance optimizer holding the same beliefs, what expected return does each individual asset have to offer, in equation form, just to keep supply and demand for every asset in balance? That question — asset pricing, not portfolio construction — is what CAPM answers, and it is the founding, single-factor case of the entire "Factor Models" category this lesson opens: every later lesson here (Fama-French & Carhart, the Fama-MacBeth cross-sectional test, Arbitrage Pricing Theory, Barra-style risk models) is a direct extension of, or a direct empirical rebuttal to, the one-factor model built below.

💡
Plain-English intuition

If everyone holds the same "best" portfolio, prices have to make that consistent

Markowitz's mean-variance math says: given your beliefs about returns and covariances, there is one specific combination of risky assets — call it the tangency portfolio — that, combined with borrowing or lending at the risk-free rate, gives the best possible risk/return trade-off available. A cautious investor holds mostly the risk-free asset and a little of the tangency portfolio; an aggressive investor borrows at the risk-free rate to hold more than 100% of it. Either way, the risky part of everyone's holdings is the exact same portfolio, just scaled up or down — this is called the two-fund separation theorem, and it falls straight out of the shape of the efficient frontier the Markowitz lesson derives.

CAPM's single extra assumption is that every investor shares the same beliefs about expected returns and covariances — so everyone computes the identical tangency portfolio. Now ask: if literally every investor in the market holds some scaled version of the same one risky portfolio, and nothing else, what portfolio must that be? It has to be the portfolio of every risky asset in existence, held in proportion to its market value — because that is the only portfolio whose composition is consistent with all the buying and selling clearing at once. If some stock weren't in that common portfolio at its true market-cap weight, nobody would be holding the shares that are actually outstanding, which can't happen in equilibrium. That common, everyone-holds-it-in-proportion portfolio is called the market portfolio, and CAPM is simply the arithmetic of what each individual asset's expected return has to be for that to be a stable, self-consistent state of the world.

Section 02

The math, step by step

The derivation in full requires more optimization machinery than this lesson re-derives from scratch (it's a constrained-optimization argument over the efficient frontier, the same kind of argument the Markowitz lesson mentions but doesn't spell out either) — but the four logical moves that get you from "everyone optimizes the Markowitz way" to "here is the pricing equation for each asset" are worth stating explicitly, because each one is doing real conceptual work:

1

Identical beliefs → identical tangency portfolio

If every investor optimizes the same mean-variance problem with the same expected-return vector and the same covariance matrix, every investor's Markowitz optimizer spits out the exact same risky-asset tangency portfolio.

2

Two-fund separation

Every investor then holds only a personal mix of that one tangency portfolio and the risk-free asset — more risk-free for the cautious, leveraged tangency exposure for the aggressive. Nobody holds any other combination of risky assets.

3

Market clearing forces that portfolio to BE the market

If every dollar of risky-asset demand in the economy is some scaling of one common portfolio, the only composition consistent with every share of every company actually being held by someone is the market-value-weighted portfolio of all risky assets. The tangency portfolio and the market portfolio become the same object.

4

Reading off each asset's required return

Since the market portfolio is now known to be mean-variance-efficient, a short piece of algebra on the efficient-frontier condition (holding this portfolio can't be improved by nudging any one asset's weight) reduces directly to the pricing equation below — every asset's risk premium is proportional to its own contribution to the market portfolio's variance.

The CAPM equation. The result of that chain of reasoning is one equation, relating any asset \(i\)'s expected return to the risk-free rate, the market's expected return, and one asset-specific sensitivity coefficient:

\[ E[R_i] = R_f + \beta_i\big(E[R_m] - R_f\big) \]
R_fthe risk-free rate — the return you'd earn taking on zero risk
E[R_m] − R_fthe market risk premium — the extra return investors demand to hold risky assets at all
β_iasset i's sensitivity to that market risk — the entire cross-sectional prediction lives here
E[R_i]asset i's required, equilibrium expected return

Read it in plain language: an asset's expected return equals the risk-free rate, plus a piece of the market's own risk premium scaled by how much of that market-wide risk this specific asset is exposed to. An asset with \(\beta_i=0\) (no exposure to market-wide moves at all) should expect to earn exactly the risk-free rate, no more. An asset with \(\beta_i=1\) should expect to earn exactly the market's own return. An asset with \(\beta_i=2\) is twice as exposed to market-wide swings as the market itself, and CAPM says it must be compensated with twice the market risk premium on top of the risk-free rate to be worth holding at all.

Beta, defined — and it's not a new formula. The coefficient doing all the work is:

\[ \beta_i = \frac{\mathrm{Cov}(R_i, R_m)}{\mathrm{Var}(R_m)} \]
Cov(R_i,R_m)how much asset i's returns move together with the market's
Var(R_m)how much the market moves on its own
β_iexactly the Correlation & Linear Regression primer's OLS slope, β̂ = Cov(X,Y)/Var(X)

This is not new machinery bolted onto CAPM — it is literally the ordinary-least-squares slope formula derived in the Correlation & Linear Regression primer, \(\hat\beta = \mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)/\mathrm{Var}(X)\), applied to one specific, economically meaningful choice of "\(X\)": the market's own excess return. Run the regression

\[ R_i - R_f = \alpha_i + \beta_i\,(R_m - R_f) + \epsilon_i \]

— asset \(i\)'s excess return regressed on the market's excess return — and the fitted slope \(\hat\beta_i\) coming out of that regression is exactly the CAPM beta used in the pricing equation above. This is why the earlier primer bothered to say, in its own "how practitioners use this" section, that regressing an asset's returns on a benchmark's returns "is literally the CAPM beta" — that line was written for this lesson to cash in. Nothing about the mechanics changes: same covariance-over-variance ratio, same minimize-squared-residuals derivation, same formula. The only thing CAPM adds on top of the regression is an economic claim about what the resulting slope is for: not just a descriptive best-fit line, but the specific quantity that determines how much expected return an asset is entitled to in equilibrium.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Beta answers one question: when the whole market has a good day or a bad day, how much does this one asset tend to move along with it? A beta of 2 means "twice as jumpy as the market" — up more on good market days, down more on bad ones. A beta of 0 means "doesn't care what the market does at all." CAPM's whole idea is: you should only get paid extra, above the safe risk-free rate, for the jumpiness that's tied to the whole market moving — because that's the risk nobody can diversify away by just holding more stuff.

The Capital Market Line vs. the Security Market Line

These two lines get confused constantly, including by people who've used both for years, because they both plot "expected return" on the vertical axis — but the horizontal axis, and which portfolios are even allowed to appear on the line, are completely different.

Capital Market Line (CML)

Horizontal axis: total risk, \(\sigma_p\) (standard deviation). Only efficient portfolios sit on it — specifically, combinations of the risk-free asset and the market portfolio, and nothing else:

\[ E[R_p] = R_f + \frac{E[R_m]-R_f}{\sigma_m}\,\sigma_p \]

The slope, \((E[R_m]-R_f)/\sigma_m\), is exactly the market portfolio's own Sharpe ratio. An individual mispriced stock, or an inefficiently diversified portfolio, does not belong on this line at all — it will plot somewhere to the right of it, carrying risk it isn't being paid for.

Security Market Line (SML)

Horizontal axis: systematic risk only, \(\beta_i\). Every correctly-priced asset sits on this line — efficient portfolios and single, individually risky, badly diversified stocks alike:

\[ E[R_i] = R_f + \beta_i\big(E[R_m]-R_f\big) \]

This is the CAPM pricing equation itself, drawn as a line: intercept \(R_f\), slope \(E[R_m]-R_f\). A stock's own idiosyncratic, diversifiable risk plays no role in where it sits on this axis at all — only its beta does. An asset sitting off this line is the entire subject of the empirical-failure section later in this lesson.

The clean way to keep them straight: the CML is about the price of total risk, and only rewards the specific portfolios that hold no risk beyond what's compensated (the risk-free asset and the market portfolio, blended). The SML is about the price of one specific slice of risk — the piece correlated with the market — and applies to literally everything, including a single badly-diversified stock that would never itself sit on the CML, because idiosyncratic risk simply doesn't enter the SML's horizontal axis at all.

Section 03

The assumptions — and why stating them plainly matters

CAPM's derivation leans on a short list of assumptions, every one of which is a known, deliberate simplification rather than a claim about how markets actually work:

⏱️

Single period

Investors choose a portfolio once, for one horizon, and hold it — no rebalancing, no multi-period planning, no path-dependence in the decision at all.

🧠

Identical expectations

Every investor agrees, exactly, on every asset's expected return, variance, and covariance with every other asset — the assumption that forces everyone onto the same tangency portfolio in Step 1 above.

💸

No taxes or transaction costs

Trading is free and instantaneous, and every investor faces identical, frictionless prices — nothing discourages continuously rebalancing toward the market portfolio.

🏦

Unlimited risk-free borrowing/lending

Any investor can borrow or lend as much as they want at the single risk-free rate \(R_f\) — the assumption that makes the two-fund separation theorem's straight-line CML possible at all.

Every one of these is known to be false, and that is the entire point. Investors disagree constantly about expected returns; trading has real costs; almost nobody can borrow at the risk-free rate, let alone in unlimited size. CAPM is not offered as a photographically accurate description of markets — it is a deliberately stripped-down baseline, built to be the simplest model that still produces a specific, testable, quantitative prediction (the SML) for every asset's expected return. A model this clean being wrong in specific, measurable ways is not a failure of the exercise; it's what makes the wrongness usable — exactly the empirical-failure section below turns that wrongness into the entire research agenda the rest of this category is built on.
🧮
Worked example

Three assets, one market index, five years of returns

A toy universe: a market index and three assets, five years of (invented, illustrative) annual returns, and a 2% risk-free rate, matching this repo's other worked examples' convention:

Market (%)8   −3   15   6   9
Asset A (%)5   −0.5   8.5   4   5.5
Asset B (%)15   −1.5   25.5   12   16.5
Asset C (%)4.4   −4.4   10   2.8   5.2

Step 1 — the market's own mean and variance. Mean: \((8-3+15+6+9)/5 = 35/5 = 7\%\). Deviations from that mean: \([1,-10,8,-1,2]\). Variance: \((1+100+64+1+4)/5 = 170/5 = 34\).

Step 2 — beta for each asset, via \(\beta=\mathrm{Cov}(R_i,R_m)/\mathrm{Var}(R_m)\):

\[ \beta_A = \frac{17.0}{34} = 0.50 \qquad \beta_B = \frac{51.0}{34} = 1.50 \qquad \beta_C = \frac{27.2}{34} = 0.80 \]

(Showing the arithmetic for A: A's mean is \(4.5\%\), deviations \([0.5,-5,4,-0.5,1]\); pairing with the market's deviations \([1,-10,8,-1,2]\) and averaging the products gives \(\mathrm{Cov}(A,R_m)=(0.5+50+32+0.5+2)/5=85/5=17.0\). B and C follow the identical procedure on their own return columns.)

Step 3 — CAPM's predicted expected return for each, using \(R_f=2\%\) and \(E[R_m]-R_f=7-2=5\) points of market risk premium:

\[ E[R_A] = 2 + 0.50(5) = 4.5\% \qquad E[R_B] = 2 + 1.50(5) = 9.5\% \qquad E[R_C] = 2 + 0.80(5) = 6.0\% \]

Step 4 — compare against each asset's own realized average return (Asset A: \((5-0.5+8.5+4+5.5)/5=4.5\%\); Asset B: \((15-1.5+25.5+12+16.5)/5=13.5\%\); Asset C: \((4.4-4.4+10+2.8+5.2)/5=3.6\%\)):

Asset Apredicted 4.5% · realized 4.5% · alpha = 0.0%
Asset Bpredicted 9.5% · realized 13.5% · alpha = +4.0%
Asset Cpredicted 6.0% · realized 3.6% · alpha = −2.4%

Asset A's realized return matched CAPM's prediction exactly — in this toy universe, its returns were built as a pure linear function of the market, so its beta captures everything about it and its alpha is exactly zero. Assets B and C did not: B earned four points more than its beta alone justified, C earned two-and-a-bit points less. That leftover — the residual after beta has done all the explaining it can — is exactly what gets called alpha in practice, and it's exactly the quantity a multi-factor model (or a genuinely skilled active manager) is trying to explain or capture on purpose, rather than dismiss as noise.

Section 04

The empirical failure this whole category exists to address

CAPM's own theory predicts that alpha — the SML residual computed above for every asset — should be zero, for every asset, always: beta is supposed to be a complete summary of the risk that matters. In real markets, tested against decades of actual returns, that prediction does not hold up. The canonical empirical citation is Fama & French (1992), "The Cross-Section of Expected Stock Returns": sorting real stocks by market beta and comparing average returns across those sorted groups showed a strikingly weak relationship between beta and realized return — nowhere near what CAPM's straight-line SML predicts. What the data showed a much stronger relationship with instead: firm size (small-cap stocks earned systematically higher average returns than large-cap stocks of similar beta) and book-to-market ratio ("value" stocks — a high book value relative to market price — earned systematically higher average returns than "growth" stocks of similar beta).

State this plainly: CAPM beta alone leaves a real, well-documented amount of the cross-section of average stock returns unexplained. This isn't a minor statistical footnote — it's one of the most replicated empirical results in all of asset pricing, and it is exactly the finding that redirected decades of subsequent research toward multi-factor models. The toy worked example above, where Assets B and C carried nonzero alpha even in an invented dataset, is a small-scale illustration of precisely this phenomenon playing out for real, at scale, in actual markets.

This is exactly the setup for the next lesson in this category. If a single factor (market beta) doesn't fully explain average returns, the natural next move is to add more factors that do — factors built specifically around the size and value anomalies Fama and French found. Fama-French & Carhart, next in this series, is the direct empirical response to the exact gap this section just described: it doesn't get derived here, only named as the destination this failure points to.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

CAPM's promise is: tell me one number (beta) and I'll tell you exactly how much extra return you deserve. When people actually checked this promise against real stock returns for decades, beta turned out to be a genuinely useful clue, but not nearly the whole story — smaller companies and "cheap relative to their books" companies kept earning more than their beta said they should, over and over, in sample after sample. That gap between the promise and the reality is exactly why people built bigger models with more clues (more "factors") instead of just the one.

🎯
Real-world trading scenario

CAPM as a cost-of-equity input, not a trading signal

CAPM's single most common real-world use isn't picking stocks to trade at all — it's corporate finance: estimating a company's cost of equity, one input into a weighted-average cost of capital (WACC), which in turn is the discount rate used to value a company's future cash flows or judge whether a capital project clears its hurdle rate. A corporate finance team estimates a company's beta (typically via the same OLS regression of the stock's returns on a broad index's returns, over some lookback window), plugs it into the SML equation with a chosen risk-free rate and market risk premium, and reads off a required return — used as "the return equity investors need, given this company's systematic risk," not as a forecast of what the stock will actually do next quarter.

For FX specifically, this application doesn't translate cleanly at all: unlike an equity market, there is no single, obvious "market portfolio" of every currency held at its market-value weight — no analogue to a global equity index that every FX investor could be assumed to hold a piece of. The same wrinkle the Markowitz & Black-Litterman lesson flags for reverse-engineering an equilibrium return vector in FX applies here just as directly: CAPM's derivation leans on equities' natural market-cap weighting scheme, and currencies simply don't have an equivalent, off-the-shelf answer to "what is the market portfolio." That's a genuine reason CAPM stays a cross-asset-pricing concept in this curriculum rather than a component this repo's own FX trading logic could adopt directly.

Section 05

How practitioners actually use this — and where it goes wrong

Outside of the cost-of-capital use above, CAPM beta shows up constantly as a risk-management and attribution tool even among people who don't believe the SML prices assets correctly: hedge a long equity book's market exposure by shorting an index-tracking instrument sized by the book's aggregate beta; decompose a fund's realized return into a "market-driven" piece (beta times the market's actual return) and an "everything else" piece (alpha), exactly the machinery the Alpha/Beta Separation lesson in this curriculum builds on for performance attribution; screen a universe of candidate stocks quickly by beta as a first-pass proxy for how defensive or aggressive a position would be. None of these uses require believing CAPM prices every asset correctly — beta as a regression-derived sensitivity measure is useful on its own terms, independent of whether the theory built on top of it holds.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where CAPM gets misread or misapplied

Confusing the CML and the SML. This is the single most common conceptual mix-up. Plotting an individual, badly diversified stock against total risk σ and expecting it to sit on the market's efficient-frontier line is simply the wrong chart — that line only ever describes efficient combinations of the risk-free asset and the market portfolio. The SML, plotted against β alone, is the one that makes a pricing claim about every individual asset.

Treating a positive alpha as proof of skill, on sight. A nonzero alpha in a backtest or a live track record is exactly as ambiguous as it was for the toy Asset B above: it could be genuine, persistent mispricing being captured, or it could be compensation for an entirely different, un-modeled risk factor CAPM's one-factor version simply doesn't include (this is precisely the size and value story Fama-French tells about their own "CAPM alpha") — or it could just be noise in a short sample. A single-factor alpha estimate, by itself, settles none of this.

Using a backward-looking beta as if it were a forward-looking constant. Beta, estimated by regression over some historical window, drifts — a company's business mix, leverage, or the composition of "the market" itself can all change. The Kalman Filter lesson's time-varying hedge-ratio machinery is the direct fix for exactly this instability, applied to the identical OLS-slope quantity this lesson builds.

Forgetting the assumptions when reaching for the formula. Every assumption in Section 03 is doing real work in the derivation. Applying the SML to price an asset in a market where investors clearly disagree wildly, or where borrowing at the risk-free rate is simply not available to the investor in question, is applying a model outside the conditions that were used to derive it — a common source of "CAPM doesn't work" complaints that are really "CAPM's assumptions don't hold here" complaints in disguise.

Verified against this repo's actual code. A repo-wide search for CAPM-style "beta" turned up a genuine, working system — bot/modules/beta_estimator.py, portfolio_beta.py, beta_deviation.py, beta_rebalancer.py, and RegimeV2/beta_regime_table.py — that runs the identical rolling-OLS regression this lesson derives, \(y=\alpha+\beta x\), fit via ordinary least squares, and even wraps it in a Kalman filter (the same time-varying-beta idea flagged in the pitfalls above). It is real, working code, but it is not CAPM: instead of one "market portfolio," it regresses each FX pair's returns against three separate macro factor proxies (beta_dxy via inverted EURUSD, beta_rates via USDJPY, beta_vix via inverted USDCHF), and the output feeds a portfolio-level exposure rebalancer, not an expected-return pricing equation. Same regression arithmetic as this lesson's beta; different, multi-factor, hedging-oriented purpose — worth knowing exactly where the line between "this repo's beta" and "CAPM's beta" actually sits, rather than assuming either that nothing like this exists here or that this already is CAPM.

✓CAPM is Markowitz's equilibrium consequence. If every investor optimizes with identical beliefs, everyone holds the same tangency portfolio, and market clearing forces that portfolio to be the market portfolio itself.

⚠CAPM's assumptions — single period, identical expectations, no frictions, unlimited risk-free borrowing — are all known to be false. That's exactly why it's a tractable baseline, not a finished description of markets.

✓Beta is useful independent of whether CAPM's pricing claim holds. Hedging, exposure decomposition, and attribution all lean on the same OLS-derived sensitivity number without needing the SML itself to be exactly right.
Section 07

Self-test

Q1. Write the CAPM equation and state, in one sentence, what each term means.
\(E[R_i] = R_f + \beta_i(E[R_m]-R_f)\). \(R_f\) is the risk-free rate; \(E[R_m]-R_f\) is the market risk premium, the extra return demanded for holding risky assets at all; \(\beta_i\) is asset \(i\)'s sensitivity to market-wide moves, \(\mathrm{Cov}(R_i,R_m)/\mathrm{Var}(R_m)\); and \(E[R_i]\) is the equilibrium expected return the asset must offer to be worth holding given that sensitivity.
Q2. Why is CAPM's beta described in this lesson as "not a new formula" relative to the Correlation & Linear Regression primer?
Because \(\beta_i=\mathrm{Cov}(R_i,R_m)/\mathrm{Var}(R_m)\) is exactly the OLS slope formula \(\hat\beta=\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)/\mathrm{Var}(X)\) derived in that primer, applied to one specific choice of variables: regress asset \(i\)'s excess return (\(Y\)) on the market portfolio's excess return (\(X\)). CAPM adds an economic interpretation on top — that this slope determines the asset's required equilibrium return — but the underlying regression arithmetic is identical, not a separate technique.
Q3. What is the two-fund separation theorem, and what role does it play in getting from Markowitz's framework to CAPM?
Two-fund separation says that every mean-variance investor, regardless of their personal risk tolerance, holds only a personal mix of one common risky "tangency" portfolio and the risk-free asset — cautious investors hold more risk-free and less tangency exposure, aggressive investors lever up their tangency exposure, but nobody holds any other combination of risky assets. If every investor also shares identical expectations (CAPM's key extra assumption), everyone's tangency portfolio is the same portfolio, and market-clearing then forces that one common portfolio to be the market-value-weighted market portfolio — the step that turns Markowitz's individual-optimization framework into CAPM's economy-wide pricing equation.
Q4. A colleague plots an individual, poorly-diversified stock against total risk (σ) and says it should sit on the Capital Market Line. What's wrong with this?
The CML only ever describes efficient portfolios — specifically, combinations of the risk-free asset and the market portfolio, plotted against total risk σ. An individual stock carrying idiosyncratic, diversifiable risk that isn't compensated will generally sit to the right of the CML, not on it, because it's paying for risk (its own idiosyncratic variance) the CML doesn't reward. The line that does make a pricing statement about that individual stock is the Security Market Line, plotted against β alone — every correctly-priced asset, diversified or not, belongs on the SML, but only efficient portfolios belong on the CML.
Q5. In the worked example, Asset B had a beta of 1.5 and a realized average return of 13.5%, against a CAPM-predicted 9.5%. What does that 4.0% gap represent, and why does CAPM's own theory say it should be zero?
That 4.0% gap is Asset B's alpha — the part of its realized average return that beta and the market risk premium alone don't explain. CAPM's theory holds that beta is a complete summary of the priced risk in an asset's returns, so in a world where CAPM held exactly and markets were in the equilibrium the model describes, every asset's expected alpha should be exactly zero — any leftover return is, by the theory's own logic, return the asset shouldn't be earning. A real, persistent nonzero alpha is either genuine mispricing being captured, compensation for a real risk factor the one-factor model simply omits, or noise — which is exactly the ambiguity the empirical failure section and the next lesson in this category exist to resolve.
Q6. What specific empirical finding from Fama & French (1992) motivates the next lesson in this category, and why is it a genuine problem for CAPM rather than a minor footnote?
Sorting real stocks by market beta and comparing their average returns showed a much weaker relationship than CAPM's SML predicts, while firm size (small-cap stocks) and book-to-market ratio ("value" stocks) both showed a much stronger relationship with average returns than beta did. It's a genuine problem, not a footnote, because it's one of the most replicated results in empirical asset pricing: CAPM's core claim is that beta alone should be a complete summary of priced risk, and decades of out-of-sample evidence across markets and time periods consistently show it isn't. That specific, well-documented gap is what Fama-French & Carhart, the next lesson in this category, was built to directly address by adding size and value factors on top of market beta.

Further reading
  • William F. Sharpe, "Capital Asset Prices: A Theory of Market Equilibrium under Conditions of Risk," Journal of Finance, 19(3), 1964 — the original CAPM paper, deriving the Security Market Line from mean-variance equilibrium; Sharpe shared the 1990 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences in part for this work.
  • John Lintner, "The Valuation of Risk Assets and the Selection of Risky Investments in Stock Portfolios and Capital Budgets," Review of Economics and Statistics, 47(1), 1965 — an independent derivation of the same equilibrium pricing result, arrived at from a different angle around the same time as Sharpe's.
  • Eugene F. Fama and Kenneth R. French, "The Cross-Section of Expected Stock Returns," Journal of Finance, 47(2), 1992 — the canonical empirical paper showing market beta alone poorly explains the cross-section of average stock returns, and that size and book-to-market do much better; the direct motivation for the next lesson in this category.
  • Harry Markowitz, "Portfolio Selection," Journal of Finance, 7(1), 1952 — the mean-variance framework CAPM is built on top of; see this curriculum's own Markowitz & Black-Litterman lesson for the full derivation of the efficient frontier and tangency portfolio CAPM assumes.
