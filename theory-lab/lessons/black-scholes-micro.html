Lesson 11 · Options & Derivatives

Black-Scholes, Priced Without Guessing Direction

An option's price sounds like it should depend on where you think the underlying is headed. Black-Scholes shows it doesn't — a stock-and-cash portfolio can be built to copy the option's payoff exactly, and two things with identical payoffs must cost the same. That single replication trick is where the formula, and the Greeks built on top of it, all come from.

Section 1

What you're actually pricing

A call gives the right to buy at strike K; a put gives the right to sell at K. Both only pay off in one direction.

Try it
Below K, worthless — nobody exercises the right to buy above market price. Above K, the payoff climbs one-for-one with the underlying, uncapped.
Above K, worthless — nobody exercises the right to sell below market price. Below K, the payoff climbs as the underlying falls, capped at K (the underlying can't go below zero).

Both shapes are simple to draw at expiry. The hard question — the one Black-Scholes answers — is what that lopsided, capped-on-one-side payoff is worth today, with time still left for the underlying to move.

Section 2

The trick: clone the option out of stock and cash

Imagine you sold the call above. That looks like open-ended risk — if the stock rockets, you owe the buyer the difference. Black-Scholes' insight: you don't have to forecast where the stock goes. You can build your own copy of the option's payoff using nothing but the stock itself and a risk-free bond, continuously adjusted as price moves.

Hold more stock (Δ closer to 1) when the option is deep in-the-money, behaving almost like owning the stock outright. Hold less (Δ closer to 0) when it's deep out-of-the-money, behaving like it's worthless. Rebalance that mix constantly and correctly, and at expiry your homemade stock-plus-cash portfolio matches the option's payoff in every possible future.

Two things that pay off identically in every scenario must cost the same today — otherwise anyone could buy the cheap one, sell the expensive one, and pocket free money. That "replicating portfolio" argument is what pins down a single fair price, and building it only ever required knowing how volatile the stock is — never which direction it's expected to go.
The single most important fact

Work through the hedging math and the underlying's expected return, μ, cancels out completely. It never survives to the pricing formula. Your opinion about whether EUR/USD is going up or down next year has, formally, zero effect on the fair price of an option on it — only how much it moves around matters.

Section 3

What the argument assumes about the world

The replication trick only works cleanly if the underlying behaves in a specific, simplified way.

📈

Prices are lognormal

The underlying follows geometric Brownian motion — a steady drift plus random wobbles sized as a percentage of the current price. Prices can never go negative, and returns compound multiplicatively.

📏

Volatility is constant

σ is assumed flat — the same today, next month, and at every strike. This is the model's single biggest simplification, and it's false in every real options market.

⚡

No jumps

Prices move continuously, never gapping. Real markets gap on earnings, central bank surprises, and flash crashes — exactly what the next lesson's jump-diffusion model adds back in.

🔄

Frictionless, continuous rebalancing

The hedge can be adjusted infinitely often, instantly, with no transaction costs and no position limits. Real dealers rehedge in discrete steps and pay spread on every trade.

Where this needs qualifying

None of these four assumptions survive contact with a real market untouched. That's not a reason to discard the model — it's the reason practitioners quote a different σ per strike (the "volatility smile"), and the reason a whole family of extensions exists to relax exactly these assumptions one at a time.

Section 5

What N(d₁) and N(d₂) actually mean

Two lookups into the same bell curve, playing two related but distinct roles.

N(d₂) — a probability

The odds, under a specific pricing convention, that the option finishes in the money — that S ends up above K at expiry. It weights "what you'd expect to pay" by how likely you are to actually pay it.

N(d₁) — a value weighting

A closely related quantity that weights "what you'd expect to receive," conditional on finishing in the money. It also happens to equal the option's delta — met again shortly.

Read the whole formula as one sentence: the call's value is what you'd expect to receive if it finishes in the money, minus what you'd expect to pay to exercise it, each probability-weighted and discounted back to today.
Where this needs qualifying

N(d₂) is not the market's real-world belief about the odds of finishing in the money. It's computed under a "risk-neutral" convention that prices everything as if it drifted at the risk-free rate r rather than its true expected return — a substitution that falls directly out of the replication argument, not out of anyone's actual forecast. Real-world odds differ, usually higher for a risky asset, since investors demand a risk premium above r.

Section 9

The Greeks are just this formula's sensitivities

A "Greek" is a partial derivative of the price with respect to one input — how much the price moves, everything else held fixed.

Δ

Delta = N(d₁)

Price change per $1 move in the underlying. It's exactly the hedge ratio from the replication argument — a dealer who sold a call buys Δ shares to stay hedged.

Γ

Gamma = ∂Δ/∂S

How fast delta itself changes. Highest for at-the-money options close to expiry — meaning the hedge goes stale fastest exactly when time is shortest.

Θ

Theta = ∂C/∂t

Time decay — value lost simply because a day passed. A long-options trader bleeds theta; a short-options trader collects it, in exchange for carrying gamma risk.

ν

Vega = ∂C/∂σ

Sensitivity to volatility itself. Since σ is the one input nobody observes directly, vega exposure is a bet on volatility rising or falling, independent of direction.

ρ

Rho = ∂C/∂r

Sensitivity to the rate. Usually minor for short-dated equity options, but a first-order input for FX options, where "r" is really a rate differential between two currencies.

A dealer isn't exposed to "the option price" in the abstract — they're exposed to how it moves as the world moves. The Greeks are the precise, formula-derived answer to that.
Section 10

Implied volatility: the formula, run backwards

Every input to Black-Scholes is observable except one: σ. Nobody can look up "the volatility" the way they look up spot, strike, or rate — so the market does the opposite of pricing. It takes an option's actual traded price and asks: what σ, fed into this formula, would have produced that price?

This is an inverse function, in exactly the sense of "running a formula backwards" — the same move as solving a formula for a different variable than the one it was written to output. Implied volatility isn't measured from history; it's recovered, by inverting the pricing formula against an observed market price.

Because the model assumes one flat σ but the market quotes a different implied σ for every strike and expiry — the volatility smile/skew — that pattern is itself visible evidence that the constant-volatility assumption from Section 3 is wrong. The market isn't pretending the assumption holds; it's quietly patching around it, one strike at a time.

Section 11

Where the model breaks

The assumptions from Section 3, checked against what markets actually do.

Model assumption
  • Volatility is one flat number, σ
  • Prices move continuously, no jumps
  • Hedging is continuous and free of cost
  • Options are European — exercise only at expiry
Market reality
  • Implied vol varies by strike and expiry — the smile/skew
  • Earnings, central-bank surprises, and flash crashes gap prices
  • Dealers rehedge in discrete steps and pay spread each time
  • Most retail equity/index options are American — exercisable early

The gap between idealized continuous hedging and real discrete rehedging isn't a footnote — it's a source of visible market structure. A dealer who is short gamma must buy as price rises and sell as it falls just to stay hedged, mechanically, regardless of their own view; that flow is exactly what makes high-open-interest strikes behave like magnets and walls near expiry.

Why the model still matters despite all this

None of these gaps make Black-Scholes wrong to learn — they make it the baseline every more sophisticated model (local vol, jump-diffusion, stochastic vol) is built as a correction to, not a replacement of its underlying logic. The next lesson's jump-diffusion extension exists precisely to add the missing jumps back in.

Section 12

How this actually gets used on a desk

🏷️

Quoting convention

Market makers quote and think in implied volatility, not dollar premiums, because it's comparable across strikes, expiries, and underlyings.

⚙️

Hedging engine

The Greeks tell a market maker exactly how much underlying to buy or sell to stay delta-neutral, and how fast that hedge will need revisiting.

📚

Common language

Every more sophisticated model is built as a correction to Black-Scholes' logic, which is why its vocabulary — delta, gamma, vega — never goes away.

This repo's own theory-lab lessons on open-interest walls & max pain and gamma exposure & dealer hedging build directly on the delta and gamma introduced here — they aren't independent trading folklore, they're literally this formula's first and second derivatives with respect to price. And the stress-testing tooling elsewhere in this repo asks whether a portfolio's claimed diversification survives a liquidity contraction — a question that, for any book holding options, is inseparable from how those options' hedge ratios shift as markets move.

Section 13

Quick check

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Why doesn't μ, the underlying's expected return, appear anywhere in the Black-Scholes formula?

The hedged replicating portfolio's random shocks cancel instant by instant, leaving a riskless position that must earn exactly the risk-free rate r — μ never enters that condition.
Question 2

What does N(d₂) represent in the call formula?

It's a probability computed as if every asset drifted at the risk-free rate — not a forecast of what will actually happen. N(d₁), not N(d₂), equals delta.
Question 3

S=K=100, r=2%, σ=20%, T=1 year — what does Black-Scholes price the call at, roughly?

All $8.92 is time value — intrinsic value is zero at S=K, but a year of 20% volatility still makes the asymmetric payoff worth something today.
Question 4

The "volatility smile" — implied vol differing by strike — is evidence against which model assumption?

Plain Black-Scholes assumes one flat σ. The market quoting a different implied σ per strike is direct, visible evidence that assumption doesn't hold.
Question 5

Delta tells you the hedge you need right now. What does gamma tell you?

Gamma is delta's own sensitivity to price — how urgently and how often the hedge needs revisiting. It's highest for at-the-money options close to expiry.
Section 14

What to carry forward

01

Replication, not forecasting — a continuously rebalanced stock-and-bond portfolio that clones the option's payoff pins down its price using no-arbitrage logic alone.

02

Only volatility σ enters the price — the underlying's expected return μ cancels out of the replication argument entirely.

03

The Greeks are this formula's own partial derivatives — delta is the hedge ratio, gamma is how fast that hedge goes stale, theta is time decay, vega is sensitivity to σ.

04

N(d₂) is a risk-neutral probability, not a real-world one — don't read a Black-Scholes price as a directional forecast.

05

Constant volatility is the model's biggest simplification — the smile/skew is the market visibly correcting for it, and it's exactly what the next lesson's jump-diffusion extension formalizes.

What this now lets you do

Price a vanilla European call from scratch given only S, K, r, σ, and T. Read "gamma wall" or "dealer hedging" language and know it's literally the second derivative of this formula with respect to price. Tell a risk-neutral probability apart from a real-world one whenever someone reads a Black-Scholes number as "what the market thinks will happen."
