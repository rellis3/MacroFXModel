Sharpe, Sortino, Calmar & the Deflated Sharpe Ratio

Three ways to divide return by risk, built from scratch — then the formal answer to a question every backtest eventually has to face: how much of your best reported Sharpe ratio is just the residue of trying many variations?

CONCEPT — extends the performance statistics used throughout this project and the Multiple Testing lesson ~17 min read Prereqs: Multiple Testing & the Look-Elsewhere Effect, Descriptive Statistics & the Normal Distribution
TL;DR
  • Sharpe = mean excess return ÷ its std. dev., annualized by \(\sqrt{k}\) (not \(k\)) because variance, not std. dev., is what adds linearly across independent periods — this is exactly how this project's own Sharpe calculation works.
  • Sortino swaps in downside deviation (std. dev. computed only over below-MAR periods) so upside wobble stops counting against a strategy — matches this project's own Sortino calculation's exact convention (divide by the downside count \(n_d\), not total \(n\)).
  • Calmar = CAGR ÷ worst peak-to-trough drawdown, a single realized event rather than an average dispersion statistic — this project's own Calmar calculation takes both as caller-supplied inputs rather than assuming one definition.
  • The Deflated Sharpe Ratio corrects a reported Sharpe for how many variations were tried before it was picked: in the lesson's worked example (\(N=50\) trials, \(\sigma_{SR}=0.66\)), pure luck alone is expected to produce a best-of-50 Sharpe of \(SR_0\approx1.50\), so a reported 1.80 only clears that bar by 0.30 — working out to \(DSR\approx0.81\), not the near-100% a naive zero-benchmark test would claim.
  • This project's own performance-metrics module already ships the Sharpe-honesty pair (a Sharpe standard-error function and a minimum-track-record-length function) that DSR's non-normality correction reuses, but nothing today tracks the trial count \(N\) behind a reported Sharpe — DSR itself isn't built yet.
Section 01

Why this matters

Every backtest in this repo ends the same way: a single number that's supposed to summarize "was this good." Almost always that number is a Sharpe ratio, sometimes a Sortino or a Calmar. Those three ratios are the subject of the first half of this lesson, built up from the same return/std-dev arithmetic the Math Primer already covers — nothing exotic, just three different, precise answers to "risk-adjusted how much."

The second half of the lesson is the more important one for how this repo actually works. The Lego Principle's validation rule (this project's own house rule, principle 5) already says a strategy "wins" only if it beats the incumbent on OOS Sharpe with a non-trivial trade count — but it says nothing yet about what happens to that Sharpe number when it was selected as the best of many variations tried along the way. The Multiple Testing lesson in this series showed that testing 70 pair/regime slices and reporting the "significant" survivors overstates how special they are, purely from the number of chances taken. The exact same effect happens to a Sharpe ratio, and it is arguably the more dangerous version of the two, because a Sharpe ratio looks like a single, self-contained fact about a strategy — it doesn't visibly carry a record of how many parameter sweeps, lookback windows, or entry-rule variants were tried to produce it. The Deflated Sharpe Ratio (Bailey & López de Prado, 2014) is the formal tool built specifically to put that hidden number back into the calculation.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Three ratios, three different denominators — plus one correction that changes what "good" means

The Sharpe ratio asks the simplest question: for every unit of total up-and-down wobble a strategy produces, how much excess return did you actually get? It treats a big up-swing and a big down-swing as equally "risky," because both widen the standard deviation in the denominator the same way. The Sortino ratio objects to that: nobody complains about upside surprises, so why should upside variance count against a strategy the same way downside variance does? Sortino answers the exact same question as Sharpe but only counts the bad half of the wobble. The Calmar ratio throws out variance-based risk entirely and asks a completely different, more visceral question instead: forget the average day-to-day noise — how bad did the single worst losing streak actually get, peak to trough, and how does the strategy's return compare to that one specific number?

All three ratios share a blind spot, though: none of them know how they were produced. A Sharpe of 1.8 looks identical whether it's the only thing you ever tried, or the single winner you kept after testing 50 variations and discarding the other 49. Those are not the same claim. If you flip 50 coins, one of them will land heads 8 times in a row just from volume — that coin isn't secretly a better coin, and the strategy variation with the best backtested Sharpe out of 50 isn't automatically the one with the most real edge. The Deflated Sharpe Ratio is what happens when you ask, honestly: given how many variations were actually tried, how good would the best one look purely by luck, with zero real skill anywhere in the search — and does the reported Sharpe clear that bar, not the far lower bar of "is it above zero."

Section 02

The math, step by step

Sharpe ratio. For a series of per-period returns \(R_1,\ldots,R_T\) with risk-free rate \(r_f\), define the per-period excess return \(X_i = R_i - r_f\). The Sharpe ratio is the mean excess return divided by its standard deviation:

\[ SR = \frac{\bar{X}}{\sigma_X} = \frac{\text{mean per-period excess return}}{\text{std. dev. of per-period excess return}} \]

σₓstd. dev. of per-period excess return — the risk denominator

This is a per-period number — computed on whatever bar the returns are sampled at (daily, weekly, per-trade). To compare strategies sampled at different frequencies on a common footing, it's annualized by multiplying by the square root of the number of periods per year, \(k\):

\[ SR_{annual} = SR \times \sqrt{k} \]

Why \(\sqrt{k}\), specifically. This isn't an arbitrary convention — it's the exact same square-root-of-time scaling this series' Returns & Compounding primer derives for volatility, applied here to a ratio instead of to \(\sigma\) alone. For a return series that's roughly independent period to period (no strong autocorrelation), summing \(k\) of them to get an annual figure sums their means linearly but sums their variances linearly too — and variance, not standard deviation, is the additive quantity for independent draws:

\[ \mu_{annual} \approx k\,\bar{X}, \qquad \sigma^2_{annual} \approx k\,\sigma_X^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \sigma_{annual} \approx \sqrt{k}\,\sigma_X \]

Divide the annualized mean by the annualized standard deviation and the \(k\)'s don't cancel evenly — the mean scales by a full factor of \(k\) while the standard deviation scales by only \(\sqrt{k}\), leaving a net \(\sqrt{k}\) behind:

\[ SR_{annual} = \frac{k\bar{X}}{\sqrt{k}\,\sigma_X} = \sqrt{k}\cdot\frac{\bar{X}}{\sigma_X} = \sqrt{k}\cdot SR \]

This is precisely why "annualize a Sharpe ratio by \(\sqrt{k}\)" is not a rule of thumb — it falls straight out of the same "variance adds, standard deviation doesn't" fact that makes daily volatility scale to a 20-day horizon by \(\sqrt{20}\) rather than by 20, which is the exact scaling this project's own vol math (its horizon-scaled \(\sigma\)) depends on throughout. It also carries the same caveat forward: the derivation assumes the per-period returns are close to independent. A strategy whose returns are meaningfully autocorrelated (trending equity curves, overlapping holding periods) violates that assumption, and naive \(\sqrt{k}\) annualization then overstates the true annualized Sharpe — a point Lo (2002) makes formally and one worth remembering any time an annualized Sharpe looks unusually clean.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

The Sharpe ratio is basically "how much extra return did you earn for every unit of bumpiness along the way." Two strategies can make the same amount of money, but the one with a smoother ride — fewer scary swings — gets rewarded with a higher Sharpe, because it delivered that return with less white-knuckle turbulence. The oddly specific square-root-of-time scaling is just the honest way to convert a daily bumpiness number into a fair yearly comparison — it isn't an arbitrary rule, it falls out of how randomness actually stacks up over many days.

Sortino ratio. Sharpe's standard deviation \(\sigma_X\) treats a period that beats expectations and a period that misses it as equally "risky," because both contribute to variance the same way — a squared deviation doesn't know which side of the mean it came from. For a strategy with real upside/downside asymmetry (a trend-follower with small frequent losses and rare large wins, say), that's a real distortion: Sharpe effectively penalizes the strategy for having good months, because good months widen \(\sigma_X\) exactly as much as bad ones do. The Sortino ratio (Sortino & van der Meer, 1991; Sortino & Price, 1994) fixes this by replacing full standard deviation with downside deviation — a standard deviation computed only over the periods that fell short of some minimum acceptable return (MAR), typically zero:

\[ DD = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n_d}\sum_{i:\,X_i < MAR} (X_i - MAR)^2}, \qquad Sortino = \frac{\bar{X} - MAR}{DD}\times\sqrt{k} \]
DDdownside deviation — std. dev. computed only over below-MAR periods
MARminimum acceptable return threshold — zero in this repo
n_dcount of periods that fell below the MAR — the divisor

where \(n_d\) is the count of periods that fell below the MAR. This matches this project's own Sortino calculation exactly — it accumulates squared deviations only over \(r < 0\) periods, divides by the count of those periods, and annualizes by the same \(\sqrt{k}\) as Sharpe (some textbook treatments divide by the total period count \(n\) instead of just the downside count \(n_d\); this project's implementation follows the semi-deviation-of-losses convention above, which is one standard variant among a few in the literature). The intuitive payoff: a strategy whose variance is disproportionately made of favorable big wins gets a Sortino higher than its Sharpe, because the wins that inflate Sharpe's denominator don't touch Sortino's at all — Sortino is answering "how risky is this, counting only the part of the wobble an investor would actually call bad."

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine grading a rollercoaster ride by counting every twist and turn as equally scary, even the fun upward loops. Sharpe does that — a big surprise win counts against a strategy exactly like a big surprise loss does, just because both are "surprises." Sortino only counts the drops that actually made your stomach lurch — the downside moves — and lets the upside surprises off the hook entirely, which is a fairer way to score a strategy that mostly surprises you pleasantly.

Calmar ratio. Sharpe and Sortino both use a dispersion statistic — an average measure of how spread out returns are — in the denominator. The Calmar ratio (Young, 1991) throws that idea out entirely and uses something that isn't a dispersion measure at all: the single worst realized peak-to-trough decline in the equity curve.

\[ Calmar = \frac{CAGR}{|MaxDD|} \]
CAGRcompound annual growth rate
MaxDDworst realized peak-to-trough equity decline, one specific event

matching this project's own Calmar calculation, which takes both quantities from the caller rather than assuming a single definition of "annual return" (definitions vary too much across engines to bake one in silently — the same reasoning documented in that module's own header comment about why Sharpe used to mean different things in different backtest cards). \(MaxDD\) is not an average of anything; it's a single specific realized event — the worst drop the equity curve actually experienced, start to finish, in the exact historical path that occurred. That's a fundamentally different kind of risk statistic from \(\sigma\), and it's why Calmar can disagree sharply with Sharpe.

When Calmar tells you something Sharpe doesn't. Picture two strategies with the same annualized return. Strategy A wobbles moderately almost every day but has never had a genuinely deep drawdown — its worst peak-to-trough loss is a shallow 6%. Strategy B is quieter on most ordinary days (a lower day-to-day \(\sigma\), so a higher Sharpe) but is exposed to occasional violent moves — a short-volatility or carry-style profile that mostly looks calm until it doesn't — producing a genuinely brutal 45% drawdown once in the sample. Sharpe, built from average dispersion, rewards Strategy B for its quiet typical day and barely notices the one catastrophic stretch buried in the tail of the same distribution it's averaging over. Calmar, built from the single worst realized outcome, flips the ranking completely: Strategy A's Calmar is high, Strategy B's is low, because Calmar is specifically measuring the thing an actual trader or investor experiences as "the worst it got," not "how noisy was a typical day." A strategy with a modest Sharpe but a genuinely small max drawdown is often more usable in practice — easier to keep trading through, less likely to trigger a redemption or a loss of nerve — than a high-Sharpe strategy that is occasionally brutal, even when their average risk-adjusted return numbers look similar on paper.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Sharpe asks "how bumpy is a typical day." Calmar asks a completely different question: "what was the single worst stretch this thing ever put you through, and how does your yearly gain compare to that?" A strategy can feel calm almost every day and still have one nightmare month buried in the data — Sharpe barely notices that one bad month because it's averaging over hundreds of calm ones, but Calmar is built entirely around that one worst stretch, because that's the part an actual investor remembers and has to survive.

The Deflated Sharpe Ratio — the problem it solves. This is the direct Sharpe-ratio analogue of the Multiple Testing lesson's central point, and this project's own house rule already names the general version of it: "count the cells and state the chance-baseline (multiple testing): finding a few 'winners' among 70 slices is what noise does. Survivors must beat chance and be IS-consistent." That rule was written for p-values across slices. A Sharpe ratio search has exactly the same structure — a researcher backtests \(N\) variations of a signal (different lookbacks, thresholds, filters), gets \(N\) different Sharpe ratios back, and reports only the best one. The best-of-\(N\) Sharpe is inflated by the search itself, in exactly the way the best-of-70 p-value is inflated by testing 70 slices — more trials means more chances for one to look good purely from the noise in a finite backtest sample, with zero real edge required anywhere in the search.

The Deflated Sharpe Ratio (Bailey & López de Prado, 2014) is the formal correction. It takes as inputs: the number of trials \(N\) actually run, the variance of the Sharpe ratios observed across those \(N\) trials (\(\sigma_{SR}^2\) — how spread out the search's own results were), the reported track record's length \(T\), and that same track record's skewness \(\gamma_3\) and kurtosis \(\gamma_4\) — the same fat-tail diagnostics this project's own skewness and excess-kurtosis functions already compute, and the same ones already feeding its minimum-track-record-length function's "fat-tail-adjusted" figure per this project's own house rule. It produces two outputs: first, a corrected benchmark \(SR_0\) — the Sharpe ratio you'd expect the best of \(N\) purely-lucky, zero-skill trials to show, which becomes the bar the reported Sharpe has to clear instead of the naive bar of zero; second, the DSR itself — a probability between 0 and 1 that the true, population Sharpe ratio of the selected strategy is actually above zero, after both corrections.

The trap: a Sharpe ratio carries no visible record of how many variations were tried to produce it. A 1.8 that's the only thing ever backtested and a 1.8 that's the surviving best-of-50 look identical on the page — but they are not the same claim, and only DSR tells them apart.
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

If you buy 50 lottery scratch tickets and one of them wins a decent prize, that winning ticket isn't magic — with 50 tries, someone was going to win something, purely from playing enough times. A backtested Sharpe ratio works the same way: if you test 50 variations of a strategy, the best one is going to look good even if none of the 50 have any real edge, purely because you gave luck 50 chances to show up somewhere. The Deflated Sharpe Ratio asks: how good would the best of 50 lucky-but-worthless tries look, on average — and does your actual best result beat even that inflated bar?

How \(SR_0\), the chance-expected ceiling, is built. This step is a direct application of extreme value theory (the subject of this series' EVT lesson) to a different quantity — instead of the distribution of an extreme return, it is the distribution of the maximum of \(N\) approximately Gaussian numbers (the \(N\) trial-level Sharpe ratios). A classical EVT result for the expected maximum of \(N\) i.i.d. standard normal draws gives Bailey & López de Prado's approximation:

\[ SR_0 \;\approx\; \sigma_{SR}\Big[(1-\gamma_E)\,\Phi^{-1}\!\Big(1-\tfrac{1}{N}\Big) \;+\; \gamma_E\,\Phi^{-1}\!\Big(1-\tfrac{1}{Ne}\Big)\Big] \]
Nnumber of variations tried before reporting the best one
σ_SRspread of the N trials' own Sharpe ratios
γ_EEuler-Mascheroni constant, ≈0.5772
SR₀the chance-expected ceiling — what luck alone should produce

Both terms increase with \(N\) (more trials, more room for an extreme draw) and the whole expression is scaled by \(\sigma_{SR}\) (more dispersion across the \(N\) trials' own results, more room for one of them to land far above the pack purely by chance). This is exactly the "count the cells" instinct from the Multiple Testing lesson, turned into an explicit number: more variations tried, and more spread in what those variations returned, both raise the bar the eventual "best" one has to clear before it means anything.

How the final probability is built. With \(SR_0\) as the corrected benchmark instead of zero, the DSR asks how many standard errors the observed Sharpe \(\widehat{SR}\) sits above \(SR_0\) — using a standard error that itself widens for a track record with negative skew or fat tails, rather than assuming Gaussian returns:

\[ DSR(\widehat{SR}) \;=\; \Phi\!\left(\frac{(\widehat{SR}-SR_0)\sqrt{T-1}}{\sqrt{1-\gamma_3\widehat{SR}+\frac{\gamma_4-1}{4}\widehat{SR}^2}}\right) \]
ŜR − SR₀the gap the reported Sharpe clears above the chance ceiling
Tnumber of return observations in the track record
γ₃, γ₄skew and kurtosis — widen the standard error for fat tails
DSRprobability the true Sharpe exceeds zero, after both corrections

The denominator inside that square root is not a new idea introduced just for DSR — it is the exact same skew/kurtosis-adjusted variance-inflation term already sitting inside this project's own minimum-track-record-length function (its adjustment variable: 1 - skew*sr + ((kurt-1)/4)*sr*sr), which exists there for the identical reason: a Sharpe estimate's own standard error is not the simple Gaussian one once the underlying returns are skewed or fat-tailed, and FX/macro pnl reliably is both. DSR and this project's own minimum-track-record-length function are, in effect, the same non-normality correction applied to two related questions — "how many years of live data would it take to trust this Sharpe" versus "given the search that produced it, what's the probability this Sharpe is real right now" — and both trace back to the same Bailey & López de Prado research program.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Once you know the "luck ceiling" from testing 50 variations, DSR asks the final honest question: how far above that ceiling does your actual best result sit, and how confident can you be in that gap given how much data you actually have? It turns the whole thing into one number between 0% and 100% — not "is this Sharpe good," but "given everything — the search, the sample size, even how lopsided the returns were — what's the real probability this strategy has genuine edge at all."

Worked example

Computing all three ratios from one hypothetical daily return series

Suppose a backtest produces daily excess returns with mean \(\bar{X}=0.0004\) (4 basis points/day) and standard deviation \(\sigma_X = 0.006\) (60bp/day), over \(k=252\) trading days/year. The per-period, then annualized, Sharpe ratio:

\[ SR = \frac{0.0004}{0.006} = 0.0667, \qquad SR_{annual} = 0.0667\times\sqrt{252} = 0.0667\times15.87 \approx 1.06 \]

Now suppose the days that lost money did so in a fairly tight, orderly way — losses were small and consistent rather than occasional violent gap-downs — giving a downside deviation of \(DD = 0.0045\), smaller than the full \(\sigma_X = 0.006\) because most of that total dispersion actually comes from the size of the wins, not the losses:

\[ Sortino_{annual} = \frac{0.0004}{0.0045}\times\sqrt{252} = 0.0889\times15.87 \approx 1.41 \]

Sortino (1.41) comes out meaningfully above Sharpe (1.06) here — exactly the pattern this lesson's math predicts for a strategy whose variance is disproportionately made of favorable upside moves rather than symmetric noise: Sortino simply isn't charging the strategy for the part of its wobble that nobody experiences as risk. Finally, suppose this same return stream compounds to a 9.5% annualized return (CAGR) but experienced one stretch where the equity curve fell 12% peak to trough before recovering:

\[ Calmar = \frac{9.5\%}{12\%} \approx 0.79 \]

Three numbers, three different stories about the same underlying strategy: a solid but unremarkable Sharpe, a noticeably better Sortino (because the variance that matters is asymmetric here), and a Calmar below 1 (because the worst realized drawdown was a meaningful fraction of the annual return itself — a strategy where a bad stretch can wipe out more than a year of gains, worth knowing even with a respectable Sharpe sitting right next to it).

Real-world scenario

50 variations of a mean-reversion signal, one reported Sharpe of 1.8 — how impressive is it really?

A researcher builds a mean-reversion signal and, in the course of development, backtests 50 variations of it — different lookback windows, different z-score entry thresholds, a couple of different exit rules. The best of the 50 posts an annualized Sharpe ratio of 1.8 on 60 closed trades, and that's the number that goes in the writeup: "the strategy achieves a Sharpe of 1.8." This is exactly the scenario this project's own house rule is written to catch: "finding a few 'winners' among 70 slices is what noise does. Survivors must beat chance and be IS-consistent." Fifty parameter variations is the same shape of problem as seventy regime slices — a search, with a single reported survivor.

Applying the DSR machinery above, with the trial dispersion, track length, and non-normality inputs a real sweep like this would plausibly show — \(N=50\) trials with a standard deviation of \(\sigma_{SR}=0.66\) across their Sharpe ratios, \(T=60\) trades, and a return distribution with skew \(\gamma_3=-0.4\) and kurtosis \(\gamma_4=7\) (moderately fat-tailed and left-skewed, unremarkable for a stop-driven FX mean-reversion book that occasionally gets caught in a breakout):

\[ SR_0 \approx 0.66\big[0.4228\times\Phi^{-1}(0.98) + 0.5772\times\Phi^{-1}(0.9926)\big] \approx 0.66\times2.28 \approx 1.50 \]

That's the expected-by-chance ceiling: with 50 variations tried at this dispersion, pure luck with zero real edge anywhere in the search is expected to produce a best-of-50 Sharpe around 1.50. The reported 1.80 clears that ceiling by only 0.30 — not by the full 1.80 it appears to clear if you compare it to zero. Running that gap through the full DSR formula, with the track record's own skew and kurtosis widening the standard error:

\[ DSR \approx \Phi\!\left(\frac{(1.80-1.50)\sqrt{59}}{\sqrt{1-(-0.4)(1.8)+\frac{7-1}{4}(1.8)^2}}\right) = \Phi\!\left(\frac{0.30\times7.68}{2.57}\right) \approx \Phi(0.89) \approx 0.81 \]

An 81% probability that the true Sharpe ratio is above zero — elevated, genuinely more than a coin flip, but a long way from the near-certainty a raw 1.80 suggests when judged in isolation. Contrast that with what a naive test would say if it ignored the 50 trials entirely and simply asked "is 1.8 significantly above zero": the same track record's own standard error gives a z-score of \(1.8\sqrt{59}/2.57\approx5.4\), a probability effectively indistinguishable from 100%. That gap — near certainty on a naive read, 81% once the search itself is priced in — is the multiple-testing correction, made concrete for a single Sharpe ratio instead of a table of p-values. And 81% is still not the end of the story: it corrects for the search, it does not replace this repo's own OOS discipline (Lego Principle 5) — a signal still needs to clear a true out-of-sample split with a non-trivial trade count on data the search never touched at all, on top of clearing its own DSR.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

Sharpe, Sortino and Calmar are reported side by side on essentially every institutional tearsheet, precisely because they answer genuinely different questions and none of them subsumes the others: Sharpe for a general risk-adjusted-return comparison, Sortino when a strategy's return profile is known or suspected to be asymmetric (options-selling, trend-following, anything with a convex or concave payoff shape), and Calmar specifically when the audience — an allocator, a risk committee, a trader deciding whether they can personally stomach the strategy — cares about the worst realized outcome more than the average one. None of the three is "the" right metric; the right move is to name which question is being asked before quoting a number, exactly the kind of "name the benchmark before claiming improvement" discipline this project's own working agreement already asks for.

📊

Sharpe

📈

Reach for this when the return profile is known or suspected asymmetric — options-selling, trend-following, convex or concave payoffs.

🩹

Calmar

Use when the audience cares most about the worst realized outcome — an allocator, a risk committee, a trader who has to live through the drawdown.

The Deflated Sharpe Ratio, and the broader research program it comes from (Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado & Zhu's related work on backtest overfitting), is used differently — less as a routine tearsheet line and more as a gate a result has to pass before it's taken seriously at all. Quant research groups that run large parameter sweeps or feature searches (which is most of them) treat an un-deflated "best Sharpe from the grid search" as close to meaningless on its own, precisely because grid searches are engineered to find outliers — that's the entire point of running one. The honest question after any sweep isn't "what was the best Sharpe," it's "given how many things I tried, is the best Sharpe still better than what an empty search with the same shape would have handed me for free." DSR is the answer to that second question, stated as a number instead of a gut feeling.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where the Sharpe family, and its deflated version, quietly go wrong

Reporting a Sharpe ratio annualized by \(\sqrt{k}\) on autocorrelated returns. The \(\sqrt{k}\) derivation above assumes roughly independent per-period returns. Trend-following strategies, overlapping-window backtests, and anything with genuine serial correlation in its returns will show a naively annualized Sharpe that overstates the true one — a subtle inflation that has nothing to do with edge and everything to do with the annualization formula's own assumption being violated.

Reaching for Calmar or Sortino only after Sharpe already looks unflattering. All three ratios should be named up front as answering different questions, decided by what the audience actually cares about — not selected after the fact because one of them happens to flatter a particular result. Cherry-picking the metric that looks best is a much smaller-scale version of the exact multiple-testing problem DSR exists to correct.

Undercounting \(N\) when computing a Deflated Sharpe Ratio. DSR is only as honest as the trial count fed into it. A researcher who formally logs 10 backtest runs but informally eyeballed and discarded 20 more along the way (adjusting a threshold "by feel," rerunning, trying again) has really run something closer to 30 trials, not 10 — and every trial that isn't counted understates \(SR_0\) and overstates the resulting DSR. This is the identical undercounting failure mode the Multiple Testing lesson flags for informal, unlogged slice-testing; here it inflates a Sharpe ratio instead of a p-value.

Treating the \(N\) trials as independent when they're really correlated. The extreme-value-theory approximation behind \(SR_0\) is cleanest when the \(N\) trial outcomes are close to independent draws. In practice, a parameter sweep over adjacent lookback windows (19-day vs 20-day vs 21-day) produces highly correlated equity curves and Sharpe ratios, not independent ones — the true "effective" number of independent trials is smaller than the raw count of variations tried, meaning a literal-\(N\) DSR calculation on a dense grid search is itself somewhat conservative, but not by an amount the formula quantifies on its own.

Treating a high DSR as proof, not evidence. DSR corrects for the specific selection bias of trying \(N\) variations and keeping the best. It says nothing about costs, regime shift, data snooping in the feature design itself (rather than the parameter sweep), or whether the strategy still works on data the entire search — not just the reported trial — never touched. A DSR of 0.9 on an in-sample search is still an in-sample number; this project's own OOS bar (Lego Principle 5) is a separate, still-mandatory hurdle, not one DSR substitutes for.

Section 04

Key Takeaways

✓Three ratios, three different questions. Sharpe divides by total wobble, Sortino by downside-only wobble, Calmar by the single worst realized drawdown — none subsumes the others.
✓Annualize Sharpe by \(\sqrt{k}\), not \(k\). Variance adds linearly across independent periods, standard deviation doesn't — the same square-root-of-time scaling behind this repo's own vol math.
✓Sortino stops charging a strategy for good months. By only accumulating deviations below the MAR, it rewards asymmetric, upside-heavy return profiles that Sharpe penalizes.
✓Calmar surfaces the worst realized event, not an average. A high-Sharpe strategy can still be practically unusable if its one bad stretch was brutal — Calmar is built to catch exactly that.
✓A reported Sharpe hides how many variations produced it. The Deflated Sharpe Ratio benchmarks the reported Sharpe against \(SR_0\), the chance-expected ceiling for a best-of-\(N\) search — not against zero.
⚠Undercounting \(N\) silently inflates DSR. Informal reruns and "nudge it by feel" iterations count as trials too — leaving them out understates \(SR_0\) and overstates the resulting probability.
⚠A high DSR is not proof, and it isn't a substitute for OOS. It only corrects for the selection bias of trying \(N\) variations — costs, regime shift, and this repo's own out-of-sample bar (Lego Principle 5) are separate, still-mandatory hurdles.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. Why does annualizing a Sharpe ratio use \(\sqrt{k}\) rather than \(k\) itself?
Because mean and variance scale differently under summation of (roughly) independent periods: the annualized mean scales linearly by \(k\), but variance also scales linearly by \(k\), which means annualized standard deviation scales only by \(\sqrt{k}\). Dividing the \(k\)-scaled numerator by the \(\sqrt{k}\)-scaled denominator leaves a net \(\sqrt{k}\) — the same square-root-of-time volatility scaling derived in the Returns & Compounding primer, applied here to a ratio. It also means the scaling is only valid when returns are close to independent period to period; meaningful autocorrelation makes naive \(\sqrt{k}\) annualization overstate the true Sharpe.
Q2. Why does it make more intuitive sense to penalize only downside deviation, as Sortino does, for a strategy with asymmetric returns?
Standard deviation treats a period that beats expectations and one that misses it as equally "risky," because a squared deviation from the mean doesn't distinguish direction. For a strategy whose variance comes disproportionately from favorable big wins (a trend-follower, say), Sharpe effectively punishes it for having good outcomes, since those widen the denominator exactly as much as a bad outcome would. Sortino only accumulates deviations below a minimum acceptable return, so a strategy isn't charged in its risk measure for the part of its variability that nobody would actually call risk.
Q3. Why can a strategy have a great Sharpe ratio but be unusable in practice because of its Calmar ratio?
Sharpe is built from an average dispersion statistic (standard deviation) computed over the whole return series; Calmar is built from a single specific realized event (the worst peak-to-trough drawdown). A strategy can be quiet on most ordinary days — giving it a high Sharpe — while still carrying exposure to a rare but genuinely severe drawdown that a variance-based statistic barely registers because it's one stretch inside a much longer, calmer series. Calmar surfaces exactly that worst-case realized outcome, which is often what actually determines whether a real investor or trader can keep following the strategy through a bad stretch, independent of how good its average risk-adjusted return looks.
Q4. What specifically does the Deflated Sharpe Ratio correct for that a plain Sharpe ratio doesn't?
A plain Sharpe ratio doesn't know how it was produced — it looks identical whether it's the only strategy ever tested or the single survivor kept after testing many variations. DSR corrects for exactly that: it uses the number of trials \(N\) and the spread of Sharpe ratios across those trials to compute \(SR_0\), the Sharpe a purely lucky, zero-skill best-of-\(N\) search would be expected to produce, and then benchmarks the reported Sharpe against \(SR_0\) instead of zero. It additionally widens or narrows the standard error of that comparison using the track record's own skewness and kurtosis, rather than assuming Gaussian returns — the same non-normality correction already used in this project's own minimum-track-record-length function.
Q5. In the DSR formula, what does it mean if \(\sigma_{SR}\) (the spread of Sharpe ratios across the N trials) is large rather than small?
A larger spread across the trials means the search's own outcomes were more dispersed — some trials did much better or worse than others by chance alone — which gives pure luck more room to produce an extreme best-of-\(N\) result. That raises \(SR_0\), the chance-expected ceiling, which in turn makes it harder for the actually reported Sharpe to clear the bar by a meaningful margin. A tight, low-dispersion set of trial outcomes implies a lower \(SR_0\) and makes a given reported Sharpe look comparatively more impressive, all else equal.
Q6. Why is undercounting the number of trials \(N\) one of the most damaging mistakes when computing a Deflated Sharpe Ratio?
\(SR_0\), the chance-expected ceiling the reported Sharpe must clear, rises with \(N\). If a researcher only logs the formal backtest runs but doesn't count the informal threshold nudges and reruns tried along the way, the true number of variations explored is larger than the \(N\) fed into the formula. That understates \(SR_0\), which in turn overstates the final DSR — producing a falsely reassuring probability that the true Sharpe is positive. It's the identical undercounting failure this series' Multiple Testing lesson warns about for informal, unlogged slice-testing, here inflating a Sharpe-based probability instead of understating a p-value's true multiple-testing burden.
