The Research Pipeline: Hypothesis to Honest Backtest

A disciplined path from "I have an idea" to "here is what the evidence actually says" — five stages, in order, where skipping the order is the most common way a research process quietly turns into curve-fitting.

CONCEPT — a research methodology, not a signal ~14 min read Prereqs: Multiple Testing & the Look-Elsewhere Effect
TL;DR
  • A hypothesis needs an economic or behavioral mechanism stated before the data is searched — "why would this be true" — not a pattern discovered by scanning charts until something lines up.
  • Test the minimal-degrees-of-freedom version first: a bare rule with no lookback, no threshold, no smoothing constant is close to impossible to overfit because there's nothing in it to fit to noise.
  • Add parameters only after that baseline number is measured and recorded — every parameter added afterward is overfitting risk being purchased, not free improvement.
  • Validate out-of-sample with realistic costs and a genuine train/test boundary — see Walk-Forward & Purged Cross-Validation for why even a clean-looking chronological split can still leak.
  • Pre-register what "it worked" and "it didn't work" each look like before running the test — otherwise a null result quietly gets re-narrated into "promising, needs more tuning."
  • The best-of-many-variations number needs a multiple-testing correction before it means anything — see the Deflated Sharpe Ratio and Multiple Testing.
Section 01

Why this matters

Every quantitative strategy that ever gets traded starts as an idea, and every idea eventually has to answer the same question: does this actually work on data it hasn't seen, under costs that are real? The gap between those two points — "I have an idea" and "here is honest evidence" — is where almost all of the damage in quantitative research happens, and it happens quietly. A researcher rarely sets out to fool themselves. Far more often, a perfectly reasonable-sounding sequence of steps — try a rule, tune it a little, try a slightly different version, tune that too, report the best one — turns an idea with no real edge into a backtest that looks fantastic, entirely by accident. This lesson is about the sequence of steps that keeps that from happening: not a specific strategy, not a piece of math to memorize, but an order of operations for research itself. Get the order right and a null result stays a visible null result. Get it wrong — even with every individual step done "correctly" in isolation — and noise reliably disguises itself as signal.

This lesson is deliberately light on new formulas. Most of the hard mathematics it leans on already has its own lesson elsewhere in this Theory Lab — Walk-Forward & Purged Cross-Validation for the out-of-sample mechanics, the Deflated Sharpe Ratio and Multiple Testing for what happens when many variations get tried. What this lesson adds is the connective tissue: where each of those tools belongs in the sequence, and why doing them out of order defeats the purpose of doing them at all.

💡
Plain-English intuition

A locksmith who tries every key before deciding which lock it "fits"

Imagine someone hands you a lock and a bag of a thousand keys, and asks you to find the key that opens it. You try key after key. Eventually, on attempt 640, one of them turns. Have you learned that key 640 is "the" key to that lock — some special, meaningful pairing? Not really. Given a big enough bag of keys, something was always going to turn eventually, purely from trying enough of them — that's just what a large search does, whether or not any key was actually cut for that lock. A disciplined locksmith works the other way around: they look at the lock first, reason about what kind of key it's built to accept, and try a specific, motivated guess — and if that doesn't work, they say so plainly rather than quietly reaching for key 641, 642, 643… and reporting only the one that eventually clicked. The research pipeline in this lesson is the disciplined locksmith's process, applied to trading ideas: reason about the mechanism first, test the plainest version of it honestly, and if you do end up trying many variations, say how many you tried and correct for it — rather than presenting the one key that happened to turn as if it were the only key you ever tried.

Section 02

The five stages, in order

The pipeline has five stages. Each one exists to close a specific way the previous stages can quietly mislead. Doing them out of order — especially skipping straight to "add complexity" before the minimal version has been honestly measured, or skipping straight to "validate" without ever pre-registering what a pass or fail looks like — is the single most common way a research process degrades into curve-fitting without anyone involved intending it to.

1

Hypothesis formation — grounded in a mechanism, not a pattern

Before touching any data, state why the effect should exist: what economic behavior, structural constraint, or persistent incentive would produce it. "Central banks defend a stated inflation target, so a widening gap between policy rate and target creates a directional pressure on the currency" is a mechanism. "This 14-day moving-average crossover made money in the data I looked at" is not a mechanism — it's a pattern, and a pattern found by first looking at the data and then explaining it afterward is exactly backwards. The distinction matters because a mechanism-first hypothesis is falsifiable in a meaningful way (the mechanism can plausibly stop holding, or fail to show up, and that's informative) while a pattern-first "hypothesis" is really just a description of the specific historical sample it was read off of, dressed up as a prediction about the future.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Explaining why you think a specific team will win a game before watching any games this season — based on real facts like their roster or coaching — is a mechanism. Watching the whole season first, then pointing at whichever team won and saying "I always thought they'd do well," is not — it's just describing what already happened and dressing it up as a prediction.

2

The minimal-degrees-of-freedom first test

Once the mechanism is stated, build the plainest possible rule that expresses it — ideally a bare sign or zero-crossing rule with no lookback window to choose, no threshold to calibrate, no smoothing constant to pick. A rule with zero free parameters is close to impossible to overfit, for a simple reason: overfitting means tuning a parameter until it flatters a specific historical sample, and there is nothing here to tune. Measure this version's raw performance — return, Sharpe, drawdown, whatever the honest headline numbers are — before adding anything. This number is the baseline every later, more complicated version has to beat, and it is the cleanest signal available for whether the underlying mechanism has any real content at all.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Testing the plainest possible version of an idea first is like tasting a soup with just the one ingredient you think matters, before throwing in a dozen spices. If the plain version already tastes like something, that's a real, honest signal. If you throw in the spices first, you'll never know whether the soup is good because of your original idea or because of everything you added on top to save it.

3

Add complexity deliberately, one parameter at a time

Only after the zero-parameter baseline is measured and recorded does it make sense to add a lookback length, an entry threshold, a filter, a position-sizing rule. Each parameter added is a deliberate purchase: it buys the possibility of a better fit to the specific sample being tested, and it buys, in the exact same transaction, a fresh chance of that fit being noise rather than signal. This isn't a reason never to add parameters — most usable strategies need a few — but it is a reason to add them slowly, to record what each one changes relative to the baseline, and to keep an honest running count of how many were tried. That count becomes the input to the multiple-testing correction in Stage 4.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Every extra spice you add to that soup while trying to improve it is a trade: it might genuinely improve the flavor, or it might just be masking a bland base by accident, and you won't know which until later. Keeping count of every spice you tried — even the ones you dumped back out — matters, because "I tried 20 spice combinations and picked the best-tasting one" is a much weaker claim than "the first spice I tried worked."

4

True out-of-sample validation, with realistic costs

Split the data chronologically into a period the rule was built and tuned on, and a period it never touched during development — then measure performance only on the untouched period, with realistic transaction costs and slippage included, not a frictionless fill. This is the subject of Walk-Forward & Purged Cross-Validation in full: a plain chronological split date is necessary but not sufficient, because features and labels built from rolling windows can still straddle the boundary and leak test-period information backward into training even when the split date itself looks clean. This step is also exactly where a best-of-many-variations result needs the correction covered in the Deflated Sharpe Ratio lesson — a Sharpe ratio that is the survivor of \(N\) tried variations has to clear a chance-expected ceiling that rises with \(N\), not the much lower bar of "is it above zero."

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

This is like a chef finally serving the dish to someone who never watched it being cooked, using ingredients bought after the tasting was locked in — no sneaking a taste and then quietly re-seasoning based on what they say. If the tasting happens on ingredients the chef already peeked at while cooking, it's not really a fair test anymore.

5

Pre-registration — say what "worked" and "didn't work" mean, before running the test

Before the out-of-sample test is run, write down, concretely, what result counts as the hypothesis being supported and what result counts as it failing — a specific OOS Sharpe range, a minimum trade count, a maximum acceptable drawdown, whatever the honest bar is for this idea. This has to happen before the number comes back. A researcher who only decides after seeing an OOS Sharpe of 0.3 that "well, 0.3 is actually pretty good for this asset class" has just re-narrated a marginal or null result into a maybe, using knowledge that didn't exist when the bar should have been set. Pre-registration is what keeps a null result a null result — it removes the wiggle room that turns "this didn't clear the bar" into "this is promising and needs a bit more tuning," which quietly restarts Stage 3 with the OOS data now contaminated as a second round of in-sample tuning.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Deciding what counts as "this dish is good enough to serve" before you ever taste the final version is what stops you from tasting something mediocre and quietly telling yourself "actually, that's pretty good for a first try." Writing the bar down in advance is the only thing standing between an honest "this didn't work" and a comfortable excuse.

The five stages run in order, left to right, top to bottom. The dashed red arrow marks the dangerous shortcut: reaching a disappointing result at Stage 3 or 4 and, instead of recording it honestly, quietly going back to try a new variant or a new "hypothesis" on the same dataset. 🧮
Worked example

Running the pipeline on a rate-differential FX hypothesis, stage by stage

Stage 1 — hypothesis. The mechanism: when a country's central bank policy rate sits meaningfully above a peer's, capital tends to flow toward the higher-yielding currency over medium horizons, all else equal — a textbook uncovered-interest-parity-violation carry argument, not a pattern read off a chart. This is stated before any return series is pulled.

Stage 2 — minimal test. The bare rule: go long the currency with the higher policy rate whenever the differential is positive, flat otherwise — no lookback, no threshold on "how much higher," no position sizing beyond a fixed unit. Measured honestly over the full sample with round-trip costs included, suppose this returns a raw annualized Sharpe of 0.35 with 40 holding periods. That number, however modest, is now the baseline every more complex version must beat — and its very modesty is informative: a mechanism with a real, if small, edge in this minimal form is a different animal from one that shows nothing until a dozen parameters are tuned into it.

Stage 3 — add complexity, tracked. A researcher adds one filter — only take the trade when the differential exceeds 50bp, rather than any positive value — and records this as trial 2 of a running count. Sharpe improves to 0.52. A second filter — skip trades during the top decile of realized volatility — is trial 3; Sharpe improves again to 0.61. Both changes are logged, including the ones that were tried and discarded along the way (an under-counted \(N\) is exactly the failure the Deflated Sharpe Ratio lesson warns about).

Stage 4 — out-of-sample, real costs. The final three-parameter version (basic rule + 50bp filter + volatility filter) is evaluated only on a chronological holdout the tuning in Stage 3 never saw, with realistic transaction costs, and — because rate-differential features are typically built from a slow-moving monthly series — with the purge/embargo discipline from Walk-Forward & Purged Cross-Validation applied at the split boundary. Suppose the OOS Sharpe comes back at 0.40 on 34 trades — lower than the 0.61 in-sample number, as is typical, but still positive and with a workable trade count.

Stage 5 — the pre-registered verdict. Because the bar was set in advance — say, "OOS Sharpe above 0.30 with at least 30 trades counts as supported; below that, or fewer trades, counts as not supported, regardless of how the in-sample number looked" — the 0.40-on-34-trades result has an unambiguous verdict: supported. Critically, that verdict was fixed before Stage 4 ran. If the bar had instead been decided only after seeing the OOS number come back at 0.40, "supported" would carry far less weight — it would be indistinguishable from moving the goalposts to wherever the ball happened to land.

🎯
A real-world trading scenario

The moving-average idea that "worked" after the eighth tweak

A researcher notices, informally, that a particular moving-average crossover seems to have called several recent turning points in a currency pair. There is no stated mechanism — no argument for why a crossover of this specific length should carry information about future price moves, just an observation that it "looked right" a few times. The researcher backtests it: flat. They shorten the fast average: still flat. They add a volatility filter: modestly positive in-sample. They add a day-of-week filter: better. They add an ATR-based stop: better still. By the eighth adjustment, the in-sample Sharpe looks respectable, and the write-up describes "a crossover strategy with a Sharpe of 1.4."

Run through this lesson's pipeline, the same effort looks very different. Stage 1 was skipped entirely — there was never a mechanism, only a pattern search dressed up after the fact as intuition. Stage 2's minimal version was never measured in isolation and recorded as a baseline; the "flat" first two attempts were treated as failures to fix rather than as the honest zero-edge baseline this idea actually has. Stage 3 happened, but with the trial count uncounted and no separation between what was tried and discarded versus what made the final cut — exactly the undercounted-\(N\) failure the Deflated Sharpe Ratio lesson flags, which quietly overstates how special the eighth version's result really is. And Stage 5 never happened at all: there was no bar set in advance for what "this idea has nothing" would have looked like, so there was no way for the process to produce that verdict even if it were true. Whatever the eventual out-of-sample test shows, this idea entered it having already broken three of the five stages that keep noise from disguising itself as signal.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

Institutional quant research groups formalize versions of this same discipline under different names — a "research diary" or trial log that records every variation attempted (not just the ones that made the final cut), a hard separation between a "development" dataset and a "validation" dataset that development never touches, and a sign-off step where the pre-registered success criteria are checked against the actual production plan before capital is committed. None of this is exotic; the common thread across every serious version of it is the same one this lesson builds around: the order of operations is the actual safeguard, not any individual step performed with more rigor. A perfectly executed out-of-sample test on a hypothesis that was mined from the same data it's being tested on is still worthless — the leak happened upstream, at Stage 1, and no amount of correctness at Stage 4 fixes it after the fact.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where a research pipeline quietly breaks down

  • Reverse-engineering a mechanism to fit a pattern already found. Noticing a profitable-looking pattern first and then constructing a plausible-sounding economic story for it afterward is not hypothesis formation — it's the pattern-search failure mode wearing a mechanism's clothing. A real mechanism should be falsifiable independent of the specific sample that inspired it.
  • Skipping the minimal-parameter baseline entirely. Jumping straight to a multi-parameter version means there is no honest reference point for how much of the eventual performance is the mechanism itself versus the tuning applied on top of it.
  • Undercounting trials. Informally nudging a threshold "by feel" and rerunning without logging it still counts as a trial — leaving it out understates \(N\) and overstates any later significance or Deflated Sharpe calculation, exactly as the Deflated Sharpe Ratio lesson's own pitfalls section warns.
  • Deciding the pass/fail bar after seeing the result. Setting "what counts as working" only once the out-of-sample number is already in hand is functionally indistinguishable from moving the goalposts — it defeats the entire purpose of pre-registration.
  • Treating a disappointing Stage 3 or Stage 4 result as a reason to go back and search for a better variant on the same data. This is the dashed red loop in the diagram above — it reopens the undisclosed-search problem the earlier stages exist to close, even though each individual re-run looks like ordinary, reasonable iteration.
  • Reporting only the OOS result, not the full pipeline. A clean out-of-sample number presented without the trial count, the baseline, or the pre-registered criteria strips out exactly the context a reader needs to judge how much to trust it.
Section 04

Key Takeaways

✓State the mechanism before touching the data. A hypothesis explained after the fact by a pattern already found is not a hypothesis — it's a description of one sample dressed up as a prediction.
✓Measure the zero-parameter version first, honestly, and record it. A rule with nothing to tune is close to impossible to overfit — it's the cleanest available read on whether the mechanism has real content.
✓Every added parameter is overfitting risk being purchased. Add them deliberately, one at a time, and keep an honest running count — including the ones later discarded.
✓Out-of-sample validation needs real costs and a genuinely leak-free split. A clean-looking chronological boundary can still leak through overlapping feature/label windows — see Walk-Forward & Purged Cross-Validation.
✓Pre-register the verdict criteria before the test runs. This is what stops a null or marginal result from being re-narrated into "promising, needs more tuning" after the fact.
⚠The order matters more than any single step's rigor. A technically correct OOS test on a data-mined hypothesis is still worthless — the leak happened upstream, before validation ever began.
⚠A best-of-many-variations result needs the multiple-testing correction, not just an OOS pass. Clearing an out-of-sample bar and clearing the chance-expected ceiling for how many variations were tried are two separate hurdles — see the Deflated Sharpe Ratio lesson.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. Why does a hypothesis need a stated economic or behavioral mechanism, rather than just a pattern that performed well historically?
A mechanism is a claim about why an effect should exist, stated independent of any one historical sample — which makes it falsifiable in a meaningful way and gives a reason to expect it might continue. A pattern found by scanning data until something looks good, and only explained afterward, is really just a compact description of that specific sample. It has no independent reason to generalize to new data, because it was never subjected to any test besides "did it look good in the data used to find it" — which is guaranteed to be true by construction for whatever pattern eventually got selected.
Q2. Why measure a zero-parameter (or minimal-parameter) version of a rule before adding any tunable parameters?
A rule with no free parameters has nothing available to fit to noise in a specific historical sample, so its measured performance is close to the cleanest possible signal of whether the underlying mechanism has any real content at all. Every parameter added afterward — a lookback length, a threshold, a filter — creates a new way for the rule to accidentally flatter the sample it was tuned on, so establishing the honest, un-tunable baseline first gives a genuine reference point for how much of any later improvement is the mechanism itself versus the tuning layered on top.
Q3. Why is it not enough to run a correct out-of-sample test if the hypothesis itself was found by searching the same data?
Out-of-sample validation only protects against overfitting that happens during the validation step — it says nothing about how the hypothesis was generated in the first place. If the specific rule being tested was already selected because it happened to perform well on historical data (even data outside the nominal test window, if the same broad dataset informed the idea), the leak already occurred upstream, before any train/test split existed. A technically flawless OOS test run afterward is still evaluating a hypothesis that was effectively mined from data it claims to be independent of.
Q4. What does "pre-registering" the success/failure criteria mean in practice, and what specific failure does it prevent?
It means writing down, before the out-of-sample test is run, the specific numeric bar a result has to clear to count as support for the hypothesis (e.g. a minimum OOS Sharpe with a minimum trade count) and what result counts as the hypothesis failing. It prevents a specific and very common failure: deciding, only after seeing a marginal or weak result, that the bar was actually lower than it seemed — quietly re-narrating "this didn't work" into "this is promising and just needs a bit more tuning." Without a bar fixed in advance, there is no way for a test to produce a genuine null verdict, even when the honest answer is that the idea has nothing.
Q5. In the moving-average scenario, name two of the five pipeline stages that were skipped or broken, and explain the consequence of each.
Stage 1 (mechanism-first hypothesis) was skipped: the idea came from noticing a pattern first, with no economic argument stated in advance, so the later backtest is really just re-describing the sample the pattern was found in. Stage 3 (add complexity, trial count tracked) was broken: the eight rounds of adjustment were not logged as a running trial count, so the eventual reported Sharpe understates how many variations were actually searched — the same undercounting failure that inflates a Deflated Sharpe Ratio calculation. Either failure alone would be enough to make the eventual "Sharpe of 1.4" a much weaker claim than it appears; having both compounds the problem.
Q6. Why does a best-of-many-variations Sharpe ratio need a multiple-testing correction on top of a valid out-of-sample split?
An out-of-sample split only checks whether a specific, already-selected rule generalizes to unseen data with realistic costs — it doesn't account for how that rule was chosen in the first place. If the rule reported is the survivor of \(N\) variations tried, the reported Sharpe (even measured honestly out-of-sample) is still the maximum of \(N\) draws, and the maximum of many draws is expected to look good purely from the number of chances taken, independent of whether any of the variations has real edge. The Deflated Sharpe Ratio corrects for exactly this by benchmarking the reported Sharpe against the chance-expected ceiling for a best-of-\(N\) search, rather than against zero — a separate, additional hurdle from the OOS split itself.
