FFT Option Pricing (Carr-Madan)

Once a model hands you a characteristic function — Heston's does — pricing one option at a time by numerical integration works fine, until you need a whole strike curve, over and over, inside a calibration loop. Carr-Madan's 1999 trick prices the entire strip in a single FFT call.

CONCEPT — not built or tested in this repo ~16 min read Prereqs: Numerical Integration, The Heston Model, Fourier Analysis & the Frequency Domain
TL;DR
  • When a model has a known closed-form characteristic function \(\varphi_T(u)\) — Heston does — pricing one option at one strike via direct numerical integration works, but pricing a whole strike curve means repeating that integration once per strike, which gets slow fast inside a calibration loop that reprices the whole curve at every candidate parameter set.
  • Carr & Madan (1999) found a way to price an entire strip of strikes in a single Fast Fourier Transform call instead of one integration per strike — the standard practitioner technique wherever a tractable characteristic function exists.
  • The trick: the raw call price isn't square-integrable near the money, which blocks a clean Fourier transform. Damping it with \(e^{\alpha k}\) fixes that, and the damped price's transform \(\psi_T(v)\) has a clean closed form directly in terms of \(\varphi_T\).
  • Inverting that transform is exactly the shape a discretized FFT computes efficiently — one \(O(N\log N)\) call returns prices at a whole grid of \(N\) log-strikes \(k\) at once, instead of \(N\) separate numerical integrations.
  • The damping factor \(\alpha\) and the FFT's strike-spacing/grid-spacing pair are not free — there's a real, tunable trade-off between them (a discrete-FFT uncertainty-principle-style constraint), not a derivation this lesson works through in full.
  • This is a real, load-bearing computational tool used daily wherever a model has a tractable characteristic function (stochastic-volatility, jump-diffusion) — and it flatly does not apply to models without one, where Monte Carlo or a PDE solve are the fallback instead.
Section 01

Why this matters

The Heston Model lesson ended with a specific, deliberately unresolved loose end: Heston's option prices come from a semi-closed-form solution built from the model's characteristic function, evaluated by numerically integrating that characteristic function rather than by a simple algebraic formula. The Numerical Integration lesson already covers exactly this use case in its "how practitioners actually use this" section: a characteristic-function-based pricing formula reduces option pricing to a one-dimensional integral, routinely evaluated with Simpson's rule or a specialized quadrature rule — low-dimensional, smooth, and exactly where deterministic quadrature is the right tool.

That is a complete, honest answer for pricing one option at one strike. But calibrating a stochastic-volatility model to an observed volatility smile is a fundamentally different computational problem: it means repricing the entire observed strike curve — every quoted strike, at a given expiry — not once, but at every candidate parameter set a numerical optimizer tries on its way to a fit. If pricing one strike takes one numerical integration, pricing a 50-strike curve takes 50 of them, and a calibration routine that tries a few hundred parameter combinations before converging is now doing tens of thousands of one-dimensional integrations just to fit one expiry, on one day, for one underlying. That's the gap this lesson fills: Peter Carr and Dilip Madan's 1999 paper, "Option valuation using the fast Fourier transform," found a way to price an entire strip of strikes in a single Fast Fourier Transform call — turning what would be \(N\) separate numerical integrations into one \(O(N\log N)\) computation covering the whole grid at once.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Why price a curve one point at a time when a single machine can stamp out the whole thing at once?

Imagine needing 200 vanilla call prices, one for every strike on an observed option chain, from a model like Heston. The direct approach is straightforward: for each strike, set up a numerical integral involving the characteristic function, evaluate it, get one price, move to the next strike. That's 200 separate integrations — correct, but repetitive, and it gets repeated again at the next candidate parameter set a calibration routine tries, and the next, and the next, potentially thousands of times over a single overnight calibration run.

Carr-Madan's insight is that the 200 numbers being computed aren't really 200 independent problems — they're 200 samples of one underlying function (the option price as a function of strike), and that function has a clean relationship to the characteristic function in the frequency domain. Once a problem is framed that way, the Fast Fourier Transform — a single, highly optimized algorithm for converting between a function and its frequency-domain representation — can produce all 200 (or 2,000, or 200,000) prices in one shot, for roughly the cost of one or two of the original one-at-a-time integrations. Nothing about any individual option's price changes; what changes is doing the whole curve's worth of work together instead of strike by strike.

Section 02

The math, step by step

Three pieces, in the order Carr & Madan (1999) build them: (1) why the raw call price resists a clean Fourier transform and the damping fix; (2) the transform of the damped price, stated as the published result; (3) inverting that transform to recover the price, and why that inversion is exactly the shape an FFT computes efficiently.

1. The problem: the raw call price isn't square-integrable

Write the call price as a function of log-strike \(k = \ln K\), holding maturity \(T\) fixed: \(C_T(k)\). A Fourier transform needs its input function to decay — informally, to not carry infinite total "energy" — for the transform integral to converge cleanly. As \(k \to -\infty\) (strike toward zero), a call's price approaches the discounted forward price itself rather than decaying to zero — it stays large, not small. That means \(C_T(k)\), taken as-is, is not square-integrable: directly Fourier-transforming the raw call price runs into a divergence problem near the money, before any FFT machinery can even be applied.

Carr & Madan's fix is to stop transforming the raw price and instead transform a damped version of it — multiply by a decaying exponential in \(k\) so the result actually vanishes at both extremes:

\[ c_T(k) \;=\; e^{\alpha k}\,C_T(k) \]
C_T(k)the true call price at log-strike k, maturity T
α > 0the damping factor — a positive constant chosen to make c_T(k) well-behaved
c_T(k)the damped call price — this, not the raw price, is what actually gets Fourier-transformed

The role of \(\alpha\) is purely technical, not economic: it doesn't change what a call is worth, it's a mathematical device that pulls the price down toward zero fast enough, on both tails of \(k\), for the Fourier transform to exist and converge. Recovering the true price at the end just means undoing the damping — multiplying back by \(e^{-\alpha k}\) — which is exactly what the inversion formula in step 3 does.

2. The transform of the damped price — the published Carr-Madan result

This is the paper's central contribution, stated here as the correctly cited published result rather than re-derived line by line — the same house convention this curriculum uses for other closed forms (see the Black-Scholes Delta/Gamma results elsewhere in this curriculum, or Heston's own semi-closed-form pricing formula, stated rather than re-derived in the previous lesson). Carr & Madan (1999) show the Fourier transform of the damped call price, \(\psi_T(v)\), has a clean closed form directly in terms of the model's characteristic function \(\varphi_T\):

\[ \psi_T(v) \;=\; \frac{e^{-rT}\,\varphi_T\!\big(v-(\alpha+1)i\big)}{\alpha^2 + \alpha - v^2 + i(2\alpha+1)v} \]
φ_T(v−(α+1)i)

Every piece of the formula's role, in plain language: the numerator's characteristic function is where the specific model being priced — Heston, or any other model with a known, tractable \(\varphi_T\) — enters the picture; everything else in the formula is generic machinery that doesn't change from model to model. The damping factor \(\alpha\) appears throughout the denominator and inside the characteristic function's shifted argument — it's what made this closed form possible to derive at all, by fixing the square-integrability problem from step 1 before the transform was ever taken.

3. Recovering the price: the inversion, and why it's FFT-shaped

Having the damped price's transform \(\psi_T(v)\) in hand, the price itself comes back by inverting the Fourier transform — integrating \(\psi_T\) back against the complex exponential, then undoing the damping with \(e^{-\alpha k}\):

\[ C_T(k) \;=\; \frac{e^{-\alpha k}}{\pi} \int_0^{\infty} e^{-ivk}\,\psi_T(v)\,dv \]
e^(−αk)undoes the damping applied in step 1 — recovers the true, undamped call price
e^(−ivk)the same Fourier inversion kernel from the Fourier Analysis lesson, packaging a cosine and sine via Euler's identity
∫₀^∞ ... dvintegrating over the transform variable v recovers the function of k it came from — standard Fourier inversion

On its own, this single integral is no faster to evaluate at one strike \(k\) than the direct numerical-integration approach from the Numerical Integration lesson — it's still a 1D integral, evaluated with Simpson's rule or similar. The payoff shows up the moment prices are needed at a whole grid of log-strikes \(k\) at once, which is exactly the calibration situation from Section 01. Discretize the integral over \(v\) on an evenly-spaced grid, and this inversion becomes precisely a discrete Fourier transform — the exact object the Fourier Analysis lesson builds from its definition, \(X(f) = \sum_t x_t e^{-i2\pi ft}\) — evaluated simultaneously at every grid point in \(k\) that the discretization implies. That is exactly the computation the Fast Fourier Transform algorithm is built to perform efficiently: instead of \(N\) separate evaluations of the integral (one per strike, \(O(N)\) total integrations, each with its own internal cost), a single FFT call computes the whole length-\(N\) grid of prices in \(O(N\log N)\) time total. The whole strike-pricing problem has been reshaped, via the damping-and-transform construction above, into precisely the shape the FFT algorithm was built to exploit.

The Carr-Madan pipeline: one characteristic function in, a whole strike grid of prices out
Each stage's role: the characteristic function is the one model-specific input; damping (\(\alpha\)) makes it Fourier-transformable at all; \(\psi_T(v)\) is that clean closed-form transform; and a single FFT call inverts it across an entire discretized grid of log-strikes \(k\) at once, rather than repeating a one-dimensional numerical integration once per strike.

The practical discretization trade-off

Turning the continuous inversion integral into something an actual FFT call can execute means choosing a discretization: a spacing \(\eta\) between grid points in the integration (frequency) variable \(v\), and a corresponding spacing \(\lambda\) between the resulting log-strikes \(k\) the FFT hands back. Those two spacings are not independent choices — the FFT's discrete structure ties them together in a fixed relationship (\(N\eta\lambda = 2\pi\), the number of grid points \(N\) linking both sides), so picking one effectively pins down the other for a given \(N\). That is a real trade-off, structurally the same kind of uncertainty-principle-flavored tension that shows up whenever a discrete Fourier transform is involved (the same finite-sample-window resolution trade-off touched on in the Fourier Analysis lesson's spectral-leakage discussion, here applied to strike/frequency spacing instead of time/frequency spacing): a finer grid of strikes (smaller \(\lambda\>, more strikes packed into the output) forces a wider range of frequencies \(v\) to be integrated over (a larger \(N\eta\)), and vice versa. Getting this pairing right — fine enough strike resolution without needing an unreasonably wide, expensive frequency range — is a genuine, practical tuning step in any real implementation, not a detail that can be waved away; this lesson states that the trade-off exists and must be tuned, without working through the full numerical-analysis derivation of the optimal \(\eta\)/\(\lambda\) pairing.

Worked example

200 strikes, the direct way versus the Carr-Madan way

Suppose a desk needs Heston-model prices for 200 strikes at a single expiry — a realistic size for a liquid single-name or FX option chain — and this needs to happen repeatedly inside a calibration loop that tries, say, 500 candidate parameter sets before converging.

1

The direct, one-strike-at-a-time route

Per the Numerical Integration lesson, each strike's price requires its own one-dimensional numerical integration of the characteristic function (Simpson's rule or similar). 200 strikes means 200 separate integrations per parameter set; 500 parameter sets in the calibration loop means 100,000 total one-dimensional numerical integrations, each with its own function-evaluation cost, before the calibration finishes.

2

The Carr-Madan FFT route

One FFT call, with \(N\) chosen comfortably larger than 200 (FFT sizes are typically powers of two, so \(N=4096\) or similar is routine even for 200 strikes of interest — the extra grid points outside the strikes actually needed are simply discarded), prices the entire strike grid for one parameter set at once, in \(O(N\log N)\) operations. Across the same 500-parameter-set calibration loop, that's 500 FFT calls total — not 500 times 200.

3

The comparison

100,000 separate numerical integrations versus 500 FFT calls is already a 200x reduction in the number of pricing operations run — exactly the strike count, since that's precisely the dimension Carr-Madan collapses from "one integration per strike" down to "one FFT call covers every strike." The per-call cost of an FFT (\(O(N\log N)\)) is itself cheap relative to \(N\) separate integrations (\(O(N)\) integrations, each carrying its own non-trivial constant cost) — so the practical speedup compounds on top of the 200x reduction in call count, which is exactly why Carr-Madan is standard wherever a full-curve, repeated-reprice workload like calibration is involved, and far less necessary for pricing a single option at a single strike one time.

🎯
Real-world trading scenario

An overnight Heston calibration run — the difference between feasible and infeasible

A quant on a volatility desk needs to recalibrate Heston's five parameters — \(\kappa\), \(\theta\), \(\xi\), \(\rho\), \(v_0\), per the Heston Model lesson's notation — against that morning's observed implied-volatility surface: a full grid of strikes across several expiries, easily a few hundred quoted option prices in total. The calibration optimizer needs to try thousands of candidate parameter combinations, at each one repricing the entire observed surface and comparing it to market quotes, before the search converges to a fit good enough to trade off of before the market opens.

Pricing that full surface one strike, one integration at a time, at each of thousands of candidate parameter sets, is the kind of workload that can push an overnight calibration run from a routine batch job into something that simply doesn't finish before the desk needs the result. Carr-Madan's FFT approach reprices the entire strike grid, for a given expiry and parameter set, in a single call — collapsing the per-parameter-set cost from "one integration per strike" down to "one FFT, covering every strike, per expiry." That difference is exactly what separates a calibration run that reliably finishes overnight from one that doesn't converge in time to be useful — the FFT step itself isn't a new pricing model or a new source of edge, it's the piece of infrastructure that makes running the existing model's calibration at the required scale and speed practically possible at all.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

Carr-Madan's FFT method is the standard, workhorse technique wherever a model supplies a tractable characteristic function and the task requires pricing many strikes fast and repeatedly — this is not a research curiosity or an academic footnote, it is a real, load-bearing computational tool used daily on real trading desks. Its natural habitat is exactly the calibration workflow described above: fitting a stochastic-volatility model like Heston, or a jump-diffusion model like Merton's or Bates' (the Heston-plus-jumps extension named in the Heston lesson), to an observed implied-volatility surface, where the optimizer's inner loop needs a full-curve reprice at every candidate parameter set and doing that with 200 separate one-dimensional integrations per iteration would be prohibitively slow. Any affine or exponential-Lévy model with a known characteristic function — a broad and practically important class covering most of the tractable option-pricing models used in production — is a candidate for this same treatment.

The honest boundary is just as important as the capability: this method is not a universal option-pricing speedup, and it does nothing for a model that lacks a closed-form characteristic function in the first place. Models without one — many local-volatility specifications, most models where the underlying's terminal distribution has no tractable analytic form — simply have no \(\varphi_T\) to feed into the Carr-Madan machinery, and fall back to the tools the Numerical Integration lesson covers for genuinely hard cases: Monte Carlo simulation, or a full partial-differential-equation solve. Carr-Madan is a targeted fix for a specific bottleneck — many-strikes, many-reprices, given a tractable \(\varphi_T\) — not a general acceleration of option pricing as a whole.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where this goes wrong in practice

Treating the damping factor and grid spacing as free, harmless choices. \(\alpha\) has to be large enough to make the damped price genuinely square-integrable and avoid numerical instability in the transform, but too large a value can itself introduce numerical problems in a different direction (large \(\alpha\) can make the characteristic function's shifted argument behave badly for some models). Combined with the \(\eta\)/\(\lambda\) resolution-versus-range trade-off from Section 02, getting a Carr-Madan implementation to produce accurate prices across the whole strike range it's asked for is a genuine tuning exercise, not a plug-and-play default that works identically for every model and every strike range.

This only works when a closed-form characteristic function exists — it's not a universal option-pricing speedup. The entire method is built on having \(\varphi_T(u)\) in closed form to plug into \(\psi_T(v)\); a model without one has nothing for the Carr-Madan machinery to operate on, and reaching for "just use FFT pricing" as a generic acceleration technique for a model that lacks a tractable characteristic function is a category error, not a workaround.

A fast, wide-strike-grid calibration is still curve-fitting, the same caveat the Heston lesson already flags. Carr-Madan makes the calibration loop fast; it says nothing about whether the resulting fitted parameters generalize out-of-sample or represent a real trading edge, any more than a slow calibration would. Speed is an infrastructure improvement, not evidence the fitted model is correct or tradable.

Key Takeaways

✓Carr-Madan prices a whole strike strip in one FFT call, not one strike at a time. Given a model's characteristic function \(\varphi_T(u)\), it collapses N separate numerical integrations into a single O(N log N) computation.
✓The trick is damping the call price first. The raw call price isn't square-integrable near the money; \(c_T(k)=e^{\alpha k}C_T(k)\) fixes that, and its transform \(\psi_T(v)\) has a clean closed form in terms of \(\varphi_T\).
✓Inverting ψ_T(v) on a discretized grid is exactly FFT-shaped. That's the whole reason the speedup exists — the math was built specifically to land in the form an FFT algorithm computes efficiently.
⚠The damping factor α and the strike/frequency grid spacing trade off against each other. Finer strike resolution forces a wider frequency-integration range — a real tuning step, not a free parameter.
✓This is a real, load-bearing production technique — not a curiosity. It's the standard method wherever a tractable characteristic function needs fast, whole-curve calibration: stochastic-volatility and jump-diffusion models specifically.
⚠It only applies when a closed-form characteristic function exists. No φ_T, no Carr-Madan speedup — Monte Carlo or a PDE solve are the fallback for models without one.
⚠Fast calibration is not validated calibration. Speeding up the reprice loop says nothing about whether the resulting fitted parameters generalize out-of-sample — the same caveat the Heston lesson already raises about a good smile fit.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. Why can't the raw call price C_T(k) simply be Fourier-transformed directly, and what fixes that?
As log-strike \(k \to -\infty\) (strike toward zero), the call price approaches the discounted forward price rather than decaying to zero — it stays large instead of shrinking, so \(C_T(k)\) is not square-integrable and its Fourier transform integral does not converge cleanly. Carr & Madan's fix is to transform a damped version instead, \(c_T(k)=e^{\alpha k}C_T(k)\) for a positive damping factor \(\alpha\), which decays fast enough on both tails for the transform to exist; the true price is recovered afterward by multiplying back by \(e^{-\alpha k}\).
Q2. Where does the specific option-pricing model (e.g. Heston) actually enter the Carr-Madan formula for ψ_T(v)?
Entirely through the characteristic function \(\varphi_T\), evaluated at the shifted complex argument \(v-(\alpha+1)i\) in the numerator. Every other piece of \(\psi_T(v)\) — the discounting term \(e^{-rT}\) and the denominator \(\alpha^2+\alpha-v^2+i(2\alpha+1)v\) — is generic machinery that stays the same regardless of which model supplied \(\varphi_T\). Swap in a different model's characteristic function (a different jump-diffusion or stochastic-volatility specification, say) and the rest of the Carr-Madan pipeline is unchanged.
Q3. Why does discretizing the inversion integral over v turn the problem into something an FFT can compute efficiently?
Once the integration variable v is put on an evenly-spaced discrete grid, the inversion integral becomes structurally identical to a discrete Fourier transform — the same summation-of-complex-exponentials form covered in the Fourier Analysis lesson — evaluated simultaneously at every grid point in log-strike k that the discretization implies. The Fast Fourier Transform algorithm is built specifically to compute exactly that kind of discrete transform, across an entire grid at once, in O(N log N) time rather than evaluating each grid point's integral separately.
Q4. Roughly how does the operation count compare between pricing 200 strikes with 500 calibration iterations via direct numerical integration versus via Carr-Madan's FFT?
Direct integration needs one 1D numerical integration per strike per parameter set: 200 strikes × 500 iterations = 100,000 separate integrations. Carr-Madan needs one FFT call per parameter set, since a single call prices the entire strike grid at once: 500 FFT calls total, each O(N log N) rather than O(N) separate integrations. That's roughly a 200x reduction in the number of pricing operations run — the exact factor is the strike count, since that's precisely the dimension Carr-Madan collapses into a single call.
Q5. A quant wants to use Carr-Madan's FFT method to speed up pricing under a local-volatility model that has no known closed-form characteristic function. Does this work?
No. The entire Carr-Madan construction is built on having a closed-form characteristic function \(\varphi_T(u)\) to plug into \(\psi_T(v)\) — without one, there is nothing for the damping-and-transform machinery to operate on. A model lacking a tractable characteristic function needs a different tool entirely: Monte Carlo simulation or a partial-differential-equation solve, the same fallbacks the Numerical Integration lesson names for genuinely hard pricing problems. Carr-Madan is a targeted fix for a specific bottleneck (many strikes, many reprices, given a tractable φ_T), not a general-purpose acceleration that applies to every pricing model.
