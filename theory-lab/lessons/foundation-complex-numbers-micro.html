Lesson 03 · Foundation Mathematics

Complex Numbers, Underneath the Rotation

i = √−1 looks like a made-up trick with no obvious use. It isn't — it's the number system that later lets you transform an option's price into a "characteristic function," and lets a Fourier Transform decompose a signal into oscillations, both using the exact same idea this page introduces: a number that spins instead of just growing.

Section 1

i isn't on the number line — it's a rule

Every real number, squared, comes out zero or positive. No exceptions. i is what you get when you refuse to accept that as the end of the story.

i isn't a real number you can point to on a number line — it's defined to be an answer to a question every real number fails: "what number, squared, gives −1?" That's the whole definition: i² = −1.
i²

The entire definition

i² = −1. Equivalently, i = √−1. Nothing else about i needs to be memorized — everything else follows from this one rule.

↺

Not a new kind of "amount"

i doesn't measure a quantity the way 4 or −4 does. It behaves like a number in arithmetic, but its role turns out to be rotation, not size.

＋

The same trick, done before

Negative numbers were invented so subtraction always has an answer (3 − 5 needed one). Fractions were invented so division always has an answer. i is that same move, applied to square roots of negatives.

Why "imaginary" is a misleading name

"Imaginary" is a historical label from when these numbers were treated with suspicion, not a judgment that they're less real or less useful. They're exactly as rigorously defined as ordinary numbers — and they turn out to be the natural, standard tool for describing rotation, oscillation, and waves, which is precisely why option-pricing and signal-processing tools reach for them later.

Section 2

Two numbers, glued together — kept separate like apples and oranges

A complex number z = a + bi has a real part a and an imaginary part b — both are ordinary real numbers. Step through what happens when you add or multiply two of them.

One extra rule beyond ordinary algebra: whenever i² shows up, swap it for −1. That's the entire mechanical difference.
Section 3

A picture: the complex plane

Plot z = a + bi as the point (a, b) — real part along the horizontal axis, imaginary part along the vertical. This is the complex plane (or Argand plane).

Picture it
This form — a + bi, read straight off the point's coordinates — is called rectangular form. It's one of two equally valid ways to describe the same point.

Points like this aren't abstract nonsense with no representation — they plot exactly the way an ordinary ordered pair (x, y) plots on any graph. The "imaginary" axis is just as real a place on the page as the horizontal one.

Section 4

How far, and which way: modulus and argument

The same point (a, b) can also be described by its distance from the origin and its angle — that's polar form. Edit a and b and watch both update live.

Modulus — |z| = √(a² + b²)

The point's straight-line distance from the origin. Just Pythagoras' theorem, applied to the real and imaginary parts as if they were the two legs of a right triangle.

Argument — θ = arg(z)

The angle the point makes with the positive real axis, measured counterclockwise. Computed from tan⁻¹(b/a) — but the raw ratio alone can't tell a first-quadrant point from a third-quadrant one, so the actual signs of a and b have to be checked too.

Section 5

Euler's formula: e^(iθ) is just a rotation

e^(iθ) = cos θ + i sin θ. Drag the slider — the point always sits exactly on the unit circle, at angle θ.

Try it
Angle θθ = 0.00 rad (0°)
Blue = the radius (modulus fixed at 1, pure rotation). Purple dashed = the real part, cos θ. Amber dashed = the imaginary part, sin θ. Green arc = θ itself.

This is a genuinely surprising fusion: the left side, e^(iθ), is built from continuous exponential growth. The right side, cos θ + i sin θ, is built from rotation around a circle. Euler's formula says these are, in a precise sense, the same object — two toys that turn out to be built from the exact same gears underneath.

Section 6

One angle away from the internet's favorite equation

Set θ = π in Euler's formula. Since cos π = −1 and sin π = 0, the imaginary part vanishes completely.

e^(iπ)
=
cos π + i sin π
=
−1

Add 1 to both sides and you get Euler's identity: e^(iπ) + 1 = 0.

Five of the most important constants in mathematics — 0, 1, e, i, and π — each arising from a completely separate area (counting, growth, imaginary numbers, circles) appear together in one short, exact equation. That's the whole reason it gets singled out: not that it's directly useful on its own, but that it's a genuine, surprising confirmation these apparently unrelated ideas are deeply connected underneath.
Worth noticing

e^(iπ) looks like it should be some messy complex number — it's built entirely out of i, after all. It isn't. It evaluates to exactly −1, a real number with an imaginary part of precisely zero. An expression built from i doesn't guarantee a complex-valued answer.

Section 7

Where this actually shows up on a desk

In quantitative finance, complex numbers arrive almost entirely through two connected tools — both covered properly later in this curriculum, both unreadable without this page's vocabulary.

💹

Characteristic-function option pricing

Some pricing models — especially ones whose return distribution has no simple closed-form density — transform that distribution into a complex-valued "characteristic function" using E[e^(iθX)], and price options by manipulating that function instead of the raw density.

📡

The Fourier Transform

Decomposes a signal or time series into oscillating components, with every frequency represented as e^(iθ) — because Euler's formula lets one rotating complex number stand in for a paired cosine-and-sine, far more compact than carrying both separately.

Neither tool makes sense read cold. Both make sense immediately once i, the complex plane, and e^(iθ) are already comfortable — which is the entire reason this page exists before either of them.

Where this needs qualifying

Seeing an imaginary part appear partway through a characteristic-function calculation is expected and correct — the final priced quantity typically ends up real once the calculation completes (often after a Fourier inversion integral). Panicking at an intermediate complex number, rather than checking whether the final output is real as it should be, is a common early mistake.

Section 8

Reading the angle: eyeballing it vs. checking the quadrant

tan⁻¹(b/a) alone can't distinguish a first-quadrant point from a third-quadrant one — flipping both signs gives the exact same ratio.

Reading tan⁻¹(b/a) at face value
  • z = −1 + i: b/a = 1/−1 = −1, so tan⁻¹(−1) = −45°
  • Looks done — but −45° puts the point in the fourth quadrant
  • The real point, a=−1 and b=1, sits in the second quadrant
  • Wrong by 180° — a rotation error, not a rounding error
Checking the signs of a and b first
  • a=−1 (negative), b=1 (positive) → second quadrant, always
  • Correct argument: θ = 135° (3π/4 radians)
  • Check: √2·cos(135°) = −1 and √2·sin(135°) = 1 — matches z
  • Or just use atan2(b, a) — it handles the quadrant automatically
The difference isn't sophistication — a calculator's tan⁻¹ button is correct exactly as far as it goes. The extra step of checking a and b's actual signs (or letting atan2 do it) is what turns a right-looking number into an actually-right angle.
Section 9

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Compute (2 + 3i)(1 − i).

Expand: 2 − 2i + 3i − 3i². Since i² = −1, −3i² becomes +3. Combine: (2+3) + (−2+3)i = 5 + i.
Question 2

Using Euler's formula, what does e^(iπ/2) equal?

e^(iπ/2) = cos(π/2) + i sin(π/2) = 0 + i(1) = i. A quarter-turn around the unit circle from 1 lands exactly on the imaginary unit.
Question 3

The powers of i cycle every four: i, −1, −i, 1, then repeat. What is i⁵?

i⁵ = i⁴·i = 1·i = i. i⁴ always resets the cycle back to 1, so i⁵ behaves exactly like i¹.
Question 4

Is e^(iπ) a real number or a complex number?

e^(iπ) = cos π + i sin π = −1 + i(0) = −1. The imaginary part is exactly zero, not merely small — the result is genuinely real.
Question 5

What is the argument of z = −1 + i?

a=−1, b=1 puts the point in the second quadrant. The raw tan⁻¹(1/−1) = −45° needs a 180° quadrant correction to reach the true angle, 135°.
Section 10

What to carry forward

01

i is defined by i² = −1 — a new number invented so "the square root of a negative" has an answer, the same trick already used for negative numbers and fractions.

02

A complex number z = a + bi has a real part a and an imaginary part b, both ordinary real numbers, and plots as the point (a, b) on the complex plane.

03

Modulus |z| = √(a²+b²) is distance from the origin; argument θ is the angle from the positive real axis — always check the quadrant, or let atan2 do it.

04

Euler's formula, e^(iθ) = cos θ + i sin θ, fuses exponential growth with rotation — every point on the unit circle is e^(iθ) for some θ.

05

Setting θ = π gives Euler's identity, e^(iπ) + 1 = 0 — a direct consequence of the formula, not a separate result.

06

Complex numbers reach finance mainly through characteristic functions (option pricing) and the Fourier Transform — both represent oscillation as e^(iθ) instead of separate sine and cosine.

What this now lets you do

Read a + bi as a point on a plane, not abstract nonsense. Convert confidently between rectangular and polar form, handling the quadrant correctly instead of trusting a raw tan⁻¹. Recognise e^(iθ) on sight as "a rotation" the moment it reappears later — inside a characteristic function, inside a Fourier Transform — instead of being intimidated by it.
