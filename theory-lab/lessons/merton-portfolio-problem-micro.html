Merton's Portfolio Problem, or Kelly With a Volume Knob

The Kelly Criterion lesson handed you one clean formula, f* = μ/σ², for the fraction of a bankroll that grows it fastest. Robert Merton asked a bigger question: not "what maximizes growth," but "what should any investor hold, given how much risk they personally can stand." The answer turns out to contain Kelly's formula exactly inside it — as the special case of an investor who happens not to care about risk at all beyond what growth-maximizing requires.

Section 1

Kelly asked one question. Merton asks a bigger one.

The Kelly Criterion lesson derived f* = μ/σ² by maximizing expected log-growth of a repeated bet — a specific, narrow objective. Merton generalizes it.

🎯

Kelly's question

What fraction of bankroll maximizes long-run compounded growth? One derivative, set to zero, on g(f) = p·ln(1+f) + q·ln(1−f).

📐

Merton's question

What should any investor — not just a growth-maximizer — hold in a risky asset at every instant, given their own personal appetite for risk? Consumption and allocation are chosen continuously, forever.

🧰

A different tool is needed

Optimizing a running choice through time, against uncertainty, where today's decision shapes tomorrow's state, needs dynamic programming's continuous-time form: the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (HJB) equation.

🔁

The payoff

Merton's classic result, f* = μ/(γσ²), collapses exactly to Kelly's f* = μ/σ² the moment risk aversion γ is set to 1 — not an approximation, an identity.

Kelly's growth-optimal criterion isn't a separate, competing idea from utility-based portfolio theory — it's the exact point on Merton's dial where an investor's risk aversion happens to equal one.
Section 2

The control problem: wealth as a moving target

Wealth splits between a riskless asset (rate r) and a risky one (excess return μ, variance σ²). Two choices get remade every instant.

The wealth process
dWₜ
=
[(r+fₜμ)Wₜ − Cₜ] dt
+
fₜσWₜ dZₜ
The amber term is deterministic drift — riskless return plus risky excess return, minus whatever is consumed. The green term is the random shock from holding the risky asset, scaled by how much of wealth is exposed to it right now.
🎛

Two controls, chosen fresh every instant

Consumption Cₜ and allocation fraction fₜ aren't picked once — this investor re-chooses both continuously, forever, unlike Kelly's single fixed bet size.

🔗

Same engine as Kelly, more levers

This is the identical geometric-Brownian wealth process from Kelly's continuous form — just with f and C now allowed to move at every instant instead of being fixed once.

The objective is to choose the entire future path of {Cₜ, fₜ} to maximize expected discounted lifetime utility of consumption, plus (in the finite-horizon case) utility of whatever wealth remains at the end. That "entire future path" framing is exactly why this needs more than ordinary calculus.

Section 3

The value function — a GPS for wealth

Define J(W,t): the best-achievable expected utility obtainable starting from wealth W at time t, if every future decision is made optimally.

Picture a GPS navigating real-time traffic. It doesn't plan the whole trip in advance — for every location and time, it just knows one number: the best possible remaining travel time from here, driving optimally onward. J(W,t) is that same number, applied to wealth instead of location. Given J one instant ahead, picking today's best move becomes a trivial comparison — no need to re-plan the entire future.
📦

One number, infinite futures packaged inside

J(W,t) compresses every possible future consumption path, allocation path, and random wealth outcome into a single value per (wealth, time) pair.

🧩

Bellman's principle of optimality

Whatever earlier decisions were, the remaining decisions must still be optimal given the state they left behind — the entire logic dynamic programming is built on.

🌊

Itô's Lemma, not ordinary calculus

Wealth itself jitters randomly, so an ordinary Taylor expansion of J isn't valid — Itô's Lemma is the tool that correctly tracks J through an infinitesimal, noisy instant.

Where this needs qualifying

J(W,t) alone is just a symbol until a specific utility function is chosen — that's the step that turns "an expression involving J's derivatives" into an actual, computable number. The next two slides make that concrete.

Section 4

The HJB equation — solving for a function, not a number

Applying Itô's Lemma to J(W,t) and taking the dynamic-programming recursion to its continuous-time limit produces one equation.

The equation itself
0 = max{C,f} [ U(C) − ρJ + Jₜ + J_W·((r+fμ)W−C) + ½·J_WW·f²σ²W² ]
Read it as one sentence: "the best move right now equals what you get from that move immediately, plus how valuable your situation becomes right after." That's the GPS logic from the last slide, written as a differential equation instead of a turn-by-turn comparison.
Kelly's calculus
  • Maximize a single fixed function of one variable
  • Set one derivative to zero, solve algebraically
  • Works because the objective doesn't depend on any unknown future value
Merton's HJB equation
  • Maximize something that itself depends on J and its derivatives
  • The "maximize" step and "value the future" step must be solved together
  • A nonlinear PDE for a whole function J(W,t), not one number

Taking the first-order condition on f inside the braces gives f* = −(J_W / W·J_WW) · (μ/σ²) — the investor's implied relative risk tolerance, times the exact μ/σ² edge term from Kelly. A specific utility function is what turns that risk-tolerance term into an actual number.

Section 5

Solving it for CRRA utility: the Merton fraction

Plug in constant relative risk aversion (CRRA) utility, U(c) = c^(1−γ)/(1−γ), and guess that J takes a matching power form. The risk-tolerance term collapses to exactly 1/γ.

Merton's headline result
f* = μ / (γ · σ²)
A constant fraction of wealth in the risky asset — it does not depend on current wealth or on how much time remains until the horizon. A CRRA investor holds the same risky-asset weight whether wealthy or poor, young or old.
μ

The edge

Expected excess return of the risky asset over the riskless rate — the same numerator Kelly uses.

γ

The risk-aversion dial

Constant relative risk aversion. Bigger γ means a more cautious investor and a smaller optimal allocation.

σ²

The risk

Variance of the risky asset's return — the identical denominator role it plays in Kelly's formula.

f*

The Merton fraction

The constant, wealth- and horizon-independent optimal risky-asset weight everything above resolves to.

Section 6

Set γ = 1 and Kelly falls straight out

Log utility, U(c) = ln c, is the exact γ→1 limit of the CRRA family. Substitute it directly into Merton's formula.

Try it — the substitution
f*|γ=1
=
μ / (1 · σ²)
=
μ / σ²
That last box is exactly the Kelly Criterion's continuous-time formula, f* = μ/σ² — the same result the Kelly Criterion lesson derived from a completely different starting point (a Taylor expansion of expected log-wealth, not an HJB equation).

There's a clean reason it has to come out this way, not a coincidence of notation: maximizing expected log-wealth — Kelly's entire objective, E[ln Wₜ] — is identical, as an optimization problem, to expected-utility maximization under U(·) = ln(·). Kelly's growth-rate criterion and Merton's expected-utility framework were never two theories that happen to agree at one point. They are the same theory, and γ=1 is simply the name Merton's framework gives to the specific investor Kelly's derivation was describing all along.

The key insight

"Kelly sizing" is Merton's framework evaluated at the single risk-aversion value γ = 1 — not a rival theory, a special case.

The curve itself never moves — only which point on it you occupy does. Everything right of γ=1 is fractional Kelly, and it isn't a discount off "the" true answer; it's Merton's exact optimum for that investor's own honestly-stated risk aversion.

Section 8

Same edge, same volatility, three allocations

Three simulated wealth paths, identical random shocks (μ=8%, σ=20%, r=2%, ~5 years). Only the allocation fraction f differs.

Try it
Full allocation. The fastest-growing of the three paths — ending roughly +109% — but also the wildest: it dips to about −6% below the starting level partway through, the deepest drawdown of the three, before recovering.
Half allocation. Ends around +56% — roughly half the growth — but the deepest dip is only about −1.4% below start, less than a quarter as deep as the full-allocation path's dip.
Quarter allocation. Barely dips below the starting level at any point (about −0.4% at its worst) and ends with the smallest gain of the three, roughly +32% — the steadiest, least dramatic ride.
All three paths share the exact same sequence of random shocks — the only thing that changes between panels is the allocation fraction f. That isolates what "risk aversion" actually buys you: not a different edge, the same edge held less aggressively.

Section 10

Where half-Kelly actually comes from

The Kelly lesson introduced fractional Kelly as a practical hedge against estimation error. Merton's framework says there's a second, deeper reason.

The exact-optimum reading
  • An investor with γ=2 betting half-Kelly isn't discounting "the" right answer
  • Half-Kelly is their own personal, exact utility optimum
  • Most empirical estimates of revealed risk aversion sit meaningfully above γ=1
  • So most real investors' correct bet size was never full Kelly to begin with
The estimation-uncertainty reading
  • μ and σ² are themselves noisy, backtested estimates — not known exactly
  • γ is elicited (surveys, revealed behavior, risk-premia calibration), not observed either
  • Full Kelly (γ=1) is the size most exposed to an overestimated edge
  • Sizing down like a γ=2 investor also hedges that separate, practical problem
Both readings point the same direction, for different reasons: one says "half-Kelly is correct for who you honestly are," the other says "half-Kelly is safer given what you don't actually know." A practitioner using half-Kelly is usually leaning on both at once.

Section 11

Where the clean formula quietly breaks down

❓

γ is elicited, not observed

Just like a backtested μ, an investor's risk aversion must be inferred indirectly — surveys, revealed behavior, risk-premia calibration — and these methods routinely disagree with each other.

🧱

Real frictions kill the constant fraction

Add transaction costs and the optimal policy becomes a "no-trade region" around a target weight — rebalance only when drift is worth the cost — not a single constant fraction held continuously.

🌊

μ and σ aren't actually constant

Real markets have regime shifts, volatility clustering, and jumps. A regime change moves f* immediately in theory; in practice, "the optimal constant" is only as stable as μ, σ actually are.

🛠

CRRA is chosen for tractability, not realism

CRRA utility produces the clean, closed-form constant-fraction answer largely because it's mathematically convenient — other, arguably more realistic utility families don't produce a constant f* at all.

None of this is an argument against the math — it's the same discipline the Kelly lesson applied one level down: feed the formula honest, uncertain inputs, and treat its output as a ceiling to size down from, not a target to hit exactly.
Section 12

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Why does f* = μ/(γσ²) reduce to Kelly's f* = μ/σ² when γ=1, rather than just approximately?

Direct algebraic substitution: μ/(1·σ²) = μ/σ². It's exact because log-wealth maximization and expected-log-utility maximization are the same objective, not two formulas that happen to look similar.
Question 2

What does the value function J(W,t) actually represent?

J(W,t) is the "GPS number" — it packages every possible future consumption path, allocation path, and random outcome into one value per (wealth, time) pair, so today's decision can be made without re-solving the whole future.
Question 3

Two investors share the same μ and σ. One has γ=1, the other γ=2. Is the γ=2 investor being irrationally cautious?

f*(γ) = (1/γ)·f*_Kelly. Both investors are optimizing correctly — just different utility functions, because they are, honestly, different people.
Question 4

An investor with γ=4 has an optimal allocation equal to what fraction of the γ=1 (Kelly) allocation?

f*(γ) = (1/γ)·f*_Kelly, so γ=4 gives exactly a quarter of the γ=1 fraction — "quarter-Kelly," precisely, not approximately.
Question 5

Besides matching a specific γ, why do many practitioners still size below full (γ=1) Kelly?

Full Kelly assumes μ, σ² (and, one level up, γ) are known exactly. Since they're estimated, not known, sizing below the point estimate hedges estimation error — a separate, practical reason from the "different honest γ" argument.
Section 13

What to carry forward

01

Merton's problem generalizes Kelly's: choose consumption and allocation continuously to maximize expected lifetime utility, not just long-run compounded growth — via the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation.

02

The HJB equation solves for the value function J(W,t) — the "GPS" that packages every possible future outcome into one number per wealth-and-time pair.

03

For CRRA utility, the optimal risky-asset fraction is the constant f* = μ/(γσ²) — independent of current wealth or time remaining.

04

γ=1 (log utility) reproduces Kelly's f* = μ/σ² exactly, term for term — Kelly is one specific setting of Merton's risk-aversion dial, not a rival theory.

05

γ=2 gives exact half-Kelly, γ=4 gives exact quarter-Kelly — each investor's own precise optimum, and also a practical hedge against uncertain μ, σ², and γ itself.

06

Nothing about Kelly- or risk-aversion-scaled sizing exists yet in this repo's actual code — the live sizer is a fixed, vol-scaled percentage with no edge or γ input, verified directly against the source.

What this now lets you do

Read any "bet less than full Kelly" decision as a real, computable point on the μ/(γσ²) dial rather than a vague safety instinct — and separate the two honest reasons for landing there: a genuinely more risk-averse investor's own exact optimum, versus a hedge against μ, σ², and γ all being estimates rather than known quantities. Either reason is legitimate; conflating them with each other, or with "playing scared," is the mistake this lesson closes off.
