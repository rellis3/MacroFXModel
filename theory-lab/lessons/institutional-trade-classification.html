Trade Classification: Lee-Ready & Bulk Volume Classification

A raw trade print is just a price, a size, and a timestamp — nothing on the tape says whether the buyer or the seller was the one who showed up demanding immediacy. Every order-flow signal in this curriculum that needs a signed direction, including this project's own VPIN lesson, is quietly resting on an algorithm that guesses that sign from what little data is actually available.

CONCEPT — not built or tested anywhere in this repo ~15 min read Prereqs: Market Microstructure: Price Impact & the Bid-Ask Spread, Order Flow Toxicity & VPIN
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • Trade classification infers whether a trade was buyer-initiated or seller-initiated from data that doesn't say so directly — every downstream order-flow signal that needs signed direction (VPIN, PIN, order-flow imbalance) depends on some version of this inference, not on a genuine label in the data.
  • The tick rule classifies a trade by comparing its price only to the immediately preceding trade's price: higher is a buy, lower is a sell, unchanged inherits the last classified sign. It needs nothing but a price series — and it's noisy precisely because it ignores where the quote actually sat.
  • The quote rule instead compares the trade's price to the prevailing bid-ask midpoint at the time of the trade: above the midpoint is a buy, below is a sell — but a trade printing exactly at the midpoint leaves the quote rule with nothing to decide.
  • The Lee-Ready algorithm (1991) uses the quote rule as its primary classifier, applied against the quote in effect some short lag before the trade print, and falls back to the tick rule specifically to break exact-midpoint ties. It is the long-standing academic standard on historical exchange data — and its accuracy has been shown, in published work, to degrade on modern electronic and high-frequency markets.
  • Bulk Volume Classification (BVC) (Easley, López de Prado & O'Hara, 2012) classifies volume in bulk over a bar rather than trade by trade: buy-fraction \(=\Phi(\Delta P/\sigma_{\Delta P})\), the bar's standardized price change passed through the standard normal CDF. This is exactly the classification step this project's own Order Flow Toxicity & VPIN lesson already covers as VPIN's basis — this lesson names it explicitly and extends it, rather than re-deriving it from scratch.
  • A repo-wide search turns up zero implementations of Lee-Ready, the tick rule, or the quote rule anywhere in this codebase — unsurprising, since Lee-Ready needs historical quote-timestamp data of a kind FX/OTC trading typically doesn't produce in the same form exchange-listed TAQ data does.
Section 01

Why this matters

Market Microstructure: Price Impact & the Bid-Ask Spread derives the bid-ask spread from a market maker's uncertainty about whether an incoming order is informed or noise. Order Flow Toxicity & VPIN builds a live, volume-bucketed proxy for exactly that same informed-share parameter. Both lessons quietly assume something this lesson exists to make explicit: that somewhere upstream, a trade or a bucket of volume got tagged as buy-initiated or sell-initiated in the first place. A raw trade record — price, size, timestamp — carries no such tag. Exchanges and OTC venues don't publish "this trade was the buyer's idea"; they publish a print. Whether that print was the buyer lifting an offer or the seller hitting a bid has to be inferred, and how it gets inferred is the entire subject of this lesson.

This isn't a narrow technical footnote. Every one of the four lessons in this new "Market Microstructure Extensions" category — this one, Roll's spread estimator, Glosten-Milgrom's fuller treatment, and the PIN and order-flow-imbalance measures that follow — either performs this inference directly or consumes signed trade direction as an input that had to come from somewhere. Get the classification step wrong, and every metric built on top of it — a toxicity score, an informed-trading probability, an imbalance signal — is measuring an artifact of the classifier, not the market.

💡
Plain-English intuition

You didn't see who pulled the trigger — but you saw where the price landed

Imagine watching a vending machine's coin slot from across the room — too far to see whose hand is feeding it coins, but close enough to watch the display update. If the machine's displayed price just ticked up, and a moment later a purchase registers above that new, higher price, a reasonable guess is that someone was in a hurry and paid up rather than wait. If a purchase registers exactly at the old price, unchanged, the guess is murkier. Trade classification is this exact guessing game, formalized: it never observes the buyer or the seller directly, it observes the trade print and the standing quote, and it infers direction from the relationship between the two.

The tick rule is the crudest version of this guess — it doesn't even look at the "display" (the quote), only at whether the last purchase price was higher or lower than the one before it. The quote rule looks at the display directly: did this purchase happen above or below the midpoint of what's currently being asked and bid? Lee-Ready combines both, leaning on the quote rule as the main tool and reaching for the tick rule only when the quote rule genuinely can't decide — a trade that printed exactly on the midpoint, dead center between bid and ask. Bulk Volume Classification abandons the trade-by-trade guess altogether and instead asks a coarser, bar-level question: over this whole time window, did the price move up a lot, a little, or not at all — and lets that single aggregate movement decide what fraction of the window's entire volume gets called "buy."

Section 02

The math, step by step — three ways to classify a single trade

Three classification schemes are covered here, in increasing order of sophistication, followed in Section 03 by a fourth that abandons trade-by-trade classification entirely.

1. The tick rule — price history only

The tick rule looks at nothing but the sequence of trade prices already on the tape. Let \(P_t\) be the price of trade \(t\) and \(P_{t-1}\) the price of the trade immediately before it:

\[ \text{TickSign}(t) = \begin{cases} \text{Buy} & P_t > P_{t-1} \quad \text{(an "uptick")} \\ \text{Sell} & P_t < P_{t-1} \quad \text{(a "downtick")} \\ \text{TickSign}(t-1) & P_t = P_{t-1} \quad \text{(a "zero tick" — inherit the last classified sign)} \end{cases} \]
P_tthe price of the current trade being classified
P_(t-1)the price of the immediately preceding trade on the tape
zero tickprice unchanged from the last trade — the tick rule reaches backward and copies whatever sign the last classified trade got

That zero-tick case matters more than it looks: on a quiet tape with many prints at the same price in a row, every one of them after the first simply repeats the sign of the trade that set the current price level — the tick rule has no fresh information about any of them individually. The entire method's honest selling point is minimal data: a tick-rule classifier needs only a price series, no quote data at all, which is exactly why it's the fallback embedded inside Lee-Ready rather than a method used on its own when better data exists.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

The tick rule is like guessing whether someone raised or lowered the volume on a speaker by only listening to whether it got louder or quieter than a second ago — no dial to look at, just "louder than last time" or "quieter than last time." If it's exactly the same volume as last time, you just repeat your last guess, because nothing new happened to change your mind.

2. The quote rule — compare the trade to the standing quote

The quote rule ignores trade history entirely and instead compares each trade's price to the prevailing bid-ask midpoint at the moment of the trade — the quote in effect just before that trade printed:

\[ \text{Mid}_t = \frac{\text{Bid}_t + \text{Ask}_t}{2}, \qquad \text{QuoteSign}(t) = \begin{cases} \text{Buy} & P_t > \text{Mid}_t \\ \text{Sell} & P_t < \text{Mid}_t \\ \text{undecided} & P_t = \text{Mid}_t \end{cases} \]
Bid_t, Ask_tthe best bid and best ask in effect immediately before trade t printed
Mid_tthe bid-ask midpoint — the quote rule's dividing line
undecideda trade printing exactly at the midpoint — the quote rule alone has no basis to pick a side

The intuition: a trade that executes above the midpoint most likely happened because a buyer was aggressive enough to lift an offer (or pay up through it), while a trade below the midpoint most likely happened because a seller hit a bid. Unlike the tick rule, this genuinely uses information about where liquidity was actually resting, not just where the last print happened to land — which is why the quote rule is generally considered the stronger classifier of the two, whenever reliable, correctly time-stamped quote data is available to use it.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

The quote rule is like knowing the exact fair halfway price a fruit stand is offering apples at right now. If someone actually paid more than that halfway price, they were probably in a rush to buy. If they paid less, they were probably in a rush to sell. If they paid exactly the halfway price, there's no way to tell from that fact alone which side was in a hurry.

3. The Lee-Ready algorithm (1991) — quote rule first, tick rule to break ties

Lee & Ready's original algorithm, still the long-standing academic standard for classifying historical exchange-tape (TAQ-style) data, combines both rules above with one specific refinement to how the quote is timed. Rather than compare a trade to whatever quote is timestamped closest to it, Lee-Ready compares the trade to the quote that was in effect a short lag — commonly five seconds, in the original paper's exchange-tape-era convention — before the trade print. The reason is subtle but important: on the older, slower tape infrastructure the original paper was built for, a trade and the quote update it triggered could appear to have nearly the same timestamp even though the quote update was actually a consequence of the trade, not a cause of it. Stepping back by a short lag avoids comparing a trade to a quote that had already reacted to that very trade.

1

Apply the quote rule first, with the lagged quote

Compare the trade's price to the midpoint of the quote in effect approximately five seconds before the print (the original paper's convention), not the most recent quote available at the trade's own timestamp.

2

If the trade is not exactly at that midpoint, stop — the quote rule decides

Above the lagged midpoint: buy. Below it: sell. Most trades resolve here.

3

If the trade prints exactly at that midpoint, fall back to the tick rule

This is the one and only job the tick rule does inside Lee-Ready: break the specific tie the quote rule cannot resolve on its own.

Lee-Ready's accuracy has been shown, in published research, to degrade on modern electronic and high-frequency markets. This is a documented empirical finding, not editorializing: work re-examining Lee-Ready on contemporary data — notably Chakrabarty, Li, Nguyen & Van Ness — finds its classification accuracy falls meaningfully short of its performance on the slower, specialist-driven markets the algorithm was originally built and validated on. The mechanisms cited are concrete: odd-lot trades (common on modern markets, rare when Lee-Ready was designed), sub-penny pricing that packs far more possible price levels into the same tick-size band the original algorithm assumed, and quote updates now arriving on a timescale — microseconds to low milliseconds — where the original five-second lag convention is arguably too coarse to still be doing the job it was designed for.
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Lee-Ready mostly trusts the "halfway price" trick, and only falls back on the "louder or quieter than last time" trick for the rare purchase that landed exactly on the halfway mark. It was designed back when prices updated at a fairly leisurely pace. On today's markets, where prices can update thousands of times a second, some of the assumptions behind that original design — like waiting a fixed five seconds before checking the quote — don't fit as well as they used to, and researchers have actually measured that the method gets more things wrong on modern data than it did on the older data it was built for.

Worked example

Classifying a 9-trade toy tape three ways

A hypothetical instrument prints at a starting reference price of 100 (a seed trade, given, not itself classified). Nine subsequent trades follow, each shown with the prevailing bid/ask quote in effect just before it printed. Three trades are worth watching closely: trade 2 prints exactly at the midpoint (the deliberate tie case), and trades 4 and 7 are cases where the tick rule and the quote rule genuinely disagree with each other.

t (seed)price 100 — reference point only, not classified
t1price 101, quote 99/101 (mid 100) → tick: uptick → Buy. quote: 101 > 100 → Buy. Lee-Ready: Buy (agree)
t2price 101, quote 100/102 (mid 101) → tick: zero-tick, inherits t1 → Buy. quote: 101 = mid → tie. Lee-Ready falls back to the tick rule → Buy
t3price 99, quote 99/101 (mid 100) → tick: downtick → Sell. quote: 99 < 100 → Sell. Lee-Ready: Sell (agree)
t4price 99, quote 97/99 (mid 98) → tick: zero-tick, inherits t3 → Sell. quote: 99 > 98 → Buy. Lee-Ready follows the quote rule → Buy — disagrees with the tick rule
t5price 102, quote 100/102 (mid 101) → tick: uptick → Buy. quote: 102 > 101 → Buy. Lee-Ready: Buy (agree)
t6price 100, quote 100/102 (mid 101) → tick: downtick → Sell. quote: 100 < 101 → Sell. Lee-Ready: Sell (agree)
t7price 100, quote 98/100 (mid 99) → tick: zero-tick, inherits t6 → Sell. quote: 100 > 99 → Buy. Lee-Ready follows the quote rule → Buy — disagrees with the tick rule
t8price 97, quote 97/99 (mid 98) → tick: downtick → Sell. quote: 97 < 98 → Sell. Lee-Ready: Sell (agree)
t9price 98, quote 96/98 (mid 97) → tick: uptick → Buy. quote: 98 > 97 → Buy. Lee-Ready: Buy (agree)

Two things this small tape demonstrates cleanly. First, the tie at t2: the quote rule genuinely cannot decide a trade printing exactly at the midpoint, and Lee-Ready's specific fix is to reach for the tick rule only in that narrow circumstance — not as a general second opinion, just as a tiebreaker. Second, t4 and t7 show the tick rule and the quote rule are not just two routes to the same answer: at both trades, the price was unchanged from the previous print (so the tick rule, seeing no fresh price movement, simply inherited whatever sign was already running), while the quote itself had drifted down between trades, leaving the unchanged trade price sitting above the new, lower midpoint — which the quote rule reads as aggressive buying against a softening market. Lee-Ready sides with the quote rule in both cases, on the premise that the quote carries more information than a mere tick-to-tick price comparison. Whether that premise still holds up as well on modern markets is exactly the open question the Chakrabarty et al. finding above is about.

Section 03

Bulk Volume Classification — classifying a bar, not a trade

Every method above classifies one trade at a time, and every one of them needs either a clean price sequence or genuinely reliable, correctly time-stamped quote data. Bulk Volume Classification (BVC), introduced by Easley, López de Prado & O'Hara (2012) alongside VPIN, takes a deliberately different approach: instead of asking "was this specific trade a buy or a sell," it asks a coarser question about an entire bar or bucket of volume at once — given how far the price moved over this whole window, what fraction of the volume in it was probably buy-initiated? This is the exact method this project's own Order Flow Toxicity & VPIN lesson already introduces as the classification step underneath VPIN — this lesson gives that method its own full treatment rather than re-deriving it, and the notation below matches that lesson's exactly.

\[ \text{BuyFraction}_\tau = \Phi\!\left(\frac{\Delta P_\tau}{\sigma_{\Delta P}}\right), \qquad V_{\text{buy},\tau} = V_\tau \cdot \text{BuyFraction}_\tau, \qquad V_{\text{sell},\tau} = V_\tau - V_{\text{buy},\tau} \]
ΔP_τthe price change (close minus open) over bar τ
σ_ΔPan estimate of the typical bar-to-bar price-change volatility, from recent history
Φ(·)the standard normal cumulative distribution function — turns a standardized price move into a number between 0 and 1
V_τthe bar's total volume — BVC splits it, it never adds to or subtracts from it

Read it the way the VPIN lesson does: standardize the bar's price change into a z-score — how many "typical bar moves" this particular move was — then feed that z-score through the standard normal CDF to get a number between 0 and 1, and use that number directly as the fraction of the bar's volume assumed buy-initiated. A bar that closed exactly flat (\(\Delta P_\tau = 0\)) gets \(\Phi(0) = 0.5\): an even 50/50 split, the "no directional evidence" case. A bar with a large positive price move relative to its own recent volatility pushes \(\Phi(\cdot)\) toward 1 — classified as almost entirely buy volume. The rest of the bar's volume, \(1 - \text{BuyFraction}_\tau\), is classified sell — BVC always allocates the bar's full volume between the two sides, never leaving any of it unclassified.

The trade-off this buys is explicit: BVC never needs quote data at all — only a price series and a volume series, bucketed into bars — which is exactly why VPIN, built for real-time computation on high-frequency futures data where clean, correctly time-stamped quote history isn't always cheaply available, chose it over a genuine trade-by-trade classifier like Lee-Ready. What it gives up in exchange is precision at the level of any individual trade: BVC never asks whether trade number 47 specifically was a buy, only what fraction of the whole bar's volume was probably on the buy side — a statistical proxy for aggregate direction, not a verified per-trade tag.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Instead of asking "was each individual customer buying or selling apples," BVC steps back and asks one question about the whole afternoon at once: did the apple price go up a lot, a little, or not at all? If it shot up, BVC guesses most of that afternoon's apples were bought by eager buyers. If the price barely moved, BVC guesses it was a normal, even mix of buyers and sellers. It never actually checks any one customer — it's a guess about the whole crowd, based only on where the price ended up.

Worked example

BVC buy-fraction across four toy price bars

A fixed volatility estimate \(\sigma_{\Delta P} = 0.0004\) applies to all four bars below (in practice this would be estimated from recent history — held fixed here to isolate what changing \(\Delta P_\tau\) alone does). The standard normal CDF values below are the same well-known values the VPIN lesson's own worked example uses (\(\Phi(0)=0.5000\), \(\Phi(0.50)=0.6915\)... this example reuses \(\Phi(1.00)=0.8413\), \(\Phi(2.00)=0.9772\), and \(\Phi(-0.50)=0.3085\) specifically, so the two lessons' numbers are directly comparable).

Bar A\(\Delta P=+0.0008\) → \(z=\Delta P/\sigma=0.0008/0.0004=+2.00\) → \(\Phi(2.00)=0.9772\) → 97.72% buy / 2.28% sell — a large rise, classified almost entirely buy
Bar B\(\Delta P=0.0000\) → \(z=0.00\) → \(\Phi(0)=0.5000\) → 50.00% buy / 50.00% sell — a flat bar, classified as an even split
Bar C\(\Delta P=-0.0002\) → \(z=-0.0002/0.0004=-0.50\) → \(\Phi(-0.50)=0.3085\) → 30.85% buy / 69.15% sell — a moderate fall, classified mostly sell
Bar D\(\Delta P=+0.0004\) → \(z=+1.00\) → \(\Phi(1.00)=0.8413\) → 84.13% buy / 15.87% sell — a solid rise, skewed clearly buy but less extreme than Bar A

The pattern to notice: Bar A's price move is exactly twice Bar D's (\(z=2.00\) versus \(z=1.00\)), and its buy-fraction is correspondingly more extreme (97.72% versus 84.13%) — but not twice as extreme, because \(\Phi(\cdot)\) compresses as it approaches 1, exactly like any cumulative probability must. Bar B, the flat bar, lands precisely at the 50/50 midpoint that \(\Phi(0)=0.5\) guarantees by the symmetry of the normal distribution — this is mechanical, not a coincidence of these particular numbers.

Real-world trading scenario

Why FX/OTC desks lean on BVC, not Lee-Ready — and what this repo's own code actually shows

Lee-Ready needs a genuine, correctly time-stamped record of the prevailing best bid and best ask at the moment of every single trade, going back far enough to apply its lag convention correctly. Centralized, exchange-listed equity and futures markets publish exactly this kind of consolidated quote data (the TAQ dataset Lee-Ready was built for is the canonical example). FX spot and most OTC instruments are structurally different: trading is fragmented across many bilateral venues and liquidity providers, each quoting its own book, with no single consolidated "national best bid/offer" tape the way listed equities have one. Reconstructing a single, trustworthy, correctly time-stamped prevailing-quote series across that fragmented landscape — the exact input Lee-Ready requires — is a materially harder data-engineering problem than it is on a single consolidated exchange tape, which is precisely why BVC's only-needs-price-and-volume design is attractive for FX and other OTC markets specifically.

This lesson's own factual claims here were checked against this codebase directly rather than assumed. A repo-wide search for Lee-Ready, tick-rule, and quote-rule implementation code (function names, docstrings, comments) turns up zero matches anywhere in this repository — the only place any of these three terms appears at all is inside this project's own prose, in the Order Flow Toxicity & VPIN lesson's text, which mentions "the Lee-Ready tick rule" purely as a contrast case for BVC, not as a description of code that exists. Separately, that same VPIN lesson's own math section confirms, in its own words, that VPIN's buy/sell split is built specifically on Bulk Volume Classification — "a genuinely different object from a real per-trade classification," in that lesson's own phrasing — which is exactly the connection this lesson has been making explicit throughout Section 03.

Section 04

How practitioners actually use this

In practice the choice between these methods is driven almost entirely by what data is actually available, not by a pure accuracy ranking in the abstract:

Lee-Ready (quote rule + tick-rule tiebreak)

The default choice on historical exchange-listed equity and futures data with a genuine consolidated quote history — academic microstructure research on TAQ-style datasets still leans on it heavily, precisely because it's the long-standing, widely-replicated standard other studies can be compared against. Its known accuracy degradation on modern electronic markets (covered in Section 02) is a live, acknowledged caveat researchers using it today are expected to account for, not a reason it's been abandoned outright.

Bulk Volume Classification

The natural choice whenever reliable trade-by-trade quote data isn't cheaply available, or when the downstream use case (like VPIN) specifically wants a real-time, volume-bucketed signal rather than a per-trade tag. Its cost is precision at the individual-trade level — it classifies a whole bar's volume by one aggregate statistic, which cannot distinguish a genuine wave of many small informed trades from one large resting order simply being lifted by a single counterparty.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Lee-Ready is like carefully interviewing every single customer as they leave a store, one at a time, using a detailed checklist — thorough, but only possible if you actually have a clerk stationed at the door the whole time recording every entry and exit. BVC is like standing outside once an hour and just checking whether the store's total cash in the register went up a lot or a little — much less detailed about any one customer, but it works even when nobody was stationed at the door taking careful notes all day.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where trade classification quietly goes wrong

Treating any of these methods as ground truth. Every scheme in this lesson is an inference from incomplete data, not a verified record of who actually initiated a trade. Even Lee-Ready, applied correctly on the exact data it was designed for, has a published, non-zero misclassification rate — treating its output as exact is a category error the original authors never made.

Applying Lee-Ready's five-second lag convention unexamined on fast modern data. That specific lag was tuned to the tape infrastructure Lee & Ready had in 1991. Applying it uncritically to a market where quotes update thousands of times a second is exactly the kind of mismatch the Chakrabarty et al. degradation finding is about — the fix in modern research is usually a much shorter lag or a more careful sequencing rule, not abandoning the underlying logic.

Forgetting BVC classifies a bar, not a trade. A large aggregate price move over a bar can come from many small trades all pushing one direction, or from a single large trade — BVC produces the identical buy/sell split either way, because it never looks below the bar level. This is the exact same caveat this project's VPIN lesson raises about VPIN's own inputs, because it's the same underlying method.

Picking a classifier based on tradition rather than data availability. Lee-Ready is not "more correct" in some universal sense that BVC merely approximates — each method fits a specific data situation (rich, reliable quote history versus price-and-volume-only), and using Lee-Ready on data too sparse or too fast for its assumptions produces worse classifications than a properly-specified BVC would, not better ones.

\(P_t\)The price of trade \(t\), being classified.
\(\text{Bid}_t, \text{Ask}_t\)The best bid and best ask in effect immediately before trade \(t\) printed.
\(\text{Mid}_t\)The bid-ask midpoint, \((\text{Bid}_t+\text{Ask}_t)/2\) — the quote rule's dividing line.
\(\Delta P_\tau\)The price change (close minus open) over price bar \(\tau\), used by Bulk Volume Classification.
\(\sigma_{\Delta P}\)An estimate of the typical bar-to-bar price-change volatility, from recent history.
\(\Phi(\cdot)\)The standard normal cumulative distribution function — the same object as \(Z(\cdot)\) in the VPIN lesson's notation.
\(V_\tau\)Bar \(\tau\)'s total traded volume, split by BVC into \(V_{\text{buy},\tau}\) and \(V_{\text{sell},\tau}\).

Key Takeaways

✓Trade classification is inference, not a data field. No raw trade record says who initiated it — every scheme in this lesson guesses that sign from price and quote data that is actually available.
✓The tick rule needs only a price series (uptick=buy, downtick=sell, zero-tick inherits the last sign); the quote rule needs the prevailing bid-ask midpoint and is generally the stronger of the two when reliable quote data exists.
⚠Lee-Ready (1991) uses the quote rule as primary, the tick rule only to break exact-midpoint ties — and published research (Chakrabarty, Li, Nguyen & Van Ness) has shown its accuracy degrades on modern electronic markets, a documented finding, not a hypothetical concern.

Section 06

Self-test

Q1. Under the tick rule, how is a trade classified if its price is exactly unchanged from the immediately preceding trade?
A "zero tick" inherits the classification of the last trade that was classified — it is not left undecided and it is not automatically a buy or a sell. If several trades in a row print at the same unchanged price, every one of them after the first simply repeats whatever sign the trade that originally moved the price to that level received, since the tick rule has no new price movement to react to for any of the repeats.
Q2. A trade prints exactly at the bid-ask midpoint. What does the quote rule alone conclude, and what does the full Lee-Ready algorithm do differently?
The quote rule alone has no basis to pick a side — a trade exactly at the midpoint is equally consistent with a buyer or a seller having been the aggressor, so the quote rule is undecided. The full Lee-Ready algorithm handles this specific case (and only this case) by falling back to the tick rule: it compares the trade's price to the immediately preceding trade's price and classifies it as an uptick, downtick, or zero-tick exactly as the plain tick rule would. Away from this exact tie, Lee-Ready uses the quote rule alone.
Q3. A price bar has volume \(V=2{,}000\) and a standardized price change with \(\Phi(\Delta P/\sigma_{\Delta P})=0.30\). What are \(V_\text{buy}\) and \(V_\text{sell}\) under Bulk Volume Classification?
\(V_\text{buy} = 2{,}000 \times 0.30 = 600\), and \(V_\text{sell} = 2{,}000 - 600 = 1{,}400\). A buy-fraction below 0.5 means the bar's price fell relative to its own recent volatility, so BVC classifies most of that bar's volume — 70% of it here — as sell-initiated.
Q4. Name two specific factors, cited in published research, behind Lee-Ready's documented accuracy degradation on modern electronic markets.
Any two of: odd-lot trades (far more common on modern markets than when the algorithm was designed), sub-penny pricing (packing far more possible price levels into the same tick-size band the original algorithm assumed), and quote updates now arriving on a microsecond- to-low-millisecond timescale, where the original five-second lag convention no longer necessarily does the job — avoiding comparing a trade to a quote that already reacted to that very trade — it was designed to do. This is the specific, published finding of work including Chakrabarty, Li, Nguyen & Van Ness, not a general impression.
Q5. This project's own "Order Flow Toxicity & VPIN" lesson already describes a classification method as VPIN's basis. Which one, and how does it differ fundamentally from Lee-Ready?
Bulk Volume Classification (BVC). Lee-Ready classifies each individual trade by comparing it to a specific prevailing quote (with the tick rule as a narrow tiebreak) — a trade-by-trade classifier. BVC never examines any individual trade at all; it looks only at a bar's aggregate price change relative to its own volatility and assigns a fraction of that bar's entire volume to the buy side via the standard normal CDF. BVC needs only price and volume data — no quote data at all — which is exactly why VPIN was built on it rather than on a genuine per-trade classifier like Lee-Ready.
Q6. A repo-wide search for Lee-Ready, tick-rule, or quote-rule implementation code in this codebase finds nothing. Why is that an unsurprising finding rather than an oversight?
Lee-Ready requires a genuine, correctly time-stamped record of the prevailing best bid and best ask at the moment of every trade, typically the kind of consolidated quote data centralized, exchange-listed markets publish (the TAQ dataset it was built for is the canonical example). This project's trading focus is predominantly FX/OTC-style, where liquidity is fragmented across many bilateral venues with no single consolidated best-bid/offer tape — reconstructing the input Lee-Ready needs is a substantially harder data problem in that setting, which is exactly why a price-and-volume-only method like BVC is the more natural fit and the one this codebase's own VPIN lesson actually leans on.

Further reading
  • Charles M. C. Lee & Mark J. Ready, "Inferring Trade Direction from Intraday Data," Journal of Finance, 46(2), 1991 — the original paper introducing the algorithm covered in Section 02, including the quote-rule-primary, tick-rule-tiebreak design and the original five-second lag convention.
  • David Easley, Marcos M. López de Prado & Maureen O'Hara, "Flow Toxicity and Liquidity in a High-Frequency World," Review of Financial Studies, 2012 — introduces Bulk Volume Classification alongside VPIN; the same paper this project's own Order Flow Toxicity & VPIN lesson cites for BVC.
  • Bidisha Chakrabarty, Bingguang Li, Vanthuan Nguyen & Robert A. Van Ness, "Trade Classification Algorithms for Electronic Communications Network Trades," Journal of Banking & Finance, 2007 (and related follow-up work by overlapping authors) — the re-examination of Lee-Ready's accuracy on modern electronic/high-frequency market data cited in Section 02's honest-limitations callout.
  • Maureen O'Hara, Market Microstructure Theory, Blackwell, 1995 — the foundational text situating trade classification within the broader informed-vs-noise-trader framework this whole curriculum's microstructure lessons build on.
