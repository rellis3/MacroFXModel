Fourier Analysis & the Frequency Domain

Every time series can be rewritten as a sum of sine waves at different speeds — the Fourier transform is the machinery that finds which speeds are actually present, and how strongly, without ever telling you when they happened.

CONCEPT — a classical signal-processing tool, explained here as background knowledge, not built or tested anywhere in this repo ~14 min read Prereqs: Stationarity, ACF & PACF
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • The Fourier transform \(X(f) = \sum_t x_t e^{-i2\pi f t}\) rewrites a time series as a sum of sinusoids at every frequency \(f\); the power spectral density \(|X(f)|^2\) shows which frequencies carry the most energy — which cycles actually dominate the series.
  • The periodogram is the empirical estimate of the power spectral density computed from one finite sample, and it has a precise, unglamorous caveat: each periodogram ordinate is only roughly chi-squared distributed with 2 degrees of freedom, and it is not a consistent estimator — its variance does not shrink as the sample grows, only its frequency resolution does — unless the ordinates are smoothed or averaged across nearby frequencies or across repeated segments.
  • A worked example below builds a synthetic 10-point series with a known, deterministic period-5 (weekly) cycle and recovers that exact cycle from its periodogram using nothing but Euler's identity and a geometric-sum argument — no fitting, no guessing.
  • Honestly: raw FX (or equity) return series tend to produce a periodogram that is close to flat across most frequencies — consistent with near-random-walk behavior, the same story this Theory Lab's ACF/PACF and Hurst/variance-ratio lessons already tell from different angles. This tool earns its keep on volume, volatility, and seasonality series far more reliably than on raw price returns.
  • Explaining how the Fourier transform works is not a claim that spectral analysis produces trading edge here — it's background knowledge for reading a periodogram correctly, and for understanding exactly what the next lesson's wavelets fix about it.
  • How it reached trading: Granger brought spectral analysis to economics and stock prices in the 1960s (finding mostly noise and a "typical spectral shape"), and Carr & Madan's 1999 FFT method made it a standard option-pricing engine.
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

People talk about weekly cycles and monthly cycles in markets. How would I know if one is really there?

Think of a piano chord. A microphone records one messy wave, but your ear hears the separate notes. Fourier analysis is that ear for a time series. It rewrites the series as a sum of pure waves at different speeds, and tells you how strong each one is.

How does it find them?

For each speed, it multiplies the series by a test wave of that speed and adds up the results. If the series really contains that speed, the products line up and the total is big. If it doesn't, they point every which way and cancel to nothing.

Give me an example.

Ten days, two trading weeks, with a pattern that repeats every five days. Test the wave that does two cycles in ten days, and the total comes out at five. Test any other speed, and it comes out at exactly zero.

And the chart people show?

That's the periodogram. Square the total and divide by the number of days: twenty-five over ten, two and a half. One tall bar, at two cycles in ten days, which is a cycle every five days. It found the period without being told.

So I run it on my returns and look for the tall bars.

Two warnings. First, a single bar is very noisy, and more data does not fix that. More data gives you more, finer bars, each just as noisy. A periodogram from ten thousand points is as jumpy, bar for bar, as one from a hundred.

So how do you trust a spike?

Average. Cut the data into segments, compute a periodogram for each, and average them. A real cycle survives the averaging. A noise spike melts away.

And the second warning?

On raw returns there's usually nothing to find. Clive Granger and Oskar Morgenstern looked at stock prices in 1963 and found spectra close to white noise: flat. The tool earns its keep on volume, volatility and seasonal series, like an intraday volume cycle around the session opens.

Anything else it can't do?

It tells you which cycles are in the whole sample, never when they happened. That is what wavelets fix. The one-line version: Fourier tells you which rhythms are there and how loud, but a single spike is a hint, not proof.

Section 01

Why this matters

Almost everything examined elsewhere in this curriculum treats a time series as a sequence of values ordered by time — today's return, yesterday's return, the autocorrelation between a value and its lag-\(k\) self (see Stationarity, ACF & PACF). Fourier analysis asks the same series a completely different kind of question: forget the calendar order entirely, and ask instead which repeating cycles, at which speeds, would you need to add together to reconstruct this exact series? That's a genuinely different lens — a rolling window and an autocorrelation function are still fundamentally time-domain tools; a periodogram lives in the frequency domain, and some structure (clean, regular cyclicality — a day-of-week pattern in trading volume, a seasonal pattern in a macro series, an intraday liquidity cycle) is far easier to see there than by staring at a price chart or a correlogram. This lesson builds the tool from its definition up to its most important honest limitation, and sets up the next lesson (Wavelets & Time-Frequency Analysis), which exists specifically to fix the one thing a plain Fourier transform cannot tell you: when a cycle was present, not just whether one exists somewhere in the sample.

💡
Plain-English intuition

A chord is several notes played at once — Fourier analysis is the ear that separates them back out

Play a chord on a piano and a microphone records a single, messy-looking waveform — one number per instant, no obvious structure to the eye. But your ear (and a spectrum analyzer) can tell you exactly which individual notes are being played simultaneously, because each note is a pure tone at its own frequency, and the chord is nothing more than those pure tones added together. Fourier's insight, extended from sound to any time series, is that any reasonably well-behaved sequence of numbers can be decomposed the same way: as a sum of pure sine waves, each at its own frequency, each with its own strength (amplitude) and its own timing offset (phase). A time series that looks like unstructured noise in a line chart can still have one or two frequencies doing most of the work underneath — a weekly pattern, a monthly rebalancing flow, an intraday liquidity cycle — and the Fourier transform is the mathematical version of the ear that pulls those individual "notes" back out of the mixed signal.

How it reached trading

Is there really a cycle in this series?

Spectral analysis · Granger & Morgenstern, 1963; Granger, 1966 → FFT option pricing, Carr & Madan, 1999

Market and business-cycle folklore was full of cycles, weekly, monthly and multi-year, but there was no standard way to test whether a periodicity was real or an artefact of eyeballing a chart. In the 1960s Clive Granger brought the periodogram to economics: his 1963 study with Oskar Morgenstern found New York stock-price spectra close to white noise, and his 1966 Econometrica paper showed that most economic series share a "typical spectral shape", with power piled up at low frequencies rather than in sharp cycles.

The fast Fourier transform later gave markets a second, larger use. Carr & Madan (1999) priced options by applying the FFT to the characteristic function of the log price, valuing a whole grid of strikes at once; it became a standard way to calibrate Heston-type models.

On a desk today: checking a series for seasonality or a genuine cycle before trading one, seeing which horizons carry the variance, and pricing options under models known only through their characteristic function.

The math, step by step

Watch the formula build
From a series to its periodogram, one term at a time
\[ \color{#fbbf24}{x_t} \]

Start with the series. In the worked example, ten values that repeat every 5 steps: \(1.000, 0.309, -0.809, -0.809, 0.309, \ldots\)

\[ x_t\,\color{#fbbf24}{e^{-i2\pi f t}} \]

Multiply each value by a test wave at frequency \(f\). By Euler's identity this one complex number holds a cosine and a sine of that speed.

\[ X(f) = \color{#fbbf24}{\sum_{t=0}^{N-1}} x_t\, e^{-i2\pi f t} \]

Add up the products. If the series contains that speed they line up and the sum is large; if not they cancel. In the example \(X(2) = 5\) and every other interior bin is exactly 0.

\[ \text{PSD}(f) = \color{#fbbf24}{\bigl|}X(f)\color{#fbbf24}{\bigr|^2} \]

Keep only the size, squared, and drop the phase. That is the power at frequency \(f\): how much of the variance sits there.

\[ \color{#10b981}{I(f_k) = \frac{1}{N}\,|X(f_k)|^2 = \frac{5^2}{10} = 2.5} \]

Divide by \(N\) and evaluate at the frequencies \(f_k = k/N\): the periodogram. One spike at \(k = 2\), a cycle every \(10/2 = 5\) steps. One ordinate from one sample is still a noisy estimate.

Three pieces, built in order: (1) the discrete Fourier transform itself; (2) the power spectral density and its finite-sample estimate, the periodogram; (3) the periodogram's honest statistical limitation.

1. The discrete Fourier transform

For a finite series of \(N\) observations \(x_0, x_1, \ldots, x_{N-1}\) (say, daily log returns, or hourly trading volume), the discrete Fourier transform at frequency \(f\) is:

\[ X(f) \;=\; \sum_{t=0}^{N-1} x_t\, e^{-i 2\pi f t} \]
x_tthe observed value at time step t — the raw series
fa candidate frequency, in cycles per sample
e^{-i2πft}a complex sinusoid at frequency f, via Euler's identity
X(f)a complex number: how strongly (and with what phase) frequency f is present

The term \(e^{-i2\pi f t}\) is Euler's identity in disguise: \(e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta\), so \(e^{-i2\pi ft} = \cos(2\pi f t) - i\sin(2\pi f t)\) — a single complex number that packages a cosine wave and a sine wave of frequency \(f\) together. \(X(f)\) is computed by multiplying the series by that complex sinusoid at every time step and summing the result; when the series genuinely contains a component wiggling at frequency \(f\), the terms line up (constructively add) and \(X(f)\) comes out large. When it doesn't, the terms point in essentially random directions in the complex plane and mostly cancel, leaving \(X(f)\) small. In practice \(f\) is only evaluated at the \(N\) discrete frequencies \(f_k = k/N\) for \(k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1\) (this is exactly what the Fast Fourier Transform algorithm computes efficiently); because the input is real-valued, \(X(f_k)\) and \(X(f_{N-k})\) are complex conjugates of each other, so only frequencies up to \(k = N/2\) — the Nyquist frequency — carry independent information.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Play a chord on a piano and it sounds like one messy sound — but your ear can still pick out that it's actually several individual notes played together. The Fourier transform does exactly that mathematically: it takes one squiggly line of numbers and tells you which "notes" (frequencies) are hiding inside it, and how loud each one is. A note that's genuinely present in the sound reinforces itself when you check for it; a note that isn't there mostly cancels out to nothing.

2. Power spectral density and the periodogram

\(X(f)\) itself is a complex number carrying both magnitude and phase; for the question "which frequencies dominate," only the magnitude matters, and the natural quantity is the squared modulus:

\[ \text{PSD}(f) \;=\; |X(f)|^2 \]

This is the power spectral density — how much of the series' total variance sits at frequency \(f\). The true PSD is a property of the underlying (infinite, idealized) process; from one finite sample of length \(N\), the natural empirical estimate is the periodogram:

\[ I(f_k) \;=\; \frac{1}{N}\,|X(f_k)|^2 \]
I(f_k)the periodogram — the empirical PSD estimate at frequency f_k
|X(f_k)|²squared magnitude of the DFT at that frequency — total energy there
1/Nnormalizes for sample length so periodograms of different-length series are comparable

Plotting \(I(f_k)\) against \(f_k\) (or equivalently against period \(1/f_k\)) is the practical, everyday version of "finding which cycles dominate a series": a tall spike at some \(f_k\) says that frequency is carrying an outsized share of the series' total variance relative to a flat (white-noise) baseline.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

The periodogram is just a bar chart of "how much of each note is in this sound." A tall bar at some frequency means that cycle is doing a lot of the work in the series; a flat, low chart everywhere means no particular cycle stands out — the series looks like static, with no one rhythm dominating.

3. The honest caveat: the periodogram is not a consistent estimator

This is the single most important thing to know before trusting a periodogram plot. For a stationary process (see Stationarity, ACF & PACF for why that assumption matters here too), each individual periodogram ordinate \(I(f_k)\) — away from \(f=0\) and the Nyquist frequency — is approximately distributed as \(\frac{\text{true PSD}(f_k)}{2}\) times a chi-squared random variable with 2 degrees of freedom (equivalently, an exponential distribution). Crucially, that distribution's spread does not shrink as N grows. Adding more data gives you more, finer-spaced frequencies to look at — it does not make any single ordinate a more precise estimate of the true spectrum at that frequency. A periodogram computed from 10,000 observations is exactly as noisy, ordinate for ordinate, as one computed from 100.

The caveat to remember: the raw periodogram is not a consistent estimator of the power spectral density. The standard fix is to trade frequency resolution for statistical precision — smoothing adjacent ordinates together, or averaging periodograms computed from multiple overlapping or non-overlapping segments (Bartlett's method, Welch's method, multitaper methods) — which is exactly analogous to how a rolling average trades timing precision for less noise. A single sharp, unsmoothed periodogram spike from one short sample is weaker evidence of a real cycle than the same spike surviving after averaging across several independent segments.
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

You might expect that measuring something with a lot more data always makes the measurement more precise — but the raw periodogram breaks that expectation. More data gives you more frequencies to check, not a sharper answer at any one of them. Averaging several such noisy readings together, not just collecting more of them, is what actually sharpens the picture.

Worked example

Recovering a known period-5 cycle from 10 data points, by hand

Build a toy series representing two trading weeks (\(N=10\)) with a purely deterministic 5-day seasonal pattern — an artificial "day-of-week" effect — and nothing else: \(x_t = \cos(2\pi \cdot 2 \cdot t / 10)\) for \(t=0,\ldots,9\). Evaluating gives:

x = [1.000, 0.309, −0.809, −0.809, 0.309, 1.000, 0.309, −0.809, −0.809, 0.309]

Notice the pattern repeats exactly every 5 steps — this is the period built into the data, disguised as a list of numbers. Rather than grinding through all 10 complex sums of the DFT by hand, use Euler's identity to see the answer directly. Since \(\cos\theta = \tfrac{1}{2}(e^{i\theta} + e^{-i\theta})\), the series is \(x_t = \tfrac12\left(e^{i2\pi k_0 t/N} + e^{-i2\pi k_0 t/N}\right)\) with \(k_0=2\), \(N=10\). Substituting into the DFT definition:

\[ X(k) = \sum_{t=0}^{N-1} x_t e^{-i2\pi kt/N} = \tfrac12\sum_{t=0}^{N-1} e^{i2\pi(k_0-k)t/N} + \tfrac12\sum_{t=0}^{N-1} e^{-i2\pi(k_0+k)t/N} \]

Both sums are geometric sums of complex numbers on the unit circle, and a standard identity says \(\sum_{t=0}^{N-1} e^{i2\pi mt/N}\) equals \(N\) whenever \(m\) is a multiple of \(N\) (all terms point the same way and add constructively) and equals exactly \(0\) otherwise (the terms are evenly spaced around the unit circle and cancel perfectly). The first sum hits its \(N\) case only when \(k=k_0=2\); the second hits it only when \(k = N - k_0 = 8\). Every other \(k\) gives exactly zero. So:

X(2) = N/2 = 5,   X(8) = N/2 = 5,   X(k) = 0 for every other k

Converting to the periodogram, \(I(2) = I(8) = \tfrac{1}{10}(5)^2 = 2.5\), and \(I(k)=0\) elsewhere. The periodogram is exactly two clean spikes, symmetric around the Nyquist bin — at \(k=2\), corresponding to a cycle repeating every \(N/k_0 = 10/2 = 5\) steps. That is the period-5 seasonal pattern, recovered purely from the shape of the spectrum, without ever being told the period in advance. A real series would add noise on top and the spike would be less than perfectly clean (and, per the caveat above, a single spike from a 10-point sample carries very little statistical weight on its own) — but the mechanism recovering the dominant cycle is identical.

🎯
Real-world trading scenario

Looking for an intraday liquidity cycle in FX trading volume

An analyst suspects EURUSD trading volume has a recurring intraday pattern — a lull during the Asia session, a pickup at the London open, another pickup at the New York open — and wants to check whether that pattern is strong and regular enough to be worth conditioning execution timing on, rather than just eyeballing a few days of charts. Computing a periodogram of hourly trading-volume counts across many trading days is a natural fit: volume is a genuinely cyclical series driven by the calendar (session opens repeat on a strict 24-hour clock), so a spike at the frequency corresponding to a 24-hour (or 8-hour, session-to-session) cycle is exactly the kind of structure this tool is built to surface. The honest next step, given the caveat above, is to average periodograms across many separate days (Bartlett/Welch-style) rather than trust a single day's noisy spike, and to remember that finding a real, statistically-supported volume cycle is a market-structure observation — it is not, by itself, a trading signal until it's turned into a tested rule and cleared an honest out-of-sample bar.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

Spectral analysis has a long, legitimate history in economics and finance — but mostly on the right kind of series, not on raw price returns. Macroeconomists use spectral methods to study business-cycle length and to cross-check filters like the ones covered in the next lesson but one (Trend/Cycle Filters); market-microstructure researchers use it on volume, order-flow imbalance, and volatility series to look for intraday or day-of-week seasonality; signal processing engineers use it constantly for exactly the purpose it was invented for (audio, vibration, radio).

The one honest, load-bearing finding worth stating plainly: when a periodogram has been computed on raw FX or equity returns, the classic result — going back to Clive Granger and Oskar Morgenstern's early 1960s spectral studies of stock price series — is that the spectrum comes out close to flat across most frequencies, the signature of white noise, with no dominant cycle standing out from the noise floor. That's the same conclusion this Theory Lab's ACF/PACF and Hurst exponent / variance ratio lessons reach from entirely different angles — three different lenses on the same underlying question about whether returns have exploitable memory, landing in the same place for raw returns. That is exactly why this lesson's chart used a volume-style seasonal example rather than a return series: a periodogram is a much more useful tool on volume, realized volatility, or an explicitly seasonal macro series than on raw FX price changes, where there is usually very little periodic structure to find in the first place.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where a periodogram misleads

  • Treating a single spike as proven, not estimated. Per the caveat above, one periodogram ordinate has a wide chi-squared-with-2-df sampling distribution regardless of sample size — a tall, unsmoothed spike is a hint, not a statistically confirmed cycle, until it's been smoothed or replicated across segments.
  • Applying it to non-stationary raw price levels. A trending price series' spectrum is dominated by low-frequency "energy" from the trend itself, which drowns out any genuine cyclical structure — exactly the same reason the ACF/PACF lesson insists on differencing or working with returns rather than levels.
  • Spectral leakage from a finite window. A finite sample is implicitly multiplying the true (infinite) signal by a rectangular window, which smears a clean spike's energy into neighboring frequency bins; this is why real spectral-analysis software applies a tapering window (Hann, Hamming, etc.) before transforming, rather than using the raw truncated sample directly.
  • Ignoring aliasing. Any true frequency above the Nyquist frequency folds back and appears as a lower, spurious frequency in the sampled data — a real risk whenever the sampling rate wasn't chosen with the signal's likely frequency content in mind.
  • Mistaking "no dominant frequency" for "the series has no structure at all." A flat periodogram rules out clean, strict periodicity — it says nothing about non-periodic dependence (like the short-memory autocorrelation the ACF/PACF lesson covers), which a flat spectrum can still coexist with in principle, or about volatility clustering, which lives in the squared/absolute return series, not the raw return spectrum.
Scenario drill · an illustrative afternoon

A monthly cycle in EUR/USD returns?

A colleague computes one raw periodogram of two years of EUR/USD daily returns. Most bars are low and jagged, but one bar, at a period of about a month, stands clearly above the rest. They want to build a trade around the monthly cycle. What do you suggest?

  1. Trade it. It is the tallest bar in the spectrum.
    Each raw periodogram bar is roughly chi-squared with 2 degrees of freedom: very noisy on its own. Among dozens of bars, one will always stand tallest, cycle or not. The tallest bar is not yet evidence.
  2. Split the data into segments, average their periodograms, and see whether the spike survives, keeping in mind that raw return spectra are usually close to flat.
    Averaging across segments (Bartlett or Welch style) is the standard way to trade resolution for precision. A real cycle survives it; a noise spike melts. And the prior is against a cycle in raw returns: the classic finding is a near-flat spectrum.
  3. Get two more years of data and recompute the raw periodogram to sharpen the spike.
    More data gives you more, finer-spaced bars, not a less noisy bar. The raw periodogram is not a consistent estimator: each ordinate stays just as noisy however long the sample.
  4. Drop it, and drop spectral analysis for FX altogether.
    Right about this spike being weak evidence, but the tool is still useful on other FX series. Volume and volatility have real calendar cycles, such as the intraday pattern around the session opens, and a periodogram is well suited to those.

What the lesson says. This is an illustrative case, not a dated episode. The lesson's honest finding is that raw FX and equity return periodograms tend to be close to flat, going back to Granger and Morgenstern's spectral studies of stock prices in 1963, and that a single unsmoothed spike is a hint, not a confirmed cycle. Smooth or average first; then, even if a cycle survives, it still has to become a tested rule before it is a signal.

Section 04

Key Takeaways

✓The Fourier transform rewrites a series in the frequency domain. \(X(f)=\sum_t x_t e^{-i2\pi ft}\) decomposes any series into a sum of sinusoids; \(|X(f)|^2\) is the power at each frequency.
✓The periodogram is the empirical estimate you can actually compute. \(I(f_k)=\tfrac1N|X(f_k)|^2\) from one finite sample — the worked example recovers a known period-5 cycle exactly.
⚠The periodogram is not a consistent estimator. Each ordinate is roughly \(\chi^2_2\)-distributed and stays noisy no matter how much data you add — smoothing or averaging across segments is required to trust it.
⚠Raw FX/equity return periodograms tend to be close to flat. Consistent with near-random-walk behavior — the same conclusion this Theory Lab's ACF/PACF and Hurst/variance-ratio lessons reach from other angles. This tool is better suited to volume, volatility, or seasonality series.
✓Only stationary series have a stable, interpretable spectrum. Run this on returns (or another properly stationary series), not raw trending price levels.
ℹThis lesson explains a technique, not a claim of edge. Nothing here is a trading signal — it's the background needed to read a periodogram, and to understand what the next lesson's wavelets add on top.
ℹIts first market result was a null. Granger & Morgenstern (1963) found stock-price spectra close to white noise, and Granger (1966) found economic series dominated by low frequencies rather than sharp cycles, so a flat periodogram is a legitimate, common answer. The FFT's bigger desk role today is option pricing (Carr & Madan, 1999).
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. Why does the periodogram in the worked example show exactly two spikes (at k=2 and k=8) rather than one?
Because the input is real-valued, the discrete Fourier transform of a real series always satisfies \(X(N-k) = \overline{X(k)}\) (complex conjugate symmetry), so \(|X(k)|\) and \(|X(N-k)|\) are always equal. The cosine in the worked example is itself built from two complex exponentials at frequencies \(+k_0\) and \(-k_0\) (via Euler's identity), which map to bins \(k_0\) and \(N-k_0\) respectively — hence spikes at both \(k=2\) and \(k=8=10-2\). Only the bins from \(k=0\) to the Nyquist bin \(k=N/2\) carry independent information; the rest mirror them.
Q2. Why is the periodogram described as "not a consistent estimator," and what actually fixes that?
A consistent estimator's sampling variance shrinks toward zero as the sample size grows. A single periodogram ordinate's sampling distribution is approximately \(\chi^2_2\)-scaled regardless of \(N\) — more data buys more, finer-spaced frequency bins to examine, but does not itself sharpen any one bin's estimate of the true spectral density there. The standard fix trades frequency resolution for lower variance: smoothing neighboring ordinates together (a moving average across frequency) or averaging periodograms computed on several independent segments of the data (Bartlett's or Welch's method) — analogous to how averaging several noisy estimates produces one less-noisy estimate.
Q3. Why does the lesson recommend running a periodogram on returns (or volume/volatility) rather than raw price levels?
A raw price level is generally non-stationary — it trends, and its variance keeps growing with the sample length (the same point the Stationarity, ACF & PACF lesson makes about levels vs. returns). A non-stationary series' spectrum is dominated by an overwhelming concentration of energy at frequency zero (the trend itself), which swamps any genuine cyclical structure elsewhere in the spectrum and makes the periodogram effectively uninterpretable as a "which cycles dominate" tool. Differencing to returns (or otherwise working with an already-stationary series) removes the trend and lets whatever genuine periodic structure exists show up cleanly.
Q4. A colleague reports finding "a strong 17-day cycle" from a single unsmoothed periodogram computed on 90 days of FX returns. How much should that claim be trusted?
Very little, on its own. First, per the honest application framing above, raw FX returns typically produce a close-to-flat spectrum, so any single spike is more likely to be noise than a genuine cycle. Second, and independent of that prior, a single unsmoothed periodogram ordinate carries a wide \(\chi^2_2\)-style sampling distribution regardless of sample size — an isolated tall bar in one 90-day sample, without corroboration from averaging across several independent 90-day segments or from a formal significance test against the noise floor, is exactly the kind of finding the pitfalls section above warns is easy to overstate.
Q5. What does "spectral leakage" mean, and why does a finite sample cause it?
Computing a periodogram from a finite sample is mathematically equivalent to multiplying the true, infinitely long signal by a rectangular window that is 1 over the observed span and 0 elsewhere, then transforming that product. Multiplication in the time domain corresponds to convolution in the frequency domain, so the rectangular window's own spectrum (which has broad, slowly decaying side-lobes) gets smeared onto every true spectral spike — spreading ("leaking") some of that spike's energy into neighboring frequency bins rather than keeping it perfectly concentrated at one bin. Applying a smoother taper (a Hann or Hamming window) before transforming reduces this leakage relative to a hard rectangular cutoff, at some cost to frequency resolution.
