Numerical Integration, When the Area Under the Curve Has No Formula

A price is very often, underneath the vocabulary, just an area under a curve — an integral. When that curve is a textbook shape, calculus hands back a clean formula. The moment it isn't — an exotic payoff, a nonstandard distribution — there's no algebraic shortcut left, and the area has to be estimated numerically instead. This deck covers the two families of tool that do that, and the one fact that decides which one to reach for.

The whole idea
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

Why does a lesson on areas under curves belong in a trading course?

Because most prices are one. An option's fair value is its expected payoff, and an expectation is an area: the payoff weighted by how likely each outcome is. Expected shortfall is the same, an average over the loss tail.

And Black-Scholes just hands me a formula.

For the simple case, yes. Change the payoff or the distribution and the formula disappears. Then you have to measure the area numerically.

How?

The oldest trick is slicing. Chop the area into thin strips, draw a straight line across the top of each, and add up the trapezoids. That is the trapezoidal rule.

Does it work?

Take the bell curve between zero and one. The true area is about point three four one three. Four strips give point three four zero one. That is off by about a thousandth, under half a percent, from five numbers and plain arithmetic.

And with more strips?

Halve the strip width and the error drops to about a quarter. Simpson's rule does better again. It fits a gentle curve through every three points instead of a straight line, and its error falls with the fourth power of the width.

Where does Monte Carlo come in?

It is the same job done with darts. Pick random points, read the curve's height at each, and average. Average height times width is the area. In one dimension it loses to the grid badly. Its error only halves when you quadruple the number of draws.

So why does anyone use it?

Dimensions. A grid needs points along every axis. Ten points per axis is ten points in one dimension, a hundred in two, and ten billion in ten. A basket option on ten stocks is a ten-dimensional area. Monte Carlo doesn't care: a million draws is a million draws in any number of dimensions.

Has this changed how things are priced?

Phelim Boyle brought Monte Carlo to option pricing in 1977. In 1995 Paskov and Traub used structured quasi-random points on a mortgage security with three hundred and sixty dimensions. And since the 2016 market-risk rules, the tail integral behind expected shortfall feeds straight into bank capital.

One line?

One or two smooth dimensions, slice it. Many dimensions, simulate it. And either way, the answer is only as good as the curve you feed in.

How it reached trading

An option price is an expected payoff; expected shortfall is a tail average. Boyle (1977) priced options by Monte Carlo, quasi-Monte Carlo (Paskov and Traub, 1995) priced a 360-dimensional mortgage security much faster, and Gauss quadrature became standard for Heston's integrals.

Section 2

When calculus runs out of formula

Every price, probability, and expectation below reduces to the same calculus object.

∫

A price is often just an area under a curve

"What's this option worth" is, in the risk-neutral framework, an expectation — the payoff integrated against a probability density. "What's the probability X lands here" is the area under its density curve.

⊘

Some curves have no antiderivative

A lognormal density paired with a vanilla payoff gives Black-Scholes, a clean formula. An exotic payoff or a nonstandard distribution breaks that — the integral simply has no algebraic answer to solve for.

🧮

Two families of tool, same target

Deterministic quadrature lays down a fixed grid and adds up the pieces. Monte Carlo samples randomly instead. Both estimate the exact same integral — the rest of this deck is when to use which.

Black-Scholes being a clean algebraic expression is the exception, not the rule — it only happens because the lognormal density and a vanilla payoff are an unusually cooperative pairing. Change either piece and the formula disappears, but the underlying question — "what's the area under this curve" — doesn't.
Section 3

The trapezoidal rule — straight edges under a curve

Chop the interval into thin strips, replace the curved top of each strip with a straight line, and add up the trapezoids.

Concept
h/2
×
[ f(x₀) + 2f(x₁) + 2f(x₂) + ⋯ + 2f(xₙ₋₁) + f(xₙ) ]
The two endpoints are counted once each. Every interior point is shared by two neighboring trapezoids — so it's counted twice.

Split [a,b] into n equal strips of width h = (b−a)/n. Within each strip, a straight line connecting the two sample points stands in for the true curve — the strip's area is then exactly a trapezoid's area, average height times width. The trapezoidal rule's error shrinks proportionally to h² (equivalently 1/n²): doubling the strip count roughly quarters the error.

Section 3, continued

Simpson's rule — fitting parabolas instead of lines

One extra point per piece buys a parabola instead of a straight line — and a much better error rate, for free.

Trapezoidal rule

Straight lines between consecutive points. Error shrinks with h² as strips narrow.

Simpson's rule

Parabolas fitted through each triple of points hug the true curve far more closely. Error shrinks with h⁴.

h/3
×
[ f(x₀) + 4f(x₁) + 2f(x₂) + 4f(x₃) + ⋯ + f(xₙ) ]

The alternating 4-2-4-2…-4 weighting (endpoints weighted 1) falls straight out of fitting and integrating a parabola through each successive triple of points — it isn't an arbitrary convention. Halving the step size cuts Simpson's error to roughly a sixteenth of what it was, versus a quarter for the trapezoidal rule.

Where this breaks down

Both rules assume the curve is smooth enough for a local straight-line or parabolic fit to be accurate. A payoff with a kink inside one panel — a digital or barrier feature — breaks that assumption, and Simpson's impressive h⁴ rate can silently collapse back toward, or worse than, the plain trapezoidal rate. Splitting the range at any known kink is the standard fix.

Section 6

Grids don't scale — the curse of dimensionality

A d-dimensional grid needs roughly n points along every dimension — nᵈ evaluations in total.

10¹

1 dimension — 10 grid points

Trivial. A single underlying's terminal distribution, gridded at 10 points, is nothing.

10²

2 dimensions — 100 grid points

Still easy. Two correlated underlyings, same 10-per-axis density.

10³

3 dimensions — 1,000 grid points

Getting heavier, but still computable on ordinary hardware.

10¹⁰

10 dimensions — 10,000,000,000 grid points

A basket of just 10 underlyings. Ten billion evaluations — already infeasible.

A structuring desk pricing a 7-underlying basket option with even a modest 15-point-per-dimension grid needs 15⁷ ≈ 171 million evaluations for one price, and every extra underlying multiplies that by 15 again: ten underlyings would need about 577 billion. Every monitoring date on a path-dependent payoff effectively adds another dimension to this same problem.

A grid method that works comfortably in 1 or 2 dimensions can look like it should "just scale up" to 3 or 4 — the exponential cost only becomes obvious once someone actually tries to run it, by which point real development time may already be sunk into an approach that has to be thrown out.
Section 7

Monte Carlo — sampling instead of gridding

An expectation E[g(X)] of a function g of a random variable X with density p is itself an integral: E[g(X)] = ∫g(x)p(x)dx. The law of large numbers says averaging many independent draws of g(X) converges to that same expectation — draw N samples from p, evaluate g at each, and average:

∫g(x)p(x)dx
≈
(1/N) Σ g(xᵢ)
xᵢ are N independent random draws — not an evenly-spaced grid. By the Central Limit Theorem, the error of this estimate shrinks proportionally to 1/√N, regardless of the dimension of x.
Deterministic quadrature
  • Far more accurate per evaluation point, in 1-2 dimensions
  • Error rate (h², h⁴) doesn't depend on randomness
  • Cost is nᵈ — explodes exponentially with dimension d
  • Infeasible past roughly 2-3 dimensions
Monte Carlo integration
  • Slower to converge than quadrature at low dimension
  • Error shrinks at a fixed 1/√N rate — no exponential term
  • Drawing N points in 10 dimensions costs the same N evaluations as 1 dimension
  • The only tractable option once dimensionality is genuinely high

Section 8

Where practitioners actually reach for each

📐

Characteristic-function option pricing

Heston- and SABR-style models price via a 1D Fourier integral of a characteristic function — routinely evaluated with Simpson's rule or Gauss-Legendre quadrature, never simulation.

🎲

Path-dependent & basket derivatives

Every monitoring date or additional underlying adds a dimension — Monte Carlo's dimension-independent cost is what makes these tractable at all.

🧭

Quasi-Monte Carlo — the middle ground

Low-discrepancy sequences (Sobol, Halton) replace true randomness with structured points, often converging faster in practice for moderately high-dimensional, smooth problems.

Real-world scenario

A 7-underlying basket call is a genuinely 7-dimensional pricing integral. A grid at 15 points per dimension needs 15⁷ ≈ 171 million evaluations for one price, and ten underlyings would need about 577 billion. Drawing 1 million joint samples of the 7 underlyings and averaging the payoff costs exactly 1 million evaluations, dimension irrelevant — the standard reason a desk reaches for simulation, not a grid. Halving that Monte Carlo error needs 4 million draws instead of 1 million: a real, but polynomial, cost — nothing like the grid's exponential wall.

Section 9

Where this goes wrong in practice

⚠

A kink inside a Simpson's panel

A digital or barrier payoff's kink breaks the smooth-curvature assumption a parabola fit relies on — the h⁴ advantage can silently degrade back toward, or past, the trapezoidal rate.

⚠

Underestimating the curse until it's too late

A grid that works at 1-2 dimensions looks like it should scale — the exponential wall only becomes obvious once someone tries to actually run it at 5 or 7.

⚠

Treating one Monte Carlo run as the whole story

1/√N is an average-case statement about repeated sampling. A single run at finite N still carries real sampling noise — reporting a point estimate without its standard error overstates precision.

Where this needs qualifying — checked against this repo's actual code

The full lesson flags numerical integration as a concept not built or tested in this repo — that check still holds: no trapezoidal-rule, Simpson's-rule, or general quadrature code exists anywhere in this codebase today. This repo's js/backtestStats.js does have real, live Monte Carlo tooling, but it's a different application of the idea: it shuffles trade order (and bootstraps trades with replacement) to estimate path-dependent drawdown risk, not to evaluate an arbitrary integral. That's consistent with how this deck frames it — the same random-sampling machinery, one layer more fundamental here than in that resampling use case.

Section 10

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Doubling the number of strips in the trapezoidal rule (halving h) is expected to cut the error to roughly:

The trapezoidal rule's error is O(h²) — halving h roughly quarters the error. A sixteenth is Simpson's O(h⁴) rate, not the trapezoidal rule's.
Question 2

What's the key structural difference between Simpson's rule and the trapezoidal rule?

Both are deterministic 1D grid methods. Simpson's uses one extra point per panel to fit a parabola instead of a line — the source of its much better h⁴ error rate on smooth functions.
Question 3

A grid with 10 points per dimension needs 10ᵈ evaluations. For d = 10 dimensions, that's:

10¹⁰ = 10,000,000,000 — the exponential blow-up that makes a grid infeasible past a couple of dimensions, exactly what makes a 10-underlying basket untouchable by quadrature.
Question 4

Why doesn't Monte Carlo's error rate depend on the number of dimensions?

A 10-dimensional random point has more coordinates, but generating and evaluating it isn't intrinsically harder than a 1-dimensional one — the cost is exactly N evaluations either way, with no nᵈ term hiding anywhere.
Question 5

To cut a Monte Carlo estimate's error in half, roughly how many times more samples are needed?

Error ∝ 1/√N, so halving it needs N to grow by a factor of 4 (√4 = 2). A real cost, but a fixed, polynomial one — unlike a grid's dimension-dependent exponential cost.

Scenario drill · an illustrative pricing request

A basket option on ten underlyings, priced by tomorrow

A client wants a price on a basket call: the payoff depends on the weighted average of ten correlated stocks at expiry. That makes the price a ten-dimensional integral of the payoff against the joint distribution of the ten stocks. A colleague who has just learned Simpson's rule suggests laying a grid over all ten dimensions. You need a price you can defend by tomorrow morning. How do you compute it?

  1. Simpson's rule on a grid with 10 points per axis. It has the best error rate in the lesson.
    Ten points per axis is \(10^{10}\), ten billion payoff evaluations, and ten points per axis is still a coarse grid. Simpson's better rate per point is irrelevant once the number of points grows exponentially with the dimension.
  2. Monte Carlo: draw a million joint scenarios from the calibrated model, average the discounted payoff, and report the standard error alongside the price.
    The cost is a million evaluations whatever the dimension, and the standard error tells you how much of the number is noise. If it is too wide, quadrupling the draws halves it.
  3. Quasi-Monte Carlo with a Sobol sequence instead of random draws.
    A real, widely used choice that keeps the dimension-free cost and often converges faster for smooth payoffs. The catch is that you lose the clean probabilistic error bar, so check it against a plain Monte Carlo run.
  4. Monte Carlo with 1,000 draws, quoting the price to four decimal places.
    Right method, wrong confidence. The error shrinks like one over the square root of the draws, so 1,000 draws are about 32 times noisier than a million. Quoting the point estimate without its standard error overstates the precision.

What happens. This is an illustrative request, not a specific historical trade. The general lesson is well documented: high-dimensional pricing problems such as basket and path-dependent options are priced by simulation, while one- or two-dimensional pricing integrals, such as the Heston Fourier integral, are done with quadrature.

Section 11

What to carry forward

01

Some integrals — many pricing expectations, especially with exotic payoffs or nonstandard densities — have no closed-form antiderivative and must be estimated numerically.

02

The trapezoidal rule sums trapezoid-shaped strips under straight-line fits — its error shrinks with h², so doubling the strip count roughly quarters the error.

03

Simpson's rule fits a parabola through each three consecutive points instead — a much better h⁴ error rate for smooth functions, at essentially no extra conceptual cost.

04

Both are grids, and grids don't scale — a d-dimensional grid needs roughly nᵈ points, the curse of dimensionality that makes them infeasible past 2-3 dimensions.

05

Monte Carlo replaces the grid with random sampling — its error shrinks at a fixed 1/√N rate, regardless of how many dimensions the problem has.

06

The trade-off is real: quadrature is faster and far more accurate per point in 1-2 dimensions; Monte Carlo is the only tractable option once dimensionality is genuinely high.

07

How it reached trading: Monte Carlo (Boyle, 1977), quasi-Monte Carlo (Paskov & Traub, 1995) and Gauss quadrature price options; FRTB's expected shortfall (2016) made the tail integral a capital number.

What this now lets you do

Recognize when a price or an expectation has no closed-form answer and needs numerical estimation instead. Reach for the trapezoidal or Simpson's rule for a genuinely low-dimensional, smooth problem — and switch to Monte Carlo the instant a payoff depends on several correlated underlyings or monitoring dates, understanding exactly why that switch happens and roughly what accuracy-per-cost to expect from each.
