Open Interest Walls, Max Pain & Squeeze Trading

The dealer-hedging mechanism from the previous lesson, applied to a real open-interest ladder — call walls, put walls, max pain, and the genuine (and honestly incomplete) economic logic behind why price is said to gravitate toward it.

CONCEPT — not built or tested in this repo ~16 min read Prereqs: Gamma Exposure & Dealer Hedging Flows
TL;DR
  • A call wall or put wall is simply a strike whose open interest is an outlier relative to its neighbors — the previous lesson's mechanism is what turns that outlier into observed support or resistance.
  • Max pain is the candidate settlement price that minimizes the total payout option writers owe, summed across every in-the-money option in the whole chain — not a single strike's own OI compared to today's price.
  • The economic logic: at expiry, writers (collectively short every outstanding option) must pay exactly the aggregate intrinsic value of every option that finished in the money. The strike minimizing that aggregate payout is the strike where sellers collectively lose the least — the origin of "price gravitates toward max pain," alongside the separate, mechanical dealer-hedging channel from the previous lesson.
  • The worked example computes total pain by hand at four candidate strikes on a small seven-strike chain and finds the minimum away from both the call wall and the put wall — the max-pain strike is rarely one of the outlier walls themselves.
  • The put/call open-interest ratio and OI skew are simple aggregate summaries of the same chain — cheap, useful context, and no more predictive than any other positioning snapshot on their own.
  • A wall is a snapshot, not a fixed target: open interest opens and closes continuously, so a wall that held yesterday can be materially weaker — or already gone — by the next test.
Section 01

Why this matters

The previous lesson derived the mechanism: dealer hedging around concentrated open interest creates real, mechanical buying or selling pressure, and that pressure disappears once the hedge completes. This lesson applies that mechanism to reading an actual open-interest chain — turning a table of numbers (strike, call OI, put OI) into three concrete, tradable concepts: where the call wall and put wall sit, where max pain sits, and what happens mechanically when a wall is breached rather than defended. Where the last lesson answered "why does dealer hedging create pressure at all," this one answers "given a real chain of numbers, where exactly is that pressure, and how much of it is there."

💡
Plain-English intuition

A chain of numbers is really a map of where hedging pressure lives

An options chain looks like a spreadsheet — strikes down one column, call and put open interest in two more. Read correctly, it's a map. Every large number in that table is a place where, per the previous lesson, dealer hedging is likely to be either fuelling a move (as price approaches) or absorbing one (once the hedge completes). A call wall and a put wall are the two biggest concentrations bracketing the current price — read together, they sketch out a rough "expected range" for as long as that open interest stays in place. Max pain is a different kind of landmark on the same map: not the biggest single concentration, but the settlement price that would leave the option writers — collectively, across every strike — paying out the least. None of these levels are fixed; the map redraws itself every time a position opens or closes.

Section 02

The math, step by step

Max pain — the correct aggregate formula

At expiration, every option writer (the short side of the contract) owes the holder exactly the option's intrinsic value if it finished in the money, and nothing otherwise. For a candidate settlement price \(S\), a call struck at \(k\) is in the money when \(S > k\), paying \(S-k\) per contract; a put struck at \(k\) is in the money when \(S < k\), paying \(k-S\) per contract. Summing that payout across every strike in the chain — not just one — gives the total amount writers would have to pay out if the underlying settled at \(S\):

\[ \text{CallPain}(S) = \sum_k \text{OI}_k^{\text{call}} \times \max(0,\, S-k) \] \[ \text{PutPain}(S) = \sum_k \text{OI}_k^{\text{put}} \times \max(0,\, k-S) \] \[ \text{TotalPain}(S) = \text{CallPain}(S) + \text{PutPain}(S) \]
CallPain(S)
Payout owed on ITM calls
Every strike below the candidate S contributes S−k times its call OI; strikes at or above S contribute zero
PutPain(S)
Payout owed on ITM puts
The mirror image — every strike above S contributes k−S times its put OI
TotalPain(S)
Aggregate writer payout at S
Max pain is the candidate S where this whole-chain sum is smallest, not any single strike's own OI

Max pain is the candidate strike that minimizes \(\text{TotalPain}(S)\), evaluating \(S\) at each strike present in the chain in turn and summing across the entire chain each time. This is the point worth being precise about: max pain is not "the strike with the smallest local call-plus-put OI compared to today's price" — it's the settlement price that minimizes the aggregate payout across every other strike in the book simultaneously. A strike can have modest open interest of its own and still be the max-pain point, if it happens to sit where the chain's overall ITM payouts are smallest; the worked example below shows exactly this.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Max pain isn't "which strike has the biggest pile of bets on it" — it's "which single settlement price would leave the casino paying out the least across every bet on the whole table at once." A strike doesn't need a huge pile of its own bets to win that title; it just needs to be the price where everyone else's bets, added up, cost the least to pay off.

Why minimize, not maximize — the real economic logic

The name is intuitive once the payout direction is clear. Every outstanding option has a writer on the other side of it — someone who is, collectively across the whole market, short that contract. At expiration, writers as a group pay out exactly the aggregate intrinsic value of whichever options finished in the money; nothing more, nothing less, and every option that finishes out of the money costs its writer nothing further. The settlement price that minimizes that aggregate payout is, by definition, the price at which option sellers collectively lose the least — and, since the payout to a writer is exactly the gain to the corresponding holder, it is also the price at which option buyers collectively gain the least. That is the origin of "price gravitates toward max pain": if you believe option writers (who tend to be larger, better-capitalized market participants) have any influence — deliberate or incidental — on where price ends up near expiry, the max-pain strike is the one outcome that is uniformly in their favor across the whole book simultaneously.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Think of every option writer as a casino that has to pay out winning tickets at expiry. The price where the casino owes the least money, added up across every ticket in the building, is naturally a price that's good for the casino — and if the casino has any pull at all over where things land, that's the price it would want. That's the entire logic behind "price gravitates toward max pain."

It is worth being explicit that this is a different mechanism from the previous lesson's dealer-hedging channel, even though both point toward similar behavior. Dealer gamma hedging is a mechanical, moment-to-moment rebalancing process that happens continuously as price moves, regardless of expiry. The max-pain argument is a payout-incentive argument about the single moment of settlement. The two can reinforce each other — and often are discussed as if they're the same thing — but one is a mechanical flow and the other is an aggregate payoff structure; conflating them is a common, avoidable source of overconfidence in "the price has to go there."

Put/call open-interest ratio and OI skew

Two simple aggregate summaries of the same chain, cheap to compute and useful as context rather than as a signal on their own:

\[ \text{P/C ratio} = \frac{\sum_k \text{OI}_k^{\text{put}}}{\sum_k \text{OI}_k^{\text{call}}} \]
≈ 1roughly balanced positioning
> 1put open interest dominates — more downside hedging/speculation outstanding
< 1call open interest dominates — more upside speculation outstanding

OI skew is a qualitative read on where that imbalance sits, not just how large it is: put open interest concentrated well below the current price, alongside call open interest concentrated near it, describes a book that's positioned defensively for a larger downside move than any upside move currently being priced for — and vice versa. Both numbers describe the current chain's shape; neither one tells you which way price is actually going next.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

The put/call ratio just asks "are there more downside bets or upside bets outstanding right now, overall?" It tells you the shape of the crowd's current positioning — like knowing more people bought umbrellas than sunglasses this week — but it doesn't tell you whether it's actually going to rain.

Worked example

Finding the call wall, put wall, and max pain from a small OI table

The chain drawn above, in table form (a generic instrument currently near 255):

Strike   Call OI   Put OI

240      1,200     6,500
245      2,000     2,600
250      2,400     2,300
255      2,200     2,200

265      1,400     900
1

Identify the call wall and put wall

Scan the call column for the largest outlier relative to its neighbors: 260 at 7,800 vs. its nearest neighbor 255 at 2,200 — a 3.5× ratio, comfortably past the "3×+ = strong" threshold used to separate a real wall from ordinary chain noise. Scan the put column the same way: 240 at 6,500 vs. its nearest neighbor 245 at 2,600 — a 2.5× ratio, a moderate-to-strong wall.

2

Compute total pain at candidate strike 245

Calls in the money (strike below 245): 235 and 240. Puts in the money (strike above 245): 250, 255, 260, 265.

\[ \text{CallPain}(245) = 800(245-235) + 1200(245-240) = 8{,}000 + 6{,}000 = 14{,}000 \] \[ \text{PutPain}(245) = 2300(5) + 2200(10) + 1600(15) + 900(20) = 11{,}500+22{,}000+24{,}000+18{,}000 = 75{,}500 \] \[ \text{TotalPain}(245) = 14{,}000 + 75{,}500 = 89{,}500 \]
3

Compute total pain at candidate strike 250

Calls in the money: 235, 240, 245. Puts in the money: 255, 260, 265.

\[ \text{CallPain}(250) = 800(15) + 1200(10) + 2000(5) = 12{,}000+12{,}000+10{,}000 = 34{,}000 \] \[ \text{PutPain}(250) = 2200(5) + 1600(10) + 900(15) = 11{,}000+16{,}000+13{,}500 = 40{,}500 \] \[ \text{TotalPain}(250) = 34{,}000 + 40{,}500 = 74{,}500 \]
4

Compute total pain at candidate strikes 255 and 260

\[ \text{TotalPain}(255) = \underbrace{800(20)+1200(15)+2000(10)+2400(5)}_{=66{,}000} + \underbrace{1600(5)+900(10)}_{=17{,}000} = 83{,}000 \] \[ \text{TotalPain}(260) = \underbrace{800(25)+1200(20)+2000(15)+2400(10)+2200(5)}_{=109{,}000} + \underbrace{900(5)}_{=4{,}500} = 113{,}500 \]
5

Max pain = 250 — lower total pain than every other candidate, including both walls themselves (260's 113,500 and 240's, computed the same way, would also exceed it). With spot near 255, put wall 240, call wall 260, and max pain 250, the read is: an expected range of roughly 240–260, with a mild pull from 255 down toward 250 as the nearest expiry approaches — not a guarantee, a probabilistic tendency built from this specific snapshot of open interest.

Real-world trading scenario

A wall that holds once, weakens, then breaks

Day one: the 240 put wall from the table above sits at 6,500 contracts. Price sells off toward it intraday, dealer hedging (selling into the decline per the previous lesson, then running out of selling pressure right at the strike) produces a clean bounce — textbook wall behavior, and it looks, from the outside, like reliable support.

Day two: the same strike's put OI is checked again before the next session and has dropped to roughly 5,100 contracts — a meaningful chunk of the positions that had built up ahead of the bounce were closed out afterward, likely by traders taking profit on the short puts (or long puts) that had just paid off. The wall is still there, but it's materially thinner than it was the day before.

Day three: price retests the same level. This time there isn't enough remaining open interest to generate the same defensive hedging flow, and the level gives way without much resistance, continuing lower. Nothing about the strike itself changed — no news, no gap — only the open interest sitting behind it did. This is precisely why a wall has to be re-checked each session rather than drawn once and trusted: it is a live, shifting snapshot of outstanding positions, not a fixed structural level.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

Two genuinely distinct ways of trading the same underlying structure show up repeatedly, along with one timing consideration carried over from the previous lesson:

↔️

Wall-to-wall range read

Treat the put wall and call wall as the rough edges of an expected range while both stay intact, with max pain as a secondary pull inside that range as expiry nears. This read assumes the walls hold — it stops being valid the moment either one decisively breaks.

🚀

Wall-break momentum read

Treat a decisive break of a major wall as the trigger for the opposite trade — per the squeeze mechanism from the previous lesson, the same open interest that built resistance or support becomes forced hedging flow in the breakout's direction once it's breached.

📅

Nearest liquid expiry, not the whole chain

Because gamma for an at-the-money strike rises sharply as expiry approaches (the previous lesson's numeric example), the walls and max pain that matter most in practice are almost always in the nearest expiry with meaningful open interest — a wall three months out carries far less mechanical force than the same-sized wall a few days from expiring.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where this gets over-trusted

Treating max pain or a wall as a guaranteed target. Both are, at best, probabilistic tendencies built from a snapshot of positioning — not a promise about where price will be at expiry. Fundamental news, a scheduled data release, or simply enough directional conviction from other market participants can override either one entirely; walls don't stop a real catalyst.

Open interest is a lagged, shifting snapshot, not a fixed target. As the scenario above illustrates, the exact same strike can be a strong wall one session and a weak one the next purely because positions closed — with no change in price at all. A wall or max-pain level computed from yesterday's close can already be stale before the next session opens.

A wall is only as strong as the fraction of its OI actually held by delta-hedging market makers. Not every option writer is a market maker running a continuous delta hedge — some open interest is held by directional traders, covered-call writers, or institutions hedging a completely different book, none of whom necessarily rebalance the way the previous lesson's mechanism assumes. A large headline OI number at a strike overstates the wall's true mechanical strength whenever a meaningful share of that OI belongs to non-hedging participants — and there's no way to observe that split directly from the open-interest figure alone.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

A wall is like a crowd standing near a doorway — it only actually blocks the door if those people are the type who'll rush to hold it shut. Some of the "crowd" counted in the open-interest number is really just people standing nearby for unrelated reasons (a static bet, a hedge on something else entirely) and won't lift a finger to defend the level. The headline number counts everyone; only some of them actually do the pushing.

Section 04

Key Takeaways

✓Max pain minimizes an aggregate, whole-chain payout — not a single strike's own OI. Evaluate total pain across every strike for each candidate settlement price, then take the minimum.
✓The "why minimize" logic is a real economic argument. Writers collectively pay out exactly the aggregate intrinsic value of ITM options; the minimizing strike is where sellers collectively lose the least.
✓Max pain and dealer-hedging pull are two distinct mechanisms that can coincide. One is a payout-incentive argument about expiry; the other is continuous mechanical rehedging — don't conflate them.
✓Walls and max pain sketch a range, not a certainty. The worked example's 240–260 range with a mild pull toward 250 is a read of one snapshot, not a forecast.
⚠A wall is a live snapshot, not a fixed structural level. It weakens or vanishes as positions close — re-check it every session, never assume it persists.
⚠Headline OI overstates true mechanical strength whenever non-hedging participants hold a meaningful share of it — and that share is never directly observable.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. Why is max pain computed by summing pain across every strike in the chain for each candidate settlement price, rather than just comparing one strike's own call and put OI to the current price?
Because at expiration, the total amount option writers must pay out depends on every option in the chain that finishes in the money, not just the options at one strike. A candidate settlement price puts every strike below it into the money for calls and every strike above it into the money for puts — the aggregate payout across all of them is what determines whether that candidate price is cheap or expensive for writers as a group. Comparing only one strike's own OI to the current price ignores the contribution of every other strike in the book and can point to the wrong level entirely, as the worked example shows (max pain landed at 250, a strike whose own OI wasn't the largest anywhere in the table).
Q2. What is the actual economic reason price is said to "gravitate" toward max pain, stated precisely?
Every outstanding option has a writer on the other side who is collectively short it. At expiry, writers as a group must pay out exactly the intrinsic value of whichever options finished in the money — nothing for the ones that finished worthless. The settlement price that minimizes that aggregate payout is the one outcome where option sellers, as a group, lose the least (equivalently, buyers gain the least). If option writers have any influence, deliberate or incidental, on where price settles near expiry, that's the one outcome uniformly in their favor — which is the actual argument behind "gravitates toward max pain," distinct from (though sometimes reinforced by) the separate mechanical dealer-hedging channel.
Q3. In the worked example, why is max pain (250) neither the call wall (260) nor the put wall (240)?
Because a wall is a local property — the single largest OI outlier at one strike — while max pain is a global property of the whole chain's aggregate payout curve. A strike can carry a huge single-side concentration (260's 7,800 calls) and still be an expensive candidate settlement price overall, because settling there also puts every strike below it deep in the money for calls. The strike that minimizes the whole-chain sum is generally somewhere the aggregate ITM payout is smallest across both sides at once, which is often closer to the middle of the OI distribution than to either extreme — exactly what the computed table shows.
Q4. Why did the put wall in the scenario stop holding on its second test, even though nothing about price or news had changed?
Because the open interest behind the wall itself had changed. After the first bounce, a meaningful share of the put positions that had built up were closed for profit, shrinking the wall's actual size from 6,500 to roughly 5,100 contracts overnight. Less remaining open interest means less dealer hedging demand available to defend the level on the next approach — the strike itself never moved, but the positioning fueling its defense did, which is exactly why open interest has to be re-checked every session rather than assumed stable once identified.
Q5. Why does a large headline OI number at a strike potentially overstate that wall's true mechanical strength?
Because the wall mechanism (from the previous lesson) only applies to the portion of that open interest held by dealers who are actually running a continuous delta hedge. Some of the same headline OI figure can belong to directional traders holding a static position, covered- call writers, or institutions hedging an unrelated book — none of whom necessarily rebalance as price approaches the strike. Since the split between hedging and non-hedging open interest isn't observable from the OI figure itself, a large number at a strike is an upper bound on potential mechanical force, not a guarantee of it.
