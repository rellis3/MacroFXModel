The SABR Model, Built for Speed

Heston gave volatility an honest, mean-reverting random life — and paid for it with a Fourier inversion every time it needs recalibrating. SABR asks a narrower question: what's the simplest stochastic-volatility model whose implied-vol smile can be written down almost in closed form? Four parameters and one algebraic formula, fit fast enough to run continuously against a live, moving market — that single fact is the entire reason SABR, not the arguably richer Heston model, is what an FX or rates options desk actually recalibrates all day.

Section 1

The problem SABR was built to solve

Heston prices an option honestly. It just isn't fast enough to recalibrate all session long.

🐌

Heston's chain

Characteristic function → numerical Fourier inversion → invert Black-Scholes for implied vol. Repeat that whole chain for every trial parameter set an optimizer proposes.

⚡

SABR's shortcut

Hagan, Kumar, Lesniewski & Woodward (2002) derived an algebraic approximation straight from the four SABR parameters to an implied vol — no integral, no inversion, ever.

⚖

The actual trade

SABR's own dynamics are, if anything, simpler than Heston's — no mean reversion in volatility at all. The win is calibration speed, not superior realism.

A trader can type four numbers into a formula and read off an entire smile in the time it takes to hit enter. That speed, not superior realism, is SABR's whole reason for existing in daily practice.

Fₜ is deliberately driftless — under the appropriate forward or swap measure, a forward price is already a martingale by no-arbitrage construction. αₜ follows plain driftless geometric Brownian motion — the same lognormal SDE as the underlying in Black-Scholes, just applied to the volatility parameter instead of price, and with no mean-reversion term at all.

"SABR" names three of these four objects directly: Stochastic Alpha (the random vol level αₜ), Beta (the backbone exponent), Rho (the correlation). Vol-of-vol ν is the fourth parameter — present in the dynamics, just not in the acronym's letters.

Section 3

Four parameters, four separate jobs

Not four interchangeable dials on the same knob — each governs a geometrically distinct feature of the smile.

📏

α — level

Sets the smile's overall height, roughly the at-the-money implied vol. Raise α and the whole smile shifts up in parallel — the same job σ plays in Black-Scholes.

🦴

β — backbone

Controls how volatility scales with the forward's own level — not today's smile shape, but how the whole ATM level would shift if the forward moved. Usually fixed by convention.

📐

ρ — skew

Sets the sign and strength of the smile's asymmetry. Negative ρ tilts low strikes (puts) richer than high strikes (calls) — the shape common in FX risk-off and equity markets.

🌊

ν — curvature

Controls how U-shaped the smile is around the money. Low ν stays nearly flat; high ν fattens both wings symmetrically, largely independent of skew.

The next two slides isolate exactly two of these effects: drag through what β alone does to the backbone, then drag ρ and ν together and watch the smile itself tilt and curve live.

Section 4

β — percentage moves, or absolute moves?

Hold α fixed at 10% and ask: as the forward itself moves to a different level, how does ATM vol move with it? That curve is the "backbone."

Try it
Fₜ^β = Fₜ — percentage moves are what matter, exactly like geometric Brownian motion in Black-Scholes. The backbone is flat: ATM vol barely moves even as the forward moves a long way. FX desks commonly run β at or near 1.
A CIR/square-root-style scaling — vol moves opposite the forward, but gently. An interpolation between the two extremes, occasionally used as a middle-ground convention.
Fₜ^β = 1 — arithmetic Brownian motion; vol is an absolute quantity, unaffected by the forward's level. The backbone slopes down steeply. The "normal model" convention parts of the rates market lean on when yields sit near or below zero, where a fractional power of a negative rate isn't even defined.
All three curves hold α = 10% at today's forward and ignore the small T-scaled correction terms — this isolates β's effect on its own.

Section 6

The Hagan formula: pure algebra, not a Fourier integral

Dense-looking, but every piece is an exponent, a logarithm, or one square root.

Three factors, multiplied together
α ÷ (FK)^((1−β)/2)
×
z ÷ x(z)
×
[1 + (…) T]
The moneyness-adjusted level, the term carrying the smile's characteristic shape, and a small time-scaled correction. No integral, no numerical inversion, anywhere.
1

(FK)^((1−β)/2)

A moneyness scaling factor that collapses to exactly 1 when β = 1 — one reason β = 1 is a convenient simplifying convention.

z

z = (ν/α)·(FK)^((1−β)/2)·ln(F/K)

Carries ν's effect into the formula. Vanishes exactly at K = F, where log-moneyness ln(F/K) is zero.

x

x(z), built from ρ

z ⁄ x(z) → 1 exactly at the money, and is the ratio primarily responsible for the smile's shape away from it.

T

[1 + (…) T]

The ρ/ν-dependent correction, scaled by time to expiry — flatter for short-dated options, more pronounced for long-dated ones.

Set K = F and the general formula collapses to an at-the-money case: σATM ≈ (α ÷ F^(1−β)) × [1 + (…) T]. With β fixed by convention, that leaves exactly three unknowns — α, ρ, ν — matched against the three quotes an FX desk already watches: the ATM vol, the 25-delta risk reversal (skew), and the 25-delta butterfly (curvature). Three unknowns, three constraints, one fast, well-posed fit.

Section 8

SABR vs. Heston — different questions, not a rematch

SABR
  • Closed-form Hagan approximation — pure algebra, essentially free to evaluate
  • α, ρ, ν fit in a small, fast root-find (β fixed by convention)
  • No mean reversion in its own volatility process — a deliberate simplification
  • Industry-standard tool for quoting and interpolating FX and rates smiles, refit continuously
Heston
  • Genuine mean-reverting (CIR) variance process, with its own vol-of-vol and correlation
  • Needs a characteristic function evaluated and numerically Fourier-inverted for one price
  • Calibration repeats that numerical chain for every trial parameter set — slow
  • More dynamically complete; matters more where the path, not just today's snapshot, is priced
Neither wins in the abstract. Heston gets the risk-neutral dynamics right at the cost of a slow, numerical calibration. SABR trades away some of that dynamical rigor for a formula fast enough to refit continuously against a live, moving market.

Re-solved again, and again, every time spot ticks — because each evaluation is pure algebra, not a Fourier inversion. That's the entire practical difference this lesson is about.

FX and rates options markets don't trade a continuous strike ladder — only a handful of standard, liquid points (the at-the-money straddle, the 25-delta risk reversal, the 25-delta butterfly). Everything else a client asks for has to be interpolated or extrapolated in a way that doesn't introduce arbitrage. SABR, fit to those liquid points, is the industry's default tool for producing that full curve — precisely because refitting it is cheap enough to run continuously rather than once a day.

Section 10

Where the formula — and the model — can mislead you

The formula is an approximation, not exact

Hagan's formula is a singular-perturbation asymptotic expansion — excellent for typical, liquid strikes and moderate maturities, but known to degrade, and in some regimes to imply outright arbitrage (a non-monotonic or locally negative implied density), at very low strikes, very long maturities, or unusually high ν. "Arbitrage-Free SABR" (2014) patches this numerically, via a PDE approach, for exactly the corners where the closed form breaks down.

Where this needs qualifying

A good same-day fit isn't the same as a correct model of smile dynamics over time — SABR refit fresh each day can reproduce today's shape well without its backbone (β) correctly predicting how the whole smile should shift as the forward moves, a documented critique in the literature. And F is a forward: the right forward or swap measure, and the discounting layered on top to turn σ_B into an actual price, are instrument-specific — genuinely outside what this formula covers on its own.

Section 11

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Why does an FX or rates desk reach for SABR instead of Heston to re-quote a smile every time spot ticks?

Heston is arguably the more complete dynamical model — SABR wins on calibration speed, not superior realism. Hagan's formula turns four parameters straight into an implied vol, skipping the characteristic-function/Fourier-inversion chain Heston needs for every trial parameter set an optimizer proposes.
Question 2

Worked example: α≈9.98%, ρ=−0.25, ν=0.40, F=1.2000. Which strike carries the higher implied vol — 1.1800 or 1.2200?

Negative ρ means the forward falling and volatility rising tend to move together, which tilts the smile so low strikes (puts) price richer than high strikes (calls) — a downward-sloping skew.
Question 3

What does β actually control, separately from ρ and ν?

α sets the level and ν sets the curvature of today's snapshot smile; ρ sets its skew. β is a different axis entirely — how the whole ATM vol level would shift if the forward itself moved, e.g. flat (β=1) versus steeply sloped (β=0).
Question 4

Why is β usually fixed by convention rather than calibrated freely alongside ρ?

Different (β, ρ) combinations can reproduce nearly the same fitted smile from the same handful of market quotes. Fixing β by asset-class convention removes that ambiguity and lets ρ alone, unambiguously, carry the skew.
Section 12

What to carry forward

01

SABR trades away some of Heston's dynamical rigor for a closed-form implied-vol formula fast enough to refit continuously against a live market — speed, not superior realism, is the whole point.

02

Four parameters, four separate jobs — α is the level, β is the backbone (vol-vs-forward scaling), ρ is the skew, ν is the curvature.

03

β is almost always fixed by convention (near 1 for FX, often 0 for rates) because it's close to redundant with ρ on skew — fixing it leaves a small, well-identified three-parameter fit against three standard market quotes.

04

The worked example: α≈9.98% reproduces a 10% ATM quote, then one algebraic evaluation each reads off ~9.92% and ~10.09% at strikes 1.667% either side of the forward — no repricing loop.

05

The Hagan formula is an asymptotic approximation, not exact — it can misbehave, even implying arbitrage, at extreme strikes/maturities or high ν, which "Arbitrage-Free SABR" (2014) patches numerically.

06

This repo carries SABR (and Heston) as theory only — no SABR fit, no Heston fit, and no options-pricing engine is wired into a live strategy here today. Verified against the current codebase while building this deck, not carried over unchecked.

What this now lets you do

Read a live FX or rates vol quote sheet (ATM / risk reversal / butterfly) and know exactly which SABR parameter each one pins down. Recognise instantly whether a smile move is a level shift (α), a skew tilt (ρ), a curvature change (ν), or a backbone effect (β). Understand why a SABR calibration finishes almost instantly while a Heston calibration doesn't. And sanity-check a vendor's SABR fit by checking the arbitrage-prone corners — deep out-of-the-money, long-dated, high ν — rather than trusting the closed form blindly everywhere.
