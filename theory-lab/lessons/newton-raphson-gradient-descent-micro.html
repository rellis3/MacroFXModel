When Algebra Runs Out, Iteration Takes Over

Some equations simply don't rearrange — a bond's price doesn't invert into its yield, an option's price doesn't invert into its volatility. Newton-Raphson and gradient descent are the two foundational tricks that answer these questions anyway: not by solving in one clean algebraic move, but by taking a reasonable guess and repeatedly correcting it until the correction becomes too small to matter.

Section 1

Some formulas simply won't invert

A bond's price is a known formula in its yield. There is no algebraic move that turns that formula around and isolates the yield.

💵

Bond yield-to-maturity

Price is easy to compute from a yield. Going backwards — "what yield produces this quoted price?" — has no closed-form answer once there's more than one cash flow.

📈

Option-implied volatility

Black-Scholes gives a price from a volatility, never the reverse. "What σ reproduces this market price?" is the exact same shape of problem, one lesson-tier up.

Recall from earlier in this curriculum that running a formula backwards is exactly what an inverse function does. The catch here is that these particular inverses don't exist in closed form — no rearrangement isolates the yield or the volatility the way "subtract 3, divide by 2" isolated x in a linear equation. When a clean inverse doesn't exist, the fallback is to search for the answer numerically instead of writing it down algebraically.
Where this needs qualifying

Nobody actually runs a bare, unguarded search loop against live pricing. Production solvers wrap the core idea in guardrails — a sensible starting guess, a capped step size, a fallback method if things go sideways. The next several slides build up exactly why those guardrails are needed.

Section 2

Follow the tangent line down to zero

Newton-Raphson's whole trick, in one sentence: look at the slope right where you're standing, and walk along that straight line — not the curve — until it hits zero.

The move
xₙ
→ walk the tangent line to its zero →
xₙ₊₁
Because a smooth curve looks almost exactly like its own tangent line very close to a root, this gets you dramatically closer with each step — as long as you're already reasonably close and the curve isn't doing anything strange (like going nearly flat) near where you're standing.

Setting the tangent line's equation to zero and solving for the crossing point gives the whole method in one formula: xₙ₊₁ = xₙ − f(xₙ) / f′(xₙ). Repeat with the new xₙ, and stop once the correction becomes negligibly small.

Section 3

Three pieces, one correction

f(xₙ)

How far off you are

The current guess's error — how far the function is from the zero you're hunting for. Zero here means "found it."

f′(xₙ)

The local slope

How fast f is changing right at xₙ — this is what turns "how far off" into "how far to move," and it's also the method's single point of failure.

xₙ₊₁

The new guess

Current guess minus the correction f(xₙ)/f′(xₙ). Feed it back in and repeat until the correction is tiny.

Because the correction divides by the slope, a large slope means a small, careful correction, while a slope near zero means a huge, possibly wild one. Nearly everything about when this method works — and when it doesn't — traces back to that one division.
Section 4

Watch it converge, geometrically

f(x) = x² − 4 has a root at x = 2. Starting from x₀ = 3, three tangent-line steps land within a hundred-thousandth of it.

Hand-verified: 3 → 2.166667 → 2.006410 → 2.000010
Each tangent line (blue, then amber, then green) is drawn straight from the curve down to the axis — exactly the geometric move the formula computes. Notice how much the error shrinks each step: 1.0 → 0.167 → 0.0064 → 0.00001. That's quadratic convergence — correct digits roughly double every iteration, once you're close enough to the root.

Section 6

Where Newton-Raphson fails

⚡

A near-zero derivative

The correction f(xₙ)/f′(xₙ) explodes when f′(xₙ) is tiny — the tangent line is nearly flat, so its zero-crossing can be enormously far away.

📍

A bad starting point

Far from the root, the tangent line is a poor stand-in for the actual curve — the method can overshoot wildly or land on a completely different root than intended.

🔁

An infinite cycle

As shown on the last slide, certain starting points can send the method bouncing between the same two values forever, never settling anywhere.

Production pricing engines almost never run pure, unguarded Newton-Raphson. The standard fix: start from a problem-informed guess (the coupon rate for a bond near par; an approximation formula for implied vol), cap how large a single step is allowed to be, and fall back to a slower but guaranteed-to-converge method like bisection whenever a step leaves a known bracket around the true root.

Section 8

A different question: not "where's zero," but "where's smallest"

Gradient descent solves a related but distinct problem — minimizing a function instead of finding where it crosses zero.

↗

The gradient

The direction (and steepness) of the fastest local increase. In one dimension it's just f′(x); in many, it's a vector of partial derivatives.

↘

Step the opposite way

Since the gradient points uphill, stepping in the negative gradient direction moves downhill — toward smaller values of f.

α

The learning rate

How far to step each time, chosen in advance: xₙ₊₁ = xₙ − α·∇f(xₙ). The one knob you get to turn — and the subject of the next slide.

Picture standing on a hilly landscape, blindfolded, trying to reach the lowest point. You can feel which direction is steepest-downhill right under your feet — that's the gradient — so you take a step that way. Newton-Raphson could see the whole tangent line; gradient descent can only feel the slope, one step at a time.

Section 10

What if you also knew the curvature?

Gradient descent
  • Uses only first-derivative (slope) information
  • The step size α is hand-chosen, and has to be tuned
  • Cheap per step — this is why it scales to millions of parameters
Newton's method for optimization
  • Also uses curvature — the second derivative, or the Hessian in many dimensions
  • xₙ₊₁ = xₙ − H⁻¹∇f(xₙ) picks direction and step size automatically
  • Expensive per step — inverting a p×p Hessian is costly once p is large

For f(x) = (x − 3)², the second derivative f″(x) = 2 everywhere, so Newton's step is xₙ₊₁ = xₙ − f′(xₙ)/2 — exactly the α = 0.5 case from the slider. That's not a coincidence: for a perfectly bowl-shaped function, Newton's automatically-chosen step is the one learning rate that reaches the minimum in a single move, because it reads the curvature directly instead of guessing it.

Why gradient descent still wins at scale

Forming and inverting a Hessian for a model with millions of parameters — a modern neural network, say — is computationally infeasible. That's exactly why large-scale machine learning still trains on gradient-descent variants (with momentum, or adaptive per-parameter rates like Adam) rather than pure Newton's method, despite Newton's faster convergence per iteration.

Section 11

How this actually gets used — and misused

✅

Trusting the output without checking the root

A bad starting guess or a function with multiple roots can make the method converge quickly and confidently to the wrong answer — fast convergence isn't the same claim as "converged to what you wanted."

🎚

Picking one learning rate and never revisiting it

A rate that works well on one calibration problem can be far too large or small for a different one — exactly why adaptive methods (AdaGrad, RMSProp, Adam) exist.

🏔

Mistaking convergence for correctness

A zero gradient only guarantees a stationary point — on a non-convex function that could be a local minimum far worse than the true global one. The next lesson is entirely about when that distinction stops mattering.

Section 12

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Geometrically, what does one Newton-Raphson step actually compute?

That's the entire derivation: set the tangent line's equation to zero and solve for the crossing point — that crossing point is xₙ₊₁.
Question 2

Why can Newton-Raphson fail badly when f′(xₙ) is close to zero?

A near-flat tangent line has a zero-crossing that can sit enormously far from where you're standing — this is the "flat derivative" failure mode, the same one behind a deep out-of-the-money option's implied-vol solve going wrong (vega near zero).
Question 3

On f(x) = (x − 3)², what happens with a learning rate just past α = 0.5?

The update simplifies to xₙ₊₁ − 3 = (1 − 2α)(xₙ − 3). Just past α = 0.5, that factor turns slightly negative — each step overshoots to the other side, but since |1 − 2α| is still under 1, the error keeps shrinking in magnitude.
Question 4

An implied-vol solver reports 412% for a deep out-of-the-money option. What's the most likely cause?

Deep out-of-the-money options barely react to volatility — vega, the derivative Newton-Raphson divides by here, is close to zero. That's the flat-derivative failure mode applied directly to a real pricing formula.
Question 5

Gradient descent converges — the final gradient is essentially zero. Does that guarantee the global minimum was found?

A zero gradient marks a stationary point — a local minimum, a local maximum, or a saddle. Whether a local minimum is automatically the global one depends on convexity, the subject of the next lesson in this category.
Section 13

What to carry forward

01

Some formulas don't invert algebraically — bond yield and implied volatility are the classic examples — so an iterative numerical search takes the place of a closed-form solution.

02

Newton-Raphson walks the tangent line to its zero: xₙ₊₁ = xₙ − f(xₙ)/f′(xₙ), converging quadratically (digits roughly double each step) once it's close to a root.

03

That speed is fragile — a bad starting guess, a near-zero derivative, or a function with multiple roots can send it into a wild jump or an endless cycle instead of the answer.

04

Gradient descent solves a related but different problem — minimizing, not root-finding — by stepping a fixed distance α downhill along the negative gradient: xₙ₊₁ = xₙ − α∇f(xₙ).

05

The learning rate α is a genuine tradeoff: too small converges reliably but slowly; too large overshoots, oscillates, or diverges outright — there's no universally "safe" default.

06

Newton's method for optimization bridges both ideas by also using curvature (the Hessian) to pick the step automatically — faster per iteration, but too expensive to invert at the scale of a modern machine-learning model.

What this now lets you do

Read what an iterative solver is actually doing under the hood, and recognize when its output should be distrusted — didn't converge, oscillating, or landed on an implausible root — rather than trusting a returned number by default. Understand why "learning rate" is the single most-discussed hyperparameter in machine learning: it's the same tradeoff, at massive scale, derived here on a one-line quadratic.
