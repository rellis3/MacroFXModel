Transfer Entropy & Information-Theoretic Causality

Granger causality asks whether one series' past linearly improves a forecast of another. Transfer entropy asks the same directional question without assuming the relationship is linear at all — and comes with the same hard warning attached: it measures predictive information flow, never causation in the formal sense.

CONCEPT — background math, not built or tested anywhere in this repo ~21 min read Prereqs: Entropy, KL Divergence & Mutual Information; ARIMA & VAR (for the Granger causality bridge)
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • Granger causality (see ARIMA & VAR) tests whether \(X\)'s lags significantly improve a linear forecast of \(Y\) beyond \(Y\)'s own history — useful, but structurally blind to nonlinear predictive relationships.
  • Transfer entropy is the nonlinear generalization: \(TE_{X\to Y} = I(Y_t\,;\,X_{t-1} \mid Y_{t-1})\) — the mutual information between \(Y\)'s future and \(X\)'s past, with \(Y\)'s own past held fixed (conditioned out), so only the genuinely new information \(X\) adds is counted.
  • Transfer entropy is directional and generally asymmetric: \(TE_{X\to Y} \ne TE_{Y\to X}\) — that asymmetry is exactly what gives it a causality-like signal, the same way Granger causality's directionality does.
  • The worked example below constructs an exact, fully-derived case — \(X\) is pure noise driving \(Y\) through a noisy one-step channel — where \(TE_{X\to Y}\approx 0.531\) bits while \(TE_{Y\to X}=0\) bits exactly, real numbers from a real (if synthetic) process, not fitted or estimated from a sample.
  • Mandatory caveat: transfer entropy detects predictive information flow — it is not causation in the formal, interventionist (Pearl do-calculus) sense. An unobserved common driver can produce a nonzero, asymmetric transfer entropy with no direct causal link at all, exactly as with Granger causality.
  • How it reached trading: within two years of Schreiber's 2000 physics paper it was measuring information flow between stock indices (Marschinski & Kantz, 2002), as a nonlinear extension of Granger-style lead-lag tests.

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

I want to know which market moves first. I've been using Granger causality. Why would I need anything else?

Granger asks whether one series' past improves a straight-line forecast of another. If the link only shows up above a threshold, or only in big moves, a straight line can miss it completely.

So what's the alternative?

Transfer entropy. It asks the same question with information instead of a regression. Once I already know Y's own past, does X's past tell me anything new about Y's future?

Why hold Y's own past fixed?

Because most series predict themselves a bit. Think of mood. A good mood today makes a good mood tomorrow likely, whatever the weather. You only credit the weather with what it adds on top.

Give me a number.

Let X be fair coin flips, a fresh one every day. Let Y today copy yesterday's X, except that one bit in ten gets flipped by noise. Before you look at X, Y today is a full bit of uncertainty. Once you know X yesterday, only the noise is left, about point four seven of a bit. So X tells you about point five three bits about Y.

And the other way round?

Zero, exactly. X is a brand-new coin flip each day. Nothing in anyone's past helps predict it. So you get a big number one way and nothing the other way. That lopsidedness is the whole point.

So X causes Y.

Careful. Here it does, because we built it that way. In real data, a hidden driver can hit both series with different delays. Picture a risk-off shock that reaches one market a little sooner. You'd see the same one-way pattern with no direct link at all.

So what is it actually good for?

A nonlinear check on a lead-lag result. Within two years of the 2000 paper that introduced it, Marschinski and Kantz were using it to measure information flow between the Dow Jones and the DAX. And for normally distributed, linear data it gives the same answer as Granger, so it only earns its extra data cost when the link is nonlinear.

Anything that trips people up?

Estimating it. The coin example is exact. Real data needs binning choices, lots of history, and a test against shuffled series, or small samples will show you flows that aren't there.

One line?

Transfer entropy tells you whose past helps predict whose future, in any shape, but it never tells you who is pushing whom.

Section 01

Why this matters

The ARIMA & VAR lesson built Granger causality: fit a restricted VAR using only \(Y\)'s own lags, fit an unrestricted one adding \(X\)'s lags too, and run an F-test on whether the added lags significantly improve the fit. That test is exactly as powerful as the model underneath it — and the model underneath it is linear. If \(X\)'s past predicts \(Y\)'s future through a threshold, an interaction, or any other nonlinear channel, a linear VAR's coefficients can come back statistically indistinguishable from zero even though real, usable predictive information is sitting right there in the data — the identical structural blind spot the previous lesson's \(Y=X^2\) example demonstrated for correlation.

Transfer entropy asks the Granger-causality question — does \(X\)'s past add real predictive content for \(Y\), beyond \(Y\)'s own history — using mutual information instead of a linear regression's R². Because mutual information catches any form of dependence, not just a linear one, transfer entropy is the model-free generalization of the same directional idea: not "does adding \(X\)'s lags improve a linear fit of \(Y\)," but "does knowing \(X\)'s past reduce genuine uncertainty about \(Y\)'s future, beyond what \(Y\)'s own past already tells you." Both are directional, predictive-improvement tests — transfer entropy simply drops the linearity assumption Granger causality is built on.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Does the weather report tell you anything new about tomorrow's mood, once you already know today's?

Suppose you want to know whether the weather affects someone's mood. It's not enough to check whether weather and mood are related in general — moods have their own momentum; a good mood today makes a good mood tomorrow more likely regardless of weather. The right question is narrower: once you already know today's mood, does also knowing today's weather help you predict tomorrow's mood any better? If yes — if the weather adds something mood's own momentum didn't already give you — that's the transfer-entropy question, answered honestly: genuinely new predictive information flowing from weather into mood, with mood's own self-predictability factored out first so you don't mistake "mood predicts itself" for "weather predicts mood."

The direction matters and doesn't have to run both ways. Weather plausibly affects mood more than mood affects weather (nobody's grumpiness changes tomorrow's forecast) — a real, asymmetric, one-directional flow of predictive information. Transfer entropy is built specifically to measure that asymmetry: how much genuinely new information moves from \(X\)'s past into \(Y\)'s future, versus how much moves the other way, computed as two separate numbers that are allowed to (and usually do) disagree.

How it reached trading

Who leads whom, when the link isn't linear

Transfer entropy · Schreiber, 2000 → financial lead-lag from Marschinski & Kantz, 2002

Lead-lag between markets was measured with Granger's 1969 test: does X's past improve a linear forecast of Y? That misses links that only appear in the tails, in volatility, or above a threshold, and mutual information, which does catch nonlinear dependence, cannot say which way the influence runs.

Schreiber's 2000 measure fixed both, and finance took it up within two years: Marschinski & Kantz (2002) built an improved estimator and applied it to information flow between the Dow Jones and DAX indices. Through the 2010s it became a standard tool in financial econometrics and econophysics for mapping flows between stock indices, credit and bond markets, and later crypto assets.

On a desk it is a nonlinear cross-check on a Granger result (which market moves first, and does that hold without assuming linearity?) and a way to draw directed edges for spillover networks. It needs a lot of data and a significance test against shuffled series.

Section 02

The math, step by step

Watch the formula build
Where transfer entropy comes from, one term at a time
\[ I(Y_t\,;\,\color{#fbbf24}{X_{t-1}}) \]

Start with plain mutual information: how much does \(X\)'s past tell you about \(Y\)'s future? On its own this cannot tell new information apart from things \(Y\) already knew about itself.

\[ TE_{X\to Y} = I(Y_t\,;\,X_{t-1} \color{#fbbf24}{\mid Y_{t-1}}) \]

Hold \(Y\)'s own past fixed. Now only the information \(X\) adds beyond \(Y\)'s own memory is counted. That conditional mutual information is transfer entropy.

\[ TE_{\color{#fbbf24}{Y\to X}} = I(\color{#fbbf24}{X_t}\,;\,\color{#fbbf24}{Y_{t-1}} \mid \color{#fbbf24}{X_{t-1}}) \]

Swap the roles for the reverse direction. It conditions on a different series' past, so it is a different calculation and need not give the same number.

\[ TE_{X\to Y} = 1 - \color{#fbbf24}{H(\varepsilon)} = 1 - H(0.1) \approx 0.531 \text{ bits} \]

Plug in the worked example. \(Y_t\) is one full bit of uncertainty; knowing \(X_{t-1}\) leaves only the channel noise, \(H(0.1) \approx 0.469\) bits.

\[ \color{#10b981}{TE_{X\to Y} \approx 0.531 \text{ bits} \;\neq\; TE_{Y\to X} = 0 \text{ bits}} \]

\(X\) is fresh coin flips, so nothing in the past predicts it and the reverse direction is exactly zero. A one-way flow of predictive information, which is still not proof of a cause.

From Granger causality to a conditional mutual information

Recall Granger causality's operational definition from the ARIMA & VAR lesson: \(X\) "Granger-causes" \(Y\) if adding \(X\)'s lags to a regression of \(Y\) on its own lags significantly improves the fit. Rewrite that same idea using entropy instead of regression R². \(Y_t\)'s own history already carries some predictive power over \(Y_t\) — measurable as the mutual information between \(Y_t\) and \(Y_{t-1}\), \(I(Y_t; Y_{t-1})\). The question Granger causality asks is whether \(X_{t-1}\) adds anything beyond that. The natural information-theoretic version of "beyond" is conditioning: hold \(Y_{t-1}\) fixed, and ask how much mutual information remains between \(Y_t\) and \(X_{t-1}\) once \(Y_{t-1}\)'s own contribution has been accounted for. That conditional mutual information is transfer entropy:

\[ TE_{X\to Y} = I(Y_t\,;\,X_{t-1} \mid Y_{t-1}) \]

Every symbol here was already defined in the previous lesson — transfer entropy adds nothing new to the notation, only a conditioning term. Recall conditional mutual information generalizes the ordinary mutual information from the previous lesson exactly the way conditional entropy generalizes entropy: it's the mutual information you'd compute within each fixed value of \(Y_{t-1}\), averaged over how likely each value of \(Y_{t-1}\) is:

\[ I(Y_t;X_{t-1}\mid Y_{t-1}) = \sum_{y_{t-1}} p(y_{t-1}) \sum_{y_t,x_{t-1}} p(y_t,x_{t-1}\mid y_{t-1}) \log_2\frac{p(y_t,x_{t-1}\mid y_{t-1})}{p(y_t\mid y_{t-1})\,p(x_{t-1}\mid y_{t-1})} \]
p(y_{t-1})how likely each value of Y's past is — the outer average
inner sumordinary mutual information between Y_t and X_{t-1}, computed only within that slice of Y_{t-1}

Like ordinary mutual information, \(TE_{X\to Y} \ge 0\) always (it is, at its core, an average of KL divergences, each individually nonnegative by Gibbs' inequality), with equality exactly when \(X_{t-1}\) adds nothing to predicting \(Y_t\) beyond what \(Y_{t-1}\) already provides.

The directional asymmetry — why this behaves like a causality signal

Define the reverse quantity the same way, with the roles of \(X\) and \(Y\) swapped:

\[ TE_{Y\to X} = I(X_t\,;\,Y_{t-1} \mid X_{t-1}) \]

Nothing about the definitions forces \(TE_{X\to Y}\) and \(TE_{Y\to X}\) to be equal — and in general, they aren't. Unlike ordinary mutual information (which is symmetric, proved in the previous lesson), transfer entropy conditions each direction on a different series' own past — \(TE_{X\to Y}\) holds \(Y_{t-1}\) fixed, \(TE_{Y\to X}\) holds \(X_{t-1}\) fixed — so the two quantities are genuinely different computations, not the same number viewed from two angles. That built-in asymmetry is exactly what gives transfer entropy a causality-like character: a real driving relationship running from \(X\) to \(Y\) but not the reverse should show up as \(TE_{X\to Y} > 0\) alongside \(TE_{Y\to X} \approx 0\) — a clean directional signature a symmetric measure like ordinary mutual information could never produce, no matter how it was computed.

\(TE_{X\to Y}\) measures only the blue arrow — new information from \(X\)'s past that improves predicting \(Y\)'s future — with \(Y\)'s own memory (the gray arrow, dashed box) held fixed so it isn't double-counted. Worked example

A noisy one-way channel, computed exactly — not estimated from a sample

Construct a simple process where the true direction of information flow is known by construction, so the transfer entropy calculation can be checked exactly rather than estimated from noisy data. Let \(X_t\) be i.i.d. fair-coin flips (\(0\) or \(1\), each with probability \(0.5\), completely independent across time — \(X\) has no memory of its own past at all). Let \(Y_t = X_{t-1} \oplus N_t\) (XOR), where \(N_t\) is an independent "flip" with probability \(\varepsilon = 0.1\) (a noisy channel: 90% of the time \(Y_t\) faithfully copies \(X_{t-1}\); 10% of the time the bit is flipped in transmission).

Direction 1 — does \(X\)'s past inform \(Y\)'s future, beyond \(Y\)'s own past? Because \(X_t\) is i.i.d. across time, \(Y_{t-1}\) (which depends only on \(X_{t-2}\) and \(N_{t-1}\)) carries no information about \(X_{t-1}\) at all — conditioning on \(Y_{t-1}\) changes nothing here, so \(TE_{X\to Y}\) reduces to the ordinary mutual information between \(Y_t\) and \(X_{t-1}\), which is exactly the mutual information of a binary symmetric channel with flip probability \(\varepsilon\):

\[ TE_{X\to Y} = I(Y_t;X_{t-1}) = H(Y_t) - H(Y_t\mid X_{t-1}) = 1 - H(\varepsilon) = 1 - H(0.1) \approx 1 - 0.469 = 0.531 \text{ bits} \]

(\(H(Y_t)=1\) bit because \(Y_t\) inherits a fair 50/50 marginal distribution from \(X_{t-1}\) XORed with independent noise; \(H(Y_t\mid X_{t-1}) = H(\varepsilon)\), the binary entropy of the channel's flip probability, computed exactly in the previous lesson's chart.)

Direction 2 — does \(Y\)'s past inform \(X\)'s future, beyond \(X\)'s own past? By construction, \(X_t\) is pure i.i.d. noise — it has no memory of anything, including its own past and certainly including \(Y\)'s past. So:

\[ TE_{Y\to X} = I(X_t;Y_{t-1}\mid X_{t-1}) = 0 \text{ bits, exactly} \]

\(TE_{X\to Y}\approx 0.531\) bits, \(TE_{Y\to X}=0\) bits exactly — a real, provable, one-directional information flow, constructed so the arithmetic is exact rather than estimated. This is what the "directional asymmetry" claim above looks like as an actual number, not an assertion.

The mandatory caveat: transfer entropy measures whether \(X\)'s past carries predictive information about \(Y\)'s future that isn't already in \(Y\)'s own past. It is not causation in the formal, interventionist sense (Judea Pearl's do-calculus: "if I intervened and forced \(X\) to a different value, would \(Y\) change"). A nonzero, asymmetric transfer entropy is fully consistent with there being no direct causal link at all — an unobserved common driver hitting both series with slightly different timing produces exactly this signature, the same failure mode already flagged for Granger causality. Treating a transfer-entropy result as proof of a causal mechanism, rather than as "X's history carries predictive content for Y," is a textbook overclaim.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

Transfer entropy was introduced by Thomas Schreiber (2000) explicitly as a model-free alternative to Granger causality for nonlinear systems, and a foundational later result (Barnett, Barrett & Seth, 2009) proved that for jointly Gaussian variables under a linear model, transfer entropy and Granger causality are mathematically equivalent up to a constant — which is a genuinely useful fact, not a coincidence: it confirms transfer entropy is a strict generalization, reducing exactly to the linear test in the case where linearity actually holds, and adding real detection power only when it doesn't. In neuroscience and physiology, transfer entropy between recorded signals (EEG channels, heart-rate and respiration) is a standard tool for mapping directional information flow between subsystems where the underlying dynamics are known to be nonlinear. In econometrics and quantitative finance, it has been used as a complement to Granger-causality-based lead-lag studies precisely where a linear VAR is suspected to be too restrictive a model for the true relationship — a candidate widening of the same yield-spread/FX lead-lag question the ARIMA & VAR lesson's scenario poses, done without pre-committing to linearity.

Real-world trading scenario

Widening the yield-spread/FX lead-lag question beyond linearity

The ARIMA & VAR lesson's scenario poses a VAR(2)/Granger-causality test on the DE–US yield spread and EUR/USD, asking whether the spread's lags significantly improve a linear forecast of FX returns. Suppose that test came back weak or null — no significant linear Granger-causal relationship in either direction. Before concluding "the spread carries no predictive information for FX at all," a researcher following this lesson's logic would ask a narrower, honest follow-up: is the true relationship perhaps nonlinear — for instance, the spread matters only once it moves beyond some threshold, or its effect on FX depends on the sign of a third, unmodeled variable (say, a broader risk-sentiment regime) — in a way a linear VAR simply cannot represent? Computing \(TE_{\text{spread}\to\text{FX}}\) and \(TE_{\text{FX}\to\text{spread}}\) directly would answer that question without assuming linearity first. A transfer entropy result that comes back clearly asymmetric and nonzero, where the linear Granger test came back null, would be a genuinely different and more informative finding than the linear result alone — though it would still need the same honest treatment: a real out-of-sample check, and the causal caveat above stated plainly, before it is treated as anything more than "the spread's history carries predictive content for FX that a linear model missed." No such test has been run in this repo; this is an illustration of what the tool is for, not a result.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where this goes wrong in practice

"Transfer entropy" being read as "proof of causation." This is the single most common overclaim with this tool, and it is exactly what the mandatory caveat above exists to prevent. A nonzero, directional transfer entropy is a real, useful, model-free predictive-flow finding — treating it as equivalent to a controlled experiment's causal claim is not.

Confounding by an unobserved common driver. If a third, unmeasured process drives both \(X\) and \(Y\) with slightly different timing (a shared regime shock, a macro surprise both series react to at different speeds), transfer entropy can register a clean, asymmetric, nonzero result in one direction with no direct link between \(X\) and \(Y\) at all — structurally the same failure mode as Granger causality's confounding problem, restated for a nonlinear measure rather than solved by it.

Estimation bias on finite, real samples. This lesson's worked example is exact by construction, specifically to make the arithmetic checkable. Estimating transfer entropy from a real, finite time series requires choosing a binning scheme, embedding dimension, or kernel-density estimator for the joint distributions involved — every one of those choices introduces estimation bias, and a naive estimator can report a spuriously large transfer entropy purely from overfitting a small sample's joint density, the identical concern already flagged for mutual information in the previous lesson.

Ignoring lag structure. The definition above uses a single lag (\(X_{t-1}\), \(Y_{t-1}\)) for simplicity, matching this lesson's worked example. A real relationship may operate at a different or multi-step lag; picking the wrong lag can hide a genuine directional flow the same way picking the wrong VAR order can hide a genuine linear one.

Scenario drill · an illustrative research review

Granger says nothing, transfer entropy says something

You tested whether the DE–US yield spread leads EUR/USD. The linear Granger test came back null in both directions. A colleague then estimated transfer entropy on the same daily data and found a clearly positive flow from the spread to EUR/USD, with almost nothing the other way. What do you do with the result?

  1. Write it up as "the spread causes EUR/USD" and size a trade on it.
    Transfer entropy measures predictive information flow, not cause. A shared driver, such as a change in risk sentiment that reaches the bond market slightly before FX, could produce exactly this one-way pattern. And nothing has been checked out of sample yet.
  2. Ignore it. The linear test was null, so the transfer entropy must be noise.
    A null linear test is exactly the case where transfer entropy can add something: a threshold or regime-dependent link that a straight line cannot represent. Throwing it out unchecked repeats the blind spot the tool exists to fix.
  3. Test it against shuffled series, recheck it out of sample and at other lags, and look for a common driver that could hit both.
    This treats the result as what it is: a candidate nonlinear lead-lag finding. The shuffle test guards against estimation bias, the out-of-sample check guards against overfitting, and the confound check keeps the claim at "predicts", not "causes".
  4. Accept it as "the spread's history predicts EUR/USD beyond its own history" and go straight to a backtest.
    The wording is honest, but you have skipped the significance test. Real-data transfer entropy estimates are biased upwards on finite samples, so the flow may not be there at all.

What the lesson says. This is an illustrative case built on the lesson's own spread and FX scenario, not a reported result. A clearly asymmetric transfer entropy where the linear test was null would be a more informative finding than the linear result alone, but it still needs a real out-of-sample check, a test against shuffled data, and the causal caveat stated plainly before it counts as anything more than predictive content a linear model missed.

Key Takeaways

✓Transfer entropy is Granger causality's nonlinear generalization. \(TE_{X\to Y}=I(Y_t;X_{t-1}\mid Y_{t-1})\) asks the same "does X's past add predictive value beyond Y's own history" question, using mutual information instead of a linear regression's fit.
✓It reduces exactly to Granger causality in the linear/Gaussian case. Barnett, Barrett & Seth (2009) proved the equivalence — transfer entropy adds detection power only when the true relationship is genuinely nonlinear.
✓The directional asymmetry is the whole point. \(TE_{X\to Y}\ne TE_{Y\to X}\) in general — the worked example proves it exactly: 0.531 bits one way, 0 bits the other, by construction.
✗Transfer entropy is not causation in the formal sense — full stop. It measures predictive information flow, never a Pearl-style interventionist causal claim. An unobserved common driver can produce a clean, asymmetric, nonzero result with zero direct causal link.
⚠Real-sample estimation is harder than this lesson's exact example. Binning/kernel choices for a finite sample introduce estimation bias — treat any reported transfer entropy number from real data with the same skepticism as an estimated mutual information.

Self-test

Q1. Write the definition of \(TE_{X\to Y}\) and explain in one sentence what each part of the conditioning does.
\(TE_{X\to Y} = I(Y_t; X_{t-1} \mid Y_{t-1})\) — the mutual information between \(Y\)'s future value and \(X\)'s past value, computed with \(Y\)'s own past held fixed. Holding \(Y_{t-1}\) fixed (conditioning on it) removes the part of \(Y_t\)'s predictability that comes from \(Y\) predicting itself, so what's left over and attributed to \(X_{t-1}\) is only the genuinely new information \(X\)'s past contributes.
Q2. Why is transfer entropy, unlike ordinary mutual information, generally asymmetric — \(TE_{X\to Y}\ne TE_{Y\to X}\)?
Ordinary mutual information \(I(X;Y)\) is symmetric because both directions condition on nothing — they compare the same joint distribution to the same independence benchmark, just with axes relabeled. Transfer entropy conditions each direction on a different series' own past: \(TE_{X\to Y}\) holds \(Y_{t-1}\) fixed, while \(TE_{Y\to X}\) holds \(X_{t-1}\) fixed. These are two structurally different conditional mutual informations, not the same calculation viewed from two sides, so nothing forces them to agree — and in a genuinely one-directional system (like the worked example's noisy channel), they generally won't.
Q3. In the worked example, why does \(TE_{Y\to X}\) come out to exactly 0, not just "small"?
\(X_t\) was constructed as i.i.d. fair-coin flips — completely independent across time and independent of everything else in the system, including \(Y\)'s past. \(TE_{Y\to X}=I(X_t;Y_{t-1}\mid X_{t-1})\) asks whether \(Y_{t-1}\) carries any predictive information about \(X_t\) beyond \(X_{t-1}\)'s own contribution. Since \(X_t\) is independent of literally everything that came before it by construction, there is no information anywhere in the system's past — from \(X\) or from \(Y\) — that predicts it even slightly better than a coin flip. The conditional mutual information is therefore exactly zero, not approximately zero, because the independence is exact by construction, not merely a limiting case.
Q4. A colleague reports "transfer entropy from the VIX to our FX pair is 0.3 bits and highly asymmetric versus the reverse direction — this proves the VIX causes moves in our pair." What's wrong with that claim?
A nonzero, asymmetric transfer entropy is real evidence of a directional, predictive information flow — it is not evidence of causation in the formal, interventionist sense. A shared, unobserved driver (a broad risk-off event that hits the VIX slightly faster than it hits this particular FX pair, purely due to differences in market microstructure or trading hours) could produce exactly this signature — VIX's past predicting the pair's future beyond the pair's own history, with no reverse relationship — without the VIX doing anything to the pair directly. The honest claim is "the VIX's history carries predictive information about this pair that isn't already captured by the pair's own history" — a real and useful finding, but a narrower one than "causes."
Q5. Why does the Barnett, Barrett & Seth (2009) equivalence result matter for how you should think about transfer entropy versus Granger causality?
It proves transfer entropy is a genuine generalization of Granger causality, not a separate, unrelated concept that happens to share a directional flavor. For jointly Gaussian variables under a linear model, the two measures are mathematically equivalent up to a constant — meaning if the true relationship really is linear, transfer entropy recovers the same answer Granger causality would give. The value transfer entropy adds is specifically in cases where that linearity assumption fails; it costs nothing when the linear model was already adequate; and it can reveal a genuine nonlinear relationship a linear VAR would report as null.
Q6. Why would you expect real-world transfer entropy estimates to be noisier and more prone to false positives than the worked example above?
The worked example computes transfer entropy exactly, because the joint and conditional probability distributions of the constructed process are known exactly by design. Real data never comes with known distributions — every real transfer entropy estimate has to first estimate the joint and conditional probability distributions from a finite sample, typically via binning or kernel density estimation, both of which introduce bias, especially with limited data or fine binning. A naive estimator run on a small, noisy sample can report a spuriously large or falsely asymmetric transfer entropy purely from overfitting the estimated joint density to that sample's idiosyncrasies — the identical estimation-bias risk already flagged for mutual information, now compounded by the extra conditioning dimension transfer entropy adds.
