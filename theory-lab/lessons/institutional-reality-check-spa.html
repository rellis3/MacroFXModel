White's Reality Check & Hansen's SPA Test

The Deflated Sharpe Ratio corrects one reported Sharpe for an estimate of how many trials produced it. These two tests skip the estimate entirely — they bootstrap the full return history of every candidate strategy you actually ran, directly against each other.

CONCEPT — this repo's own stationary block bootstrap (statsCore.js) is real, battle-tested machinery, but every use of it in this repo tests ONE strategy/statistic at a time; the one place a best-of-N correction exists (volReversionCore.js, best of 4 horizons) uses a parametric Bonferroni adjustment, not a bootstrap-of-the-max — and a sharper version of this exact problem (best of ~600 configs per pair) is named an unaddressed risk elsewhere in this repo. TL;DR
  • White's Reality Check (2000) tests whether the best of \(N\) candidate strategies truly beats a benchmark, or is just the luckiest of \(N\) noisy draws. The test statistic is \(\bar V = \max_k \bar d_k\), the largest of the \(N\) candidates' own sample mean excess returns.
  • Because strategy returns are autocorrelated over time and often cross-correlated with each other (variations on a theme share the same underlying bets), a textbook normal critical value for a max-of-\(N\) statistic is the wrong tool. WRC instead stationary block-bootstraps the whole panel — the exact machinery this series' own Bootstrapping & Monte Carlo lesson builds, applied here to a maximum across candidates instead of to one series alone.
  • WRC has a known weakness: throwing a pile of obviously-bad candidates into the test widens its bootstrap null more than necessary, costing real statistical power. Hansen's SPA test (2005) fixes exactly that — studentizing each candidate by its own bootstrap-estimated spread, and stopping a clearly-inferior candidate from acting like it "could have won" in the bootstrap world — and is the field's preferred modern successor to WRC, not a rival idea.
  • In this lesson's worked panel of 5 toy strategies, one strategy's raw Sharpe (0.93) looks almost as strong as the genuine edge's (1.19) purely because it happened to realize very low variance — its actual mean edge is a third the size, and a bootstrap test shows it is nowhere close to distinguishable from noise (\(p\approx0.24\)), while the genuine edge survives comfortably.
  • Like the Deflated Sharpe Ratio, both tests are only as honest as the declared candidate set — quietly running 50 variants and only submitting the best 5 to the test defeats the entire exercise, and no formula catches that; it is a discipline problem, not a statistical one.
Section 01

Why this matters

Sharpe, Sortino, Calmar & the Deflated Sharpe Ratio ends on a specific correction: a reported Sharpe ratio, picked as the best of \(N\) variations tried during development, has to clear a chance-inflated benchmark \(SR_0\) — not zero — before it means anything. That correction is powerful, but notice exactly what it needs as input: the count \(N\) of trials run, and the dispersion of the \(N\) resulting Sharpe ratios, \(\sigma_{SR}\). Neither of those inputs is the trials' actual return data. DSR takes a statistical shortcut — model the best-of-\(N\) Sharpe as the maximum of \(N\) roughly Gaussian numbers, plug in a count and a spread, and use extreme value theory to work out what chance alone should produce.

White's Reality Check (Halbert White, 2000) and Hansen's SPA test (Peter R. Hansen, 2005) attack the identical question — "did I just get lucky searching over many strategies?" — from the opposite direction. Instead of summarizing each trial down to one Sharpe ratio and then reasoning about the shape of a maximum, they keep every candidate strategy's full period-by-period return history and directly bootstrap the whole panel — resampling all \(N\) return series together, recomputing the same "who's best right now" statistic on each resample, and building an actual empirical distribution of what the best-of-\(N\) result looks like under pure luck, with zero real edge required anywhere. No Gaussian approximation for the maximum, no extreme-value-theory formula for \(SR_0\) — the bootstrap resampling does that job directly, using the real data's own autocorrelation and cross-strategy correlation instead of an assumed shape.

This is the direct reason both lessons are prerequisites here. DSR gave this series the entire "best of \(N\) is inflated by the search itself" framing and the \(N\), \(T\) notation this lesson reuses in the same roles. Bootstrapping & Monte Carlo already built the stationary block bootstrap this lesson leans on directly — its own \(l\) (block length) and \(B\) (number of resamples) notation carries over unchanged. What's new here is narrow but consequential: the bootstrap gets pointed at a maximum across many candidate series, not a single statistic computed on one series.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Don't guess how lucky the lottery could get — actually run the lottery again

The Deflated Sharpe Ratio's approach is a bit like estimating how likely it is that someone wins a big prize among 50 lottery players by assuming ticket-winning amounts follow a bell curve and computing the expected maximum from the formula for that curve. It's a reasonable shortcut, and it works well when the bell-curve assumption is close enough. White's Reality Check and Hansen's SPA test take a more direct route: they still have all 50 players' actual, specific results sitting right there, so instead of assuming a shape, they literally replay the lottery draw over and over — reshuffling the 50 players' real ticket outcomes (in contiguous chunks, so any streaks of luck within one player's ticket history survive the reshuffle), and each time recording who "wins" — the biggest payout among the 50 — under a world engineered so nobody has a real edge. Do that thousands of times and count how often the replayed lottery's best result is at least as good as what actually happened. That fraction is the answer to "how suspicious should I be of this result" — no bell-curve assumption required, because the real data supplied its own distribution.

Hansen's refinement adds one more piece of realism. Some of the 50 players clearly had terrible tickets — deep in last place, nowhere close to winning. Letting their reshuffled outcomes still compete for "biggest payout" in the replayed lottery, the way White's original method does, wastes some of the test's sensitivity: it's spending statistical power worrying about players who were never realistically going to be the story. Hansen's fix quietly benches the clearly-terrible players from that competition in the replay (while still counting them, honestly, as part of the search that was run) and puts every remaining player's payout on a common footing by dividing out how volatile each one's own results tend to be. The question sharpens from "who had the single biggest number" to "who beat their own normal range of outcomes by the most, once the also-rans are stopped from padding the comparison" — which is a more sensitive, harder-to-fool test of the exact same claim.

Section 02

White's Reality Check, step by step

The setup. There are \(N\) candidate strategies (the parameter sweep, the different lookbacks, the competing signal variants — whatever the actual search produced), each with a return series over the same \(T\) time periods. For each candidate \(k = 1,\dots,N\) and period \(t = 1,\dots,T\), define the excess return over a fixed benchmark — buy-and-hold, zero, or an incumbent strategy already in production:

\[ d_{k,t} = R_{k,t} - R_{t}^{\,bench} \]
d_{k,t}candidate k's excess return over the benchmark, period t
R_{k,t}candidate k's own realized return, period t
R_t^benchthe fixed benchmark's return, same period t

Each candidate's own sample mean excess return is \(\bar d_k = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} d_{k,t}\). White's null hypothesis is that the best candidate's expected excess return is no better than the benchmark's — i.e. every candidate's true, population mean excess return is \(\le 0\), so any apparent winner is just the luckiest draw among \(N\) noisy trials with nothing real behind it. The test statistic is the largest of the \(N\) sample means:

\[ \bar V = \max_{k=1,\dots,N} \bar d_k \]
V̄WRC's test statistic — the best candidate's own sample mean excess return
max_ktaken across every candidate actually run, not just the reported winner

Why an ordinary critical value is the wrong tool here. A single strategy's mean excess return, on its own, could in principle be tested with a textbook t-test — if its returns were independent over time. Two things break that shortcut for \(\bar V\) specifically. First, financial returns are typically autocorrelated (this series' own stationarity, GARCH, and Hurst-exponent material all document exactly this), so the usual \(\sqrt{T}\)-based standard error is wrong on a single series before the max is even taken. Second — and this is the genuinely new problem — \(\bar V\) is a maximum over \(N\) correlated candidates, not one number: strategies built as variations on a theme (adjacent lookback windows, closely related entry filters) share much of the same underlying market exposure, so their sample means move together, not independently. There is no simple closed-form distribution for "the maximum of \(N\) autocorrelated, cross-correlated things" the way there is for a single Gaussian mean. WRC's answer is to stop looking for a formula and instead bootstrap the empirical distribution of \(\bar V\) itself.

The stationary block bootstrap, applied to the max. This is precisely the machinery Bootstrapping & Monte Carlo already built — Politis & Romano's (1994) stationary bootstrap draws block lengths themselves at random from a geometric distribution (mean block length \(l\)) rather than fixing one, resampling contiguous runs so that whatever short-term serial dependence exists inside a real block survives in the resample, exactly as that lesson describes for a single series. WRC applies the identical resampling idea with two specific changes:

1. One shared block-index path, applied to every candidate at once

Rather than bootstrapping each candidate's series separately, WRC draws a single sequence of resampled time-indices and applies it jointly to all \(N\) candidates' returns for that replicate. This preserves not just each candidate's own serial correlation but the cross-candidate correlation too — if two strategies both had a bad week because of the same market move, that joint bad week stays joint in the resample, rather than being scattered independently across candidates.

2. Recentered at zero, then a max is taken, not reported alone

Each candidate's returns are first demeaned by its own sample mean, \(d_{k,t} - \bar d_k\), before resampling — this enforces the null hypothesis directly on the bootstrap world (every candidate has exactly zero true edge in the resampled universe, by construction). A replicate's mean is computed for every candidate from that demeaned, resampled series, and the largest of those \(N\) resampled means becomes one bootstrap replicate of \(\bar V\) itself — matching the maximum-taking step in the real statistic exactly.

Repeating this \(B\) times (the same \(B\) — number of bootstrap replicates — as the Bootstrapping lesson, typically 1,000–10,000+) builds up an empirical null distribution \(\{\bar V^*_1,\dots,\bar V^*_B\}\) for the best-of-\(N\) statistic under "zero true edge everywhere." The bootstrap p-value is the fraction of replicates at least as extreme as the real observed \(\bar V\):

\[ p_{WRC} = \frac{1 + \#\{b : \bar V^*_b \ge \bar V\}}{1 + B} \]
#{...}how many of the B replicates matched or beat the real V̄
+1 / +1the Davison–Hinkley correction — keeps p from reading as exactly 0, the same convention this repo's own block-bootstrap brick (statsCore.js) already uses

A small \(p_{WRC}\) means the best-of-\(N\) result observed in the real data would be rare even under a world engineered so that no candidate has any true edge at all — evidence the apparent winner is more than best-of-luck. A large \(p_{WRC}\) means the observed "best" result is exactly the kind of thing \(N\) noisy, edge-free candidates would produce anyway.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine five kids each flip their own coin 16 times and you write down each kid's number of heads. One kid got the most heads — is that kid's coin secretly weighted? To check without any coin-flipping theory, you could literally hand out five completely fair coins, have five (make-believe) kids flip them the same number of times, write down the best score among THAT batch, and repeat that whole five-kid contest thousands of times. If the "winning" score from thousands of fair-coin contests is almost always at least as good as your real kid's winning score, your real result isn't suspicious — fair coins produce results that good all the time when five kids are competing. That's the entire idea: instead of doing the coin-flipping math by formula, just run the fair-coin contest for real, a lot of times, and see how often it beats what you actually saw.

Section 03

WRC's weak spot, and how Hansen's SPA test fixes it

White's Reality Check is correct, but it has a well-documented practical weakness that shows up the moment a real candidate set includes obviously bad strategies alongside a genuinely promising one — which is almost always, since a real parameter sweep rarely produces \(N\) equally plausible candidates.

The problem: WRC's bootstrap null gets wider than it needs to be. Demeaning every candidate at zero — including the ones whose real performance was clearly, unambiguously terrible — gives every single one of them, in the bootstrap replay, an equal shot at producing the largest resampled mean. A strategy that was obviously dominated on the real data still gets to act, in the resampled world, as if it might have tied for best. The more junk candidates included, the wider the bootstrap distribution of \(\bar V^*\) becomes, which raises the bar the real \(\bar V\) has to clear — lower statistical power to detect a genuinely good strategy, purely because it was tested alongside a pile of obviously-bad ones.

Hansen's SPA test (2005) — "Superior Predictive Ability" — targets this exact weakness with two specific changes, while keeping WRC's core bootstrap machinery intact:

📏

1. Studentize before taking the max

Instead of comparing raw sample means directly, SPA divides each candidate's mean by its own bootstrap-estimated standard error, \(t_k = \bar d_k / s_k\), before taking the maximum across candidates. This puts a low-volatility candidate and a high-volatility candidate on a comparable footing — exactly the same reasoning a Sharpe ratio applies to a single strategy, now applied across the whole candidate set before the "who's best" comparison is made.

🚫

2. Don't let clearly-inferior candidates pad the null

Rather than demeaning every candidate to exactly zero, SPA only gives that "benefit of the doubt" recentering to candidates that weren't clearly, convincingly bad on the real data. A candidate whose own studentized statistic sits meaningfully below zero keeps something closer to its own actual (negative) performance in the bootstrap world instead — denying it the chance to drift up and pretend it could have tied for best purely from resampling noise.

Formally (in the simplified form this lesson uses — Hansen's own paper derives a threshold that shrinks slowly with \(T\) via a law-of-the- iterated-logarithm argument, tighter than the fixed cutoff used here for clarity), each candidate gets its own recentering point \(g_k\):

\[ g_k = \begin{cases} \bar d_k & \text{if } \bar d_k / s_k < -c \quad \text{(clearly inferior — keep its own mean)} \\[4pt] 0 & \text{otherwise (benefit of the doubt — recenter at zero, as WRC does for everyone)} \end{cases} \]
ca fixed cutoff (this lesson uses c=1 own-standard-error below zero) — Hansen's real rule uses a threshold that shrinks with T
g_kwhere candidate k's bootstrap replicates get recentered — 0 for contenders, its own mean for clear losers

and the bootstrap replicate for candidate \(k\) is its resampled deviation from its own realized mean, shifted back by \(g_k\) and studentized by \(s_k\):

\[ Z^*_{k,b} = \frac{\big(\bar d^{\,*}_{k,b} - \bar d_k\big) + g_k}{s_k}\,, \qquad Z^*_b = \max_{k} Z^*_{k,b} \]

Comparing the real observed studentized statistic \(Z = \max_k \bar d_k / s_k\) against the empirical distribution of \(\{Z^*_1,\dots, Z^*_B\}\) gives \(p_{SPA}\), computed the same way as \(p_{WRC}\) above. Because obviously-inferior candidates no longer get to inflate the null the way they do in WRC, \(p_{SPA}\) is typically smaller — more power to detect a real winner — for the exact same underlying data. This is why SPA is described as the modern, generally-preferred successor to WRC rather than a competing, unrelated idea: it reuses WRC's entire bootstrap engine and null-hypothesis framing, and repairs one specific, well- understood source of lost power.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

If you're judging a footrace and three of the ten runners visibly tripped and walked the whole way, still counting their (terrible) times as "maybe they could have won" when you imagine the race being rerun is silly — they clearly couldn't have. Hansen's fix is exactly that: stop giving the obviously-last-place runners a fair shot at "winning" the imaginary rerun, since real evidence already says they weren't competitive, and put every remaining runner's finishing time in context of how consistent that particular runner normally is, not just the raw number of seconds.

\(N\)Number of candidate strategies actually run — same role as the Deflated Sharpe lesson's \(N\).
\(T\)Number of time periods in the shared return history — same role as the Deflated Sharpe lesson's \(T\).
\(d_{k,t}\)Candidate \(k\)'s excess return over the benchmark at period \(t\).
\(\bar d_k\)Candidate \(k\)'s own sample mean excess return over all \(T\) periods.
\(s_k\)Candidate \(k\)'s own sample standard deviation (or bootstrap-estimated spread) of \(d_{k,t}\).
\(\bar V\)White's Reality Check statistic — \(\max_k \bar d_k\), the best candidate's raw mean excess return.
\(Z\)Hansen's SPA statistic — \(\max_k \bar d_k / s_k\), the best candidate's studentized mean excess return.
\(g_k\)SPA's per-candidate recentering point — 0 for a contender, \(\bar d_k\) itself for a clearly-inferior candidate.
\(l\)Mean block length in the stationary bootstrap — same role as the Bootstrapping lesson's \(l\).
\(B\)Number of bootstrap replicates drawn — same role as the Bootstrapping lesson's \(B\).
\(p_{WRC}, p_{SPA}\)The two tests' bootstrap p-values — the fraction of replicates at least as extreme as the real observed statistic.

B — Mean-Reversion (noise)0.30, −0.80, 0.60, −0.40, −0.10, 0.90, −0.60, 0.20, −0.90, 0.50, 0.70, −0.30, −0.50, 0.40, −0.20, 0.10
C — Carry (lucky-Sharpe noise)0.30, 0.00, 0.40, −0.10, 0.20, 0.35, −0.05, 0.25, 0.10, 0.30, −0.15, 0.20, 0.25, 0.05, 0.30, 0.10
D — Breakout (noise)−1.20, 1.50, −0.80, 0.90, −1.60, 1.10, −0.40, 1.30, −1.00, 0.60, −1.40, 0.80, −0.60, 1.00, −1.10, 0.70

Every value is a percent return for that period. Computing each candidate's sample mean \(\bar d_k\), standard deviation \(s_k\), raw (unannualized) Sharpe \(\bar d_k / s_k\), and studentized statistic \(\bar d_k / (s_k/\sqrt{T})\) directly from these 16 numbers each:

A — Momentummean = 0.469, s = 0.394, raw Sharpe = 1.19, t = 4.75
B — Mean-Reversionmean = −0.006, s = 0.552, raw Sharpe = −0.01, t = −0.05
C — Carrymean = 0.156, s = 0.168, raw Sharpe = 0.93, t = 3.72
D — Breakoutmean = −0.013, s = 1.089, raw Sharpe = −0.01, t = −0.05
E — Contrarianmean = −0.163, s = 0.502, raw Sharpe = −0.32, t = −1.30

Notice the trap before running any bootstrap. Strategy C's raw Sharpe (0.93) is the second-highest in the whole panel — comfortably ahead of every other noise strategy and not far behind Momentum's genuine 1.19 — purely because C's realized standard deviation (0.168) happened to come out tiny over this short sample. Its actual mean edge (0.156) is barely a third the size of Momentum's (0.469). A researcher scanning for "best Sharpe" would notice C; the test statistic that actually matters here is the mean, not the ratio.

The observed WRC statistic is the largest of the five means: \(\bar V = \max(0.469,\,-0.006,\,0.156,\,-0.013,\,-0.163) = 0.469\), achieved by Momentum. The observed SPA statistic is the largest of the five t-stats: \(Z = \max(4.75,\,-0.05,\,3.72,\,-0.05,\,-1.30) = 4.75\), also Momentum — this time by a wider margin, since studentizing shrinks Carry's apparent edge over the noise candidates rather than inflating it.

Running the stationary block bootstrap described above — mean block length \(l=2\), demeaning every candidate before resampling, one shared block-index path applied jointly to all five series, \(B=5{,}000\) replicates — on this exact panel gives (one representative run; genuinely fresh randomness each time will land close but not identical):

\[ p_{WRC}(\bar V = 0.469) \approx 0.0028, \qquad p_{SPA}(Z = 4.75) \approx 0.0002 \]

Momentum's edge survives comfortably under both tests, and more confidently under SPA — exactly the power-recovery SPA is built to provide, on data where WRC already succeeds. E (Contrarian, \(t=-1.30\)) is the only candidate this cutoff flags as clearly inferior for SPA's recentering rule; it keeps its own negative mean rather than getting benched at zero, denying it any chance to drift upward in the replay.

Now the punchline for Carry's tempting Sharpe: running that same bootstrap procedure but asking whether Carry's own mean (0.156) clears the best-of-five noise bar gives \(p \approx 0.24\) — not remotely significant. Despite a raw Sharpe that looked almost as strong as the real edge's, Carry's actual mean outperformance is exactly the kind of thing five noise-driven candidates produce by chance anyway. An impressive-looking Sharpe and a statistically real edge are two different claims, and this is precisely the gap the Deflated Sharpe lesson's worked example makes for a single reported Sharpe — here it's made directly from the full candidate panel's own return histories, with a genuine bootstrap standing in for that lesson's extreme-value-theory formula.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where WRC and SPA quietly get misapplied

The declared candidate set has to be the real candidate set. This is the single most important caveat, and it is identical in spirit to the Deflated Sharpe lesson's warning about undercounting \(N\). Running 50 variations, discarding the 45 that looked bad, and submitting only the best 5 to a WRC or SPA test produces a test that is completely worthless — not slightly optimistic, worthless — because the whole method's honesty depends on the bootstrap seeing every candidate that was genuinely in contention, junk included. A test correctly run on a dishonestly curated candidate set will still return a confident-looking p-value; nothing about the arithmetic can detect that the candidate set itself was cherry-picked upstream. This is a discipline problem, not a statistical one, and no later step in the pipeline can fix it after the fact.

Treating a small p-value as proof of a tradable edge. Exactly as the Deflated Sharpe lesson insists a high DSR is evidence, not proof — surviving WRC or SPA says a candidate's outperformance is unlikely to be pure best-of-\(N\) luck on this historical sample. It says nothing about transaction costs, regime shift, or whether the strategy still works on data the whole search never touched at all — this repo's own OOS bar (Lego Principle 5) remains a separate, still-mandatory hurdle on top of a passed reality check, not a hurdle it substitutes for.

Forgetting the benchmark is a choice that changes the answer. \(d_{k,t} = R_{k,t} - R_t^{bench}\) is only as meaningful as the benchmark chosen — zero, buy-and-hold, or an incumbent strategy already running are three different, defensible questions ("is any candidate better than nothing," "better than doing nothing active," "better than what's already deployed"), and switching the benchmark can flip which candidate is even eligible to be the observed maximum. Name the benchmark up front, exactly as this project's own working agreement insists for any claimed improvement.

Mistaking "not significant" for "definitely fake." A genuinely modest, real edge over a short sample (16 periods, in this lesson's own toy panel) can easily fail to clear a bootstrap bar simply because there isn't yet enough data for the test to have real statistical power — this is exactly why this repo's own validation rule insists on a non-trivial trade count (≥30) before treating an OOS result as evidence either way. A null result from a short track record is a "not yet distinguishable from noise," not an epitaph.

Section 04

How practitioners actually use this

WRC and SPA are gate-keeping tools, used the same way the Deflated Sharpe Ratio is used — applied once, near the end of a research process, to the full honest set of everything that was tried, not as a routine tearsheet line computed on whatever happens to be reported. A quant research group running a large parameter sweep treats "my best backtested variant beats the incumbent" as an unverified claim until it clears exactly this kind of test against the entire sweep, not just the reported winner. SPA has effectively superseded WRC as the default choice in the econometrics and quant-finance literature precisely because it recovers power without sacrificing anything WRC already got right — there's rarely a reason to reach for the older test once SPA is implemented, beyond historical continuity or a specific paper's convention.

Real-world scenario

What this repo already has, and what it's still missing

This repo's own statsCore.js ships a genuine, correct stationary (Politis–Romano) block bootstrap — blockResample and blockBootstrapIC — reused across backtestStats.js (max-drawdown Monte Carlo), the frozen expectation snapshot pipeline (scripts/freeze_expectation.mjs), a live volatility-reversion significance test (js/volReversionCore.js), and a Python COT factor test (analysis/cot_factor/factor_test.py). This is real, working machinery — the exact building block this lesson's bootstrap needs — and it is already battle-tested in this codebase.

What it has never been pointed at is a genuine multi-candidate test of the kind this lesson describes. Every one of those call sites bootstraps one series or statistic at a time: one strategy's Sharpe or drawdown confidence interval, one predictor's own IC against its own serial-dependence null. The one place this repo explicitly corrects for testing several things at once — js/volReversionCore.js's "best of 4 horizon tests" — uses a parametric Bonferroni adjustment (multiply the raw p-value by the number of horizons actually tested), not a bootstrap of the max statistic across horizons. That is a real, honest correction, but it is a different, more conservative tool than WRC or SPA, not an instance of either.

The sharper version of the exact problem this lesson exists to solve is named directly, and flagged as unresolved, in this repo's own honest self-assessment: "The headline numbers (55–65% win rates, ~20–28% CAGR, Sharpe > 2 optimizer configs) are largely selected as best-of-N (e.g. best of ~600 random configs per pair)... Best-of-N ranked on the same data is multiple-comparisons bias: with enough configs, something will look great on noise... Mitigation: combinatorially-purged cross-validation... plus a deflated-Sharpe adjustment for the number of trials run." Purged CV and a deflated- Sharpe adjustment are exactly the mitigations already named — a genuine bootstrap test of the full ~600-candidate panel's own return histories, the way this lesson describes, is not among them. The pieces to build one — the stationary block bootstrap itself, plus the multiple-testing instincts already documented throughout this repo's review docs — are already sitting in this codebase; assembling them into an actual WRC/SPA-style test of a full candidate panel has not been done.

Key Takeaways

✓WRC and SPA test the full candidate set's real return histories directly, where the Deflated Sharpe Ratio corrects one reported Sharpe using an estimated trial count and dispersion.
✓The test statistic is a maximum across candidates, \(\bar V=\max_k \bar d_k\) for WRC — which is exactly why a textbook single-series critical value doesn't apply.
✓A stationary block bootstrap, applied jointly across all candidates, builds the empirical null distribution of that maximum — the same machinery the Bootstrapping & Monte Carlo lesson introduces, pointed at a max instead of one series.
⚠WRC is conservative when junk candidates are included — demeaning every candidate at zero lets obviously-bad ones widen the null and cost real power.
✓Hansen's SPA test studentizes and stops clearly-inferior candidates from padding the null, recovering power on the same data — the field's preferred modern successor, not a rival method.
✗An impressive raw Sharpe is not the same claim as a statistically real edge. This lesson's worked example shows a noise strategy's Sharpe (0.93) looking nearly as good as a real edge's (1.19) while its own mean fails the bootstrap test outright (\(p\approx0.24\)).
✗Both tests are worthless on a dishonestly curated candidate set. Submitting only the best-looking survivors of a larger, undisclosed search defeats the entire method — no formula can detect it after the fact.
Section 06

Self-test

Q1. What exactly is White's Reality Check's null hypothesis, and what is its test statistic?
The null hypothesis is that the best of \(N\) candidate strategies has a true, population expected excess return over the benchmark that is no better than the benchmark itself (\(\le 0\)) — i.e. any apparent winner is just the luckiest of \(N\) noisy trials with no real edge anywhere. The test statistic is \(\bar V = \max_k \bar d_k\), the largest of the \(N\) candidates' own sample mean excess returns.
Q2. Why can't a textbook normal-distribution critical value be used to judge \(\bar V\) directly?
Two separate problems stack up. First, financial returns are typically autocorrelated, so even a single candidate's own sample mean doesn't have the simple i.i.d.-based standard error a textbook t-test assumes. Second, and specific to this test, \(\bar V\) is a maximum across \(N\) candidates that are usually cross-correlated with each other (variations on a theme share underlying market exposure) — there is no simple closed-form distribution for the maximum of many autocorrelated, cross-correlated quantities. WRC sidesteps both problems by bootstrapping the empirical distribution of \(\bar V\) directly from the real, dependent data instead of assuming a shape.
Q3. What specific weakness in WRC does Hansen's SPA test fix, and how?
WRC demeans every candidate at zero before bootstrapping, including candidates that were obviously, clearly bad on the real data — letting them act, in the resampled world, as if they had an equal shot at producing the largest resampled mean. The more junk candidates included, the wider WRC's bootstrap null gets, costing statistical power. SPA fixes this two ways: (1) studentizing each candidate's statistic by its own bootstrap-estimated spread before taking the max, putting different-volatility candidates on a comparable footing, and (2) only recentering candidates that weren't clearly inferior at zero — a clearly-inferior candidate keeps something closer to its own actual (negative) performance in the bootstrap world, denying it a chance to pad the null. Both changes recover power on the same underlying data.
Q4. In this lesson's worked example, why did Strategy C ("Carry") — with the second-highest raw Sharpe in the whole panel — fail a bootstrap test of its own mean excess return?
Carry's raw Sharpe (0.93) looked strong specifically because its realized standard deviation (0.168) happened to come out very small over this short 16-period sample — a Sharpe ratio rewards low variance as much as it rewards a genuine mean edge. Carry's actual mean excess return (0.156) was barely a third the size of the genuinely edged strategy's (0.469), and testing that mean against the same best-of-five bootstrap null gave \(p\approx0.24\) — nowhere close to significant. An impressive Sharpe purely from low realized variance is exactly the kind of result five noise-driven candidates can produce by chance, which is why the test looks at the mean (and, for SPA, the studentized statistic) rather than trusting a raw Sharpe ranking directly.
Q5. How does WRC/SPA's basic approach differ structurally from the Deflated Sharpe Ratio's, even though both correct for the same underlying "best of N" problem?
The Deflated Sharpe Ratio compresses each of the \(N\) trials down to a single Sharpe ratio, then uses an extreme-value-theory formula — fed an estimated trial count \(N\) and the dispersion \(\sigma_{SR}\) of those \(N\) Sharpe ratios — to work out what the best-of-\(N\) Sharpe should look like under pure luck. WRC and SPA instead keep every candidate's full period-by-period return history and directly stationary-block- bootstrap the entire panel, jointly resampling all \(N\) series and recomputing the real "who's best" statistic on each resample to build an actual empirical null distribution — no Gaussian-maximum approximation and no separate dispersion estimate required, because the bootstrap uses the real data's own autocorrelation and cross-candidate correlation directly.
Q6. A researcher ran 50 variations of a strategy, quietly discarded the 45 that looked unpromising, and submitted only the remaining 5 (including the eventual "winner") to a Reality Check test, which came back with a small, significant p-value. Is that p-value trustworthy?
No. WRC and SPA are only honest when the candidate set fed into the bootstrap is the complete, real set of everything that was actually tried. Discarding the 45 unpromising variants before running the test means the bootstrap never saw the true scale of the search — it computed a correct answer to the wrong question ("is the best of these 5 real?" instead of "is the best of these 50 real?"). The resulting p-value can look confidently significant while being completely uninformative about the real, much larger search that actually happened. This mirrors the Deflated Sharpe lesson's warning about undercounting \(N\) exactly — it is a discipline failure the arithmetic has no way to detect after the fact.
