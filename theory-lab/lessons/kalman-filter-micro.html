Lesson 8 · Time Series & Regimes

A Hedge Ratio Isn't a Constant — It's a Moving Target

Run OLS over the last 60 days and you get one number for how much asset A moves with asset B — treated as fixed until tomorrow's regression, on a new window, quietly gives you a different one. The Kalman filter replaces that constant with a running belief: a best estimate, plus how sure you are of it, updated one new observation at a time. Same recursion NASA used to land on the moon, repurposed for a hedge ratio that drifts instead of jumps.

Section 1 · How it reached trading

A hedge ratio that won't sit still

Kalman filter · state-space econometrics in the 1980s → rates and stat-arb desks

How it reached trading

Before it, a hedge ratio came from a rolling OLS window: one frozen number that jumped whenever an old point rolled out. Built for control engineering, the filter reached economics in the 1980s by treating a regression coefficient as a hidden state that drifts.

Rates desks used it to read level, slope and curvature off noisy yields (Diebold, Rudebusch & Aruoba, 2006); stat-arb used it for spreads and drifting hedge ratios (Elliott, van der Hoek & Malcolm, 2005). It is now the default way to track a time-varying beta.

Section 2

Why a "fixed" relationship is quietly wrong

A rolling-window regression has a built-in artifact that has nothing to do with the market.

▭

Equal weight in, zero weight out

Every point inside a 60-day window counts identically; every point outside it counts exactly zero. Day 61 matters as much as yesterday — until it rolls off the back and vanishes completely.

⤺

Jumps that aren't real

β can lurch the moment an old, unremarkable data point falls out of the window — a discontinuity caused by your choice of window length, not by anything that happened in the market.

〰

Relationships actually drift

Correlations widen and narrow with the macro regime; a sector sensitivity shifts as a business mix shifts; a lead-lag relationship strengthens or weakens with volatility. A hedge ratio is rarely a true constant.

⚖

Neither extreme works

Frozen for 60 days is too slow to react. Re-fit from scratch on a new window is too jumpy and throws away everything already learned. You want neither.

The Kalman filter reframes the problem entirely: stop assuming the ratio is a constant to be estimated, and instead treat it as a hidden, slowly-drifting quantity you can never observe directly — updated by every new noisy reading, with no window to choose and nothing to re-fit from scratch.
Section 3

Track a belief, not just a number

A GPS for a quantity you can't see directly.

The intuition

Imagine tracking a boat at night with only a noisy radar ping once a minute. You never see the boat directly — only blips with error in them. But you're not starting from zero each minute: boats don't teleport, so your position from a minute ago is still a good guess of where it probably is now — just a little less certain, since time has passed. Then the new ping arrives. You don't trust it alone (radar is noisy) and you don't ignore it either (it's the only new information you have) — you blend the two, weighted by how confident you were in the prediction versus how noisy the sensor is known to be.

A hedge ratio that drifts is exactly the same tracking problem, with the boat's position replaced by an unobservable number.
x̂

A running estimate

Your current best guess of the hidden value — the hedge ratio you'd actually use today.

P

A running uncertainty

How much you trust that estimate right now. This is the piece a plain moving average has no equivalent of at all.

Section 4

Predict: carry the estimate forward, admit it's gone stale

Before looking at today's data at all, project yesterday's belief forward.

The predict step
x̂ₜ|ₜ₋₁ = x̂ₜ₋₁|ₜ₋₁
and
Pₜ|ₜ₋₁ = Pₜ₋₁|ₜ₋₁ + Q
The estimate itself doesn't move — a pure random-walk state assumes no predictable direction, just a small nudge each step. But the uncertainty always grows, inflated by the process variance Q: the longer you go without checking, or the more the relationship is believed to drift, the less confident the prediction gets.

This growth in P is what keeps the filter honest. Without it, an old estimate would stay permanently over-confident and would barely budge no matter what new data showed up — Q is the dial that says "I believe this relationship can genuinely change," and a larger Q means a filter that's more willing to move.

A modeling choice, not a law of nature

"Today equals yesterday plus noise" is called a random-walk state equation — it assumes no trend and no tendency to revert toward any particular value. If the true quantity is plausibly mean-reverting instead, this is the wrong assumption, and a different state equation would be more honest.

Section 5

Update: blend the prediction with what you just saw

The new observation arrives. First measure the surprise.

The update step
eₜ = yₜ − x̂ₜ|ₜ₋₁
→
Kₜ (the gain)
→
x̂ₜ|ₜ = x̂ₜ|ₜ₋₁ + Kₜeₜ
eₜ is the innovation — observed minus predicted, literally "the surprise." The updated estimate is the old prediction plus that surprise, scaled by the gain Kₜ. Uncertainty shrinks afterward: Pₜ|ₜ = (1 − Kₜ)Pₜ|ₜ₋₁ (H = 1 here, a direct read on the ratio).

Notice what's doing the work: the update never throws away the prediction and never ignores the new reading either. It pulls the estimate part of the way from where it was toward what the new data implies — and exactly how far that pull goes is the single most important number in the whole method.

Section 6

The Kalman gain: how much to trust the new reading

Kₜ = Pₜ|ₜ₋₁ / (Pₜ|ₜ₋₁ + R) — a ratio between 0 and 1. Pick a regime.

Try it
prediction uncertainty small
vs
R huge
Kₜ → 0. A single day's reading is mostly noise. The filter barely moves the estimate — it mostly keeps carrying forward what it already believed.
Q huge
vs
R small
Kₜ → 1. The true state is believed to move fast, so any prior estimate goes stale quickly, and each new reading is trustworthy. The updated estimate jumps almost all the way to the new observation.
P ≈ R
→
K ≈ 0.5
Prediction uncertainty and observation noise are comparable, so the filter splits the difference — roughly half old belief, half new evidence.
Not the same thing as an EMA

An exponential moving average uses one fixed smoothing constant, chosen in advance and never revisited. The Kalman gain is derived, and re-adapts on its own every single step — because Pₜ|ₜ₋₁ itself keeps changing (it grows during predict, shrinks during update).

Section 7

Worked example: tracking a hedge ratio through two days

Start: x̂ = 1.20, P = 0.0400, Q = 0.0009, R = 0.0100, H = 1.

Try it
predict: x̂=1.20, P=0.0409
→
K₁ = 0.0409/0.0509 ≈ 0.804
→
x̂=1.321, P=0.0080
e₁ = 1.35 − 1.20 = 0.15. The gain is large because prior uncertainty (0.0409) still exceeds R (0.0100) — this early, the filter isn't confident yet, so it leans heavily on the new reading. The estimate moves 80% of the way from 1.20 toward 1.35.
predict: x̂=1.321, P=0.0089
→
K₂ = 0.0089/0.0189 ≈ 0.471
→
x̂=1.311, P=0.0047
e₂ = 1.30 − 1.321 = −0.021. The gain dropped from 0.804 to 0.471 with no parameter touched — purely because P shrank after Day 1's update, so the prior is now more trustworthy relative to a single noisy reading.

Keep this recursion running and the gain keeps settling toward a steady-state value set entirely by the ratio Q/R (here 0.09 — small, so the steady-state gain settles low). Nobody set an N-day lookback anywhere; the filter's effective memory fell straight out of two variance inputs.

Section 10

The trading case: drift smoothly, don't refit and jump

Same underlying data, two ways of tracking the relationship between two correlated instruments.

Rolling-window OLS
  • Every point in the window weighted identically, every point outside it weighted zero
  • β can jump the moment an old point rolls off the back — a window-length artifact, not a market event
  • "Effective memory" is the window length N, chosen directly and fixed
  • Re-fitting from scratch discards everything already learned
Kalman-tracked ratio
  • Old information discounted gradually through predict/update, never dropped off a cliff
  • No window to choose — memory emerges from the Q/R ratio
  • Uncertainty is tracked explicitly (P), not just the point estimate
  • Reacts to genuine drift without whipsawing on every noisy print, when Q and R are set sensibly
This is the standard alternative to rolling-window OLS in statistical arbitrage and pairs trading: track the hedge ratio between two cointegrated instruments with a Kalman filter instead of a rolling regression, so it reacts smoothly to genuine drift instead of exhibiting the equal-weight-in, zero-weight-out discontinuity a fixed window always has. The same idea extends to a time-varying market beta or factor loading — any coefficient conventionally estimated by static OLS.
Section 11

Where this is used, and where "adaptive" stops meaning "principled"

🚀

Born tracking a spacecraft

Kálmán published the recursion in 1960; its first famous use was fusing noisy inertial-sensor readings for the Apollo guidance computer. GPS, robotics, and self-driving localization all use the same core idea.

📐

Only optimal if linear-Gaussian

The clean closed form here is exactly optimal when the state truly follows a linear equation and both noise sources are genuinely Gaussian. Nonlinear cases reach for the Extended or Unscented Kalman Filter (EKF/UKF) instead.

⚠

Q and R are still chosen inputs

Grid-searching them against backtest performance doesn't remove the overfitting risk a fixed window has — it relocates it into a less legible, more sophisticated-looking parameterization.

🎯

Reactive isn't the same as adaptive

Set Q too large relative to R and the steady-state gain approaches 1 — the filter starts tracking every noisy print almost exactly, which defeats the point of estimating a stable relationship at all.

Where this needs qualifying

Real return series routinely show fat left tails. A standard Kalman filter run on such data is still a reasonable linear estimator, but it's no longer provably optimal, and its uncertainty estimate P can understate real tail risk. And nothing here is built for hedge ratios or validated in this repo — any real use needs to beat the incumbent on true out-of-sample performance first.

Section 12

Quick check

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

In the predict step, if the state is a pure random walk, what actually happens?

x̂ₜ|ₜ₋₁ = x̂ₜ₋₁|ₜ₋₁ (no new data used yet), but Pₜ|ₜ₋₁ = Pₜ₋₁|ₜ₋₁ + Q — uncertainty always inflates during predict, because time passing means the state may have wandered.
Question 2

If observation noise R is huge relative to process variance Q, what happens to the Kalman gain?

K = P/(P+R). A huge R relative to P swamps the denominator, so K → 0 and the update barely moves the estimate.
Question 3

In the worked example, the gain fell from 0.804 (Day 1) to 0.471 (Day 2) with no input changed. Why?

P went from 0.0409 (Day 1's prediction) to 0.0089 (Day 2's prediction, after shrinking to 0.0080 post-update and regrowing by Q). A smaller P relative to the fixed R = 0.0100 means a smaller gain — purely a consequence of the recursion, no parameter touched.
Question 4

Why is a Kalman-tracked hedge ratio not the same object as a rolling-window OLS beta, even though both get called "adaptive"?

A rolling window weights every point inside it identically and every point outside it zero — a discontinuity chosen directly as N. The Kalman filter's effective memory emerges from Q/R and can even change step to step, as the worked example showed.
Question 5

What's the single biggest pitfall in choosing Q and R?

A window length of 60 days is at least an honest, auditable knob. A (Q, R) pair optimized post hoc against in-sample performance is the same overfitting risk wearing a more sophisticated disguise.
Section 13

What to carry forward

01

A relationship estimated once — like a hedge ratio from a rolling regression — is rarely a true constant, and a fixed window has a built-in jump artifact that has nothing to do with the market.

02

The filter tracks a belief, not a number: a running estimate x̂ plus a running uncertainty P, updated one observation at a time.

03

Predict carries the estimate forward and inflates P by the process variance Q — accounting for time passing, before any new data is used.

04

Update blends in the new observation by the Kalman gain K = P/(P+R), then shrinks P — the gain is derived, not a fixed smoothing constant like an EMA's.

05

The gain settles toward a steady state set by Q/R — the filter's own, self-chosen "effective window length," with no N to pick.

06

Q and R are still chosen inputs — grid-searching them against a backtest is the same overfitting risk as tuning a window length, just harder to spot.

07

On a desk it replaces the rolling-window hedge ratio: pairs traders track a drifting beta with it, and rates desks read curve factors off noisy yields with the same recursion.

What this now lets you do

Read K = P/(P+R) on sight and know exactly which direction it moves as either variance changes. Explain, precisely, why a Kalman-tracked hedge ratio is not the same object as a rolling-window beta. Recognise when "adaptive" language is masking an under-damped, over-reactive filter — and know that Q/R needs validating out of sample, not tuning to look good in it.
