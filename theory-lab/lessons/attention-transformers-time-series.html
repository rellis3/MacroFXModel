Attention & Transformers for Time Series

Instead of relaying information step by step through a chain of hidden states, let every output position look directly at every input position and decide, by computed relevance, how much attention each one deserves — the mechanism behind the architecture that has displaced recurrent networks for most large-scale sequence modeling.

CONCEPT — explained here, not built or tested anywhere in this repo ~19 min read Prereqs: Recurrent Networks: LSTM & GRU
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • An RNN or LSTM processes a sequence one step at a time — every step must wait for the previous one, so training cannot parallelize across time, and even with gating, information from far back can still degrade over very long sequences.
  • Scaled dot-product attention fixes both problems at once: \(\text{Attention}(Q,K,V)=\text{softmax}\!\left(\dfrac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V\) (Vaswani et al., "Attention Is All You Need," NeurIPS 2017) lets every output position directly weigh and combine every input position in one computation, with no step-by-step relay.
  • Self-attention applies this within a single sequence (queries, keys, and values all come from the same input); multi-head attention runs several attention computations in parallel with different learned projections, so different heads can specialize in different kinds of relationships between positions.
  • The direct cost of "everyone looks at everyone" is an \(n\times n\) attention matrix — \(O(n^2)\) time and memory in sequence length \(n\). Time-series-specific architectures like the Temporal Fusion Transformer (Lim et al., 2021) and Informer (Zhou et al., 2021) exist specifically to make long-sequence forecasting tractable despite this cost.
  • Mandatory honest framing: transformers are now the dominant architecture for large-scale sequence modeling, but they need substantially more data and compute than LSTMs to realize that advantage. For shorter, lower-data time series — which most individual market series genuinely are — the benefit over a well-tuned classical or LSTM approach is not guaranteed and must be tested empirically, not assumed from the model's reputation in NLP or vision.
  • How it reached trading: It reached markets through text first: FinBERT (2019–23) read filings and earnings calls better than finance word lists, and time-series variants followed.
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

Everyone is talking about transformers. What is attention, in plain words?

Start with what came before. A recurrent network reads a price series one day at a time. Each day's memory is built from the day before. It is a game of telephone.

So news from sixty days ago has been retold sixty times by the time it reaches today.

Exactly, and it gets diluted on the way. Nothing can run in parallel either, because day five hundred has to wait for day four hundred and ninety-nine.

And attention?

Attention throws out the line. It was published in 2017, for machine translation. Every position looks directly at every other position, all at once. Think of a room where everyone can talk to everyone.

How does it decide who to listen to?

Each position carries three things. A query: what am I looking for. A key: what do I contain. A value: what I would actually hand over. You compare the query with every key, and a closer match gets a higher score.

Give me numbers.

One query and three past days. The match scores come out as one, zero and one. You scale them down by the square root of the key size, which is two here. Then a step called softmax turns them into weights that add up to one.

And they come out as?

About forty percent, twenty percent and forty percent. The two days that matched get twice the weight of the one that didn't. The output is just a blend of the three values with those weights.

Why scale at all?

With long vectors the raw scores get big, and softmax piles nearly all the weight on one position. Learning stalls. Scaling keeps the weights soft enough to learn.

What's the catch?

Two catches. Everyone comparing with everyone costs the square of the sequence length. And transformers need a lot of data. They won in language and vision, on huge datasets. Two years of daily FX is tiny and noisy by comparison.

So where do they actually help a trader?

Text, mostly. FinBERT, from 2019 on, read filings and earnings calls better than finance word lists. On short price series, a tuned LSTM or a linear model is still the benchmark to beat.

And if I do try one on prices?

Mask the future. Each day may only look at earlier days. Without that mask the backtest looks brilliant, and it is pure look-ahead.

One line?

Attention lets every day look at every other day and weigh it by relevance. Use it where the data is big enough, and prove it beats the simple model first.

Section 01

Why this matters

The previous lesson ended on a specific, honest limitation: gating substantially mitigates the vanishing-gradient problem in an LSTM, but it does not eliminate it, and every recurrent architecture — vanilla RNN, LSTM, or GRU alike — shares a second, structural constraint that has nothing to do with gradients at all. Computing \(h_t\) requires \(h_{t-1}\) to already exist, which requires \(h_{t-2}\), all the way back to the start of the sequence. That dependency chain cannot be parallelized across time during training, no matter how much compute is available — step 500 simply cannot begin until step 499 has finished. Attention removes this constraint entirely by replacing "relay information forward, one step at a time" with "let every position look directly at every other position, all at once" — a structurally different mechanism, not merely a faster implementation of the same idea.

This lesson picks up exactly where the previous one left off, and closes the same honest loop it opened: attention is a genuine, well-evidenced fix for two specific problems (training parallelism and long-range degradation) — it is not a categorical upgrade that makes the previous lesson's architecture obsolete for every use case, a distinction the mandatory honest framing below returns to directly.

💡
Plain-English intuition

A room where everyone can talk to everyone, instead of a game of telephone

The previous lesson's recurrent network was a game of telephone: a message is whispered from person to person down a line, and by the time it reaches the end, information from early in the line has been diluted by every retelling in between. Attention throws out the line entirely. Instead, imagine a room where every person can speak directly to every other person at once, and each listener decides — based on how relevant each speaker's message actually is to what they're currently trying to figure out — how much weight to give each voice. Nobody's message has to survive a long relay to be heard; it's heard directly, weighted by relevance, with the number of "hops" needed to reach any other position always exactly one.

That weighting-by-relevance is precisely what the attention formula computes: for a given position asking "what do I need right now" (a query), every other position offers "here's what I contain" (a key, used to compute relevance) and "here's the actual content I'd contribute" (a value, used in the final weighted combination). The query is compared against every key to get a relevance score, those scores are turned into weights that sum to one, and the output is just a weighted blend of every position's value — exactly the "everyone speaks, weighted by relevance" picture, made numeric.

How it reached trading

Reading the filings, and weighting the right days

Transformers · into finance through text first (FinBERT, 2019–23), then time series

Before transformers, turning text into a trading signal mostly meant counting words. Loughran & McDonald (2011) showed that general-purpose word lists misread finance (a "liability" is not bad news on a balance sheet) and built finance-specific ones, but a word count still cannot read "not as strong as expected". Attention, built in 2017 for machine translation, reads each word in the context of all the others.

Finance adopted it through text. Dogu Araci's FinBERT (2019) tuned Google's BERT for financial sentiment, and Allen Huang, Hui Wang and Yi Yang's FinBERT (published 2023) was trained on filings, analyst reports and earnings-call transcripts and classified finance sentiment better than the dictionaries. Bloomberg trained its own finance language model, BloombergGPT, in 2023. On the time-series side, forecasting variants such as the Temporal Fusion Transformer (2021) show which past days and which inputs a forecast relied on.

On a desk today, transformers mostly turn news, filings and earnings calls into scores that feed signals. On short price series their record is contested, and a tuned LSTM or linear model is still the benchmark to beat.

Section 02

The math, step by step

Watch the formula build
Where scaled dot-product attention comes from, one term at a time
\[ \color{#fbbf24}{QK^T} \]

Dot every query with every key. The result is an \(n\times n\) grid of raw relevance scores. In the worked example below, the one query scores \([1,\ 0,\ 1]\) against the three past steps.

\[ \frac{QK^T}{\color{#fbbf24}{\sqrt{d_k}}} \]

Scale the scores down by the square root of the key size. With \(d_k = 2\) the row becomes \([0.707,\ 0,\ 0.707]\). This stops the scores from growing with \(d_k\) and saturating the next step.

\[ \color{#fbbf24}{\text{softmax}}\!\left(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) \]

Softmax turns each row into weights that are non-negative and sum to one: \([0.401,\ 0.198,\ 0.401]\). The two steps whose keys match the query get about twice the weight.

\[ \color{#10b981}{\text{Attention}(Q,K,V)} = \text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)\color{#fbbf24}{V} \]

Multiply by the values: each output is a weighted blend of every position's value. In the example, \(0.401[2,0] + 0.198[0,2] + 0.401[1,1] = [1.203,\ 0.797]\), all in one step with no relay.

Queries, keys, and values

Start from a sequence of \(n\) input vectors (e.g. one vector per time step, already embedded to some dimension \(d_{model}\)), stacked as rows of a matrix \(X\). Three separate learned linear projections turn \(X\) into three different views of the same sequence:

\[ Q = XW^Q, \qquad K = XW^K, \qquad V = XW^V \]
Qqueries — "what am I looking for," one row per output position
Kkeys — "what do I contain," one row per input position, compared against every query
Vvalues — "what I'd actually contribute," combined according to the relevance weights
W^Q, W^K, W^Vlearned weight matrices producing each of the three views from the same input X

When \(Q\), \(K\), and \(V\) are all derived from the same sequence \(X\), this is self-attention — every position in a sequence attends to every position in that same sequence, including itself. (The original transformer also uses cross-attention, where queries come from one sequence and keys/values from another — relevant to encoder-decoder setups, but self-attention is the core mechanism this lesson focuses on.)

Scaled dot-product attention

With \(Q\), \(K\), and \(V\) in hand, the full attention operation (Vaswani et al., 2017) is:

\[ \text{Attention}(Q,K,V) = \text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V \]
QK^Tevery query dotted with every key — an n×n matrix of raw relevance scores
√d_ka scaling factor, d_k = dimensionality of each key/query vector
softmax(·)turns each row of scores into weights that are non-negative and sum to 1
· Vthe softmax weights combine the value vectors into the final output, one weighted blend per query
QK^T / √d_k
The relevance scores
How much each key matches each query, scaled to keep the softmax well-behaved
softmax(·)
The attention weights
Turns raw scores into a proper weighting — non-negative, sums to 1 per query
weights · V
The output
A weighted blend of every position's value vector — the whole point of the mechanism

Read the whole formula in the order it's computed: compare a query against every key by dot product (a large dot product means "this key is very relevant to this query"), scale those scores down, turn them into a proper probability-like weighting with softmax, then use those weights to combine the value vectors. Every output position performs this same computation independently and simultaneously against every input position — no position has to wait for any other position's computation to finish, which is exactly the training-parallelism property the previous lesson's recurrence structurally could not offer.

Why the \(\sqrt{d_k}\) scaling matters. Vaswani et al. note that for larger \(d_k\), the dot products \(QK^T\) tend to grow large in magnitude (the sum of \(d_k\) roughly independent products grows with \(d_k\)), which pushes the softmax into regions where its gradient is extremely small — the softmax saturates, and one score dominates every other almost completely, well before training has had a chance to learn a genuinely useful weighting. Dividing by \(\sqrt{d_k}\) counteracts this growth in scale, keeping the softmax's inputs in a range where its gradient stays usable throughout training.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Picture a query as a question you're holding up, and every key as a label on a box, saying what's inside. You compare your question against every box's label at once and get a relevance score per box — "this box seems very related to what I'm asking," "this one, not so much." Softmax turns those raw scores into percentages that add up to 100%, and then you open every box a little, taking more from the highly relevant ones and almost nothing from the irrelevant ones, and mix it all together. That mixed result is the attention output — one blend, built by directly comparing your question to every single box at once, not by asking box 1, then box 2, then box 3 in sequence.

Multi-head attention

A single attention computation learns one particular notion of "relevance." Multi-head attention runs \(h\) independent attention computations in parallel, each with its own learned projections, then combines them:

\[ \text{MultiHead}(Q,K,V) = \text{Concat}(\text{head}_1,\dots,\text{head}_h)W^O, \qquad \text{head}_i = \text{Attention}(QW_i^Q, KW_i^K, VW_i^V) \]
head_ione attention computation using its own learned Q/K/V projections
Concat(·)stack every head's output side by side
W^Oa final learned projection mixing the concatenated heads back into one output

Because each head has its own separately learned \(W_i^Q, W_i^K, W_i^V\), different heads are free to specialize during training — one head might learn to attend mostly to short-range, recent relationships in a sequence, another to a longer-range or seasonal pattern, another to something not easily described in words at all. Nothing forces this specialization explicitly; it emerges from training multiple differently-initialized, independently-projected attention computations side by side and letting each one find whatever relevance pattern reduces the loss most from its own vantage point.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

One attention head is one way of judging relevance — like one friend reading a room and deciding who's talking to whom. Multi-head attention is asking several friends to each read the same room independently, each with their own personal sense of what "relevant" means, and then combining everyone's read at the end. One friend might notice who's standing physically close together; another might notice who's making eye contact; another might notice the topic of conversation. No single friend has to catch everything — the combination is stronger than any one of them alone.

Positional information and the O(n²) cost

Attention itself has no built-in notion of order — swap the rows of \(X\) and the set of pairwise dot products is unchanged, just relabeled. The original transformer restores order information by adding a positional encoding (a fixed or learned vector unique to each position) directly onto each input embedding before attention is applied — a detail this lesson notes rather than derives in full, since the core mechanism above is unchanged by exactly which positional scheme is used.

The direct cost of "every position compares against every other position" is the \(n\times n\) matrix \(QK^T\) — \(O(n^2)\) time and memory in the sequence length \(n\). For a sentence of a few dozen tokens this is trivial; for a long financial time series — years of daily bars, or months of intraday bars — \(n\) can be large enough that this quadratic cost becomes a genuine practical constraint, and is the specific problem the time-series-specific architectures below were built to address.

\(n\)Sequence length — the number of time steps (or tokens) being attended over.
\(d_k, d_{model}\)Dimensionality of the key/query vectors, and of the model's overall embedding.
\(h\)Number of attention heads run in parallel.
\(\text{softmax}\)Turns a vector of raw scores into non-negative weights summing to 1, row-wise.
\(W^Q,W^K,W^V,W^O\)Learned linear projection matrices — for queries, keys, values, and the final multi-head output mix.
Sequential relay (RNN/LSTM, previous lesson) versus direct attention (every position to every position)
In the recurrent chain, position 4's hidden state only ever sees position 3 directly — everything from position 1 has already been compressed and re-compressed twice by the time it reaches h₄. In the attention panel, a single output draws a direct line to all four input positions at once, in one computation, with the line weight (the softmax output) determining how much each position actually contributes — no relay, no compounding compression.

\(e^{0.707}=2.028,\ e^{0}=1,\ e^{0.707}=2.028\); sum \(=5.056\).

Positions 1 and 3 — the two keys most aligned with the query — end up weighted about twice as heavily as position 2.

Real-world trading scenario

Interpreting which historical days a forecast actually weighted

Picture a self-attention layer over the last 60 trading days of a macro feature panel, producing a single forecast for next week's realized volatility. Unlike a plain LSTM's final hidden state, which compresses the whole 60-day history into one vector with no direct record of which specific days mattered most, the attention weights themselves are a readable-out artifact: for the forecast's query, the softmax weights over all 60 keys say, explicitly, "day 41 and day 58 received far more weight than day 12." This is precisely the kind of built-in interpretability the Temporal Fusion Transformer (Lim et al., 2021) is designed to expose deliberately, through dedicated attention and variable-selection components, rather than as an incidental side-effect of the mechanism. Reading attention weights this way is genuinely useful for understanding what a trained model is doing — it is not, on its own, evidence that what the model is doing constitutes real predictive edge, which remains a separate, empirical question exactly as it is for every other model in this curriculum.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

The raw \(O(n^2)\) attention cost is the specific, named problem behind several real, time-series-focused transformer variants:

📊

Temporal Fusion Transformer

Lim, Arık, Loeff & Pfister, International Journal of Forecasting, 2021. Combines attention with recurrent layers and dedicated variable-selection networks purpose-built for multi-horizon forecasting with mixed static, known-future, and observed-past inputs — explicitly designed for interpretability, not just accuracy, exposing which time steps and which input variables a forecast actually relied on.

⚡

Informer

Zhou, Zhang, Peng, Zhang, Li, Xiong & Zhang, AAAI, 2021. Introduces a ProbSparse self-attention mechanism that avoids computing the full \(n\times n\) attention matrix by identifying and keeping only the most informative query-key pairs, reducing complexity from \(O(n^2)\) toward \(O(n\log n)\) — purpose-built for long-sequence time-series forecasting where the full quadratic cost is otherwise prohibitive.

Both variants keep the same core scaled dot-product idea from this lesson — a query, a set of keys, a weighted combination of values — while changing how much of the full \(n\times n\) comparison actually needs to be computed, or what other architectural pieces (recurrence, variable-selection gating) get combined alongside it for a forecasting task specifically, rather than the general-purpose language modeling setting the original transformer was built for.

Transformers need substantially more data and compute than LSTMs to realize their advantage — this is not guaranteed to transfer to a typical market series. Transformers are, today, genuinely the dominant architecture for large-scale sequence modeling — that is a well-evidenced statement about the method in the settings (large text and vision corpora) where it was developed and most thoroughly tested. It is not, on its own, evidence that a transformer will outperform a well-tuned classical statistical model or a well-tuned LSTM on a typical individual market time series, which is usually far shorter and far noisier than the datasets that made the architecture's reputation. Attention's parameter count and appetite for data scale differently than a recurrent network's, and without enough data, a transformer's larger capacity can just as easily produce more convincing overfitting rather than a real accuracy gain. Whether a transformer earns its complexity on a specific series is an empirical question, answered only by honest, leakage-free out-of-sample comparison against simpler baselines — never assumed from the architecture's reputation in an unrelated domain.
⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where attention and transformers quietly go wrong on time series

Reaching for a transformer by reputation, not by evidence on the series at hand. The single most common mistake this lesson's honest framing is built to head off: a transformer's fame in language and vision does not transfer automatically to a specific, short, noisy financial time series. The only valid evidence is a fair, leakage-free comparison against a well-tuned simpler baseline on that exact series.

Forgetting positional encoding, then wondering why order doesn't seem to matter. Raw attention has no notion of sequence order on its own — omitting or mishandling positional encoding produces a model that is, structurally, blind to whether a big move happened yesterday or three months ago, which defeats the entire point of applying this to a time series in the first place.

Look-ahead leakage through the attention mask. An attention layer used for forecasting must be explicitly masked so a given position can only attend to earlier positions, not later ones — without a causal mask, the model can trivially "attend to the future," producing spectacular-looking backtest performance that is pure look-ahead bias, not a real forecasting result.

Treating attention weights as a causal explanation. A high attention weight on a particular day shows that the model's internal computation relied heavily on that day's representation — it is a real, readable fact about the trained model, but it is not by itself proof that day was causally important to the underlying market process, a distinction worth keeping precise when using attention weights for interpretability.

Scenario drill · an illustrative research review

A transformer backtest that looks too good

A colleague trained a transformer on two years of daily EURUSD data to forecast the next day's direction. The backtest hit rate is far above anything the team's tuned LSTM ever managed. Reading the code, you notice two things: the attention layer has no causal mask, and nobody ran the tuned LSTM or a linear model on the same walk-forward split. What do you do before any money goes near it?

  1. Allocate to it. Transformers dominate language and vision, so a big jump over the LSTM is what you would expect.
    Reputation in another domain is not evidence on this series. Two years of daily data is short and noisy, exactly where a transformer's extra capacity tends to buy more convincing overfitting rather than real accuracy.
  2. Add a causal mask, rerun it walk-forward out of sample, and compare it with the tuned LSTM and a linear baseline on the same split.
    Without the mask, each day can attend to later days, so the backtest may be pure look-ahead. Once masked, the only evidence that counts is a fair, leakage-free comparison with simpler models on the same data.
  3. Read the attention weights to check the model is relying on sensible past days.
    Useful for understanding the model, and weight on future days would expose the leak. But a sensible-looking weight pattern is not proof of edge, and it does not replace the masked, out-of-sample comparison.
  4. Add more heads and layers to push the result further.
    More capacity on a short series makes overfitting easier, and it does nothing about the missing mask. You would be polishing a result that may not exist.

What this teaches. This is an illustrative case, not a historical event. The lesson's two rules decide it: a forecaster without a causal mask can attend to the future, so a spectacular backtest is the first sign of leakage, not of edge; and a transformer earns its place only by beating a well-tuned simpler model out of sample on the same series.

Section 05

Self-test

Q1. What specific limitation of recurrent networks, discussed in the previous lesson, does attention directly remove — and how?
Recurrent networks compute h_t sequentially, requiring h_{t-1} to already exist, so the dependency chain cannot be parallelized across time during training regardless of available compute. Attention removes this by letting every output position compute its relevance to every input position directly and simultaneously — via QK^T — with no step depending on any other step's computation finishing first. This restores full training parallelism across the sequence dimension, something no recurrent architecture, gated or not, can offer.
Q2. Write the scaled dot-product attention formula and explain, term by term, what each piece is doing.
Attention(Q,K,V) = softmax(QK^T/√d_k)V. QK^T computes a raw relevance score between every query and every key via dot product; dividing by √d_k rescales those scores to keep the softmax's gradient well-behaved as d_k grows; softmax turns each row of scaled scores into non-negative weights summing to 1; multiplying by V uses those weights to combine the value vectors into the final output — one weighted blend per query position.
Q3. Why does the formula divide by √d_k specifically, rather than leaving the raw dot products unscaled?
For larger d_k, the dot product QK^T tends to grow large in magnitude, since it sums d_k roughly independent products. Large-magnitude inputs push the softmax into a saturated regime where one score dominates almost completely and the softmax's gradient becomes extremely small, which stalls learning. Dividing by √d_k counteracts this scale growth, keeping the softmax's inputs in a range where its gradient remains usable throughout training, as noted by Vaswani et al. (2017).
Q4. What problem do time-series-specific transformer variants like the Temporal Fusion Transformer and Informer address, and how does each approach it?
Both address the O(n²) time and memory cost of full attention, which becomes a genuine practical constraint for long financial or macro sequences. The Temporal Fusion Transformer (Lim et al., 2021) combines attention with recurrent layers and dedicated variable-selection networks, purpose-built for interpretable multi-horizon forecasting with mixed input types. Informer (Zhou et al., 2021) introduces a ProbSparse self-attention mechanism that avoids computing the full n×n attention matrix by keeping only the most informative query-key pairs, reducing complexity from O(n²) toward O(n log n).
Q5. A team observes that transformers dominate state-of-the-art language and vision benchmarks, and concludes a transformer will therefore outperform their well-tuned LSTM on a two-year daily FX return series. What's wrong with that reasoning?
Transformers need substantially more data and compute than LSTMs to realize their architectural advantage, and their dominance was established on large text and vision corpora — datasets far larger than a two-year daily FX series, which is short and extremely noisy by comparison. Without enough data, a transformer's larger capacity can produce more convincing overfitting rather than a genuine accuracy gain, so the benefit over a well-tuned LSTM or classical baseline is not guaranteed and must be tested empirically, out-of-sample, on that exact series — the model's reputation in an unrelated domain is not evidence about this specific, much smaller dataset.

Gomez, Łukasz Kaiser, and Illia Polosukhin, "Attention Is All You Need", Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems (NeurIPS), 2017 — the original transformer paper: scaled dot-product attention, multi-head attention, and positional encoding.
  • Bryan Lim, Sercan Ö. Arık, Nicolas Loeff, and Tomas Pfister, "Temporal Fusion Transformers for Interpretable Multi-horizon Time Series Forecasting", International Journal of Forecasting, 37(4), 2021 — the time-series-specific transformer variant referenced above, combining attention, recurrence, and variable-selection gating with an explicit interpretability goal.
  • Haoyi Zhou, Shanghang Zhang, Jieqi Peng, Shuai Zhang, Jianxin Li, Hui Xiong, and Wancai Zhang, "Informer: Beyond Efficient Transformer for Long Sequence Time-Series Forecasting", Proceedings of the AAAI Conference on Artificial Intelligence, 2021 — the ProbSparse attention mechanism reducing attention cost for long sequences.
  • Dzmitry Bahdanau, Kyunghyun Cho, and Yoshua Bengio, "Neural Machine Translation by Jointly Learning to Align and Translate", ICLR, 2015 — the earlier alignment/attention mechanism in a recurrent encoder-decoder, the direct conceptual predecessor to the transformer's fully attention-based approach.
  • Ian Goodfellow, Yoshua Bengio, and Aaron Courville, Deep Learning, MIT Press, 2016 — background on sequence modeling and encoder-decoder architectures that motivate the shift to attention covered in this lesson.
