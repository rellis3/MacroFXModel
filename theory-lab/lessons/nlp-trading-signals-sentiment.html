NLP for Trading Signals: Sentiment, Embeddings & FinBERT

Central bank statements, headlines, and earnings calls carry information in text, not just numbers — this lesson is the computational formalization of the hawkish/dovish reading a human analyst already does by eye.

TL;DR
  • Central Bank Policy already builds the qualitative vocabulary — hawkish, dovish, hedged, data-dependent — a human analyst uses to read a statement. This lesson asks how a model reads the same text and turns it into a number.
  • Word2Vec (Mikolov, Chen, Corrado & Dean, 2013) represents words as dense vectors positioned so words used in similar contexts end up close together in vector space, trained via skip-gram (predict surrounding context words from a target word) or CBOW (predict a target word from its surrounding context).
  • BERT (Devlin, Chang, Lee & Toutanova, 2019) reads a whole sentence bidirectionally at once via the transformer architecture, producing context-aware representations rather than one fixed vector per word regardless of context.
  • FinBERT (Araci, 2019) is BERT further trained on financial text specifically, producing a sentiment score (e.g. hawkish/dovish, or positive/negative/neutral) directly from raw text — the direct computational analogue of a human analyst's read.
  • A FinBERT-style sentiment score, tracked across successive central bank meetings, could serve as one additional input alongside the dot-plot reading in Central Bank Policy and the Taylor Rule's formal reaction function in The Taylor Rule — a sketch, not a claim this has been built or validated here.
  • Mandatory honest framing: sentiment scores are noisy, can misread sarcasm and hedged language, and are not calibrated probabilities of anything real-world. This is a widely-used real industry technique — explicitly not a claim it is validated for any specific use in this repo.
  • Real-world episode: "Twitter mood predicts the stock market" (Bollen, Mao & Zeng, 2011) reported 87.6% accuracy on the Dow's daily direction from one of six tweet "moods", tested on a few weeks of data. 🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

When the Fed statement drops, I read it and get a feel: more hawkish than last time, or less. Can a model do that?

That is exactly what this lesson builds. Turn that feel into a number a system can use.

Where do you start?

With words. The old way just counts them, and to a counter, inflation is no closer to risk than to coffee. Word2Vec, from 2013, fixes that. It learns a short list of numbers for each word from the company it keeps.

So hawkish ends up near tighten and raise.

Right, without anyone labeling them. But it has a flaw. Each word gets one fixed vector. Hold, in "the Fed will hold rates", gets the same vector as hold in "hold my coffee".

And BERT fixes that?

Yes. BERT, from 2019, reads the whole sentence at once, in both directions, so each word is read in its context. FinBERT is BERT trained further on financial text, so it knows that "attentive to inflation risks" is a loaded phrase, not filler.

Give me a number.

The lesson sketches three statements. "Inflation has moderated" scores about plus zero point one on a hawkish scale. "Highly attentive to inflation risks" jumps to plus zero point five five. "Further tightening will depend on incoming data" slips back to plus zero point three five.

So I'd trade the jump?

Careful. One reading is noisy. Models trip on sarcasm and hedges, and zero point five five is not a fifty-five percent chance of anything. The trend across meetings is the useful part.

Has text ever actually worked as a signal?

The famous case says be careful. In 2010 a paper turned nearly ten million tweets into seven mood series. One mood, "Calm", called the Dow's daily direction with eighty-seven point six percent accuracy.

That sounds incredible.

It was tested on a few weeks of December 2008, after trying seven series at several lags. A fund launched to trade it closed within about a year, and a 2017 replication could not find the effect.

One line?

Turning text into a number always works. Whether that number predicts anything is a separate claim, and only an out-of-sample test can settle it.

Section 01

Why this matters

Central Bank Policy spends an entire lesson on vocabulary precisely because a central bank's real signal lives in language, not just in the headline rate decision: whether guidance is "firm" or "data-dependent," whether a hold reads as a hawkish surprise, whether the committee's tone shifted from the previous statement even though the rate itself didn't move. A trained human reader picks all of this up by eye, fluently, from the words themselves. Central bank statements are only one example of a much broader category — news headlines, earnings-call transcripts, analyst notes, and social media all carry information in raw text that a purely numeric feed (the policy rate, the reported EPS number) does not capture on its own. This lesson is about the machinery that lets a model do computationally what that human reader already does qualitatively: turn text into a number a trading system can actually consume.

💡
Plain-English intuition

From "read it and get a feeling" to "read it and get a number"

An experienced FX analyst reading a Fed statement doesn't consciously diagram every sentence — they get a fast, holistic impression: "this reads more hawkish than last time," or "that hedge phrase wasn't in the previous statement." The whole arc of this lesson is turning that impression into an explicit, reproducible computation. First, teach a model what individual words mean by how they're typically used (word embeddings). Then, teach a model to read a whole sentence at once, the way a human does, rather than word by word in isolation (transformers, BERT). Finally, specialize that reading skill specifically for finance, where "asset" doesn't mean the same thing as in general English, and where "the committee remains attentive to inflation risks" is a loaded, specific phrase, not a bland aside (FinBERT). Each step is closer to the fluent, context-aware read a human analyst does automatically.

\[ \text{hawkish} \;\to\; \color{#fbbf24}{[\,0,\ \dots,\ 1,\ \dots,\ 0\,]} \]

The crude start: a one-hot vector, a 1 in this word's slot and 0 everywhere else. Every word is equally far from every other, so there is no meaning in it.

\[ \text{hawkish} \;\to\; \color{#fbbf24}{\mathbf{v}_{\text{hawkish}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}} \]

Word2Vec swaps it for a dense vector of a few hundred numbers, learned with skip-gram or CBOW so that words used in similar contexts sit close together: "hawkish" near "tighten" and "raise".

Word embeddings — Word2Vec

Tomas Mikolov, Kai Chen, Greg Corrado & Jeffrey Dean introduced Word2Vec in "Efficient Estimation of Word Representations in Vector Space," arXiv:1301.3781, 2013 (cited in full below). The core idea, stated precisely: represent every word in a vocabulary as a dense vector of real numbers — typically a few hundred dimensions, far smaller than the vocabulary size — positioned in that vector space so that words used in similar contexts end up close together. "Hike" and "raise" end up near each other in the resulting space not because anyone hand-labeled them as synonyms, but because they tend to appear surrounded by similar neighboring words across a large corpus of real text.

one-hot word
Where it starts
A sparse, meaningless vector — a 1 in one position, 0 everywhere else, with no notion of similarity between words
dense embedding vector
Where it ends up
A few hundred real-valued numbers, learned so that nearby vectors correspond to words used in similar contexts
distance in vector space
What it buys
A now-computable notion of "how similar are these two words," derived purely from usage patterns

Word2Vec learns these vectors via one of two closely related, conceptually simple training objectives, both trained on nothing but raw, unlabeled text:

Skip-gram

Given one target word, predict the words that surround it in a small context window. Fed the target word "hawkish," the model is trained to assign higher probability to context words like "tighten," "rates," and "inflation" than to unrelated words — and it's this repeated prediction task, over millions of sentences, that forces the model to place "hawkish" near other words that share its typical company.

CBOW (Continuous Bag of Words)

The mirror image: given the surrounding context words, predict the single target word in the middle. Fed the context "the committee remains ___ about inflation risks," the model learns to assign higher probability to words like "attentive" or "concerned" than to words that would never plausibly fill that slot — again, purely from how often each candidate word actually appears in that surrounding company across the training corpus.

Neither objective ever sees a hand-labeled definition of any word — the entire representation is learned purely from statistical co-occurrence patterns in raw text. A useful, well-known consequence: the geometry of the resulting vector space captures relationships as well as similarity — the classic illustrative example is that the vector arithmetic \(\text{vec(king)} - \text{vec(man)} + \text{vec(woman)}\) lands close to \(\text{vec(queen)}\), because the learned space encodes the "royal" and "gender" relationships as roughly consistent directions.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine you'd never heard the word "hawkish" before, but you read a thousand sentences that use it, and every single time it shows up near words like "raise," "tighten," and "inflation." Even without anyone ever defining "hawkish" for you directly, you'd eventually guess it means something to do with tightening policy — just from noticing its company. Word2Vec does exactly that trick automatically, at huge scale, and instead of a fuzzy guess it produces an actual list of numbers (a vector) that captures "the company this word tends to keep."

Transformer-based sentiment — BERT and FinBERT

Word2Vec has a real limitation: it assigns exactly one fixed vector to each word, regardless of context — "hold" gets the same vector whether it means "the committee will hold rates steady" or "hold my coffee." Jacob Devlin, Ming-Wei Chang, Kenton Lee & Kristina Toutanova's BERT — "BERT: Pre-training of Deep Bidirectional Transformers for Language Understanding," NAACL 2019 (cited in full below) — fixes exactly this by reading an entire sentence bidirectionally at once, using the transformer architecture's attention mechanism to let every word's representation be informed by every other word in the sentence, both before and after it, simultaneously. The output is a context-aware representation: the same word "hold" gets a different vector depending on the sentence it actually appears in, because the surrounding words are folded directly into how it's represented.

bidirectionalevery word's representation is built using both the words before it and the words after it, at the same time — not read strictly left-to-right
attentionthe transformer mechanism that lets the model weigh how much each other word in the sentence should influence a given word's representation
pre-trainingBERT is first trained on a huge, general corpus (masked word prediction, sentence-pair prediction) before ever seeing a finance-specific example

BERT itself is trained on general text — Wikipedia, books — and knows nothing finance-specific out of the box. Dogu Araci's FinBERT — "FinBERT: Financial Sentiment Analysis with Pre-trained Language Models," arXiv:1908.10063, 2019 (cited in full below) — is stated plainly here: FinBERT is BERT, further trained on financial text specifically. Starting from BERT's general-language pre-training, FinBERT is fine-tuned on finance-domain text and labeled financial-sentiment examples, so it learns the finance-specific meaning of vocabulary that general BERT would read differently — "bearish," "guidance cut," "beat estimates" carry precise financial connotations a general-purpose model wasn't specifically trained to weight correctly. The output of this fine-tuned model, run on a piece of financial text, is a sentiment score directly from raw text — for example a probability distribution over positive/negative/neutral, or, framed for a central-bank-statement use case, a hawkish/dovish score — produced end-to-end without a human ever hand-scoring that specific sentence.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Word2Vec is like a dictionary that gives every word exactly one fixed definition card, no matter which sentence it shows up in. BERT is more like a person who reads the whole sentence before deciding what any one word in it really means — "hold" in "the Fed will hold rates" gets read completely differently from "hold" in "hold the door." FinBERT is that same whole-sentence reader, except it's spent extra time specifically studying financial documents, so it knows that "the committee remains attentive to inflation risks" is a loaded, specific phrase in a Fed statement — not a bland, throwaway sentence the way a general reader might mistake it for.

A named scenario

Sentiment as one additional input alongside the dot plot and the Taylor Rule

Consider a monitoring workflow that already tracks two inputs covered elsewhere in this curriculum: the Fed's dot-plot median (from Central Bank Policy) and the gap between the actual policy rate and what the Taylor Rule's formula, \(i = r^{*}+\pi+a(\pi-\pi^{*})+b(y-y^{*})\), mechanically implies (from The Taylor Rule). A FinBERT-style sentiment score, computed on the full text of each statement and tracked meeting-over-meeting, could sit alongside those two as a third input — one that captures information the dot plot and the Taylor Rule structurally cannot, because both of those are built from numbers, while a genuine tone shift in the statement's language (the "highly attentive to inflation risks" hedge above) can appear before it shows up in either the dots or the Taylor Rule gap.

What this scenario deliberately does not claim: that such a pipeline has been built, backtested, or shown to add predictive value over the dot plot and Taylor Rule inputs alone, anywhere in this repo. It is presented purely to illustrate where a text-derived score would slot into an existing, already-covered analytical picture — a sketch of the mechanism, not a validated addition to it.

Worth remembering: a sentiment score is noisy. Models can misread sarcasm, miss a hedge phrase's real weight, or be thrown off by context the training data never covered — a "not unreasonable" can register very differently from a genuinely neutral statement depending on subtleties a model may or may not catch. A sentiment score is not a calibrated probability of anything real-world happening next. This is a real, widely-used industry technique across trading desks and research shops — that is explicitly not the same claim as it being validated for any specific use in this repo.
Section 03

It actually happened: "Twitter mood predicts the stock market"

It actually happened

87.6% accuracy, a hedge fund, and a replication that didn't come back

In 2010 three researchers, Johan Bollen, Huina Mao and Xiaojun Zeng, posted a paper with a headline nobody could resist; it was published in the Journal of Computational Science in 2011. They took about 9.8 million tweets from 2008 and turned each day's text into numbers, exactly the text-in, number-out step this lesson describes. They used two lexicon tools: one gave a plain positive/negative score, the other scored six "mood" dimensions (Calm, Alert, Sure, Vital, Kind, Happy).

The plain positive/negative score did not help predict the Dow Jones Industrial Average. But one of the six moods, "Calm", appeared to lead the Dow by a few days. Fed into a neural network, it called the direction of the Dow's daily close with 87.6% accuracy. The test window behind that number was a few weeks of trading days in December 2008.

The press loved it, and the paper became one of the most cited in the field. In 2011 a London hedge fund was launched to trade on Twitter sentiment, and it closed within about a year. In 2017 Lachanski and Pav ("Shy of the Character Limit", Econ Journal Watch) tried to reproduce the result. They could not recover the predictive power.

Read it against this lesson's pitfalls. One of seven sentiment series, at some of several lags, on a test set of weeks, is a search with many chances to find something by luck. The method that mattered was the one that turned text into a number. The step that failed was the one this lesson keeps returning to: whether the number adds anything out of sample.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

You check seven different lucky charms against three weeks of football results, and one of them is right almost every time. You tell everyone. But with seven charms and only three weeks, one of them was bound to look lucky. Next season, the charm doesn't work.

Where the textbook version breaks

The textbook pipeline assumes the score measures a stable property of the text and that the link from score to price is tested on data the researcher never looked at while choosing the model. Here the mood dimensions, the lags and the network were all choices made with the results in view, and the test window was a few weeks. Even a real effect would have a second problem: text that matters is read by many machines at once, so it gets priced in minutes, which is what this desk found when it scored Fed statements.

Desk test: nothing found cb-tone-direction: the statement's tone is priced in half an hour, then it's gone. This lesson's most tempting use, a hawkish/dovish shift in the FOMC statement as a next-day dollar lean, was tested here on N=82 meetings. First-30-minute reaction vs next-day dollar: sign agreement 46%, t = 0.32. Lexicon Δhawkishness vs next-day: t = −0.75, though it IS priced inside 30 minutes (t = 2.04). |Δhawkishness| vs next-day size: t = 0.54. Do not carry the tone forward as a lean.
Section 04

How practitioners actually use this

In practice, text-derived sentiment rarely stands alone as a signal; it's typically one feature among many feeding a broader model, precisely because of the noise problem named above — a single sentiment score from a single document is a weak, high-variance read, but averaged and tracked across many documents (many headlines about the same event, many analyst notes on the same earnings call, successive statements from the same committee) the noise partially cancels and the trend becomes more informative than any single reading. Desks that use this kind of signal typically validate it the same way any other candidate feature gets validated — checking whether it adds information beyond what's already captured by numeric data, over a genuinely out-of-sample period, rather than trusting a sentiment score simply because it comes from a sophisticated-sounding model.

Word embeddings and full transformer models also serve as building blocks well beyond a single sentiment score: a Word2Vec-style embedding of financial vocabulary can feed a downstream classifier or clustering step, and a fine-tuned BERT/FinBERT model's intermediate representations (not just its final sentiment output) can be used as engineered features in their own right — the sentiment score is the most legible, headline-friendly output of this machinery, but not the only useful one.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where NLP sentiment gets over-trusted

  • Trusting a single document's score. One headline, one paragraph is a high-variance, noisy read — the trend across many documents or many meetings is far more informative than any single sentiment reading.
  • Missing sarcasm, negation, and hedged language. "Not unreasonable to expect inflation to moderate" and "reasonable to expect inflation to moderate" can be read very differently by a model than by a careful human reader — models can and do get subtle negation and hedging wrong.
  • Reading a sentiment score as a calibrated probability. A FinBERT output like "0.72 hawkish" is not a 72% chance that policy tightens — it is a model's internal confidence measure, useful for relative comparison across documents, not a real-world probability.
  • Applying a general-purpose sentiment model to finance-specific text. Without FinBERT-style fine-tuning, a general sentiment model can badly misread finance vocabulary — "aggressive growth," "bearish," and "beat expectations" carry meanings a general-English model was never specifically trained to weight correctly.
  • Assuming the technique's popularity is evidence of validated edge here. NLP sentiment scoring is genuinely widely used across the industry — that fact says nothing about whether any specific pipeline built on it has been backtested or shown to add value in this repo.
Scenario drill · 2011

A fund wants to trade Twitter mood

A widely reported paper says one mood series built from about 9.8 million tweets, "Calm", called the Dow's daily direction with 87.6% accuracy. The test window was a few weeks of trading days in December 2008, after seven sentiment series were tried at several lags. A new London fund will trade the signal and asks you to allocate. What do you do?

  1. Allocate. 87.6% is far above chance, and the paper is everywhere.
    Seven series, several lags and a test window of weeks give many chances to find something by luck. Press coverage and citations measure attention, not out-of-sample evidence.
  2. Ask for the signal's record on data the researchers never saw, with the number of series and lags tried counted against it, before committing anything.
    This is the step the lesson keeps returning to. Turning text into a score always works; whether the score adds anything has to be shown out of sample, with the search that found it taken into account.
  3. Allocate a small pilot and watch the live results.
    A small live test limits the damage and does produce genuinely out-of-sample evidence. But it pays to run a test that a fresh historical check could have done first, and a few months of daily direction calls is itself a noisy sample.
  4. Build your own version with FinBERT and more mood series, and keep whichever works best.
    A better text model does not fix the problem. Adding more series and keeping the winner widens the search that produced the original result, making a lucky fit more likely, not less.

Section 06

Self-test

Q1. State Word2Vec's core idea in one sentence, and name its two training objectives.
Represent words as dense vectors positioned so that words used in similar contexts end up close together in vector space. The two training objectives are skip-gram (predict the surrounding context words given one target word) and CBOW, Continuous Bag of Words (predict the target word given its surrounding context words) — mirror images of the same underlying co-occurrence signal.
Q2. What specific limitation of Word2Vec does BERT's bidirectional, context-aware design fix?
Word2Vec assigns exactly one fixed vector to each word regardless of the sentence it appears in — "hold" gets the same representation whether it means "the Fed will hold rates" or "hold my coffee." BERT reads an entire sentence bidirectionally via attention, so every word's representation is built using both the words before and after it, letting the same word receive a different, context-appropriate representation depending on the actual sentence it's used in.
Q3. What exactly is FinBERT, relative to BERT?
FinBERT is BERT further trained (fine-tuned) on financial text specifically. It starts from BERT's general pre-training on broad text like Wikipedia and books, then continues training on finance-domain text and labeled financial-sentiment examples, so it learns the finance-specific meaning of vocabulary — like "bearish," "guidance cut," or "beat estimates" — that a general-purpose BERT model was never specifically trained to weight correctly.
Q4. Why does the lesson insist a sentiment score is not a calibrated probability, even when a model outputs something that looks like one (e.g. "0.72 hawkish")?
A number like 0.72 coming out of a FinBERT-style classifier is the model's internal confidence measure given its training and architecture — useful for relatively ranking or comparing documents against each other — but it is not a real-world probability that, say, the central bank tightens policy with 72% odds. Treating it as a calibrated real-world probability confuses a model's internal output scale with an actual, validated forecast of what will happen.
Q5. Why does the lesson recommend tracking a sentiment score's trend across many documents or meetings, rather than trusting a single reading?
A single document's sentiment score is a high-variance, noisy read — one model misreading one hedge phrase or one instance of sarcasm can swing the number meaningfully. Averaging across many documents about the same event, or tracking the trajectory across successive meetings, lets much of that per-document noise partially cancel out, so the trend becomes a more informative signal than any single isolated reading.
