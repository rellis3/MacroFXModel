Graph Theory & Networks 2 · Graph Theory & Networks

Correlation Networks, Pruned Down to a Picture

A correlation matrix for even a modest basket is a wall of numbers nobody actually reads — N(N−1)/2 pairwise values, none of them showing you the shape of the whole basket at once. This lesson turns that wall into a graph, then prunes the graph down to the handful of edges that matter: its Minimum Spanning Tree. It's the direct sequel to Graph Theory Fundamentals & Centrality Measures, applying that lesson's vocabulary — nodes, edges, weights — specifically to a correlation matrix.

The whole idea
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

I've got a correlation table for five currencies. Ten numbers. I can't see any shape in it.

Nobody can. So turn it into a map. Each currency is a dot, and the distance between two dots says how differently they move.

Why not just use the correlation as the distance?

Because it points the wrong way. A correlation of one means identical, and identical should be zero distance. So Rosario Mantegna flipped it in 1999. Take one minus the correlation, double it, and take the square root.

Give me a number.

Euro and sterling at a correlation of point eight. One minus point eight is point two. Double it, point four. The square root is about point six three. Unrelated pairs land at about one point four one. Perfect opposites land at two.

Fine. Now I have ten distances. Still a tangle.

So prune it. Keep the cheapest set of links that still reaches every currency, with no loops. That is the minimum spanning tree. For five dots it always has four links.

How do you find it?

Kruskal's way is simple. Sort the distances, shortest first. Take each one unless it would close a loop. Euro to sterling, keep. Euro to Swiss franc, keep. Sterling to franc, skip, they already meet through the euro.

And the finished picture?

The euro is the hub, touching three of the four links. The yen, weakly tied to everything, hangs off the Aussie dollar by one long link. The table had that information. The tree makes you see it.

So the yen is my diversifier.

In calm markets. Remember March twenty twenty. Funds needed dollars, so they sold even Treasuries and gold. Correlations converged. If every correlation in our basket went to point nine, every distance would shrink to about point four five, shorter than the tightest calm link.

So the tree collapses.

Into one clump. The diversifiers moved in exactly when you needed them to stay away. A tree built on January's data described a market that no longer existed by mid March.

So the one-line version?

The tree shows you the structure of your book today. It is a picture, not a backtest, and the calm tree is not the crisis tree.

Section 1

A table nobody can eyeball

A correlation heatmap already contains this information. The problem is how fast it grows.

5

5 instruments → 10 pairs

Small enough to scan a table by eye. This is the size of the worked example running through this whole deck: EUR, GBP, CHF, JPY, AUD.

20

20 instruments → 190 pairs

A realistic cross-asset basket. Nobody looks at 190 correlation numbers and sees a clean clustering structure — the pattern is in there, but it isn't visible.

50

50 instruments → 1,225 pairs

N(N−1)/2 grows roughly with the square of N. Doubling the basket roughly quadruples the numbers a human would need to hold in mind at once.

Between "the whole basket, compressed into a handful of factors" (the PCA lesson) and "exactly one pair, tested for an equilibrium" (the cointegration lesson) sits a third view this lesson builds: the basket's full pairwise structure, kept intact, but turned into something a person can actually look at.

Section 2

Correlation points the wrong way to be a distance

In any graph, a small edge weight should mean "close." Correlation runs backwards from that. Step through the three checkpoints.

Try it
ρ = 1
→
d = √(2(1−1)) = 0
Zero distance. Moving in perfect lockstep is, correctly, "as close as two instruments can be."
ρ = 0
→
d = √(2(1−0)) ≈ 1.41
The neutral, "no relationship" distance — √2, right in the middle of the 0-to-2 range.
ρ = −1
→
d = √(2(1−(−1))) = 2
The maximum possible distance. Moving in perfect opposition is, in this metric, as far apart as two series can be.
This is Mantegna's (1999) transform: d_ij = √(2(1 − ρ_ij)). It's specifically chosen because it satisfies the triangle inequality — a requirement for shortest-path and spanning-tree machinery to behave the way graph theory expects. A simpler transform like 1 − ρ alone doesn't reliably satisfy that.

Section 3

Every pair, connected — which is still the problem

Complete graph

Compute d_ij for every pair of N instruments and every node connects to every other node — N(N−1)/2 edges, one per correlation. This is the same adjacency-matrix idea from the previous lesson, just built from distances instead of raw connection strengths.

Still unreadable

A fully-connected graph on 20 instruments has 190 edges drawn on top of each other. "Closeness" is now well-defined for every pair — but a picture with every possible line drawn on it is a mess of crossings, not a clustering structure a person can see.

The fix isn't a better layout algorithm — it's throwing most of those edges away, deliberately, while keeping the graph connected. That's exactly what a spanning tree does.

Section 3, continued

The cheapest road network that still reaches every town

Imagine a planner given the cost of building a road directly between any two towns, told: connect every town to the network, somehow, for the least total construction cost, with zero redundant loops. That specific object — the lowest-total-weight way to connect every node with no cycles — is a Minimum Spanning Tree.
✓

Touches every node

A spanning tree reaches every instrument in the basket — nothing is left isolated.

⊘

No cycles

Any loop is, by definition, at least one edge too many — a redundant road that adds cost without connecting anything new.

N−1

Exactly N−1 edges

The minimum number of edges that can still connect N nodes at all. Many spanning trees exist; the minimum one is the one whose edges sum to the lowest total weight.

Replace "towns" with instruments and "road cost" with the Mantegna distance from Section 2, and the same construction answers a market-structure question: which pairs are the tightest, most direct links in the whole basket, and how do smaller clusters connect to the rest through the fewest, cheapest bridges?

Both greedy choices ("shortest available, unless it closes a cycle") and both provably optimal — Kruskal's algorithm never has to backtrack or reconsider an earlier pick.
Section 4, continued

From a tangle to a backbone

Same five nodes, same ten distances. Left: every edge exists. Right: only the four that survive pruning.

Complete graph — 10 edges

N(N−1)/2 = 10 edges for 5 nodes. No edge stands out as more important than any other.

Minimum spanning tree — 4 edges

EUR sits at the hub of 3 of the 4 edges. JPY — weakest correlated with everything — attaches only through its single least-bad relationship, AUD.

Nothing about the underlying data changed between these two pictures — only which edges are drawn. The MST doesn't invent structure; it reveals structure that was always in the correlation matrix but wasn't visible at a glance.

Section 5

Where this actually gets used

🌳

Hierarchical clustering

Often drawn alongside a dendrogram — repeatedly merging the closest clusters gives the same backbone from a different algorithmic angle.

📊

Portfolio sanity checks

A basket whose MST is one long chain looks structurally different from one with a dense hub — a fast qualitative diversification read before running any optimizer.

🔗

Network stability monitoring

Some studies track how MST topology changes across regimes — the tree "flattens" toward one dominant hub in stressed markets — as a structural, not just numerical, crisis signal.

🧮

A different lens than PCA or RMT

The PCA lesson collapses the matrix into ranked, orthogonal factors. The Random Matrix Theory lesson asks which eigenvalues are signal versus noise. The MST keeps every pairwise relationship intact as a sparse graph. Three lenses, one correlation matrix.

Where this needs qualifying

Mantegna's original 1999 demonstration is an equities result — a Dow Jones basket, where the tree recovered recognizable industry clusters. Applying the same transform to an FX or cross-asset basket, as this lesson's worked example does, is mathematically sound but a different application than the one Mantegna tested. As of this lesson, that FX/cross-asset extension remains a concept — it is not built or tested anywhere in this repo's actual code (checked directly: no MST, graph-pruning, or correlation-to-distance logic exists in this repo's source alongside the correlation and eigenvalue tooling in js/diversificationCore.js and related files). Treat the FX framing as a plausible hypothesis worth testing carefully, not a proven technique.

Section 5, continued

A picture is not a backtest

What the MST tells you
  • Which pairs are the tightest, most direct links in the basket
  • Whether an instrument is a structural hub or a lonely outlier
  • How smaller clusters bridge into the rest of the basket, and how cheaply
What it doesn't tell you
  • Not fixed — a tree built on calm data and one built on crisis data can disagree about the hub
  • Tree distance isn't "hops away" — a node two edges from the hub isn't half as correlated as one directly connected
  • Small baskets are unstable — two close distances can swap which one the tree keeps with only modest changes to the correlation estimate
  • Seeing a genuine outlier is a diversification read, not proof that adding it improves risk-adjusted returns
Section 6 · It actually happened

March 2020: every distance shrank at once

It actually happened

As COVID-19 spread, the S&P 500 fell about a third from its February peak to 23 March, and the VIX closed at a record 82.69 on 16 March. Then came the dash for cash. Funds facing margin calls and redemptions sold whatever still had a bid, Treasuries and gold included. For several days long Treasury yields rose while stocks fell, gold fell with them, and the dollar surged, until the Fed cut to zero, bought Treasuries in size and widened its dollar swap lines. With every correlation near 0.9, every distance d = √(2(1−ρ)) is about 0.45, shorter than the worked example's tightest calm edge (0.63).

Where the textbook version breaks

An MST is one correlation matrix from a trailing window, which quietly assumes correlations hold. In March 2020 a common need for cash drove every asset in the same week, a regime change faster than any window. January's tree described a market that was gone by mid-March.

Section 8

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Why can't a raw correlation coefficient be used directly as a graph distance?

A distance metric needs "close" to mean a small number. Raw correlation has it backwards — perfectly correlated pairs would register as maximally far apart — so a transform like d = √(2(1−ρ)) is needed first.
Question 2

Using d = √(2(1−ρ)), what is the distance when ρ = 1?

√(2(1−1)) = √0 = 0. Identical behavior means zero distance, exactly as a proper distance metric should behave.
Question 3

What does a Minimum Spanning Tree guarantee about the edges it keeps?

A spanning tree touches every node with no cycles, using exactly N−1 edges. The MST is specifically the one, among all possible spanning trees, whose edges sum to the lowest total weight.
Question 4

In the worked example, why did Kruskal's algorithm skip GBP–CHF (distance 0.89) even though it's shorter than edges added afterward?

By the time the sorted list reached GBP–CHF, both endpoints were already in the same connected component (via EUR–GBP and EUR–CHF). Adding it would close the EUR-GBP-CHF triangle into a cycle without connecting any new node — rejected regardless of how short it is.
Question 5

Is applying Mantegna's transform to an FX/cross-asset basket a technique this repo has validated?

The transform and MST construction don't care what asset class the data comes from, so it runs correctly on FX data. But whether the resulting FX tree is economically meaningful is a separate empirical question — this repo has not tested it, and no MST/graph-pruning code exists here yet.
Your call
Scenario drill · 16 March 2020

Your diversifier has stopped diversifying

You run a currency book across EUR, GBP, CHF and AUD, and you added JPY because a tree built on calm data showed it hanging far from the hub. It is Monday. Last night the Federal Reserve cut rates to zero. Funds facing margin calls are selling whatever has a bid, Treasuries and gold included, and the dollar is surging. The VIX will close at a record 82.69 today. What do you do with your tree?

  1. Trust it. JPY is the outlier in your tree, so your book is still diversified.
    The tree was built on a window that described a market that no longer exists. When correlations rush toward 1, every distance shrinks toward 0, and the outlier moves into the clump. The calm tree is not the crisis tree.
  2. Assume the distances have collapsed, treat the book as closer to one trade, and cut gross size until you can re-estimate on recent data.
    This is what the transform says. If correlations go to 0.9, every distance falls to about 0.45, shorter than your tightest calm edge of 0.63. Five positions then behave like one large one, so the size that was right for five separate bets is too big.
  3. Rebuild the tree on the full past year so the estimate is more stable.
    A longer window is steadier, but it is steadier because it is mostly calm data. It will under-report how far correlations have converged this week. Better than nothing, but it answers the wrong question.
  4. Add Treasuries and gold as hedges, since they sit far from currencies and stocks in a calm tree.
    These are exactly the assets being sold for cash this week. Long Treasury yields were rising while equities fell, and gold was falling with stocks. A hedge chosen from a calm-window distance can fail together with the risk it hedges.

What happened. The dash for cash ran on through mid March. The S&P 500 bottomed on 23 March, about a third below its February peak, the same day the Fed made its asset purchases open-ended, after large Treasury buying and wider dollar swap lines. Only after that did the old relationships start to come back. A book sized on its calm-window tree was far less diversified than it looked.

Sources: Federal Reserve FOMC statement, 15 Mar 2020; Federal Reserve press release on open-ended purchases, 23 Mar 2020; S&P 500 close, 19 Feb and 23 Mar 2020 (FRED: SP500); Cboe VIX close, 16 Mar 2020 (FRED: VIXCLS).

Section 9

What to carry forward

01

A raw correlation matrix hides its own structure — N(N−1)/2 numbers is too many for a human to read as a shape, even though the information is technically all there.

02

Correlation isn't a distance until it's transformed — Mantegna's d = √(2(1−ρ)) flips the direction, so identical behavior becomes zero distance and perfect opposition becomes the maximum, 2.

03

Computing every pairwise distance gives a complete graph — fully connected, but still too dense to read as a picture.

04

The Minimum Spanning Tree is the prune — exactly N−1 edges, no cycles, lowest possible total weight, built by Kruskal's (sort, add if no cycle) or Prim's (grow outward from a seed) algorithm.

05

The worked 5-node example revealed a hub-and-outlier shape — EUR as the structural hub, JPY reached only through AUD — that the raw ten-number table didn't show at a glance.

06

The FX/cross-asset application is a plausible extension of an equities result, not a validated one — and nothing in this repo's code builds or tests it yet.

07

The calm tree is not the crisis tree: in March 2020's dash for cash, correlations converged and the diversifiers moved in. Everything-down days precede a wider week on this desk's data (SPX500 +0.31 ATR), with direction at the ordinary drift.

What this now lets you do

Turn any basket's correlation matrix into a distance graph and prune it to its backbone, so a basket's clustering structure — who's really close to whom, who's the genuine outlier — becomes a picture instead of a table you'd have to scan number by number. Read that picture correctly: as a structural hint worth investigating, not as a finished backtest result on its own.
