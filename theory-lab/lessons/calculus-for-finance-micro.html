Deeper Foundations 2

Calculus, or the Math of Change and Accumulation

Three lessons already in this Theory Lab quietly lean on calculus without teaching it: Ornstein-Uhlenbeck writes a "rate of change," Black-Scholes builds a whole equation out of how price reacts to the underlying, and Kelly Criterion pulls a σ²/2 correction out of nowhere. This deck builds the three ideas underneath all of that — the derivative, the integral, and the Taylor series — from nothing, so none of it has to be taken on faith later.

The whole idea
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

I last did calculus at school. Do I really need it to trade?

You use it every day already. Think of a car. The speedometer tells you how fast you are going right now. That is a derivative: a rate of change at one instant.

And the odometer?

The odometer adds up every instant of speed into a total distance. That is an integral. The two are mirror images: one undoes the other.

How do you get "right now" out of a formula, though?

Measure the average speed over a gap, then shrink the gap towards zero. For x squared, the average slope from x to a little further on works out to two x plus the gap. Shrink the gap away and the slope is exactly two x.

And Taylor series?

A way to guess a value nearby from what you know at one point. First guess: nothing changes. Second: keep going at today's slope, the tangent line. Third: add a correction for how the curve bends.

Show me it working.

Take the log of one plus a return. The bent guess is the return minus half its square. For a five percent move the error is about four hundred-thousandths. For a thirty percent move it is about seven thousandths, nearly two hundred times bigger. Great for small steps, worse for big ones.

Where does that show up on a desk?

Average that same shape over many periods and you get volatility drag: compound growth is roughly the average return minus half the variance. At twenty percent volatility, that is about two percent a year.

And options?

Delta is the derivative of the option price with respect to the underlying. A delta of zero point six means a one dollar move in the stock moves the option about sixty cents. Gamma is the bend, the second-order correction. That's why desks watch both.

Who brought this into finance?

Robert Merton, around nineteen sixty-nine to seventy-one, using Itô's calculus for random paths. In nineteen seventy-three it gave Black, Scholes and Merton the option price. The second-order term that survives randomness is exactly where volatility enters.

So the one-liner?

A derivative is speed right now, an integral is distance so far, and a two-term Taylor guess explains delta, gamma and volatility drag, as long as the move is small.

Section 1 · How it reached trading

Hedging something that never stops moving

How it reached trading

Robert Merton · 1969–71 · Black, Scholes & Merton · 1973

Ordinary calculus assumes smooth paths; prices jiggle at every scale. Merton (1969–71) brought Itô's stochastic calculus (1940s probability theory) into finance, and in 1973 it gave the option price.

The key: for a random path the second-order Taylor term doesn't vanish. That is σ²/2, behind volatility drag, gamma P&L and convexity.

Section 2

Average speed vs. the speedometer, right now

Drag the slider. As the second point slides toward the first, the secant line rotates into the tangent line.

Try it
Gap size, hh = 22.00
Secant line — the red and purple points are far apart, so this line's slope is only an average rate of change over the whole gap.

Slide h all the way down and the two points merge into one: the line stops being a secant connecting two points and becomes the tangent — the single line that just grazes the curve at that instant. Formally, average rate of change is rise over run, [f(x+h) − f(x)] / h. The derivative is what that ratio approaches as h shrinks all the way to zero: f′(x) = lim(h→0) [f(x+h) − f(x)] / h. A fixed, non-zero h always gives an approximation; only the limit gives the exact instantaneous value.

Section 3

One derivative, worked in full: d/dx(x²) = 2x

Every step of the algebra, so nothing is taken on faith.

Try it
f(x+h)
=
(x+h)²
=
x² + 2xh + h²
Expanding the square produces one term linear in h (2xh) and one quadratic in h (h²) — the h² term is what carries this curve's "curviness."
(x² + 2xh + h²) − x²
=
2xh + h²
The x² term cancels exactly against −f(x), leaving only the two terms that involve h.
(2xh + h²) / h
=
2x + h
Both remaining terms share a factor of h, so it cancels cleanly — valid because this division happens before h reaches zero, not after.
lim(h→0) (2x + h)
=
2x
The leftover h term shrinks to exactly zero. What survives, 2x, is the derivative — exactly, not approximately.
A straight line f(x) = x has no surviving h term at all — f(x+h) − f(x) = h, giving a derivative of exactly 1 everywhere. The h² term is precisely what a curve has that a line doesn't.

This same limit machinery generalizes into rules you'd otherwise memorize by rote: for f(x) = xⁿ, the derivative is always nxⁿ⁻¹ (here, n=2 gives 2x¹ = 2x). A constant added anywhere contributes nothing, because it cancels in the subtraction step exactly like x² did above. Every "differentiation rule" is this same four-step limit, done once in general instead of by hand each time.

Section 4

The integral: area you don't have to add up by hand

A different question: not "how fast is f changing," but "how much area sits under the curve?"

Chop the interval from a to b into many thin rectangles of width Δx, each with height roughly f(x) at that slice, add up the rectangle areas, then let the slices get infinitesimally thin. The jagged sum becomes the exact, smooth area: ∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx = lim(Δx→0) Σ f(xᵢ) Δx.

The shortcut nobody skips

Summing infinite slices by hand is tedious, so nobody does it. The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus — next slide — turns that entire sum into a two-step subtraction.

Section 5

The speedometer and the odometer are mirror images

Derivative — the speedometer

Tells you your speed right now. Given a distance-traveled function, the derivative reads off the instantaneous rate at any instant — the tangent-line slope from Section 2.

Integral — the odometer

Tells you total distance so far. Given a speed function, the integral accumulates every instant of speed into a running total — the shaded area from Section 4.

The mirror relationship isn't a coincidence — it's a theorem. The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus says differentiation and integration are exact inverses: if F is any antiderivative of f (meaning F′(x) = f(x)), the definite integral collapses to plain subtraction.

∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx
=
F(b) − F(a)
Read plainly

Find a function whose derivative is your curve, evaluate it at the two endpoints, subtract. The odometer reading at the end minus the odometer reading at the start gives total distance — you never had to add up infinitely many instants of speed.

Section 6

Taylor series: guessing a function from one point alone

If all you know is a function's value and its rate(s) of change at one point x, how good a guess can you make nearby?

0

Zeroth order — f(x)

The flat guess: assume nothing changes at all. f(x+h) ≈ f(x). Fine only if the function barely moves.

1

First order — f(x) + f′(x)h

Add the tangent-line rise: today's value, plus slope times step size. This is exactly the tangent line from Section 2, extended a finite distance h.

2

Second order — + ½f″(x)h²

Correct for the curve bending away from that straight tangent line. f″(x), the derivative of the derivative, measures exactly how fast the slope itself is changing.

…

Higher orders exist too

A full Taylor series keeps adding terms (f‴(x)h³/6, and on) for an ever-closer match. Finance almost always stops at second order — accurate enough for small moves, far simpler than carrying more.

f(x+h) ≈ f(x) + f′(x)h + ½f″(x)h² — each added term is a correction for exactly what the previous term missed. This second-order version is the workhorse used throughout the rest of this curriculum.
Section 7

Where the σ²/2 shape actually comes from

Expand f(x) = ln(1+x) around x = 0 using the second-order Taylor formula. Three numbers are needed.

Three ingredients
f(0) = ln(1)
=
0
f′(x) = 1/(1+x)
→
f′(0) = 1
f″(x) = −1/(1+x)²
→
f″(0) = −1
Plug all three into f(0+h) ≈ f(0) + f′(0)h + [f″(0)/2]h² :
0 + (1)h + (−1/2)h²
=
h − h²/2

Relabel the step h as a per-period return R, and this is exactly the shortcut used — without derivation — back in the Returns primer: ln(1+R) ≈ R − R²/2. It isn't a separate finance approximation someone invented; it's the general-purpose Taylor formula with three specific numbers dropped in. The minus-one-half is not a design choice — it falls straight out of f″(0) = −1 for this particular function.

Section 8

Same shape, twice: volatility drag and Kelly

Average the identity ln(1+R) ≈ R − R²/2 across many periods and the σ²/2 term in the Kelly lesson falls out directly.

avg[ln(1+R)]
=
g
≈
avg[R] − avg[R²]/2

The left side, averaged, is the compounded growth rate g (because log returns add across time). On the right, avg[R] is the arithmetic mean return μ; for a return series whose mean is small relative to its spread, avg[R²] is well approximated by the variance σ². Put the averaged sides back together: g ≈ μ − σ²/2 — the exact volatility-drag formula the Kelly Criterion lesson uses. Same h − h²/2 shape, relabeled once per period and once again after averaging.

Section 9

Derivatives you're already trading with

📐

Delta is dC/dS

An option's delta is literally the first-order Taylor term of its price function with respect to the underlying — read off a risk screen instead of derived from a limit by hand.

∿

Gamma is the curvature correction

ΔC ≈ δΔS is a first-order (tangent-line) estimate, good only for small moves. Gamma is d²C/dS² — the second-order term that corrects it for a larger move, exactly ½γ(ΔS)².

🎯

Optimizing = setting a derivative to zero

Kelly's growth-optimal fraction and a regression's least-squares coefficients are both found the same way: differentiate the objective, solve where it's flat.

∫

Integrals as probability and P&L

A CDF is the integral of a density. An equity curve is the integral of a P&L rate over time — the odometer-from-speedometer idea, applied to trading.

Where this needs qualifying

Real price series only exist at discrete points, so there's no literal h→0 to take. Practitioners approximate a derivative with a finite difference — Δf/Δx over the smallest available step, or a rolling-window regression slope — and accept the extra noise that comes with picking a window. And every Taylor approximation is local: delta alone, without the gamma correction, is the single most common way this local-ness bites in practice.

Section 10

Quick check

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Why is the derivative defined with a limit, h→0, instead of just picking a small fixed h?

A fixed, non-zero h always describes the average rate of change over that gap. Only shrinking h all the way to zero removes the dependence on gap size and leaves the single instantaneous slope.
Question 2

In expanding (x+h)², what does the h² term represent, and why does it vanish in the final derivative?

A straight line has no h² term at all — that's exactly the difference between a line and a curve. Dividing by h leaves a single h behind, and that term correctly goes to zero as h→0.
Question 3

What does the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus actually say?

∫ₐᵇ f(x)dx = F(b) − F(a) for any antiderivative F of f. This is exactly why nobody sums infinite slices by hand.
Question 4

Where does the σ²/2 in g ≈ μ − σ²/2 come from?

f(x)=ln(1+x) has f(0)=0, f′(0)=1, f″(0)=−1. Plugging into f(0+h) ≈ f(0)+f′(0)h+f″(0)h²/2 gives h − h²/2; averaging over periods turns that into μ − σ²/2.
Question 5

Why is delta alone insufficient for a large move in the underlying?

ΔC ≈ δΔS ignores curvature. Adding ½γ(ΔS)² — the second-order Taylor term — captures the part of the move delta alone misses.
Your call
Scenario drill · an illustrative overnight gap

Delta says you lose $6. Do you?

You have sold the lesson's call option: price $4.30, underlying at $100, delta 0.60. For this drill, suppose its gamma is 0.03. Overnight the underlying gaps up $10, to $110. Your quick risk screen uses delta only and says the option gained about \(0.60 \times 10 = \$6.00\), so you lost about $6.00 per option. What do you expect the real loss to be?

  1. About $6.00. Delta is the derivative, so it gives the answer.
    Delta is only the first-order term: the tangent line. A $10 gap is not a small step, and the option's price curve bends away from that line.
  2. More than $6.00. Add gamma's term, \(\tfrac12\gamma(\Delta S)^2\): half of 0.03 times 100 is $1.50, so about $7.50. Then confirm by repricing.
    This is the second-order Taylor expansion: \(\Delta C \approx \delta\,\Delta S\) \(+\, \tfrac12\gamma\,(\Delta S)^2\). For a short option, the curvature always works against you, whichever way the big move goes.
  3. Less than $6.00. Big moves are rare, so the estimate is probably too high.
    Rarity has nothing to do with it once the move has happened. A call's value curves upward, so for a rise it gains more than the tangent line says, not less.
  4. No idea until the option is fully repriced.
    Full repricing is the most exact answer and worth doing. But the Taylor terms tell you the direction and rough size immediately, which is the point of having them.

What to take from it. This is an illustrative situation, not a historical event: the price, spot and delta come from the lesson's example, and the gamma of 0.03 is assumed for the drill. With those numbers the delta-gamma estimate is \(6.00 + 1.50 = \$7.50\), a quarter more than delta alone. The bigger the move, the more the second-order term matters.

Section 11

What to carry forward

01

A derivative is the tangent-line slope — the limit of a secant line's average rate of change as the gap between two points shrinks to zero.

02

An integral is accumulated area under a curve, and the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus proves it's the exact inverse of a derivative — find an antiderivative, evaluate at two endpoints, subtract.

03

A Taylor series builds an estimate one correction at a time: zeroth order is flat, first order adds the tangent line, second order corrects for curvature.

04

ln(1+R) ≈ R − R²/2 and g ≈ μ − σ²/2 are the identical second-order Taylor expansion of ln(1+x), applied once per period and once again after averaging — not two separate finance shortcuts.

05

Delta and gamma are literally Taylor terms — delta is the first-order derivative of an option's price with respect to the underlying, gamma is the second-order curvature correction.

06

Every Taylor approximation is local — accurate near the expansion point, and increasingly wrong the larger the step, exactly as the worked example's error growth showed.

07

Merton (1969–71) brought Itô's calculus to finance; the surviving second-order Taylor term (σ²/2) drives volatility drag, gamma P&L and Black–Scholes–Merton.

What this now lets you do

Read dX_t = θ(μ−X_t)dt + σdW_t in the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck lesson as exactly what it is — a rate of change. Recognise delta and gamma on a risk screen as first- and second-order Taylor terms rather than options-specific magic. Rederive "20% vol costs about 2% a year" from scratch instead of memorizing it, and know exactly why the minus-one-half is there.
