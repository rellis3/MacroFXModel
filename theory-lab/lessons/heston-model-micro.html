The Heston Model, When Volatility Gets Its Own Volatility

Black-Scholes prices every option off one fixed volatility, forever. Real options markets don't cooperate — the same underlying, same expiry, prices meaningfully differently across strikes, a shape called the volatility smile (or skew). Heston's 1993 fix is to stop treating volatility as a constant at all: make it a second, mean-reverting stochastic process in its own right, correlated with the asset it belongs to. That one structural change is what lets a model generate a smile organically — at the cost of a formula that needs a numerical Fourier step instead of simple algebra, and five parameters to calibrate instead of one.

The whole idea
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

Black-Scholes wants one volatility number for the whole life of the option. I have never seen volatility sit still for a week.

Nobody has. Volatility clusters. Calm spells and wild spells come and go. Steven Heston's 1993 model takes that seriously. Volatility gets its own equation, running alongside the price.

What does that equation say?

Mostly, it is a rubber band. Variance gets pulled back toward a long-run level, harder the further away it is. On top of that it gets its own random shocks, so it never moves in a straight line.

Give me a number.

Say volatility is thirty percent today and its long-run level is twenty. Set the pull speed to two a year. Then half the gap in variance closes in about a third of a year. That is roughly a hundred and twenty-six days.

Does that sound sensible?

It is a useful sanity check on any fitted model. If the half-life came out at two days, or ten years, you would distrust the fit.

So where does the smile come from?

From one extra number: the correlation between price shocks and volatility shocks. In equities it is usually negative. Prices fall, volatility jumps. People call it the leverage effect.

And that makes puts dearer.

Exactly. A big fall tends to arrive together with a volatility spike, so the left tail is fatter than the right. A put pays off in exactly that state. Back out a Black-Scholes volatility for each strike and the low strikes come out higher. That is the skew.

When did the market start caring?

October 1987. Before the crash, index option vols were fairly flat across strikes. Afterwards, out-of-the-money puts stayed richer, and the skew never went away. Heston gave desks a model that could produce it, with near closed-form prices.

And for currencies?

The skew points at whichever side of the pair the market fears, and the sign of the correlation flips if you flip the quote. Bates added jumps in 1996 to fit Deutsche mark options.

So is the skew free money? Sell the expensive puts?

No. It is a structural price for a real risk: falls and vol spikes arriving together. Selling it is taking that tail risk. And a model that fits today's smile tells you nothing on its own about a trading edge.

One line?

Let volatility move on its own, tie its shocks to price with a negative correlation, and the flat line tilts into the skew.

After the 1987 crash, a skew that would not go away

How it reached trading

After October 1987, index puts stayed permanently richer than at-the-money options. Black-Scholes cannot do that, and earlier stochastic-vol models lacked correlation or fast pricing.

Heston (1993) correlated CIR's square-root variance with the price and priced through a characteristic function. His title names currency options; Bates (1996) added jumps for Deutsche mark options. It is now a base for many exotics models.

Section 2

Black-Scholes assumes one volatility. Markets don't cooperate.

Plain Black-Scholes prices every option off a single constant σ, held fixed for the option's entire life.

What the model assumes vs. what actually happens
The GARCH lesson in this Theory Lab already proved this empirically: volatility has memory, and calm and turbulent regimes persist and decay over time. Black-Scholes, taken literally, has no room for that at all.

The tell in the options market is direct: two options on the same underlying, same expiry, different strikes, routinely trade at meaningfully different implied volatilities. A single true constant σ could never produce that — if it's genuinely one number, backing it out from any strike should give the same answer every time. It doesn't.

Heston's 1993 fix is, informally, GARCH's own core insight — "variance is itself a process with dynamics, not a fixed input" — carried from discrete-time econometrics into the continuous-time calculus option pricing runs on.
Section 3

Where the smile actually comes from

Same underlying model, three settings. Watch the curve change shape.

Try it
One number, everywhere. Black-Scholes has exactly one volatility parameter per underlying and expiry — there's no mechanism by which it could differ by strike, no matter which σ is chosen.
Symmetric smile, no tilt. Vol-of-vol alone (ξ≠0) bows the curve upward at both wings — a smile, but perfectly symmetric, because variance's wiggles aren't yet linked to which way price moves.
Skewed, and realistic. Add a negative ρ and the curve tilts: low strikes (puts) sit at meaningfully higher implied vol than at-the-money, because a big down-move is disproportionately likely to arrive with a vol spike attached — the shape real options markets actually show.

Two dials, two different jobs: ξ (vol-of-vol) controls how much the curve bows into a smile at all; ρ (correlation) controls whether that smile tilts into an asymmetric skew. Set both to zero and the curve flattens back to plain Black-Scholes.

Section 4

The fix: stop treating variance as a number. Give it its own life.

Same price equation as Black-Scholes — one swap, inside the volatility term.

The one change that matters
σ (a constant)
→
√v_t (its own SDE)
dS_t = μS_t dt + √v_t S_t dW_t¹ — identical to Black-Scholes' price equation, except the thing multiplying the noise is no longer a fixed number. v_t is now a second, time-varying, randomly evolving process that needs its own equation of motion.
Weather that has its own weather. Black-Scholes treats volatility the way a naive forecaster treats temperature: "the average this month is 20°C, so every day is 20°C." Heston instead builds a second little model whose entire job is describing how "today's volatility" drifts, wiggles, and reverts toward a seasonal average — and lets it be correlated with the main price process, so vol tends to spike exactly when price falls.

Heston supplies exactly one new equation for v_t, plus one correlation parameter tying it to price. That's the entire model: two coupled stochastic differential equations instead of one.

Section 5

Reading the variance equation, term by term

dv_t = κ(θ − v_t) dt + ξ√v_t dW_t² — four pieces, each with a job.

κ

Speed of mean reversion

How fast variance gets pulled back toward its long-run level after a shock. Larger κ, faster snap-back.

θ

Long-run variance

The level v_t reverts toward. When v_t > θ the drift pulls down; when v_t < θ, it pulls up.

ξ

Vol-of-vol

How large variance's own random wiggles are. Bigger ξ means variance swings harder around θ instead of reverting smoothly.

√

CIR scaling

The noise term is scaled by √v_t, not a flat constant — this keeps variance from ever diffusing into negative territory, unlike a plain OU process.

This is the exact "rubber band" mean-reversion this Theory Lab's Ornstein-Uhlenbeck lesson already covers — κ(θ − v_t) here is structurally identical to that lesson's κ(μ − X_t), just pointed at variance instead of a price spread. Heston's only real addition on top of plain OU is the √v_t scaling — this specific variant is called a CIR process (Cox-Ingersoll-Ross), the same square-root diffusion originally built for interest rates, another quantity that shouldn't go negative.
Section 6

The correlation ρ is the whole story behind the skew

Same two SDEs, one setting flipped.

ρ = 0 — no leverage effect
  • Price shocks and variance shocks are independent
  • Vol-of-vol (ξ) alone still bows the curve — a symmetric smile
  • No reason for puts to cost more than calls at the same distance from at-the-money
  • Doesn't match what real equity markets actually show
ρ < 0 — the leverage effect
  • A down-move in price and an up-move in variance tend to arrive together
  • Fattens the left tail of the return distribution specifically
  • Out-of-the-money puts price with structurally higher implied vol than at-the-money
  • This is the skew almost every equity index actually shows

Empirically ρ is usually negative in equity markets — the "leverage effect," originally explained for equities by falling stock prices mechanically raising a firm's leverage ratio, though similar spot/vol co-movement shows up in FX too, where no literal corporate leverage mechanism exists — there its sign depends on the pair and which way it is quoted (inverting the pair flips ρ).

Slide ρ toward zero and the curve straightens into a symmetric smile. Push it positive (empirically rare, but mechanically valid) and the tilt flips — now it would be calls pricing richer than puts, because up-moves and vol spikes would be the pair that co-occurs.

Section 8

How fast does a variance shock actually fade?

The same mean-reversion "rubber band" from the OU lesson has a half-life — the time for half a shock's gap to θ to close.

Try it — change any input, live
→
Half-life of a shock
126.5 days (0.35 yr)
Long-run vol √θ = 20.0%
Current vol √v₀ = 30.0%
Half-life = ln(2) / κ = 0.3466 years = 126.5 days
Guard: κ must be a positive, finite number — a zero or negative reversion speed means variance never reverts, so half-life shows "—" instead of an error. θ and v₀ must be non-negative for their vol read-outs to exist.

This isn't Heston's actual option-pricing formula — it's the deterministic-drift half-life of the mean-reversion term alone, the same ln(2)/κ used for any OU-style "rubber band." It's a fast, honest sanity check on a calibrated κ: does a half-life of 126 days sound like a plausible speed for volatility regimes to fade, or does it imply something absurd?

Section 9

Realism isn't free

What Heston costs, compared to what it buys.

Black-Scholes
  • One parameter: σ
  • Fully closed-form — a cumulative-normal formula, plug and go
  • Fast enough for any calculator
  • Structurally cannot produce a smile or skew, ever
Heston
  • Five parameters: κ, θ, ξ, ρ, v₀
  • Semi-closed-form — needs the model's characteristic function, evaluated by numerical Fourier integration
  • Fast enough to calibrate repeatedly through a trading day, but not algebra-on-paper fast
  • Generates a realistic smile/skew directly from its specification
Where this needs qualifying

"Semi-closed-form" means there's a precise pricing expression, not an approximation — but evaluating it needs a numerical integration step, and naive implementations of that integral are known to hit branch-cut discontinuities in the complex logarithm that silently misprice for some parameter combinations. That subtlety is a well-documented practical trap in the numerical-methods literature, not something this lesson derives or resolves.

Section 10

Heston isn't the last word, and it isn't free of traps

+

Bates: stochastic vol plus jumps

Literally Heston's two SDEs with a Merton-style jump term added to the price process — continuous stochastic vol shapes the smile, discrete jumps cover short-dated tail risk pure diffusion can't reach.

≈

Local & local-stochastic vol

Calibrates a vol surface directly to match observed option prices exactly at a point in time — a tighter fit today, at the cost of less clean forward-looking dynamics than Heston's parametric SDEs.

⚠

The Feller condition

2κθ ≥ ξ² is needed to guarantee v_t never hits exactly zero. Many real calibrations to option data land outside this region anyway, with real numerical consequences.

🕓

Calibration is a snapshot, not a law

(κ,θ,ξ,ρ) fitted to today's option surface are risk-neutral values, not real-world dynamics, and routinely come out materially different a week later, especially after a regime shift. A tight in-sample fit is curve-fitting by construction, not a validated edge.

Section 11

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

What does Heston replace Black-Scholes' constant σ with?

Heston's core move is making variance itself a mean-reverting random process — not a scheduled function of time, and not a jump term (that's Bates' later addition on top of Heston).
Question 2

In dv_t = κ(θ − v_t) dt + ξ√v_t dW_t², what does θ represent?

θ is the long-run variance level. Correlation is a separate parameter, ρ; time-to-expiry doesn't appear in this SDE at all.
Question 3

A negative correlation ρ between the price shock and the variance shock produces...

Negative ρ (the leverage effect) fattens the left tail specifically, disproportionately raising the price — and backed-out implied vol — of low-strike puts. That's an asymmetric skew, not a symmetric smile (that shape comes from vol-of-vol alone, at ρ = 0).
Question 4

Why is Heston's pricing formula called "semi-closed-form" rather than fully closed-form like Black-Scholes?

There is a precise expression, but it needs a numerical Fourier/characteristic-function integration step — not simple algebra, and not Monte Carlo either (that's a different, separate numerical approach Heston's semi-closed-form specifically avoids needing).
Question 5

What happens to the Heston model if ρ = 0 and ξ → 0?

ξ≠0 (variance actually moves) is what curves the smile, and ρ≠0 (that movement is linked to price direction) is what tilts it into a skew. Switch both off and there's nothing left to generate a smile with.
Your call
Scenario drill · after 19 October 1987

Out-of-the-money puts look too expensive

On 19 October 1987 the Dow Jones Industrial Average fell about 22.6% in one day. In the months after, you notice that index puts struck well below the market trade at a much higher implied volatility than at-the-money options on the same expiry. Your pricing model uses one flat \(\sigma\), and by it those puts are overpriced. What do you conclude?

  1. The puts are mispriced. Sell them and wait for the skew to flatten back.
    The flat-σ model cannot produce a skew at all, so its verdict of "overpriced" says more about the model than the market. Selling puts here is taking on exactly the crash risk the premium pays for.
  2. Treat the skew as a real feature: falls tend to arrive with volatility spikes, so downside puts are worth more. Price and hedge off the skewed vols, not one flat number.
    This is the Heston reading. Volatility moves, and its shocks are negatively correlated with price, which fattens the left tail. The skew is the market pricing a real asymmetry, not a free lunch to arbitrage away.
  3. Keep the flat-σ model, but use the at-the-money vol and ignore the far strikes.
    It avoids the worst trade, but it misprices every strike away from the money and gives the wrong hedge for downside options, which is where the risk now sits.
  4. Buy the at-the-money options instead, since they look cheap next to the puts.
    "Cheap compared to the puts" is still a flat-σ judgement. Nothing says at-the-money vol is too low; the gap between strikes is the structure, not the error.

What happened. The skew did not go away. Before October 1987, implied volatility on index options was fairly flat across strikes; afterwards, out-of-the-money puts stayed richer and the skew became a permanent feature of index options. Heston's 1993 model was one answer: stochastic volatility with a negative price-vol correlation produces that skew from its own dynamics.

Sources: Federal Reserve History, “Stock Market Crash of 1987” (the Dow’s 22.6% fall); Rubinstein, “Implied Binomial Trees”, Journal of Finance, 1994 (index-option smile before and after the crash); Heston, Review of Financial Studies, 1993.

Section 12

What to carry forward

01

Black-Scholes assumes one constant σ for every strike and expiry — a real implied-vol surface that slopes and curves is direct evidence that assumption is false.

02

Heston's fix: replace σ with √v_t, where v_t is its own mean-reverting stochastic process (a CIR-type SDE) — GARCH's "variance has its own dynamics" insight, ported to continuous time.

03

The variance SDE is OU mean-reversion pointed at variance: κ sets reversion speed, θ the long-run level, ξ the vol-of-vol, with a √v_t scaling that keeps it non-negative.

04

The correlation ρ between price shocks and variance shocks is what turns stochastic vol into a smile: ξ≠0 alone gives symmetric curvature, ρ≠0 tilts it into the asymmetric put-skew markets actually show.

05

The cost of realism: no simple closed-form price — Heston needs a characteristic-function/Fourier numerical step, and calibration means fitting five parameters (κ,θ,ξ,ρ,v₀), not one.

06

A tight calibration fit is not a validated trading edge — parameters are a snapshot of today's option surface, not a physical law, and the Feller condition (2κθ≥ξ²) is often violated in practice.

07

How it reached trading: Heston (1993) answered the post-1987 skew, Bates (1996) took it to FX options with jumps, and it now anchors exotics pricing.

What this now lets you do

Read a sloped implied-vol curve and know exactly which model parameter is responsible for its shape — ξ for how curved, ρ for how tilted. Recognise Heston-style "volatility with its own dynamics" thinking (and its discrete-time GARCH cousin) wherever it reappears later in this curriculum. Understand why a smile alone justifies neither a simple constant-vol story nor a jump-only one — and why a good calibration fit is a curve-fit, not proof of an edge.
