Lesson 8 · Options in Practice

Hedging with Options, or Paying a Known Price for a Better Worst Case

You already own something, or owe something, and one bad outcome could hurt more than you can bear. Options let you cap that outcome for a premium. This guide shows how the protective put, the collar and the put spread are built, how a company hedges a currency payment, what each costs, and when the premium is not worth paying. Educational, not advice: options can lose the whole premium, and sold options can lose more.

The whole idea
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

I hold a hundred thousand dollars of an index fund and the next six months worry me. Someone said buy a put. What does that actually do?

A put is the right to sell at a fixed price. Say the fund is at one hundred and you buy a six-month put struck at ninety-five. If the fund ends at seventy, you still sell at ninety-five. So the most you can lose on the shares is five percent.

And what does that right cost?

About two dollars sixty-eight for every hundred of fund. Call it two point seven percent, paid today, gone if you never needed it. Add it to the five and your worst case is about seven point seven percent. That is the whole trade: you swap a possible big loss for a certain small one.

Two point seven percent for six months sounds like a lot.

It is, and that is the first lesson. Repeat it every six months and you pay over five percent a year, which is most of what you hope shares will earn. Insurance is not free, because the seller prices it for the risk.

Can I make it cheaper?

Only by giving something up. Sell a call at one hundred and ten and its premium pays for the put almost exactly, so the collar costs nothing. But your gains now stop at ten percent. Or buy a put spread: the ninety-five put, minus an eighty-five put. It costs about two dollars, but below eighty-five you are exposed again.

So a cheaper hedge is a different shape.

Always. When a hedge is cheap, ask where the hole is.

How many contracts for a big portfolio?

Size it by beta. Two million dollars, a beta of one point two, an index at five thousand, a hundred dollars a point. That works out at four point eight contracts, so you round to five. And the hedge only works as well as the index tracks what you own.

What about a company with a currency bill?

Same logic. An American firm owes ten million euros in six months. A forward fixes the rate at one point oh eight eight one, a little worse than today. That is not a fee, it is the gap between dollar and euro interest rates. An option costs a premium but lets you gain if the euro falls. The no-premium versions, the collar, the participating forward and the seagull, all sell some of that benefit, and the seagull hands the protection back if the euro soars.

When is an option hedge poor value?

After the shock. On the nineteenth of February twenty twenty the VIX closed at fourteen. On the sixteenth of March it closed at eighty-two point six nine, a record. On a simple flat-volatility illustration, a three-month put ten percent out of the money went from about a quarter of one percent of the portfolio to seven percent. Same insurance, twenty-seven times the price.

So the one-line version?

Decide the loss you cannot live with, price the ways to cap it, take the cheapest shape whose hole you can afford, and set the budget before the panic, not during it.

Section 1

A hedge buys a better worst case, and it is never free

A portfolio manager with a drawdown limit, a company with a euro bill and an investor with one large holding are in the same position. None of them is forecasting. They are buying a limit.

💸

Paying too much

Premium is a certain loss. A put rolled every few months can cost most of the return you hold the asset to earn.

🕳

A hole you did not look for

Cheaper structures give something up: a cap on gains, or no cover below a lower strike.

🎯

Hedging the wrong thing

An index put on stock-specific holdings leaves basis risk. A mismatched hedge is a separate bet.

⏳

Hedging after the shock

Protection costs most when everyone wants it, because the premium is the volatility.

You do not insure the house because you expect a fire. Judge a hedge by its worst case and its price, never by whether the bad thing happened.

Beta is the correlation times the ratio of volatilities (0.8 × 22.5 ÷ 15 = 1.2). A perfectly sized hedge removes only ρ² = 64% of the variance. The rest is basis risk, and a beta from last year may not hold in the next fall.

Shorter hedges are cheaper each time and not cheaper per year. Turn it round: a budget of 2% a year buys a three-month put struck near 89 (11% out of the money). The budget fixes how much disaster you can insure.

Where this needs qualifying

These are costs if nothing happens. When the market does fall, the put pays and the picture changes. The honest question is whether the price was fair for the protection. On average, bought index puts have earned negative returns, which is the cost side of insurance.

Section 7

Currency cover: forward or option?

A US importer owes €10m in six months. EURUSD is 1.0800, dollar rate 4.5%, euro rate 3.0%, volatility a flat 8% (hypothetical).

Forward
  • Fixes 1.0881, no premium
  • The 81.3 pips over spot are the interest gap between dollars and euros, not a fee
  • If the euro falls to 1.00 you still pay 1.0881: it costs $881,305 against going unhedged
  • Firm obligation: the bank may ask for collateral if the rate moves against it
Euro call at 1.10
  • Premium 0.0188 per euro: 1.74% of the amount, $187,776
  • Pays market below 1.10 and 1.10 above it, plus the premium
  • Worst case 1.1188: worse than the forward, like an insurance excess
  • Keeps the benefit if the euro falls: at 1.00 you pay 1.0188
Forward for certainty, option for contingency: a bid that may not be won, a payment that may change, a benefit you want to keep.

Section 10

When options are poor value

📈

After a volatility spike

You pay the panic price. Consider a smaller size, a spread, or a collar that sells some of the dear volatility too.

🔒

The exposure is certain

A fixed invoice is cheaper to cover with a forward, which has no premium.

🔁

Rolled short-dated, all year

At-the-money puts rolled monthly can cost more than the return you hold the asset for.

✂️

The position is small

Selling part of it does the same job with no premium and no spread.

Where the textbook version breaks

This lesson's toy uses one flat volatility for every strike. Real puts trade at higher volatilities than calls, so a "free" collar usually needs a lower cap or a small net payment. And a hedge is a position with Greeks: a long put bleeds in calm markets and marks up in a crisis, which is the behaviour you paid for.

Section 11

Quick check

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

You own a share at 100 and buy a 90 put for 3. What is the most you can lose at expiry?

Below 90 the put cancels the share loss, so the profit is stuck at 90 − 100 − 3 = −13. The premium sits on top of the gap to the strike.
Question 2

A collar costs nothing upfront. What has been paid instead?

The call you sold returns its premium today and takes back every point above its strike. The premium has been swapped for the upside.
Question 3

A $3m portfolio has beta 0.9. The index is at 4,000 and the multiplier is 100. How many contracts is the beta-sized hedge?

N = 0.9 × 3,000,000 ÷ (4,000 × 100) = 2,700,000 ÷ 400,000 = 6.75.
Question 4

Why does a US company pay more than spot to buy euros six months forward?

F = S × e^((r_d − r_f)T). With dollars at 4.5% and euros at 3.0% the euro trades at a forward premium. It is interest, not a fee and not a forecast.
Question 5

Where does a seagull stop protecting you?

The seagull covers the band between its two call strikes. Above the higher strike each extra cent costs again, so it fails in the very tail a treasurer worries about.
Your call
Scenario drill · 16 March 2020

The index is down 30%, the VIX is at a record, and the committee wants cover

You manage a $10m portfolio that tracks the S&P 500. It is unhedged. At the close on 16 March 2020 the index is 2,386.13, 29.5% below its peak of about four weeks earlier, and the VIX has just closed at a record 82.69. Your risk committee wants protection for the next three months. For the arithmetic, suppose three-month options are priced at a flat 60% volatility, with no interest (an illustration, not a quote). What do you do?

  1. Buy three-month at-the-money puts on the whole portfolio.
    At the illustrative price this costs about 11.9% of the portfolio, $1,192,354 on $10m. The index would need to fall more than that again just for you to break even, and you would have paid close to the highest price on record for the cover.
  2. Buy three-month puts 10% out of the money on the whole portfolio.
    Cheaper, at about 7.0%, and it does set a floor. But the worst case is then 10% plus 7.0%, about 17.0% of a portfolio that has already fallen by about 30%, and you are still paying a panic price for it.
  3. Buy the 90% put and sell a 120% call against it, a collar, so that the volatility you sell pays for most of the volatility you buy.
    At 60% the 120 call is worth about 5.4%, so the collar costs only about 1.6% net. The worst case is a further 11.6%, and the price is a cap on gains at +20% for three months. Real call volatilities sit below put volatilities (skew), so the real net cost is higher, which you should check before trading. The idea is the point: when volatility is dear, sell some of it too.
  4. Wait for volatility to fall before buying any protection.
    You stay fully exposed to the next leg down while you wait. A week later, on 23 March, the index closed at 2,237.40, another 6.2% lower, and the VIX was still 61.59.

What happened. The index closed at 2,237.40 on 23 March, its closing low and 33.9% below the February peak, then recovered: on 16 June it closed at 3,124.74, 31.0% above the 16 March close, and it set a new record close of 3,389.78 on 18 August. On those illustrative prices the plain 90% put would have cost 7.0% and expired worthless over the three months. The collar would have capped the recovery at +20% less its net cost, 18.4%, against the index's +31.0%. Hindsight favours the unhedged, and it proves nothing about the decision: in a different path the cap would have been irrelevant and the floor everything. The choice is graded on prices and mandate at the time. The option prices here are illustrative flat-volatility Black-Scholes values, not market quotes.

Sources: Cboe Volatility Index (VIX), daily close, via FRED series VIXCLS; S&P 500 index, daily close, via FRED series SP500 (S&P Dow Jones Indices).

Section 12

What to carry forward

01

A hedge buys a better worst case for a certain cost. The protective put's floor is K − S₀ − P, set the day you buy. On average it costs money, and that is what insurance is.

02

Every cheaper hedge is a different shape. A put spread loses cover below the lower strike. A collar gives up gains above the cap. Judge a structure by its worst case, not its premium.

03

Size by exposure: contracts = beta × value ÷ (index × multiplier). A hedge removes ρ² of the variance at best, and the rest is basis risk.

04

Forward for certainty, option for contingency. The forward's points are an interest gap, not a fee. Collars, participating forwards and seagulls pay for the option by giving something up, and the seagull gives protection back in the far tail.

05

Set the budget in calm. A rolled 95 put costs roughly 4% to 6.4% a year at 20% volatility if nothing happens, and in March 2020 the same put cost about 27 times as much on illustrative prices.

06

It actually happened, 2020: VIX 14.38 on 19 February, a record 82.69 on 16 March. The premium is the volatility on the day you buy.

What this now lets you do

State a hedge's worst case, cost and cap before you trade it; size it by exposure and not by feel; choose between a forward and an option for a currency payment; and spot when a "free" structure has been paid for in the tail or in the upside.
