Second-Order Greeks: Vanna, Charm, Vomma & More

Gamma already asks how Delta responds to price. Six more named Greeks ask the same question for every other pair of inputs — and two of them generalize the dealer-hedging mechanism from a price-driven flow into a volatility-driven and a pure-time-driven one.

CONCEPT — explained here, not built or tested anywhere in this repo ~20 min read Prereqs: Black-Scholes-Merton Option Pricing, Gamma Exposure & Dealer Hedging Flows

TL;DR
  • Second-order Greeks ask how a first-order Greek (Delta, Gamma, Vega) itself responds to price, volatility, or time — one derivative deeper than the Black-Scholes lesson went.
  • Vanna (\(\partial\Delta/\partial\sigma = \partial\nu/\partial S\)) and Charm (\(\partial\Delta/\partial t\)) are the two that show up most in real dealer-flow discussion — they generalize the gamma-exposure mechanism from "price move triggers rehedging" to "a vol move alone triggers rehedging" and "time passing alone triggers rehedging."
  • Vomma (\(\partial\nu/\partial\sigma\)) is vega's own convexity — the formal basis for "volga trades" and other vol-of-vol positioning.
  • Speed, Color, and Zomma are gamma's sensitivities to price, time, and vol respectively — genuinely niche in daily practice, but real for very large books or fast-moving vol regimes.
  • Vanna and vega are literally the same mixed partial derivative computed two different ways — \(\partial^2C/\partial S\partial\sigma = \partial^2C/\partial\sigma\partial S\) — a non-trivial, testable symmetry (Clairaut's/Schwarz's theorem), not a coincidence of notation.
  • Aggregated across open interest, Vanna and Charm exposure generalize dealer-GEX into "vanna flows" and "charm flows" — real, mechanically sound, and typically smaller in dollar magnitude than gamma flows, not a claim that they're separately tradeable on their own.
  • Real-world episode: on 15 January 2015 the Swiss franc floor went and EUR/CHF gapped by close to 30% in minutes while implied vol jumped from very low levels. Price and vol moved hugely together, so the Vanna and Vomma terms a risk report treats as rounding errors became the P&L, and then the expansion itself stopped meaning anything. The popular claim that vanna/charm flows push prices around options expiry is untested here.
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

I know gamma: price moves, dealers re-hedge. What are second-order Greeks for?

Price isn't the only thing that moves. Implied volatility moves, and time passes every day for certain. Each of those can shift a dealer's delta too, with the price standing still.

Start with volatility.

That's vanna: how delta changes when implied vol changes. Take a call struck at a hundred and five with the stock at a hundred, twenty-five percent vol, six months to go. Vanna is about zero point four nine. In plain terms, every point of implied vol adds about half a delta point.

So if vol rises before a central bank meeting and the price sits still?

The delta on those calls rises anyway. A dealer who is short them has to buy the underlying to stay hedged. Real buying on a day the price did nothing. Then the announcement lands, vol collapses, and the same dealer sells it back.

So that flow tells me nothing about direction.

Nothing at all. It's vol repricing, not a view.

And charm?

Charm is delta drifting because a day went by. It gets biggest in the last few days before expiry, the same window where gamma explodes.

Are these flows as big as gamma flows?

No. On the lesson's toy books, the vanna book moves about six hundred and sixty thousand dollars per vol point. The gamma book moves about seventeen million per one percent move. Roughly twenty-six times smaller.

So I can mostly ignore them.

Mostly, until price and vol jump together. The fifteenth of January, twenty fifteen. The Swiss National Bank had held the franc at one twenty per euro for more than three years, and option vol was very low. That morning it dropped the floor without warning.

And the franc went where?

The euro fell to about zero point eight five francs within minutes, close to thirty percent. Vol jumped at the same time. For anyone short out-of-the-money puts, the vanna and vomma terms their risk report called rounding errors became the whole loss. Then the price gapped past every strike, and even those terms stopped meaning anything.

So the one-line version?

Vol and time move your hedge too, quietly most days. When price and vol jump together, the small terms become the P and L.

Section 01

Why this matters

The Black-Scholes-Merton lesson derives Delta, Gamma, Theta, Vega, and Rho as the option-pricing formula's first partial derivatives with respect to price, price again, time, volatility, and rate. The Gamma Exposure & Dealer Hedging Flows lesson then took exactly one of those — Gamma — and showed what happens when you aggregate it across an entire open-interest book: a real, mechanical forecast of how much forced buying or selling a price move will trigger from dealers rehedging.

That lesson only ever asked "what happens when price moves?" Price is not the only thing that moves. Implied volatility moves — often sharply, around macro data releases, central bank decisions, or earnings — independent of where spot itself sits. And time passes, every single day, with total certainty, regardless of what price or volatility do. Gamma tells you nothing about either of those. The six Greeks in this lesson fill that gap: they are the second derivatives that answer "how does the dealer's hedge respond to a vol move alone, or to a day passing alone," generalizing the exact same rehedging mechanism the gamma lesson established into two more dimensions. Two of the six — Vanna and Charm — are genuinely important for anyone reading real options-flow commentary; the other four are covered honestly, including an honest statement of how rarely they come up in daily practice.

💡
Plain-English intuition

The same question, asked one level further

Gamma is Delta's sensitivity to price: "if the underlying moves $1, how much does my hedge ratio itself need to change?" That's already a second derivative of the option's price — but it only asks about price moving twice. These six Greeks ask the same kind of question about every other pair of inputs the option cares about: how does Delta respond to a volatility move, or to time passing? How does Vega respond to a further volatility move? How does Gamma itself — already a rate of change — keep changing as price moves further, as time passes, or as volatility shifts?

None of this is exotic new math. Every one of these six is just another partial derivative of the exact same Black-Scholes formula, taken with respect to a different pair of variables than Delta or Gamma used. What makes two of them — Vanna and Charm — worth knowing well is that they describe real forces on a dealer's hedge that have nothing to do with price moving at all: a vol spike alone, or a day passing alone, each force a dealer to trade, exactly the way a price move forces gamma hedging. The other four are genuinely more of a curiosity for most traders — worth knowing they exist, rarely worth tracking daily.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Gamma already asks "if price moves, how much does my hedge ratio need to change?" These six Greeks ask that same kind of question about the other two things that can also change on their own — how jumpy the market feels (volatility) and how much time is left. Two of them, Vanna and Charm, matter because they explain real trading that happens even on a day when the price itself barely moved at all.

Section 02

The math, step by step

Watch the formula build
Where the second-order terms sit in a risk report, one term at a time
\[ \Delta V \approx \color{#fbbf24}{\Delta\,dS} \]

Start with a book's first-order exposure to price: delta times the price move.

\[ \Delta V \approx \Delta\,dS + \color{#fbbf24}{\tfrac12\Gamma\,dS^2} \]

Add gamma, the curvature in price. This is the term the dealer-GEX lesson aggregates.

\[ \Delta V \approx \Delta\,dS + \tfrac12\Gamma\,dS^2 + \color{#fbbf24}{\nu\,d\sigma} \]

Add vega: the book's exposure to a move in implied volatility.

\[ \Delta V \approx \Delta\,dS + \tfrac12\Gamma\,dS^2 + \nu\,d\sigma + \color{#fbbf24}{\text{Vanna}\,dS\,d\sigma + \tfrac12\text{Vomma}\,d\sigma^2} \]

Add the cross term in price and vol (vanna) and vega's own curvature (vomma). With small moves these are products of two small numbers, so they look like rounding errors.

\[ \color{#10b981}{\Delta V \approx \Delta\,dS + \tfrac12\Gamma\,dS^2 + \nu\,d\sigma + \text{Vanna}\,dS\,d\sigma + \tfrac12\text{Vomma}\,d\sigma^2 + \Theta\,dt} \]

Add the passage of time. Each coefficient is a Greek; charm is how the delta in the first term itself drifts as \(t\) moves. When \(dS\) and \(d\sigma\) are both large at once, the vanna and vomma terms stop being small.

Each of these six is a standard, published closed form for a non-dividend European option — none of them are derived from first principles here (that would mean re-deriving the Black-Scholes PDE's higher-order sensitivities from the integral, which is outside this lesson's scope, exactly as the original Greeks were presented as derived results in the Black-Scholes lesson). What is shown explicitly for each is the definitional relationship — which first-order Greek, differentiated with respect to which variable, produces it — since that relationship is what makes each one meaningful rather than an arbitrary formula to memorize. All six reuse \(d_1\) and \(d_2\) exactly as defined in the Black-Scholes lesson, and \(\varphi(\cdot)\) is the standard normal density (not the CDF \(N(\cdot)\) used there) — the height of the bell curve at a point, \(\varphi(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-x^2/2}\).

Which first-order Greek, differentiated with respect to what, gives which second-order Greek
Delta feeds Vanna (via σ) and Charm (via t); Vega feeds Vanna (via S — the same box Delta feeds, which is the point) and Vomma (via σ); Gamma feeds Speed, Color, and Zomma (via S, t, and σ respectively). Delta's own σ-derivative and S-derivative converging on the same Vanna box is the mixed-partial symmetry derived below — it isn't a drawing coincidence. Gamma's own price-derivative (the classic second-order Greek from the prior lesson) isn't re-shown here since it's already covered. Theta and Rho have lesser-known second derivatives of their own (sometimes called Veta and Vera/Rhova) that aren't in scope for this deep dive — shown greyed out only for completeness of the first-order layer.
🔄

Vanna

\[ \text{Vanna} = \frac{\partial \Delta}{\partial \sigma} = \frac{\partial \nu}{\partial S} = \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial S\,\partial\sigma} = -\varphi(d_1)\frac{d_2}{\sigma} \]

How much Delta shifts per unit change in implied vol (×0.01 for a 1-vol-point change) — equivalently, how much Vega shifts for a $1 move in the underlying. Why it matters: a delta hedge computed at one implied-vol level can drift out of hedge purely from a vol move, with the underlying not having moved at all — an often-overlooked hedging fact.

⏳

Charm (delta decay)

\[ \text{Charm} = \frac{\partial \Delta}{\partial t} = -\varphi(d_1)\left[\frac{2r\tau - d_2\sigma\sqrt{\tau}}{2\tau\sigma\sqrt{\tau}}\right] \]

How much Delta drifts from one day passing alone, all else fixed. Matters for dealers deciding overnight or weekend hedge rebalancing, and — mirroring Gamma's own late-life acceleration — is especially large in the last few days before expiry.

📈

Vomma (Volga)

\[ \text{Vomma} = \frac{\partial \nu}{\partial \sigma} = \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial \sigma^2} = \nu\,\frac{d_1 d_2}{\sigma} \]

Vega's own sensitivity to vol — Vega's convexity. A position with positive Vomma (e.g. a long strangle) gains Vega disproportionately as vol itself rises, the formal basis of "volga trades" and other vol-of-vol positioning.

🚀

Speed

\[ \text{Speed} = \frac{\partial \Gamma}{\partial S} = -\frac{\Gamma}{S}\left(\frac{d_1}{\sigma\sqrt{\tau}} + 1\right) \]

Gamma's own rate of change with price — a third derivative of the option's price. Honestly: rarely actively traded or quoted day to day, but matters for very large gamma books, or in extreme/gap moves where Gamma itself changes fast enough within a single move to become non-negligible.

🎨

Color

\[ \text{Color} = \frac{\partial \Gamma}{\partial t} \]

Gamma's decay over calendar time — tells a gamma-scalper how their gamma exposure itself will evolve day to day even with no price move, which matters for planning a multi-day gamma-scalping strategy.

🌀

Zomma

\[ \text{Zomma} = \frac{\partial \Gamma}{\partial \sigma} \]

Gamma's sensitivity to vol. Relevant when vol itself is moving fast (e.g. a vol regime shift) — the Gamma a delta-hedger thought they had can change materially before any price move, purely from implied vol repricing.

Vanna's symmetry — a genuinely elegant, testable fact

Vanna is defined two different ways in the formula above: as Delta's sensitivity to vol, and as Vega's sensitivity to price. These read like two different quantities that happen to share a name. They are not — they are the exact same number, computed two different ways:

\[ \text{Vanna} = \frac{\partial}{\partial \sigma}\!\left(\frac{\partial C}{\partial S}\right) = \frac{\partial}{\partial S}\!\left(\frac{\partial C}{\partial \sigma}\right) = \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial S\,\partial\sigma} = \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial \sigma\,\partial S} \]
∂(∂C/∂S)/∂σshift Delta, see how it changes with vol
∂(∂C/∂σ)/∂Sshift Vega, see how it changes with price
identical resultnot a coincidence — a theorem
Why this is guaranteed, not lucky: Clairaut's theorem (also called Schwarz's theorem) states that for any function with continuous second partial derivatives, mixed partial derivatives are equal regardless of the order you differentiate in. The Black-Scholes price \(C(S,\sigma,t)\) is smooth enough (a standard, checkable property of the closed-form formula) for the theorem to apply, so \(\partial^2C/\partial S\partial\sigma\) and \(\partial^2C/\partial\sigma\partial S\) are guaranteed equal, not merely observed to be equal in this one case. Practically: you can compute Vanna by asking "how does my hedge ratio move if vol jumps a point" or by asking "how does my vol exposure move if the underlying jumps a dollar," and you are guaranteed to get the same number either way — which is exactly why Vanna is worth a single name rather than two.
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

If you climb a hill and measure "how much does my altitude change per step north" versus "how much does my altitude change per step east," those are naturally different questions. But there's a special, provably-true fact about smooth hills: how your northward slope changes as you walk east always equals how your eastward slope changes as you walk north. Vanna is exactly that fact applied to option prices — asking it two different ways is guaranteed to land on the same number.

Worked example

Computing Vanna by hand for a slightly out-of-the-money call

Take \(S=100\), \(K=105\) (a call struck a bit above spot — out of the money), \(r=0.02\), \(\sigma=0.25\), \(T=0.5\) years (6 months). First, \(d_1\) and \(d_2\), using the exact same formula as the Black-Scholes lesson:

\[ d_1 = \frac{\ln(100/105) + (0.02+0.25^2/2)(0.5)}{0.25\sqrt{0.5}} = \frac{-0.04879 + 0.02563}{0.17678} \approx -0.1311 \] \[ d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T} = -0.1311 - 0.17678 \approx -0.3078 \]
d₁ ≈ −0.1311slightly negative — the call is out of the money
d₂ ≈ −0.3078more negative than d₁, as always

Next, the standard normal density at \(d_1\):

\[ \varphi(-0.1311) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-(-0.1311)^2/2} \approx 0.3989 \times 0.9914 \approx 0.3955 \]

Now assemble Vanna:

\[ \text{Vanna} = -\varphi(d_1)\frac{d_2}{\sigma} = -(0.3955)\left(\frac{-0.30782}{0.25}\right) = -(0.3955)(-1.2313) \approx 0.4870 \]
Vanna ≈ 0.4870positive — makes sense for an OTM call, below

The sign is worth checking, not just accepting: \(d_2\) here is negative, so the two minus signs in \(-\varphi(d_1)(d_2/\sigma)\) cancel to a positive Vanna. Economically, that's the right direction — this call is out of the money, so a rise in implied vol raises the probability of finishing in the money, which raises Delta; Vanna being positive says exactly "Delta goes up when vol goes up" for this option. As with Vega, this raw number is per 1.00 (a full 100 vol points) of \(\sigma\); multiplying by 0.01 rescales it to "per 1 vol point," the convention used below: \(0.4870 \times 0.01 \approx 0.00487\) — roughly half a delta-point of shift for every 1-point move in implied vol (e.g. 25% → 26%), with the underlying not having moved a cent.

Real-world trading scenario

A vol spike into a macro event, with spot barely moving

A dealer desk is short a large book of slightly out-of-the-money calls on a major currency pair heading into a closely watched central bank decision two days out. In the hours before the announcement, implied volatility on the front-week options climbs sharply — traders bidding up vol in anticipation of a large move, a completely normal pre-event pattern — while spot itself drifts in a tight 20-pip range, barely moving at all.

Per the worked example above, this book's positive Vanna means Delta on those calls is rising purely because implied vol rose, with no price move behind it. The desk's automated hedging system flags a growing short-delta position (the short calls' delta is rising) it needs to buy underlying against to stay flat — and to whoever's watching the tape, that produces real, observable order flow into the underlying on a day when price has barely moved. Anyone reading that flow as "someone thinks the pair is about to break out directionally" would be reading it wrong: it's a vanna flow, not a gamma flow, and it says nothing about direction — it's a mechanical consequence of vol repricing, not a directional signal at all.

Then the announcement lands, vol richly priced beforehand collapses immediately afterward (the standard post-event "vol crush"), and the same Vanna mechanism runs in reverse: Delta drifts back down purely from vol falling, forcing the desk to sell back some of what it just bought — again with no requirement that price itself has moved much either way. A desk that only watches Gamma would have missed both legs of this flow entirely, because neither leg was triggered by price.

Section 03

Aggregating Vanna and Charm across open interest — a second, distinct flow mechanism

The dealer-GEX lesson built one formula, \(\text{GEX}_k = \Gamma_k \times \text{OI}_k \times 100 \times S^2 \times 0.01\), that turns a single option's Greek into a dollar figure describing an entire strike's open-interest-driven hedging pressure. Vanna and Charm generalize exactly the same aggregation logic — the difference is which market variable is assumed to move.

Dollar Vanna exposure

The dollar delta-shift per 1-vol-point move, aggregated across the open interest sitting at strike \(k\):

\[ \text{VannaExp}_k = \text{Vanna}_k \times \text{OI}_k \times 100 \times S \]
Vannaₖ×OIₖ×100shares' worth of delta-shift per unit σ, aggregated across the strike
×Sconverts shares to dollars, mirroring dealer-GEX's own ×S step

One scaling note worth being explicit about, the same way the dealer-GEX lesson was explicit about its own \(S^2 \times 0.01\) term: computed from the raw closed form with \(\sigma\) as a decimal, this quantity is "per 1.00 (100 vol points) of \(\sigma\)." To read it as "per 1-vol-point move" — the convention this formula is stated in, and the way options-flow analytics quote it — multiply by 0.01, exactly as dealer-GEX rescales from "per $1 move" to "per 1% move."

The mechanism this dollar figure describes is genuinely distinct from gamma-driven hedging. Recall from the dealer-GEX lesson: for OI the dealer is net short (calls customers bought, say), the dealer holds a long hedge position exactly matching the customers' aggregate Delta. If that OI carries positive Vanna (as the worked example's out-of-the-money call did), a rise in implied vol alone — with spot unchanged — raises the customers' aggregate Delta, which means the dealer's required hedge grows too, forcing genuine buying with no price move behind it at all. This is what practitioners call a vanna flow: a real, actively discussed phenomenon in modern options-flow analysis, particularly relevant on days with large implied-vol swings — heading into or out of major macro events — even when spot itself has barely moved, exactly like the scenario above.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

A vanna flow is dealer buying or selling that gets triggered purely because the market suddenly feels more nervous or calmer — not because the price actually went anywhere. If you saw a stock get bought up on a day it barely moved, that's not proof someone knows a rally is coming; it might just be dealers rebalancing because volatility itself ticked up.

Dollar Charm exposure

The delta-shift per day passing, in shares of the underlying, aggregated across the open interest at strike \(k\) (multiply by \(S\) for dollars, as VannaExp does):

\[ \text{CharmExp}_k = \text{Charm}_k \times \text{OI}_k \times 100 \]
Charmₖ×OIₖ×100delta-shift in shares of underlying (×S for dollars) — a pure time effect, no vol-point rescale

A similar scaling note applies here: the closed form for Charm above uses \(t\)/\(\tau\) in years, so the raw output is an annualized rate. Dividing by 365 converts it to "per calendar day," the convention this exposure formula assumes and the way a desk actually plans day-to-day rebalancing.

The mechanism: as expiry approaches, aggregate dealer Delta exposure shifts purely from time passing — no price or vol move required at all — forcing a predictable, time-driven rebalancing flow practitioners call a charm flow. This mirrors, through a different partial derivative, exactly the late-life acceleration the dealer-GEX lesson established for Gamma itself: Gamma for an at-the-money option scales roughly as \(1/\sqrt{T}\) and explodes as expiry nears (the lesson's own worked example showed roughly a 5.5× jump from 30 days to 1 day out). Charm shows the same acceleration in the same regime, for the same reason — both are driven by \(T\) shrinking toward zero — so charm flows tend to be largest in the final few days before expiry, the identical calendar window the dealer-GEX lesson flagged for gamma-driven pinning and squeeze effects.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

A charm flow is dealer buying or selling that happens purely because a day went by — no price move, no volatility move, just the calendar ticking forward. It's like a parking meter that keeps draining even while your car sits perfectly still; time alone is enough to force the dealer to adjust their hedge, and that effect gets stronger the closer expiry gets.

Worked example

A toy three-strike dollar Vanna aggregation

Fix \(S=100\) (so \(S \times 0.01 = 1\), simplifying the arithmetic exactly the way the dealer-GEX lesson's own toy example picked \(S=100\)) and, for simple arithmetic, use the dealer-GEX lesson's cruder "dealers net short every strike" simplification (\(s_k=-1\)) rather than its standard long-calls/short-puts convention. Vanna at the 105 strike is the 0.4870 derived above; the 95 and 100 strikes use smaller, made-up round figures purely to show the arithmetic. (With the same inputs, Black-Scholes Vanna is actually about −0.375 at 95 and +0.05 at 100: Vanna changes sign as the strike moves through the money, so a real book's strikes partly offset each other.)

Read this as: under that short-everything simplification, a full 1-point rise in implied volatility across this toy book forces roughly $661,000 of net delta-hedge rebalancing (here, buying, driven by the negative sign and positive Vanna at every strike) — with spot unchanged. Set this next to the dealer-GEX lesson's own three-strike toy example, which under the same short-everything simplification gives roughly $17.0 million for comparable open interest: dollar Vanna exposure scales with \(S\), not \(S^2\) the way dollar Gamma exposure does, which is one genuine mechanical reason vanna flows run smaller in typical dollar magnitude than gamma flows for similar-sized books (how much smaller also depends on the shocks compared, 1 vol point against a 1% move) — a consequence of the exponent difference between the two formulas.

Read this plainly, not as a trading claim: vanna and charm flows are directionally real and mechanically sound — they follow from exactly the same aggregation logic dealer-GEX uses, over the same open interest, under the same dealer-sign assumption (still an assumption, not an observed fact, exactly as the dealer-GEX lesson flagged). But they are a second-order effect layered on top of gamma exposure: smaller in typical magnitude (illustrated numerically above), harder to observe cleanly against the noise of ordinary trading flow, and this lesson is explaining the mechanism, not claiming a specific trading edge from monitoring it.
Section 04

It actually happened: 15 January 2015, when the small terms became the P&L

Honest framing first. "Vanna and charm flows" are mostly a practitioner narrative: there is no well-documented public episode in which they can be shown to have moved a market. What is well documented is a day on which the second-order terms themselves, on an ordinary short-option book, stopped being second order.

It actually happened

15 January 2015: the Swiss franc floor, seen through a risk report

From September 2011 the Swiss National Bank held a floor at 1.20 francs per euro, promising to buy unlimited euros to defend it. For more than three years EUR/CHF sat just above the line, realised volatility collapsed, and implied volatility on EUR/CHF options fell to very low levels. Selling out-of-the-money EUR/CHF puts, options that pay only if the floor breaks, looked like collecting rent on an event the central bank had promised would not happen.

On the morning of 15 January 2015 the SNB removed the floor without warning and cut its deposit rate to −0.75%. EUR/CHF traded as low as about 0.85 within minutes, a fall of close to 30%, before recovering part of the way the same day, and many orders were filled far from where they had been placed. The broker losses (FXCM's rescue loan of about $300 million from Leucadia, the insolvency of Alpari's UK arm) are told in The Four Option Positions. This lesson's angle is what the Greeks on a short-put book said that morning.

A Greek-by-Greek risk report is a Taylor expansion of the book's value:

\[ \Delta V \approx \Delta\,dS + \tfrac12\Gamma\,dS^2 + \nu\,d\sigma + \text{Vanna}\,dS\,d\sigma + \tfrac12\text{Vomma}\,d\sigma^2 + \Theta\,dt \]

On a normal day \(dS\) and \(d\sigma\) are small, so the cross term \(\text{Vanna}\,dS\,d\sigma\) and the \(\text{Vomma}\,d\sigma^2\) term are tiny next to delta and vega. That is the premise of this lesson's own aggregation section. On 15 January both \(dS\) and \(d\sigma\) were enormous at the same time, so their product was not small any more. For a short out-of-the-money put both terms work against the seller: as vol rises, Vanna makes the position's long delta grow while spot is falling, and Vomma makes its short vega grow, so each further vol point costs more than the last. A book hedged flat on delta and vega was, by construction, unhedged against exactly these terms.

Then the move went further than any expansion can follow. Options that were far out of the money at breakfast were deep in the money minutes later, past every strike in between, with no tradeable prices along the way. At that point even the second-order terms describe a market that no longer exists.

Where the textbook version breaks

Second-order Greeks are coefficients of a Taylor expansion, valid for small, simultaneous moves in price, vol and time along continuous paths that can be re-hedged. A peg removal moves spot by more than a quarter and vol by multiples in a single repricing. The expansion is then being evaluated far outside the range where it works: the third-order terms this lesson calls niche (Speed, Zomma) are not small, and nothing can be re-hedged in between.

Not tested here Vanna and charm flows into options expiry: untested here. The tempting claim is that dealers' vol- and time-driven re-hedging pushes index prices in a predictable direction in the days around monthly expiry. A fair test would need a daily dealer Vanna and Charm exposure series computed point-in-time from that morning's open interest, a pre-registered direction and window (for example, the five sessions into monthly expiry), matched non-expiry weeks with the same volatility as controls, and a held-out period. None of that has been run.

The nearest thing the desk has measured is a gamma result, not a vanna or charm one. In its dealer-gamma study (gex-range) the vol-matched range effect was +0.306 on non-event days and +0.306 on event days, with options expiry among the event days, so expiry added nothing to the gamma range effect. That neither supports nor rules out a charm-driven direction into expiry, and it should not be quoted as if it did.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

A risk report is like a weather forecast that says "a bit windier, a bit wetter." It works when the weather changes a little. On the day the Swiss franc jumped, the wind and the rain both went off the scale at the same moment, and the bits the forecast normally ignores, like what happens when it's windy and wet, turned out to be the whole story.

Section 05

How practitioners actually use this

📊

Vol-event flow commentary

Options-flow analytics that already publish dealer GEX estimates increasingly publish a Vanna and Charm read alongside it, specifically to explain rebalancing flow on days when spot barely moved but implied vol did.

🧭

Explaining, not predicting

Vanna/Charm exposure is mostly used after the fact, to explain why a hedging flow occurred on a quiet-price day — closer to a forensic tool than a forward-looking entry signal.

📐

Volga trades on the Vomma side

A trading desk with a view that realized vol-of-vol will rise (not just vol itself) can build a position with deliberately positive Vomma — e.g. a long strangle — to profit disproportionately as implied vol itself becomes more volatile.

🗓️

Charm in overnight/weekend risk

A desk deciding how much to rebalance before a weekend (when no trading can happen but time still passes) uses Charm directly to estimate how stale its Delta hedge will be by Monday open, purely from time decay.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where people get this wrong

Assuming every desk actively monitors all six. Speed, Color, and Zomma are genuinely niche in daily practice — most trading desks never quote them and most retail options-flow tools don't publish them. Vanna and Charm are the two that actually show up in real dealer-flow discussion; treating all six as equally important overstates how much of this is actively used day to day.

Confusing a vanna/charm flow with a directional signal. Exactly like dealer GEX, these are descriptions of mechanical hedging pressure, not forecasts of where price is going. A vanna-driven buy order says "vol moved and dealers had to rehedge," not "the market expects the pair to rally."

Forgetting the dealer-sign assumption travels with every one of these. \(s_k\) — whether the dealer is actually net long or short a given strike — is exactly as unverifiable here as it was for Gamma. Every Vanna/Charm exposure estimate inherits that same uncertainty; it doesn't get more certain just because the formula looks more sophisticated.

Treating vanna/charm flows as comparable in size to gamma flows. The toy aggregation above showed roughly $661,000 of Vanna-driven flow against roughly $17.0 million of Gamma-driven flow (same short-everything simplification) for comparable open interest — roughly 26× apart, a little over one order of magnitude (and quoted per different shocks: a 1-point vol move against a 1% price move). Vanna and Charm explain real, distinct flow on days gamma alone can't account for, but they are not a comparably sized force in typical markets.

Scenario drill · 15 January 2015

A risk report that says the tail is a rounding error

It is the morning of 15 January 2015. EUR/CHF sits just above the Swiss National Bank's 1.20 floor, as it has since September 2011, and implied volatility on EUR/CHF options is very low. Your book is short out-of-the-money EUR/CHF puts, hedged flat on delta and vega, and the risk report shows Vanna and Vomma close to zero. A colleague wants to double the size: the bank has promised to defend the floor. What do you do?

  1. Double it. Flat on delta and vega, second-order terms near zero, and a central bank standing behind the floor.
    The report's small terms are small only for small moves. A floor break moves price and vol hugely at the same time, so the vanna and vomma cross terms become the P&L, and then the price gaps past every strike where no hedge can be adjusted.
  2. Size the short-put book for a gap through every strike, or buy far-out puts to cap the loss, whatever the risk report says.
    This sizes for the event the position is short, not for the small-move expansion. A cap or a size you can survive does not depend on Vanna, Vomma or any re-hedge working in a market with no prices.
  3. Keep the size but put a stop-loss order under the market.
    A stop is better than nothing, but it assumes there will be a price near your stop. In a gap the order fills wherever the next trade happens, which can be far from where you placed it.
  4. Double it, and hedge Vanna and Vomma precisely with other options.
    Neutralizing the second-order terms helps against moderate joint moves in price and vol. It does nothing once the move is so large that the whole expansion stops describing the book.

What happened. That morning the SNB removed the floor without warning and cut its deposit rate to −0.75%. EUR/CHF traded as low as about 0.85 within minutes, a fall of close to 30%, before recovering part of the way the same day, and many orders were filled far from where they had been placed. Implied volatility jumped from very low levels. FXCM needed a rescue loan of about $300 million from Leucadia, and Alpari's UK arm became insolvent.

Section 06

Key Takeaways

✓Second-order Greeks ask how a first-order Greek itself responds to price, vol, or time. Gamma already asked this for Delta-vs-price; these six extend the same question to every other pair of inputs.
✓Vanna and Charm are the two that matter most in practice. They generalize the gamma-hedging mechanism into a vol-driven flow and a pure-time-driven flow, respectively — each real and mechanically distinct from price-triggered gamma hedging.
✓Vanna's two definitions are the same mixed partial derivative, guaranteed equal by Clairaut's/Schwarz's theorem. Not a coincidence — computing it via Delta's vol-sensitivity or Vega's price-sensitivity must give the same number.
✓Charm mirrors Gamma's own late-life acceleration. Both blow up as expiry nears because both are driven by \(T\to0\) — charm flows concentrate in the same final days the dealer-GEX lesson flagged for gamma effects.
⚠Speed, Color, and Zomma are genuinely niche. Real, correctly derived, and rarely tracked by real desks day to day — don't overstate their practical relevance relative to Vanna and Charm.
⚠Vanna/charm flows are a second-order effect on top of gamma exposure, not a comparably sized force. The toy example showed it roughly 26× (a little over one order of magnitude) smaller in dollar terms — this lesson explains the mechanism, it is not a claim of a specific tradeable edge.
⚠Every dollar exposure figure here still inherits the dealer-sign assumption from the GEX lesson. Whether dealers are actually net long or short any given strike remains unverifiable from open interest alone.
⚠Second-order terms matter most exactly when price and vol jump together, and then the expansion fails. On 15 January 2015 EUR/CHF fell close to 30% in minutes as vol jumped; a delta- and vega-flat short-put book was unhedged against Vanna and Vomma, and then against the gap itself. "Vanna/charm flows into expiry" is untested on this desk.
Section 07

Self-test

Q1. Why are Vanna computed as \(\partial\Delta/\partial\sigma\) and Vanna computed as \(\partial\nu/\partial S\) guaranteed to give the same number, rather than just happening to agree in the worked example?
Both are the mixed second partial derivative of the option price \(C(S,\sigma,t)\) with respect to \(S\) and \(\sigma\), just taken in opposite orders: \(\partial^2C/\partial S\partial\sigma\) versus \(\partial^2C/\partial\sigma\partial S\). Clairaut's theorem (also called Schwarz's theorem) guarantees that for any function with continuous second partial derivatives — a property the closed-form Black-Scholes price has — mixed partials are equal regardless of differentiation order. It's a general theorem about smooth multivariable functions, not a special property of this one worked example; any correctly computed pair of numbers for Vanna, from either direction, must agree.
Q2. In the worked example (\(S=100\), \(K=105\), \(\sigma=0.25\), \(T=0.5\)), Vanna came out positive (\(\approx0.4870\)). Explain in plain English why a positive sign makes economic sense for this specific option.
The option is out of the money (\(K=105 > S=100\)). Raising implied volatility increases the probability that the underlying ends up above the strike at expiry, which raises the option's Delta (it becomes more like a real long-stock position). Vanna being positive says exactly that: "Delta increases as \(\sigma\) increases" for this option. Had the option instead been deep in the money, its Delta would already be near its ceiling of 1.0, leaving much less room for vol to push it higher — a very different, and often much smaller or negative, Vanna value.
Q3. Why does dollar Vanna exposure scale with \(S\) while dollar Gamma exposure (GEX) scales with \(S^2\) — and what does that difference in exponent actually explain?
Dollar Gamma exposure is built to answer "how many dollars does the hedge position change by, per 1% move in \(S\)" — converting a per-$1-move share quantity to dollars requires one factor of \(S\), and converting from "per $1 move" to "per 1%-of-S move" requires a second factor of \(S\) (via \(S\times0.01\)), giving \(S^2\) overall. Dollar Vanna exposure answers a different question — "how many dollars does the hedge position change by, per 1-vol-point move" — and a vol-point isn't itself expressed in units of \(S\), so only the first \(S\) factor (converting shares to dollars) appears; there's no second \(S\)-scaled term. That one fewer power of \(S\) is exactly why, for comparable open interest, dollar Vanna exposure figures run structurally smaller than dollar Gamma exposure figures — a mechanical consequence of the units involved, not a claim about which flow is "more important."
Q4. Why does Charm's magnitude accelerate as expiry approaches, in the same way Gamma's does — and why does that matter for a dealer specifically in the final days before expiry?
Both Gamma and Charm are driven by the same \(T\to0\) behavior baked into the Black-Scholes formula's \(d_1,d_2\) terms — Gamma roughly as \(1/\sqrt{T}\) for an at-the-money option, and Charm through the same \(\sigma\sqrt{\tau}\) terms in its own closed form. As \(T\) shrinks toward zero, both blow up, and — as the dealer-GEX lesson's worked example showed numerically for Gamma (roughly 5.5× from 30 to 1 day to expiry) — the effect is not linear in calendar days lost; it accelerates. For a dealer, this means the pure-time-driven rebalancing that Charm describes (no price or vol move required) gets materially larger exactly in the last few days before expiry — the same calendar window where gamma-driven pinning and squeeze effects are already most intense, compounding rather than replacing that risk.
Q5. A commentator claims "large positive dealer Vanna exposure at a strike means the market is bullish." What's wrong with that claim?
It conflates a mechanical hedging description with a directional forecast — exactly the same category error the dealer-GEX lesson flags for Gamma. Dealer Vanna exposure describes how much delta-hedge rebalancing a change in implied volatility will force, given the current open interest and an assumed dealer-sign convention; it says nothing about which direction the underlying is expected to move. A large Vanna figure means "a vol move at this strike will force a large hedging flow," not "the market expects price to rise." Reading it as a bullish/bearish signal repeats the same mistake the Black-Scholes and dealer-GEX lessons both warn against: treating a hedging-consistent mechanical quantity as if it were a market forecast.
Q6. Why are Speed, Color, and Zomma described as "genuinely niche" rather than being given the same practical weight as Vanna and Charm in this lesson?
Speed, Color, and Zomma are all third-order derivatives of the option price (each is a first derivative of Gamma, which is itself already a second derivative) — mathematically valid and correctly derivable, but describing progressively smaller, harder-to-observe effects that matter mainly for very large gamma books or extreme, fast-moving markets. In ordinary daily practice, most trading desks and options-flow tools don't quote or monitor them, unlike Vanna and Charm, which show up regularly in real dealer-flow commentary because they explain observable rebalancing flow on quiet-price, high-vol-move or fast-approaching-expiry days. The honest distinction isn't that the other four are wrong or unimportant in principle — it's that Vanna and Charm are the two with genuine, regular practical relevance, and pretending all six are equally watched would overstate how this material is actually used.
