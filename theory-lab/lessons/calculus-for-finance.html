Calculus for Finance: Derivatives, Integrals & Taylor Series

The tangent-line slope, the area under a curve, and the local approximation built from both — the three ideas that Ornstein-Uhlenbeck, Black-Scholes, and Kelly Criterion all quietly assume you already have.

DEEPER FOUNDATION — read before Ornstein-Uhlenbeck, Black-Scholes, or Kelly Criterion ~15 min read Prereqs: none
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • A derivative is the instantaneous rate of change (the tangent-line slope at a point); an integral is the accumulated area under a curve — and the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus proves they're exact inverse operations, worked here from the limit definition \(f'(x)=\lim_{h\to0}[f(x+h)-f(x)]/h\) through a full first-principles derivation of \(d/dx(x^2)=2x\).
  • A Taylor series estimates \(f(x+h)\) from what you know at \(x\) alone: the zeroth-order guess is just \(f(x)\), the first-order term \(f'(x)h\) adds the tangent line, and the second-order term \(\tfrac{1}{2}f''(x)h^2\) corrects for curvature — that second-order version is the workhorse used throughout the rest of this lesson.
  • The lesson's payoff: \(\ln(1+R)\approx R-R^2/2\) (from the Returns/Log-Returns primer) and \(g\approx\mu-\sigma^2/2\) (the volatility-drag formula from the Kelly Criterion lesson) are not two separate finance shortcuts — both fall out of the identical second-order Taylor expansion of \(\ln(1+x)\) around \(x=0\), just relabeled once per-period and once again after averaging across periods.
  • Option delta and gamma are literally the first- and second-order Taylor terms of the option-price function \(C(S)\) with respect to the underlying — the same \(f(x)+f'(x)h+\tfrac12f''(x)h^2\) machinery, not a separate options-specific idea.
  • How it reached trading: Robert Merton brought Itô's stochastic calculus into finance (1969–71), and the Taylor expansion whose second-order term survives randomness became Black–Scholes–Merton (1973) and every delta–gamma P&L explain.

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

I last did calculus at school. Do I really need it to trade?

You use it every day already. Think of a car. The speedometer tells you how fast you are going right now. That is a derivative: a rate of change at one instant.

And the odometer?

The odometer adds up every instant of speed into a total distance. That is an integral. The two are mirror images: one undoes the other.

How do you get "right now" out of a formula, though?

Measure the average speed over a gap, then shrink the gap towards zero. For x squared, the average slope from x to a little further on works out to two x plus the gap. Shrink the gap away and the slope is exactly two x.

And Taylor series?

A way to guess a value nearby from what you know at one point. First guess: nothing changes. Second: keep going at today's slope, the tangent line. Third: add a correction for how the curve bends.

Show me it working.

Take the log of one plus a return. The bent guess is the return minus half its square. For a five percent move the error is about four hundred-thousandths. For a thirty percent move it is about seven thousandths, nearly two hundred times bigger. Great for small steps, worse for big ones.

Where does that show up on a desk?

Average that same shape over many periods and you get volatility drag: compound growth is roughly the average return minus half the variance. At twenty percent volatility, that is about two percent a year.

And options?

Delta is the derivative of the option price with respect to the underlying. A delta of zero point six means a one dollar move in the stock moves the option about sixty cents. Gamma is the bend, the second-order correction. That's why desks watch both.

Who brought this into finance?

Robert Merton, around nineteen sixty-nine to seventy-one, using Itô's calculus for random paths. In nineteen seventy-three it gave Black, Scholes and Merton the option price. The second-order term that survives randomness is exactly where volatility enters.

So the one-liner?

A derivative is speed right now, an integral is distance so far, and a two-term Taylor guess explains delta, gamma and volatility drag, as long as the move is small.

Section 01

Why this matters

Three lessons already in this Theory Lab lean on calculus without pausing to teach it. The Ornstein-Uhlenbeck lesson writes dX_t = θ(μ - X_t)dt + σdW_t and asks you to read it as "the rate of change of \(X\)" — that is a derivative. The Black-Scholes lesson builds a partial differential equation out of how an option's price changes with the underlying, with time, and with volatility — three more derivatives, all at once. The Kelly Criterion lesson finds the growth-optimal bet size by taking a derivative of a growth-rate function and setting it to zero, then derives the volatility-drag formula \(g \approx \mu - \sigma^2/2\) using something called a "second-order Taylor expansion" without stopping to say what that phrase means. None of those lessons are wrong to assume it — but if the words "derivative," "integral," and "Taylor series" are more familiar as vague memories from a school course than as tools you could actually use, this page is the detour that makes the rest of this series load-bearing rather than decorative.

Calculus, at its core, is the mathematics of change and accumulation. A derivative answers "how fast is this changing right now?" An integral answers "how much has accumulated so far?" A Taylor series answers "if I only know a function's value and its rate(s) of change at one point, how good a guess can I make about its value nearby?" Every one of those three questions turns out to be exactly the question a piece of quantitative finance is quietly asking — an option's delta, a cumulative return, a quick mental estimate of volatility drag. This lesson builds all three from first principles, with every step of arithmetic shown, so nothing later in the series has to be taken on faith.

💡
Plain-English intuition

The speedometer and the odometer

Picture driving a car. The speedometer tells you how fast you are going right now — not your average speed over the whole trip, but your speed at this exact instant, which can be different one second from now. That is a derivative: an instantaneous rate of change. The odometer tells you the total distance you've covered so far — it accumulates every instant of speed, over every instant of time, into a single running total. That is an integral: an accumulated total built up from a rate.

The two instruments are mirror images of each other, and that mirror relationship is not a coincidence — it's a theorem (the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, below). If you know your speed at every instant, you can reconstruct the total distance traveled by accumulating it. If you know the total distance traveled as a smooth function of time, you can reconstruct your speed at any instant by asking how steeply that distance function is rising right then. In finance, "distance traveled" is usually a price, a wealth level, or an option value, and "speed" is usually the thing traders actually care about day to day — a rate of change like delta, a drift, or a mean-reversion speed. A Taylor series, the third idea this lesson builds, is what lets you use the speedometer reading alone — your speed right now, plus how quickly your speed itself is changing — to make a good guess about where you'll be a few seconds from now, without needing to know the entire future trip in advance.

How it reached trading

Hedging something that never stops moving

Itô calculus in finance · Merton, 1969–71 — Black, Scholes & Merton, 1973

Ordinary calculus assumes smooth paths, but prices jiggle at every scale. A volatile asset compounds more slowly than its average return suggests, and a continuously adjusted hedge needs a correction that ordinary calculus misses. Before the late 1960s finance had no rules for either.

Robert Merton brought Kiyosi Itô's stochastic calculus, developed in the 1940s as pure probability theory, into finance in his 1969 and 1971 papers on investing in continuous time, and in 1973 the same tools gave Black, Scholes and Merton the option price from a continuously rebalanced hedge. The key is a Taylor expansion whose second-order term does not vanish for a random path; that is where \(\tfrac12\sigma^2\) comes from.

Desks use the same expansions every day: delta–gamma P&L explain, duration and convexity for bonds, and the volatility-drag gap between average and compound returns.

\(f(x)\)A function of \(x\) — a price, an option value, a wealth level, anything that depends smoothly on some input \(x\).
\(h\)A (usually small) change in \(x\) — sometimes written \(\Delta x\). Used to build the average rate of change before shrinking it toward an instantaneous one.
\(\lim_{h\to 0}\)"The limit as \(h\) shrinks toward, but never quite reaches, zero" — the formal machinery that turns an average (over some finite gap) into an instantaneous quantity (at a single point).
\(f'(x)\), \(\dfrac{df}{dx}\)The derivative of \(f\) at \(x\) — its instantaneous rate of change; equivalently, the slope of the line tangent to the curve at that point. Both notations mean the same thing.
\(f''(x)\)The second derivative — the derivative of the derivative, i.e. the rate of change of the rate of change. Measures curvature: how quickly the slope itself is changing.
\(\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\)The definite integral of \(f\) from \(a\) to \(b\) — the accumulated area between the curve and the x-axis over that interval.
\(F(x)\)An antiderivative of \(f\): any function whose own derivative gives back \(f\), i.e. \(F'(x) = f(x)\). Integration is the reverse operation of differentiation.
\(dx\)An infinitesimally small width along the x-axis — the "thickness" of each accumulated slice inside an integral, or the tiny step size a derivative is defined over.

The derivative: instantaneous rate of change

Start with something easier: the average rate of change of \(f\) between two points, \(x\) and \(x+h\). That's just "rise over run" — the change in \(f\) divided by the change in \(x\):

\[ \text{average rate of change} = \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h} \]
f(x+h)−f(x)rise — the change in f over the gap
hrun — the size of the gap

Geometrically, this is the slope of the secant line — the straight line connecting the two points \((x, f(x))\) and \((x+h, f(x+h))\) on the curve. It's an honest, useful number, but it describes the average behavior over the whole gap \(h\), not what's happening at the single point \(x\) itself. A car's average speed over an hour-long trip doesn't tell you how fast it was going in any particular second of that hour.

To get the instantaneous rate of change — the speedometer reading at the single point \(x\), not the average over a whole interval — shrink the gap \(h\) toward zero and see what the average rate of change approaches. That limiting value is the formal definition of the derivative:

\[ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h} \]
f'(x)the derivative — instantaneous rate of change at x
lim h→0shrink the gap to a single instant

As \(h\) shrinks, the secant line connecting \(x\) and \(x+h\) rotates and approaches the single line that just touches the curve at \(x\) without crossing through it nearby — the tangent line. So the derivative \(f'(x)\) is, geometrically, exactly the slope of that tangent line: the instantaneous steepness of the curve at one specific point.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

If you measured your speed by dividing the distance you drove by the time it took, over a whole hour, that only tells you your average speed for the hour — not how fast you were going at any one second. A derivative is what happens when you make that measuring window shorter and shorter, down to basically no time at all: it's your speed at this exact instant, read straight off the speedometer.

A worked derivative: \(d/dx(x^2) = 2x\), from first principles

Apply the limit definition directly to \(f(x) = x^2\), with every step of the arithmetic shown. First, expand \(f(x+h)\):

\[ f(x+h) = (x+h)^2 = x^2 + 2xh + h^2 \]
2xhthe term linear in h
h²the term quadratic in h — carries the curvature

Subtract \(f(x) = x^2\):

\[ f(x+h) - f(x) = (x^2 + 2xh + h^2) - x^2 = 2xh + h^2 \]
2xh+h²the x² term cancels exactly, leaving only the h terms

Divide by \(h\). Because both remaining terms in the numerator share a factor of \(h\), that factor cancels cleanly (this step is only valid for \(h \neq 0\), which is exactly the regime the limit is taken over — \(h\) approaches zero but the division happens before it gets there):

\[ \frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} = \frac{2xh + h^2}{h} = \frac{h(2x+h)}{h} = 2x + h \]
2x+hthe shared factor of h cancels cleanly

Finally, take the limit as \(h \to 0\). The \(2x\) term doesn't involve \(h\) at all, so it's untouched; the remaining \(h\) term shrinks to exactly zero:

\[ f'(x) = \lim_{h\to 0}(2x+h) = 2x \]
2xthe h term vanishes exactly at the limit — not approximately

So \(\dfrac{d}{dx}(x^2) = 2x\), exactly — not approximately. This is the single most-cited example in every introductory calculus course for a reason: the algebra is short enough to show completely, and it makes the mechanism unmistakable — the \(h^2\) term is what carries the "curviness" of \(x^2\) (a straight line, \(f(x)=x\), would have no surviving \(h\) term at all: \(f(x+h)-f(x) = h\), giving derivative exactly 1 everywhere, no limit needed to clean anything up), and it vanishes only because you divide by \(h\) before letting \(h\) go to zero, not after. Doing it in the other order — setting \(h=0\) first — would give the meaningless \(0/0\); the entire point of the limit is that it evaluates a fraction whose numerator and denominator are both racing to zero, and asks what ratio they're racing toward.

The integral: the reverse of differentiation

An integral answers a different question: not "how fast is \(f\) changing," but "how much area sits between the curve \(f(x)\) and the x-axis, over some interval?" The idea, built directly from the accumulation intuition above, is to chop the interval from \(a\) to \(b\) into many thin rectangular slices of width \(\Delta x\), each with height roughly \(f(x)\) at that slice, add up all the rectangles' areas, and then let the slices get infinitesimally thin (\(\Delta x \to 0\), the same limiting idea the derivative used, just applied to a sum of areas rather than a single ratio) so the jagged rectangle-approximation becomes the exact, smooth area under the curve:

\[ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \sum_{i} f(x_i)\,\Delta x \]
Σf(xᵢ)Δxsum of thin rectangle areas — the raw definition

Computing that limit of a sum directly, slice by slice, is tedious. The single most important fact in calculus — the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus — is that you almost never have to. It says integration and differentiation are exact inverses of one another: if \(F\) is any antiderivative of \(f\) (that is, \(F'(x) = f(x)\)), then the definite integral collapses to a plain subtraction:

\[ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a) \]
Fany antiderivative of f — F'(x)=f(x)
F(b)−F(a)just evaluate at the two endpoints and subtract
The payoff: integration and differentiation are exact inverses. You almost never have to sum infinitely many rectangular slices by hand — find an antiderivative, evaluate it at the two endpoints, and subtract.

Read plainly: to find the accumulated area under a rate-of-change curve between two points, find a function whose derivative is that curve, then just evaluate it at the two endpoints and subtract. This is the formal version of the speedometer/odometer relationship from the intuition box: the odometer reading (an antiderivative of speed) at the end of the trip, minus the odometer reading at the start, gives the total distance traveled — you never had to add up infinitely many instants of speed by hand, because differentiation and integration undo each other.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

An integral is just adding up a whole lot of tiny slivers of area under a curve, like stacking up a huge pile of super-thin coins until they fill the space beneath the line. The clever shortcut is that you don't actually have to stack them one at a time — if you know a function that "undoes" the curve (its antiderivative), you can check its value at the start and the end, subtract, and instantly get the whole pile's total.

Taylor series: approximating a function from a single point

The derivative gives you the tangent line at a point — and the tangent line is itself the simplest possible approximation to the curve near that point. Rearranging the average-rate-of-change idea for a small, finite step \(h\) (rather than the limit \(h \to 0\)) gives the first-order Taylor approximation:

\[ f(x+h) \approx f(x) + f'(x)\,h \]
f'(x)hthe tangent line's rise over the step h

This says: to estimate \(f\) a small step \(h\) away from a known point \(x\), start at the known value \(f(x)\) and add the tangent line's rise over that step — slope \(f'(x)\) times the step size \(h\). It's exactly the equation of the tangent line drawn in the chart above, and it's exact only in the limit \(h \to 0\); for any finite \(h\), it's an approximation whose error grows as the curve bends away from that straight tangent line.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

If you only know how something looks right this second and which way it's currently heading, you can guess pretty well where it'll be a moment from now — just keep going in that same direction. That's the first-order Taylor guess: today's value, plus "keep walking at today's speed" for one small step. It works fine for a small step, but the guess gets worse the further out you try to use it, because the real path can start curving away from that straight-line guess.

That bending-away is precisely what the second derivative \(f''(x)\) measures — how fast the slope itself is changing, i.e. the curve's curvature. Adding a correction term built from it gives the second-order Taylor expansion, the version this lesson builds up to:

\[ f(x+h) \approx f(x) + f'(x)\,h + \frac{f''(x)}{2}\,h^2 \]

Each added term is a correction for something the previous term missed: \(f(x)\) alone assumes nothing changes at all (a flat, zeroth-order guess); adding \(f'(x)h\) accounts for the curve's slope at \(x\) (a straight tangent-line guess); adding \(\frac{1}{2}f''(x)h^2\) accounts for the curve bending away from that straight line (a parabola-shaped guess that hugs the true curve more closely). A full Taylor series keeps adding higher-derivative terms (\(\frac{f'''(x)}{6}h^3\), and so on) for an increasingly exact match, but the second-order version above is already the workhorse approximation used throughout quantitative finance, because it's usually accurate enough over small moves and far simpler than carrying more terms.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

The second derivative is a fancy way of saying "is the speed itself changing." If you're walking and speeding up, a straight-line guess about where you'll be in a minute will fall short, because you're not just walking at today's speed — you're walking a bit faster with every step. Adding the second-order term is like admitting "I'm not just moving, I'm also accelerating," which makes the guess about the near future noticeably better.

Connecting Taylor series to two formulas already in this Theory Lab

Two formulas elsewhere in this series that were stated and used, without being derived on the spot, are both literally instances of the second-order Taylor expansion above. Seeing the derivation makes clear they aren't separate pieces of financial folklore — they're the same piece of calculus, applied twice.

First: \(\ln(1+R) \approx R - R^2/2\) from the Returns, Log-Returns & Compounding primer. Let \(f(x) = \ln(1+x)\) and expand around \(x=0\). Three quantities are needed: \(f(0) = \ln(1) = 0\); the first derivative \(f'(x) = \frac{1}{1+x}\), so \(f'(0) = 1\); and the second derivative \(f''(x) = -\frac{1}{(1+x)^2}\), so \(f''(0) = -1\). Plugging these three numbers into the second-order Taylor formula, with step size \(h\):

\[ f(0+h) \approx f(0) + f'(0)h + \frac{f''(0)}{2}h^2 = 0 + (1)h + \frac{-1}{2}h^2 = h - \frac{h^2}{2} \]
h − h²/2the same Taylor shape, three numbers plugged in

Relabel the step \(h\) as a return \(R\), and this is exactly \(\ln(1+R) \approx R - R^2/2\) — not a separate approximation someone invented for finance, but the general-purpose Taylor formula with three specific numbers dropped in.

Second: \(g \approx \mu - \sigma^2/2\), the volatility-drag formula from both the returns primer and the Kelly Criterion lesson. This one doesn't require a fresh Taylor expansion — it follows from averaging the same \(\ln(1+R)\approx R - R^2/2\) identity over many periods. Averaging the left side gives the mean log return, which (because log returns add across time, as the returns primer derives) equals the compounded growth rate \(g\). Averaging the right side gives the arithmetic mean \(\mu\) minus one-half the average squared return; for a return series with small mean relative to its spread, that average squared return is well approximated by the variance \(\sigma^2\). Put the two averaged sides back together:

\[ g \approx \mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2} \]
σ²/2volatility drag — the same second-order Taylor correction, averaged

Same Taylor expansion, same \(h - h^2/2\) shape, just relabeled twice — once per period as \(\ln(1+R)\approx R - R^2/2\), and once again after averaging across many periods as \(g \approx \mu - \sigma^2/2\). Both "quick mental math" shortcuts a trader might use are the identical piece of calculus wearing different clothes.

Worked example

How good is the second-order approximation, numerically?

Take \(f(x) = \ln(1+x)\) again and check the Taylor approximation \(f(h) \approx h - h^2/2\) against the true value at two different step sizes, to see the error shrink as \(h\) shrinks — exactly the behavior the derivation above predicts.

Small step, \(h = 0.05\): the true value is \(\ln(1.05) \approx 0.04879\). The Taylor approximation gives \(0.05 - (0.05)^2/2 = 0.05 - 0.00125 = 0.04875\). The error is \(0.04879 - 0.04875 = 0.00004\) — about 0.08% of the true value, small enough to ignore for almost any practical purpose.

Larger step, \(h = 0.30\): the true value is \(\ln(1.30) \approx 0.26236\). The Taylor approximation gives \(0.30 - (0.30)^2/2 = 0.30 - 0.045 = 0.255\). The error is now \(0.26236 - 0.255 = 0.00736\) — roughly 2.8% of the true value, and nearly 200 times larger in absolute size than the \(0.00004\) error at \(h=0.05\), even though \(h\) only grew sixfold. This is exactly the local-approximation warning the pitfalls section below returns to: the second-order Taylor expansion is very accurate for small steps and degrades, sometimes quickly, as the step size grows, because it's still ignoring every term from the third derivative onward.

Real-world trading scenario

Delta is a derivative you're already trading with

Suppose you're holding a call option, currently priced at \(C = \$4.30\) while the underlying is at \(S = \$100\). Your broker's risk screen shows a delta of 0.60. What that number actually means: if the underlying ticks up by \(\$1\) right now, the option's price is expected to move by roughly \(\$0.60\), not the full \(\$1\) — because an option's value is a function of the underlying price, \(C(S)\), and 0.60 is \(\frac{dC}{dS}\) evaluated at today's spot: the instantaneous slope of the option-price function at \(S=100\), read directly off a live risk screen instead of derived from a limit by hand. Delta is not "sort of like" a derivative or an approximation of one — it is one, computed by the pricing model's own machinery (the Black-Scholes lesson derives exactly this quantity in closed form).

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Delta is just "how much does the option's price move for every $1 the underlying moves, right now." A delta of 0.60 means the option acts a bit like it's worth 60 cents of the underlying — not a full dollar of it — for a small wiggle. It's the exact same "instant rate of change" idea as a speedometer, just measured on an option's price instead of a car's position.

And it's a first-order approximation in exactly the Taylor sense above: \(\Delta C \approx \delta \cdot \Delta S\) is the tangent-line estimate, good for small moves in \(S\) and increasingly wrong for large ones, because it ignores how delta itself changes as \(S\) moves — that curvature term is gamma, the option's second derivative with respect to the underlying, and it's exactly the \(\frac{1}{2}f''(x)h^2\) correction term from the second-order Taylor expansion above, applied to an option book instead of an abstract \(f(x)\). This is precisely why option desks quote and risk-manage both delta and gamma together, not delta alone — one is the first-order Taylor term, the other is the second-order correction that keeps the estimate honest for a bigger move.

The same Taylor logic explains a completely different piece of trader mental math: "if vol is 20% a year, my expected drag is about 2% a year." That's \(\sigma^2/2\) with \(\sigma = 0.20\): \((0.20)^2/2 = 0.04/2 = 0.02\), i.e. 2%. It isn't a coincidence or a memorized rule of thumb picked up on a desk — it's the same second-order Taylor expansion derived above, \(g \approx \mu - \sigma^2/2\), evaluated with one specific number. Once you've seen the derivation, the mental shortcut stops being a trick to memorize and becomes a two-line calculation you could redo from scratch.

Rates of change are estimated, not exact

Real price series only exist at discrete points, so practitioners use a finite difference as a working stand-in for the theoretical limit.

The Greeks are a Taylor expansion of an option's price. Delta (\(\partial C/\partial S\)), gamma (\(\partial^2 C/\partial S^2\)), theta (\(\partial C/\partial t\)), and vega (\(\partial C/\partial \sigma\)) are first- and second-order derivatives of the option-pricing function with respect to different inputs. A "delta-gamma" approximation to how much an option book's value will move for a given underlying shock is exactly the second-order Taylor expansion built above, applied variable by variable: \(\Delta C \approx \delta\,\Delta S + \frac{1}{2}\gamma\,(\Delta S)^2\). Risk desks use this constantly because fully repricing every option in a large book for every hypothetical market move is expensive; the Taylor approximation gives a fast, close-enough estimate from quantities (the Greeks) the desk already has on hand.

Optimization is "set the derivative to zero." Wherever a quantitative model claims to find an "optimal" value — the Kelly Criterion lesson's growth-optimal bet fraction \(f^*\), or a regression's least-squares coefficients — the standard method is exactly the calculus move used in that lesson: write the objective as a function of the parameter, differentiate it, and solve for where that derivative equals zero (where the tangent line is flat, i.e. neither rising nor falling — the hallmark of a smooth maximum or minimum). This single idea, "optimal points are where the derivative vanishes," underlies far more of quantitative finance than any one lesson name-checks explicitly.

Integrals show up as probability and as cumulative performance. A cumulative distribution function is, by definition, the integral of a probability density function — "the probability that a return is less than \(x\)" is the accumulated area under the density curve up to \(x\). An equity curve is, in continuous terms, the integral of a P&L rate over time — exactly the odometer-from-speedometer relationship the intuition box opened with, now applied to trading returns instead of distance.

Rates of change in real market data are almost always estimated, not computed exactly. A real price series only exists at discrete points in time (one close per day, one tick per trade), so there is no literal \(h \to 0\) to take — practitioners approximate a derivative with a finite difference (a plain \(\Delta f/\Delta x\) over the smallest available step, or a regression slope over a short rolling window) and treat it as a working stand-in for the theoretical limit, at the cost of a small amount of discretization and estimation error that a purely analytical derivative never has.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where calculus reasoning goes wrong in practice

Treating a finite-difference slope as if it were the exact derivative. Real data gives you \(\Delta f/\Delta x\) over some non-zero gap (a day, a rolling window), not the true limit as that gap shrinks to zero. That finite-difference estimate is itself noisy and depends on the window chosen — a shorter window tracks instantaneous change more closely but is noisier; a longer window is smoother but is really estimating an average rate of change over the window, quietly drifting back toward the secant-line concept the derivative was defined to improve on.

Extrapolating a first-order (tangent-line/delta-only) estimate too far. The Taylor approximation \(f(x+h) \approx f(x) + f'(x)h\) is a local statement — accurate near \(x\), and increasingly wrong the larger \(h\) gets, exactly as the worked example above showed numerically (a roughly two-hundred-fold jump in absolute error going from \(h=0.05\) to \(h=0.30\)). Using delta alone to estimate an option's P&L for a large underlying move, without the gamma correction, is the single most common version of this mistake in practice.

Assuming a function is differentiable everywhere. The derivative's limit definition silently assumes the limit actually exists and is the same approaching from both sides. Real financial functions can have kinks — an option's payoff at expiry has a sharp corner exactly at the strike price, where the left-hand and right-hand slopes genuinely disagree, and no single tangent line describes that point. Applying smooth-function calculus across a kink without noticing it is a quiet source of pricing and hedging errors right at the kink.

Forgetting the small-\(x\) assumption behind \(\ln(1+R)\approx R - R^2/2\) and \(g \approx \mu - \sigma^2/2\). Both formulas are second-order Taylor expansions, and every Taylor expansion carries an implicit "this holds near the expansion point" caveat. They are excellent approximations for typical daily FX moves and are measurably worse for large single-period returns (a genuine concern for crypto or single-name equities, less so for ordinary daily FX, as the returns primer notes) — reach for the exact geometric-mean formula rather than the Taylor shortcut whenever the returns involved are large.

Scenario drill · an illustrative overnight gap

Delta says you lose $6. Do you?

You have sold the lesson's call option: price $4.30, underlying at $100, delta 0.60. For this drill, suppose its gamma is 0.03. Overnight the underlying gaps up $10, to $110. Your quick risk screen uses delta only and says the option gained about \(0.60 \times 10 = \$6.00\), so you lost about $6.00 per option. What do you expect the real loss to be?

  1. About $6.00. Delta is the derivative, so it gives the answer.
    Delta is only the first-order term: the tangent line. A $10 gap is not a small step, and the option's price curve bends away from that line.
  2. More than $6.00. Add gamma's term, \(\tfrac12\gamma(\Delta S)^2\): half of 0.03 times 100 is $1.50, so about $7.50. Then confirm by repricing.
    This is the second-order Taylor expansion: \(\Delta C \approx \delta\,\Delta S\) \(+\, \tfrac12\gamma\,(\Delta S)^2\). For a short option, the curvature always works against you, whichever way the big move goes.
  3. Less than $6.00. Big moves are rare, so the estimate is probably too high.
    Rarity has nothing to do with it once the move has happened. A call's value curves upward, so for a rise it gains more than the tangent line says, not less.
  4. No idea until the option is fully repriced.
    Full repricing is the most exact answer and worth doing. But the Taylor terms tell you the direction and rough size immediately, which is the point of having them.

What to take from it. This is an illustrative situation, not a historical event: the price, spot and delta come from the lesson's example, and the gamma of 0.03 is assumed for the drill. With those numbers the delta-gamma estimate is \(6.00 + 1.50 = \$7.50\), a quarter more than delta alone. The bigger the move, the more the second-order term matters.

Section 04

Key Takeaways

✓A derivative is the tangent-line slope; an integral is the area under a curve. The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus proves they're exact inverse operations — you almost never have to sum infinitely many slices by hand.
✓A Taylor series builds an estimate one correction at a time. Zeroth order is a flat guess, first order adds the tangent line, second order corrects for curvature — each term fixes what the previous one missed.
✓\(\ln(1+R)\approx R-R^2/2\) and \(g\approx\mu-\sigma^2/2\) are the same formula, twice. Both fall out of the identical second-order Taylor expansion of \(\ln(1+x)\) around zero — not two separate finance shortcuts.
✓Delta and gamma are literally Taylor terms. Delta is the first-order derivative of the option price with respect to the underlying; gamma is the second-order correction for how delta itself changes.
⚠Every Taylor approximation is local. It's accurate near the expansion point and degrades — sometimes sharply — as the step size grows, exactly as the worked example's roughly 200-fold jump in error shows.
⚠Real market data gives you a finite difference, never the true limit. Practitioners approximate a derivative with \(\Delta f/\Delta x\) over the smallest available step, which carries its own noise and window-choice tradeoff.
ℹTaylor's second-order term is where volatility shows up. Merton (1969–71) brought Itô's calculus to finance; the \(\tfrac12\sigma^2\) term it keeps drives volatility drag, gamma P&L and Black–Scholes–Merton (1973).
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. Why is the derivative defined as a limit, \(\lim_{h\to 0}\), rather than just picking some small, fixed \(h\) and computing \([f(x+h)-f(x)]/h\)?
Any fixed, non-zero \(h\) gives the slope of a secant line — the average rate of change over that specific gap, which depends on how big \(h\) is and on how much the function curves within that gap. Two different choices of \(h\) generally give two different answers. The limit process is what removes that arbitrary dependence: as \(h\) shrinks toward zero, the secant line's slope converges to a single, well-defined value — the slope of the one tangent line at that exact point — independent of any particular choice of step size. A fixed \(h\) gives an approximation to the derivative; the limit gives the derivative itself.
Q2. In the first-principles derivation of \(d/dx(x^2)=2x\), what role does the \(h^2\) term play, and why is it correct that it vanishes in the final answer?
Expanding \((x+h)^2 = x^2 + 2xh + h^2\) shows two new terms beyond \(x^2\): a term linear in \(h\) (\(2xh\)) and a term quadratic in \(h\) (\(h^2\)). The quadratic term is what carries the function's curvature — a straight line \(f(x)=x\) would produce no such term at all. After dividing the whole difference by \(h\), the quadratic term becomes a single leftover \(h\) (from \(h^2/h\)), which genuinely does shrink to exactly zero as \(h\to 0\) — this isn't hand-waving, it's the correct limit of a term that literally goes to zero. What survives the limit, \(2x\), is the linear term's coefficient, which is why the derivative of \(x^2\) is proportional to \(x\) but the derivative of a straight line is a constant with no \(x\) at all.
Q3. In one sentence each, what does a derivative measure and what does an integral measure, and how does the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus connect them?
A derivative measures the instantaneous rate of change of a function at a point (the tangent line's slope there); an integral measures the accumulated area under a function's curve over an interval (a running total built from that rate). The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus connects them by proving they are exact inverse operations: if \(F\) is an antiderivative of \(f\) (meaning \(F'=f\)), the accumulated area from \(a\) to \(b\) can be found by evaluating \(F\) at the two endpoints and subtracting, \(\int_a^b f(x)dx = F(b)-F(a)\), without ever summing infinitely many rectangular slices by hand.
Q4. Why does the first-order Taylor term, \(f'(x)h\), correspond exactly to the tangent line, and why does a convex (or concave) function need the second-order term to be approximated well?
The tangent line at \(x\) is, by definition, the straight line passing through \((x, f(x))\) with slope \(f'(x)\); its value at a step \(h\) away is exactly \(f(x) + f'(x)h\), which is precisely the first-order Taylor approximation. A tangent line is straight, so it only matches a curved function exactly at the single point of tangency — for a convex or concave function, the true curve bends away from that straight line as \(h\) grows, and the size of that bending-away is governed by the second derivative \(f''(x)\) (curvature). Adding the \(\frac{1}{2}f''(x)h^2\) term corrects for exactly that bend, which is why it noticeably improves the approximation for any function that isn't already a straight line.
Q5. In the option-delta scenario, why is delta alone (a first-order Taylor term) insufficient for estimating the P&L of a large move in the underlying, and what corrects it?
Delta is the option-price function's first derivative with respect to the underlying, evaluated at today's spot — a snapshot of the instantaneous slope right now. Using \(\Delta C \approx \delta\,\Delta S\) alone implicitly assumes that slope stays constant over the whole move, which is only true for an infinitesimally small \(\Delta S\). For a large move, delta itself changes as the underlying moves — that changing-slope effect is gamma, the option's second derivative with respect to the underlying, and adding the correction term \(\frac{1}{2}\gamma(\Delta S)^2\) (the second-order Taylor term) captures the part of the price move delta alone misses.
Q6. Why is \(g \approx \mu - \sigma^2/2\) called an approximation rather than an exact formula, and under what condition would you expect it to break down?
It's derived by averaging the second-order Taylor expansion of \(\ln(1+R)\) around \(R=0\) — a local approximation that keeps only the first two correction terms (linear and quadratic in \(R\)) and drops every higher-order term. It is not an exact algebraic identity the way log-return additivity across time is; it holds well when per-period returns \(R\) are small relative to 1, which is the typical case for daily FX. It degrades for large per-period returns (very volatile instruments, wide single-period moves, fat-tailed regimes) where the dropped higher-order terms are no longer negligible, in which case the exact geometric-mean formula should be used instead of the Taylor shortcut.

Further reading
  • James Stewart, Calculus, Cengage Learning (any recent edition) — the standard, widely-used introductory calculus textbook; covers limits, derivatives, integrals, and Taylor series in far more depth and generality than this lesson needs, with extensive worked problems.
  • Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz independently developed the core ideas of calculus in the late 1600s, working from different starting problems (Newton from physics and motion, Leibniz from geometry and his own notation) and arriving at equivalent results; their near-simultaneous, independent development led to a well-documented priority dispute between English and Continental mathematicians in the decades that followed. Much of the standard notation used in this lesson (\(dx\), \(\int\)) comes from Leibniz.
  • Marek Capiński and Tomasz Zastawniak, Mathematics for Finance: An Introduction to Financial Engineering, Springer, 2nd edition, 2011 — an introductory, mostly discrete-time textbook on the finance these tools are applied to (time value of money, portfolio theory, binomial and Black-Scholes option pricing); it uses calculus rather than teaching it, so read it alongside this lesson, not instead of it.
