Regression Diagnostics: Durbin-Watson, Breusch-Pagan & Jarque-Bera

A regression's p-value is not a fact about the world — it's a fact about a model of the world, and that model comes with fine print. This lesson derives, from scratch, the three checks that verify the fine print actually holds before you trust the headline number: is the error serially correlated (Durbin-Watson), does its variance change across the sample (Breusch-Pagan), and does it look enough like a bell curve for small-sample inference to be valid (Jarque-Bera).

CONCEPT — formalizes a validation discipline used throughout this project ~17 min read Prereqs: Correlation & Linear Regression primer
TL;DR
  • A regression's p-value is only valid if its residuals are non-autocorrelated (Durbin-Watson), homoskedastic (Breusch-Pagan), and — in small samples — approximately normal (Jarque-Bera); failing one doesn't make \(\hat\beta\) wrong, it makes the classical standard error wrong.
  • This project's own backtest-build discipline already states this as a rule: "Add residual diagnostics to any regression-based signal: Durbin-Watson (autocorrelation), Jarque-Bera (residual normality), Breusch-Pagan (heteroskedasticity) before trusting its p-value/coefficient. A statistically 'significant' coefficient on a heteroskedastic fit has optimistic standard errors — check before leaning on it." This lesson derives why.
  • Durbin-Watson compares squared consecutive-residual changes to total residual variance; \(DW \approx 2\) means no lag-1 autocorrelation, toward 0 means positive autocorrelation, toward 4 means negative.
  • The worked scenario: an FX yield-spread regression shows a p-value of 0.01, but its residuals give \(DW = 0.6\) (implying \(\hat\rho \approx 0.7\)); with ~500 daily observations that corresponds to an effective sample size of only \(n_{eff}\approx 88\), and correcting for that (e.g. Newey-West/HAC standard errors) pushes the "significant" result back toward noise.
  • Passing all three diagnostics only certifies that the in-sample significance claim is honestly computed — it says nothing about out-of-sample durability (Lego Principle 5 still applies).
Section 01

Why this matters

Ordinary least squares gives you two different kinds of output, and it is easy to conflate them. The first is the point estimate — the fitted slope \(\hat\beta\), the number that says "for a one-unit move in X, Y tends to move this much." The second is an inference claim built on top of that estimate — a standard error, a t-statistic, a p-value, a confidence interval — the number that says "and here's how sure we should be that this isn't zero." The Correlation & Linear Regression primer derived the first kind in full: \(\hat\beta = \mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)/\mathrm{Var}(X)\), obtained purely by minimizing the sum of squared residuals, with no assumption about the residuals' distribution required anywhere in that derivation. That's worth sitting with, because it means the point estimate is fairly robust — it doesn't know or care whether its own residuals are well-behaved.

The second kind of output is not robust in the same way. The textbook formula for a regression's standard error — the one every stats package prints next to \(\hat\beta\) without comment — is derived under a specific set of assumptions about the error term \(\epsilon\): that it has constant variance across every observation (homoskedasticity), that it is uncorrelated with itself across observations (no autocorrelation), and — for small samples specifically, where the Central Limit Theorem hasn't yet had enough observations to kick in — that it is approximately normally distributed. Those three assumptions are exactly what let the classical formula translate "how spread out are the residuals" into "how uncertain am I about \(\hat\beta\)." When any of the three assumptions is badly violated, that translation breaks, and the standard error the software prints is simply the wrong number for the true sampling uncertainty in \(\hat\beta\) — most often too small, which makes a t-statistic look bigger and a p-value look smaller than the evidence actually supports. The point estimate \(\hat\beta\) can still be a perfectly reasonable guess at the slope while the significance claim built on top of it is fiction.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Picture guessing someone's height from their shoe size — that guess itself doesn't care whether your data was collected cleanly. But saying "and I'm 99% sure about this guess" is a completely separate claim, and that confidence claim only holds up if your measurements followed certain rules — no repeated measurements of the same shoe pretending to be different people, no wildly different measuring tools for big feet versus small feet. The guess can be fine even when the confidence claim built on top of it is fake.

This is precisely the gap this project's own build discipline calls out by name. Its backtest-build-discipline section states plainly: "Add residual diagnostics to any regression-based signal: Durbin-Watson (autocorrelation), Jarque-Bera (residual normality), Breusch-Pagan (heteroskedasticity) before trusting its p-value/coefficient. A statistically 'significant' coefficient on a heteroskedastic fit has optimistic standard errors — check before leaning on it." That rule is stated in prose, as something to do. This lesson is where it gets derived — what each of the three tests is actually checking, why a failure specifically inflates apparent significance rather than just "being bad in general," and what the fix looks like once a test flags a problem.

💡
Plain-English intuition

All three tests are asking the same underlying question about the leftovers

A regression's residuals — \(e_i = y_i - \hat y_i\), the part of \(Y\) the fitted line doesn't explain — are supposed to look like structureless noise. Not "small," necessarily, just patternless: no systematic wave through time, no systematic fanning-out as the fitted value gets larger, no lopsided pile-up of extreme values on one side. If the residuals show a pattern, that pattern is information the model failed to capture — and worse, that leftover information corrupts the one part of OLS that assumed there'd be nothing left to find.

Durbin-Watson asks: does today's residual predict tomorrow's? Line the residuals up in time order and check whether consecutive ones tend to sit close together (both positive in a stretch, then both negative in a stretch — a slow wave) rather than bouncing around independently. If they do, the model's errors are "sticky" — whatever caused today's miss is still partly causing tomorrow's.

Breusch-Pagan asks: does the size of the typical residual depend on where you are in the data? Plot the residuals against the fitted values (or against the predictors directly) and check whether they stay in a uniform band or visibly fan out — small and tight for some values of X, wide and scattered for others. If the spread isn't constant, treating one single number as "the" standard error for the whole sample is averaging over observations that don't actually share the same amount of noise.

Jarque-Bera asks: aside from spread and stickiness, does the overall shape of the residual distribution look normal — symmetric, without an unusually heavy tail on one side? This matters most in small samples, where the formulas for the t-statistic and its p-value lean on the residuals being approximately normal to be exactly right; with enough data the Central Limit Theorem bails you out regardless, but "enough data" is doing real work in that sentence and a trading dataset's usable, non-overlapping sample is often smaller than it looks.

All three are really the same diagnostic act performed on three different axes of the same object — the residual series — because all three are checking the same underlying promise: that what's left over after fitting the line really is noise, and not a second signal the inference machinery is silently mistaking for confidence.

1. Durbin-Watson — testing for autocorrelation. Take the residuals from a fitted regression, in their natural time order, \(e_1, e_2, \dots, e_n\). The Durbin-Watson statistic compares the size of the changes between consecutive residuals to the size of the residuals themselves:

\[ DW = \frac{\sum_{t=2}^{n}(e_t - e_{t-1})^2}{\sum_{t=1}^{n} e_t^2} \]

To see why this measures autocorrelation, consider the two extremes. If consecutive residuals are independent — no relationship between \(e_t\) and \(e_{t-1}\) — then on average \((e_t - e_{t-1})^2 = e_t^2 - 2 e_t e_{t-1} + e_{t-1}^2\), and the cross term \(e_t e_{t-1}\) averages to roughly zero because there's no systematic tendency for \(e_t\) and \(e_{t-1}\) to share a sign. That leaves the numerator averaging to about \(2\sum e_t^2\) (each residual contributing to two consecutive squared-difference terms), which makes \(DW \approx 2\) — the textbook "no autocorrelation" benchmark. Now suppose residuals are positively autocorrelated — a positive residual tends to be followed by another positive one, a negative by another negative (the wavy, sticky pattern from the intuition section). Then \(e_t\) and \(e_{t-1}\) tend to share a sign, so the cross term \(e_t e_{t-1}\) is usually positive, which makes \((e_t - e_{t-1})^2\) smaller on average than it would be under independence (consecutive values are closer together) — pushing \(DW\) below 2, toward 0 in the extreme case of residuals that barely change from one period to the next. The mirror case, negative autocorrelation (residuals that alternate sign, overcorrecting each period), pushes \(e_t e_{t-1}\) negative, inflates \((e_t-e_{t-1})^2\), and pushes \(DW\) above 2, toward its upper bound of 4.

\[ DW \approx 2(1-\hat\rho), \qquad \hat\rho = \text{lag-1 autocorrelation of the residuals} \]
ρ̂estimated lag-1 autocorrelation of the residuals
DW≈2 at ρ̂=0, →0 as ρ̂→1, →4 as ρ̂→−1

That approximation makes the whole scale readable at a glance: \(\hat\rho \approx 0\) (no autocorrelation) gives \(DW \approx 2\); \(\hat\rho \approx 1\) (residuals barely distinguishable period to period) gives \(DW \approx 0\); \(\hat\rho \approx -1\) (residuals alternate almost perfectly) gives \(DW \approx 4\). \(DW\) always falls in the range \([0, 4]\) by construction, and in practice, a value comfortably near 2 (commonly, roughly 1.5–2.5, though exact critical values depend on sample size and the number of predictors and are read off a Durbin-Watson table) is treated as consistent with no meaningful autocorrelation.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine grading how "sticky" a series of misses is — if you overshoot a dart throw today, are you likely to overshoot again tomorrow? Durbin-Watson checks exactly that, by comparing how much the misses change from one throw to the next against how big the misses are overall. Big jumpy changes between throws means the misses are independent (DW near 2); throws that barely change from one to the next means whatever's throwing you off today is still throwing you off tomorrow (DW near 0).

Why does positive autocorrelation specifically make the p-value look too good? The classical standard-error formula for \(\hat\beta\) assumes each residual is a fresh, independent draw of information — \(n\) observations means \(n\) independent pieces of evidence about the relationship, and more independent evidence means a tighter, more confident standard error. When residuals are positively autocorrelated, consecutive observations are substantially re-stating the same information rather than each contributing something new — the true number of independent pieces of evidence in the sample (the "effective sample size") is smaller than the raw row count \(n\). The classical formula, however, still divides by the raw \(n\), so it reports a standard error calibrated to more independent information than the sample actually contains. A standard error that's too small makes the t-statistic (\(\hat\beta\) divided by that standard error) too large, which makes the p-value look smaller — more significant — than the true, effective amount of independent evidence justifies. This is the same underlying failure mode the Multiple Testing lesson in this Theory Lab warns about from the opposite direction: that lesson shows how testing many independent slices inflates the chance of a false positive by overcounting how many genuinely separate chances you gave the null hypothesis to fail; positive residual autocorrelation inflates significance by the mirror mechanism — undercounting how few genuinely separate, independent observations actually back a single test. Both errors are a mismatch between the sample size a p-value formula assumes and the amount of independent information the data actually contains.

The key distinction: a diagnostic failure means the classical standard error is wrong — it does not mean \(\hat\beta\) itself is meaningless. The point estimate is derived purely from minimizing squared residuals and doesn't assume anything about their serial structure, variance pattern, or shape. What breaks is specifically the machinery that translates "spread of residuals" into "confidence about \(\hat\beta\)."
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

If you ask the same person the same question 500 times in a row, you don't really have 500 independent opinions — you have one opinion, repeated 500 times, dressed up to look like a big survey. Sticky, autocorrelated residuals are like that: the statistics formula thinks it has hundreds of independent pieces of evidence, but really it has far fewer genuinely separate ones, because consecutive days are largely echoing the same underlying miss rather than each telling you something new.

2. Breusch-Pagan — testing for heteroskedasticity. The classical standard-error formula also assumes the residual variance, \(\mathrm{Var}(\epsilon_i)\), is the same constant number for every observation \(i\) — homoskedasticity. Breusch-Pagan tests this with a deliberately simple, almost sly, trick: if the residual variance depends on \(X\), then the squared residuals themselves — which are, on average, an estimate of that variance at each point — should show a detectable relationship with \(X\). So the test:

  1. Fits the original regression \(Y = \hat\alpha + \hat\beta X + e\) and keeps the residuals \(e_i\).
  2. Runs a brand-new, second ("auxiliary") regression: the squared residuals \(e_i^2\) as the dependent variable, regressed on the same predictor(s) \(X_i\) used in the original model.
  3. Takes that auxiliary regression's \(R^2_{aux}\) and forms the test statistic \(LM = n \cdot R^2_{aux}\), which under the null hypothesis of homoskedasticity follows a \(\chi^2\) distribution with degrees of freedom equal to the number of predictors in the auxiliary regression.
\[ e_i^2 = \gamma_0 + \gamma_1 X_i + v_i \qquad LM = n \cdot R^2_{aux} \ \sim\ \chi^2_{k} \text{ under } H_0 \]
eᵢ²squared residuals — the auxiliary regression's dependent variable
R²_auxhow much of eᵢ² the predictors X can explain
LMn·R²_aux, tested against χ² — large means heteroskedastic

The logic is exactly the same OLS machinery from the primer, just pointed at a different dependent variable. If the true residual variance is constant, there is nothing for \(X\) to predict about \(e_i^2\) beyond chance — the auxiliary regression's \(R^2_{aux}\) should be small, and \(LM\) small and unremarkable against the \(\chi^2\) benchmark. If the residual variance genuinely grows (or shrinks) as \(X\) changes — the classic "fan" shape, common in return series where volatility itself varies with the level of some driver — then \(X\) has real, systematic power to predict \(e_i^2\), \(R^2_{aux}\) comes out larger than chance alone would produce, and a large \(LM\) rejects the homoskedasticity null. A significant Breusch-Pagan result means the single "one standard error fits all observations" number the classical formula reports is wrong in the same direction as before — it understates the true uncertainty in the regions of \(X\) where variance is actually largest, again making the coefficient look more precisely estimated, and its p-value more impressive, than it should.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine a bathroom scale that's usually accurate to within an ounce for light objects but wobbles by a whole pound for heavy ones — a single "margin of error" sticker slapped on the scale would be misleading for at least some of what you weigh on it. Breusch-Pagan checks for exactly that: whether a model's typical error size actually depends on where you are in the data, which would make reporting one single standard error for every prediction the same kind of misleading sticker.

3. Jarque-Bera — testing for residual normality. This test asks a different kind of question: not about serial structure or variance, but about the overall shape of the residual distribution. It combines two moments beyond mean and variance — skewness and kurtosis — into a single statistic:

\[ JB = \frac{n}{6}\left(S^2 + \frac{K^2}{4}\right) \]
Ssample skewness — asymmetry, 0 if symmetric
Ksample excess kurtosis — fatter tails than normal if positive
JBtested against χ²₂ — large means non-normal

\(S\) is sample skewness (a measure of lopsidedness — a long tail stretching out to one side of the mean while the bulk of the mass sits on the other), and \(K\) is sample excess kurtosis (how much fatter the tails are than a normal distribution's, with the normal itself defined as 0 on this "excess" scale). A perfectly normal distribution has \(S = 0\) and \(K = 0\), so under the null hypothesis of normality, \(JB\) should be small; large residual skew or excess kurtosis, or both, pushes \(JB\) up, and the statistic is compared against a \(\chi^2_2\) distribution to get a p-value for "these residuals are consistent with being drawn from a normal distribution."

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Jarque-Bera checks whether the shape of the leftover errors looks like a normal, symmetric bell curve, or whether it's lopsided (a long tail stretching one direction) or has surprisingly many extreme outliers. Why care about the shape specifically? Because with a small dataset, the usual "trust the confidence interval" math secretly assumes a bell-curve shape to be exactly right — if the shape is off and the sample is small, that borrowed trust hasn't been earned yet.

Both ingredient statistics are already built, tested, and in production in this project — a sample-skewness function (Fisher-Pearson sample skewness; positive means a longer right tail, negative a fatter left tail — the direction that matters most for a strategy's downside) and an excess-kurtosis function (kurtosis minus 3, so a normal distribution reads exactly 0 and any positive value means fatter-than-normal tails). Jarque-Bera is nothing more than those same two quantities, computed on a regression's residuals specifically, squared, weighted, and summed into one number with a known reference distribution to test against — the identical building blocks this project already uses to describe a strategy's raw return distribution repurposed to describe a regression's leftover error instead of a strategy's P&L.

Why does this matter more in small samples? The classical t-test and its p-value for \(\hat\beta\) are exact when the underlying errors are normally distributed, and are otherwise justified only asymptotically — by the Central Limit Theorem, which says that as sample size grows, the sampling distribution of \(\hat\beta\) itself approaches normality regardless of the residuals' own shape, because \(\hat\beta\) is built from a sum (a weighted average) of many observations. With enough data, non-normal residuals stop mattering much for inference on \(\hat\beta\), because the averaging in the CLT smooths the irregularity away. With a small sample — few, non-overlapping trading periods being the common case in this project, since resampling to a signal's true release frequency, per this project's own backtest-build-discipline guidance, often leaves far fewer independent rows than the raw daily bar count suggests — that averaging hasn't had enough terms to do its smoothing work yet, so a skewed or fat-tailed residual distribution translates more directly into a distorted t-statistic and an unreliable p-value. Jarque-Bera is the check for exactly that failure mode: it doesn't matter as much once \(n\) is large, but it can matter a great deal when it's small — which is precisely when a "significant" result is most tempting to over-trust.

Real-world trading scenario

A p-value of 0.01 that isn't what it looks like

A researcher regresses next-day FX returns for a currency pair on a yield-spread signal — the idea being that a widening rate differential should predict tomorrow's direction. Running the regression on two years of daily data produces a coefficient with a p-value of 0.01: by the conventional 5% threshold, a strikingly significant result, the kind of number that gets an idea greenlit for further development without a second look.

Before trusting it, the researcher checks the residual diagnostics, as the house rule requires. Durbin-Watson comes back at \(DW = 0.6\) — far below the "no autocorrelation" benchmark of 2, squarely in strongly-positive-autocorrelation territory. Using the approximation \(DW \approx 2(1-\hat\rho)\), that implies \(\hat\rho \approx 0.7\): a given day's residual is strongly predictive of the next day's residual, which makes sense here — a yield-spread signal changes slowly from day to day, so a persistent pricing error against it (the model consistently over- or under-predicting for a stretch) is exactly the kind of slow-moving mis-specification this test is built to catch.

What that means concretely: with roughly 500 trading days in the sample and \(\hat\rho \approx 0.7\), the residuals are behaving less like 500 independent observations and more like a much smaller number of effectively independent "blocks" of correlated days. A standard rule-of-thumb adjustment for the effective sample size under AR(1)-type autocorrelation is \(n_{eff} \approx n \cdot \frac{1-\hat\rho}{1+\hat\rho}\) — here, \(n_{eff} \approx 500 \times \frac{0.3}{1.7} \approx 88\). The regression's classical standard error was computed as though it had 500 independent observations backing it; the honest number is closer to what 88 independent observations would support. Recomputing the t-statistic and p-value off that much smaller effective sample size — or, more directly, using Newey-West heteroskedasticity-and-autocorrelation-consistent (HAC) standard errors, which correct for exactly this — the p-value is nowhere near 0.01 once corrected; it's plausibly back in "not distinguishable from noise" territory. The point estimate \(\hat\beta\) itself hasn't changed and might still be a reasonable read on the relationship's direction and rough size — but the confidence the raw p-value implied was manufactured by the autocorrelation, not earned by the data.

This is exactly the trap this project's own "add residual diagnostics... before trusting its p-value/coefficient" rule exists to prevent — not as an abstract statistical nicety, but as the specific mechanism by which a signal with a genuinely unremarkable relationship can produce an impressive-looking p-value purely from serially sticky errors, and get mistaken for a discovery.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

As a pre-trust gate, not a post-mortem. The standard workflow is: fit the regression, then before quoting the p-value or coefficient to anyone (including yourself, in a design doc), run all three residual checks. A clean pass on all three is what makes the classical p-value trustworthy at face value; a failure on any one doesn't necessarily kill the finding, but it does mean the reported significance needs a correction or a different inference method before it's used to make a decision.

Fixes, once a test flags a problem. Autocorrelation (Durbin-Watson) is most commonly addressed with Newey-West (HAC-consistent) standard errors, which explicitly correct for both autocorrelation and heteroskedasticity without needing to respecify the model, or by adding lagged terms that soak up the serial structure so the remaining residual is closer to white noise. Heteroskedasticity (Breusch-Pagan) is commonly addressed with heteroskedasticity-robust ("White") standard errors, or by modeling the variance directly (a GARCH-type approach, covered in this Theory Lab's GARCH & Volatility Clustering lesson, is the time-series analogue of exactly this problem). Non-normality (Jarque-Bera) in a small sample is addressed by collecting more independent observations if possible (letting the CLT do its job), using a distribution-free method (bootstrap standard errors resample the data itself rather than assuming a shape), or simply reporting the result with the appropriate caveat rather than leaning on the exact p-value.

A screening step before, not a substitute for, out-of-sample validation. Passing all three diagnostics only means the in-sample significance claim is honestly computed — it says nothing about whether the relationship holds up on data the model hasn't seen. A regression can have perfectly well-behaved, homoskedastic, normally distributed, non-autocorrelated residuals in-sample and still fail completely out-of-sample, because the diagnostics check the validity of the inference procedure, not the existence of a durable relationship. Both checks matter, and they check different things — consistent with this repo's own discipline that an in-sample number, however carefully diagnosed, is not evidence on its own (Lego Principle 5).

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where these three diagnostics get misread

Treating a diagnostic failure as proof the relationship is fake. A significant Durbin-Watson or Breusch-Pagan result means the classical standard error is wrong, not that \(\hat\beta\) itself is meaningless. The point estimate can still be a reasonable read on the relationship's direction and rough magnitude; what's broken is specifically the machinery that translates spread-of-residuals into confidence-about-\(\hat\beta\). The correct response is to recompute inference with a robust method (HAC/Newey-West, White standard errors), not to throw the coefficient away.

Assuming Durbin-Watson near 2 means the model is well specified. \(DW \approx 2\) only rules out simple first-order (lag-1) autocorrelation. It says nothing about longer-lag structure (e.g. a weekly seasonal pattern that shows up at lag 5), and it says nothing about heteroskedasticity or normality at all — those are separate axes, checked by separate tests. A clean Durbin-Watson number is one necessary check passed, not a general "all clear."

Running Breusch-Pagan (or any of these) on a regression with a badly wrong functional form. If the true relationship between \(X\) and \(Y\) is nonlinear and a straight line is forced through it, the residuals can show fan-shaped or patterned behavior that looks like a variance problem but is really a specification problem — the model is systematically wrong in different ways at different values of \(X\), not just noisier there. Always look at the actual residuals-vs-fitted scatter plot (the middle panel style above), not just the test p-value, to distinguish "genuinely non-constant noise" from "wrong functional form masquerading as non-constant noise."

Over-weighting Jarque-Bera in a large sample. Because \(JB\) scales with \(n\), a trivially small amount of skew or excess kurtosis can still produce a "significant" (rejecting-normality) result once the sample is large enough — precisely the regime where non-normality matters least for the validity of the t-statistic, thanks to the Central Limit Theorem. A significant Jarque-Bera on a dataset of 10,000 observations is a much smaller practical concern than the identical p-value on a dataset of 40. Read the actual skewness and excess kurtosis numbers themselves, not just the test's binary reject/fail-to-reject verdict, and weight the concern by how small the sample actually is.

Diagnosing in-sample and calling it done. All three tests are computed on the same fitted sample the regression was estimated on. A clean pass confirms the in-sample p-value is honestly computed; it is not, and was never meant to be, evidence that the relationship generalizes out of sample. Residual diagnostics and out-of-sample validation answer different questions and this repo requires both, not one in place of the other.

Key Takeaways

✓A p-value depends on assumptions the point estimate doesn't need. \(\hat\beta\) is robust to residual misbehavior; the standard error, t-stat, and p-value are not.
✓Durbin-Watson checks autocorrelation. \(DW\approx2\) means no lag-1 autocorrelation; toward 0 means positive (p-values look too good), toward 4 means negative.
✓Breusch-Pagan checks heteroskedasticity. It regresses squared residuals on the predictors — if they predict e², the single "one std error fits all" number is wrong.
✓Jarque-Bera checks normality, and matters most in small samples. With enough data the CLT bails you out regardless of residual shape.
✓The worked scenario: DW=0.6 implies ρ̂≈0.7, shrinking an effective sample of ~500 days to ~88 — enough to push a 0.01 p-value back toward noise once corrected.
⚠A failure doesn't kill the coefficient — it means fix the inference. Recompute with HAC/Newey-West or robust standard errors rather than discarding \(\hat\beta\).
⚠Passing all three is not out-of-sample evidence. Clean diagnostics only certify the in-sample significance claim is honestly computed — Lego Principle 5 still applies.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. Why does positive residual autocorrelation make a regression's p-value look better (more significant) than it actually is?
The classical standard-error formula assumes each residual contributes a fresh, independent piece of information, so it divides by the raw observation count \(n\) — more observations means a tighter standard error. When residuals are positively autocorrelated, consecutive observations are substantially restating the same information rather than each adding something new, so the true number of independent pieces of evidence (the effective sample size) is smaller than \(n\). The classical formula still computes as if it had \(n\) independent observations, so it reports a standard error that's too small for the amount of independent information actually present. A too-small standard error inflates the t-statistic and deflates the p-value, making the coefficient look more precisely estimated and more significant than the effective sample size justifies.
Q2. What does the Breusch-Pagan test actually do — what gets regressed on what, and what does a significant result mean?
It takes the residuals \(e_i\) from the original fitted regression, squares them, and runs a second ("auxiliary") regression of those squared residuals \(e_i^2\) on the same predictor(s) \(X_i\) used in the original model. If the true residual variance is constant (homoskedastic), \(X\) should have no systematic power to predict \(e_i^2\), so the auxiliary regression's \(R^2_{aux}\) should be small and the test statistic \(LM = n \cdot R^2_{aux}\) unremarkable against its \(\chi^2\) reference distribution. A significant result means \(X\) does systematically predict the squared residuals — the residual variance genuinely changes across the sample (heteroskedasticity), which means the single standard error the classical formula reports is wrong for at least part of the data, usually understating uncertainty where variance is actually largest.
Q3. Why does Jarque-Bera matter more in a small sample than a large one, even though the formula itself doesn't change?
The classical t-test and p-value for \(\hat\beta\) are exact under normally distributed errors, and are otherwise justified only asymptotically by the Central Limit Theorem — as sample size grows, the sampling distribution of \(\hat\beta\) (built from an average over many observations) approaches normality regardless of the residuals' own shape. With a large sample, that averaging smooths over non-normal residuals and inference stays approximately valid even if Jarque-Bera flags a departure from normality. With a small sample, the CLT hasn't had enough observations to do that smoothing, so a skewed or fat-tailed residual distribution translates more directly into a distorted, unreliable t-statistic and p-value — exactly the regime where a low p-value is most tempting to over-trust and least backed by the assumptions it needs.
Q4. A regression fails Durbin-Watson but passes Breusch-Pagan and Jarque-Bera cleanly. Is the coefficient's point estimate necessarily wrong?
No. The point estimate \(\hat\beta = \mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)/\mathrm{Var}(X)\) is derived purely from minimizing the sum of squared residuals and doesn't assume anything about the residuals' serial structure, variance pattern, or shape — it can be a perfectly reasonable estimate of the true slope even when one or more of the three diagnostics fails. What's unreliable is specifically the classical standard error, and by extension the t-statistic and p-value built on top of it — those assume no autocorrelation to be valid, and a Durbin-Watson failure means that assumption is broken. The fix is to recompute inference with an autocorrelation-robust method (Newey-West/HAC standard errors) rather than to discard the coefficient itself.
Q5. Why is a residuals-vs-fitted-value scatter plot a useful check even after running the Breusch-Pagan test formally?
Because a "fan" pattern in the residuals can come from two different underlying causes that Breusch-Pagan's p-value alone doesn't distinguish: genuine heteroskedasticity (the true noise variance really does change across the sample), or a misspecified functional form (the true relationship is nonlinear, and forcing a straight line through it produces residuals that are systematically larger in some regions simply because the line is wrong there, not because the noise is inherently more variable there). Both can trigger a significant Breusch-Pagan statistic. Looking at the actual scatter plot helps tell the two apart — a genuine variance change usually looks like uniform fanning around a flat zero line, while a misspecification problem often shows a curved or patterned mean residual, not just a widening spread — and the right fix differs (robust standard errors for the first, respecifying the model for the second).
Q6. In the FX yield-spread scenario, why doesn't a good Durbin-Watson-adjusted p-value estimate mean the original 0.01 p-value was fraudulent or the researcher did anything wrong by computing it?
The original 0.01 p-value was computed correctly according to the classical OLS formula — it's an honest answer to the wrong question, not a miscalculation. The classical formula answers "how significant would this coefficient be if the residuals were independent," and the regression's residuals simply weren't independent in this case, which is a fact about the data (a slow-moving yield-spread signal producing slow-moving pricing errors), not an error in arithmetic. Checking Durbin-Watson before trusting the p-value is exactly the step that catches this — it's the difference between quoting a number at face value and verifying the assumptions that number depends on actually hold, which is the entire point of running residual diagnostics before leaning on a regression's significance claim.
