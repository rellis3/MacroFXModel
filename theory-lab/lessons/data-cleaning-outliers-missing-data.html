Data Cleaning: Outliers, Bad Ticks & Missing Data

Fat-finger prints, stale quotes, halts, and gaps each need a different fix — and the single most common way to get the fix itself wrong is to clean using statistics computed over data that hadn't happened yet.

CONCEPT — explains real industry practice, not a claim about this repo's own data pipeline ~16 min read Prereqs: The Data Pipeline: Raw Feeds to Trading-Ready Data
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • Four concrete failure modes: bad/fat-finger prints (a single wildly-off tick), stale quotes (a feed that stops updating but doesn't disconnect), halts and gaps, and duplicate/out-of-order timestamps.
  • Standard detection: a rolling median/MAD-based outlier filter and a maximum-plausible-return-per-tick bound — both computed from data available up to that point, never the whole sample.
  • Missing-data handling is a genuine tradeoff, not a default: forward-fill (simple, but manufactures a fake flat/zero-return period), interpolation (smooths the gap, but invents data that never existed), and flagging-and-excluding (honest, but shrinks the usable sample) — each has a case where it's the wrong choice.
  • Mandatory pitfall: cleaning with whole-sample statistics ("remove anything >5σ from the full-sample mean") is itself a lookahead-bias bug, exactly like the feature-construction leaks in Feature Engineering, Leakage & Reproducibility — a cleaning step must only use information available up to that point in time.
  • Real-world episode: on 8 December 2005 a Mizuho trader sold 610,000 J-Com shares at ¥1 instead of 1 share at ¥610,000. The prints were accurate and the trades stood, at a cost of roughly ¥40 billion: an extreme print is not automatically a bad print. Whether this desk's own cleaning choices change its results is untested.
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

My data has a few crazy prints in it. Can't I just delete anything more than five standard deviations out?

That rule has two problems. The obvious one: the crazy prints inflate the standard deviation you are measuring them with. The hidden one is worse.

Go on.

If you compute that standard deviation over the whole sample, you are judging today's tick using data from next year. A live system, standing on that day, could never make the same call. It is lookahead bias, the same bug as a leaky feature.

So what should I use?

A rolling median and a rolling median absolute deviation, built only from ticks you have already seen. The median barely moves when one value goes wild, so the yardstick isn't fooled by the thing it is measuring.

Show me.

Four ticks: one point zero two, one point zero one, one point zero three, one point zero two. The median is one point zero two, and the typical deviation from it is half a hundredth. Then tick five arrives at four fifty, a keying error. That is three point four eight away, or about six hundred and ninety-six of those deviations. Even a loose threshold of ten flags it instantly.

And once it's flagged, I delete it.

Not so fast. Flagging and fixing are separate decisions. In December 2005, a trader at Mizuho meant to sell one J-Com share at six hundred and ten thousand yen. He sold six hundred and ten thousand shares at one yen.

That must have looked like garbage on the tape.

Every print was accurate. The trades stood, and the loss was roughly forty billion yen. A filter should flag that session. A cleaning rule that deleted it would erase trades that really happened.

What about gaps, when the feed goes quiet?

Three options, each wrong somewhere. Carrying the last price forward invents a calm period. Drawing a line across the gap invents prices that never traded, and peeks at the price after the gap. Marking it missing is honest, but shrinks your sample.

One line to remember?

Detect with robust statistics from the past only, then decide separately whether the print was an error or a real trade, and log every change you make.

Section 01

Why this matters

The Data Pipeline: Raw Feeds to Trading-Ready Data named cleaning as Stage 3 — the step that fixes what validation flagged. This lesson is that stage in full: the concrete failure modes a raw feed actually produces, the standard ways to detect them, and the real tradeoffs in how to handle what's missing once a bad or absent value has been identified. None of this is exotic — every one of these failure modes shows up routinely in real feeds — but each one has a specific wrong way to fix it that looks completely reasonable until it quietly corrupts everything built downstream.

💡
Plain-English intuition

A copy editor who can only read pages that already exist

Imagine a copy editor checking a manuscript for typos. A good editor reads sentence by sentence, flags a word that looks obviously wrong given everything read so far, and fixes it using only the context available up to that point. A bad editor reads the entire book first, memorizes every word choice the author eventually settles on in Chapter 20, and then goes back to "fix" a slightly unusual word in Chapter 2 based on that future knowledge — even though nobody reading the book in order would have had that context yet. The second editor isn't being more careful; they're quietly using information from the future to judge the past. Data cleaning has exactly this trap: a filter that decides whether today's tick is "outlier" or not by comparing it to statistics computed over the entire dataset, including tomorrow's data, is the second editor — technically thorough, but dishonest about what a live system standing at that moment could actually have known.

Section 02

Four concrete failure modes

1

Bad or fat-finger prints — a single wildly-off tick

A single trade or quote print at a price wildly inconsistent with everything around it — classically a manual order entry error (an extra zero, a decimal point in the wrong place), but it can also come from a feed-side transmission glitch that corrupts one message. The signature is distinctive: one isolated tick miles from its neighbors, immediately followed by the price snapping straight back to where it was before.

2

Stale quotes — a feed that stops updating but doesn't disconnect

A feed connection can remain technically "up" — no disconnect event, no error code — while the actual quotes stop refreshing, silently repeating the last known price or spread for minutes or longer. This is more dangerous than an outright disconnect precisely because nothing in the connection status signals a problem; a system consuming the feed sees a continuous stream of valid-looking messages that simply aren't reflecting the real market anymore.

3

Exchange halts and gaps

Trading genuinely stops — a circuit breaker, a scheduled market closure, a liquidity event — and then resumes, often at a meaningfully different price. Unlike a stale quote, a halt is a real market event with a real (if wide) price jump on either side of it; the cleaning task isn't to "fix" the gap as if it were an error, but to represent it honestly as a period with no valid price observations, distinct from either a bad tick or ordinary low activity.

4

Duplicate or out-of-order timestamps

Network retransmission, multiplexed feed sources, or clock-sync drift between an exchange's matching engine and a vendor's distribution layer can all produce the same tick appearing twice, or two ticks arriving in an order that doesn't match their true sequence timestamps. Left unhandled, a duplicate silently double-counts a move (inflating apparent volume or repeating a return), and an out-of-order pair can make a later validation or feature-construction step compute a "return" between two prices that were never actually adjacent in time.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Think of four different ways a phone call can go wrong. A fat-finger print is like someone shouting a completely wrong number once, then correcting themselves right away. A stale quote is like the line staying connected but the other person going silent while you keep hearing "still there, still there." A halt is like the call genuinely dropping for a real reason — no fake fixing needed, just an honest gap. And duplicate/out-of-order timestamps are like getting the same voicemail twice, or hearing the end of a sentence before the beginning. Each one needs its own kind of fix, not one universal patch.

Section 03

Standard detection approaches

Watch the rule build
A robust, only-past outlier flag, one piece at a time
\[ m_i = \color{#fbbf24}{\text{median}(x_1, \dots, x_{i-1})} \]

Start with a centre built only from ticks that have already arrived. The median, not the mean, so one wild value barely moves it.

\[ \text{MAD}_i = \color{#fbbf24}{\text{median}\big(\,|x_j - m_i|\,\big), \quad j < i} \]

Add a spread built the same way: the median of how far each past tick sits from that centre. It is the robust stand-in for a standard deviation.

\[ z_i = \color{#fbbf24}{\frac{|x_i - m_i|}{\text{MAD}_i}} \]

Measure the new tick in MADs: how many typical deviations it sits from the centre.

\[ \color{#10b981}{\text{flag } x_i \text{ if } z_i > k} \]

Flag anything beyond a threshold \(k\), such as 10. Every piece uses only data before \(i\), so a live system could make exactly the same call. Flagging is detection; what to do next is a separate decision.

Two conceptual tools do most of the work of catching the failure modes above, before any decision is made about how to handle what's been flagged.

Rolling median/MAD-based outlier filter

Rather than comparing a new tick to a mean and standard deviation (both of which are themselves distorted by the very outliers being hunted for), compute a rolling median and a rolling median absolute deviation (MAD) — the median of the absolute deviations from the median — using only data up to and including the current point. A new tick that falls many MADs from the rolling median is flagged as a candidate outlier. The median and MAD are robust statistics: unlike a mean/standard-deviation pair, a handful of extreme values barely moves them, which is exactly the property needed to detect extreme values without the detector itself being thrown off by them.

\[ \text{MAD} = \text{median}\big(\,|x_i - \text{median}(x)|\,\big) \]

Maximum-plausible-return-per-tick bound

Independently of any rolling statistic, set an absolute, instrument-specific bound on how large a single-tick-to-single-tick return can plausibly be, given that instrument's typical volatility and the minimum realistic time between two genuine consecutive prints. A tick implying a return outside that bound is flagged regardless of what the rolling statistics say — a useful second check because it catches an outlier even during a rolling window that has itself been contaminated by a recent run of bad prints.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine your class's average test score, then one kid scores a 1,000,000 by mistake. That single number wrecks the "average" — it drags it wildly upward, so the average itself stops being a useful yardstick for spotting weird scores. The median (the middle score when everyone's lined up in order) barely budges no matter how crazy that one number is, which is exactly why it's a much safer ruler to measure "how far off is this new value" against.

Section 04

Missing-data handling: three approaches, three real tradeoffs

Once a value has been flagged as bad or is genuinely absent (a halt, a dropped connection), a decision has to be made about what — if anything — fills the gap. None of the three standard approaches is universally correct; each is the wrong choice in a specific, concrete situation.

Forward-fill

Repeat the last known good value for as long as the gap lasts. Simple, and often defensible for a short gap in an illiquid instrument where "the price didn't move" is a reasonable assumption. The failure case: forward-filling across a real market event (a halt around a shock, a stale-quote period during a genuine news release) manufactures a flat, zero-return period that looks exactly like real, calm information to anything downstream — a volatility estimator or a trend feature built on top of it will read the manufactured flatness as an authentic quiet period, when in reality the feed simply stopped reporting.

Interpolation

Draw a line (linear or otherwise) between the last known good value before the gap and the first known good value after it, filling the gap with points along that line. This avoids the artificial flat-line problem and can be reasonable for a very short, clearly spurious single-tick gap — but note it uses the first value after the gap, which a live system would not yet have had, so in a backtest it is itself a small lookahead unless the fill is delayed until that value arrives. The failure case: interpolating across a longer or more consequential gap invents data that never existed — the interpolated path implies a smooth, gradual price move that may bear no resemblance to what actually happened during a halt, which in reality often resumes at a sharp, discontinuous level rather than anywhere close to the average of before-and-after.

Flagging and excluding

Mark the period as missing and exclude it from any calculation that would otherwise treat it as a real observation, rather than filling it with any value at all. This is the most honest option — it never manufactures data — but it shrinks the usable sample, and in a strategy or model that needs a specific bar count or a continuous window (a fixed-length rolling feature, a fixed-size backtest window), a run of excluded periods can force awkward downstream handling of its own. The failure case: excluding too liberally on a feed with routine short micro-gaps can throw away a meaningful fraction of otherwise-fine data for no real benefit.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

If a page is torn out of a diary, you have three honest choices: assume nothing happened that day and copy yesterday's entry (forward-fill), guess a smooth, plausible middle-ground entry that gently bridges yesterday to tomorrow (interpolation), or just admit the page is missing and skip it when telling the story (flagging and excluding). None of these recovers what actually happened — they're just different ways of being honest, or dishonest, about a gap you can't actually fill with the truth.

The trap: cleaning a series using statistics computed over the whole sample — "remove any point more than 5 standard deviations from the full-sample mean" — is itself a lookahead-bias bug, exactly the same failure mode Feature Engineering, Leakage & Reproducibility describes for a feature built from full-sample z-scores. A cleaning step is a computation like any other, and it has to obey the same rule: only use information available up to that point in time. A full-sample outlier filter uses knowledge of every future extreme value to decide whether today's tick "looks normal" — meaning a live system, cleaning its feed in real time as ticks arrive, could never reproduce the exact same cleaning decisions the backtest silently benefited from. This is genuinely the strongest overlap point between the two lessons: both are instances of the identical underlying discipline (never let time index \(i\)'s computation see data from \(> i\)) applied to two different stages of the same pipeline — cleaning the raw series here, versus building a feature from an already-clean series there.
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

It's like grading a student's answer on question 3 of a test by comparing it to the answer key for the whole test — including questions they haven't reached yet. That's cheating, even if the grader didn't mean to cheat. A live data-cleaning system, ticking along in real time, can only ever compare "right now" to what already happened — never to next week's data — so any cleaning rule that peeks at the future is a rule nobody running the system live could ever actually follow.

Worked example

Catching a fat-finger tick with rolling MAD, on six numbers

Predict first. Four ticks, 1.02, 1.01, 1.03 and 1.02, give a rolling median of 1.02 and a MAD of 0.005. Then a print arrives at 4.50. How many MADs from the median is it? Lock in a guess, then check it against the working below.

About 696. \(|4.50 - 1.02| = 3.48\), and \(3.48 / 0.005 = 696\). A threshold of 10 MADs flags it at once, using only ticks 1 to 4. The next question, whether to delete it, is a different one: see the J-Com story in section 05.

Take a short tick sequence for a spread: \(x = [1.02, 1.01, 1.03, 1.02, 4.50, 1.03]\) — position 5 is a spurious print (say, a keying error: 4.50 typed where 1.045 was meant). Checking position 5 using only positions 1-4:

Rolling median of \([1.02, 1.01, 1.03, 1.02]\) is \(1.02\). The absolute deviations from that median are \([0.00, 0.01, 0.01, 0.00]\), so the rolling MAD is \(0.005\) (the median of those four deviations). Position 5's value of \(4.50\) deviates from the rolling median by \(|4.50 - 1.02| = 3.48\) — roughly 696 MADs away. Even a generous threshold of, say, 10 MADs flags this instantly, using nothing but data from positions 1-4. A full-sample version of the same check would instead compute the median and MAD over all six values (including the spike itself, which distorts both), which is both circular — the outlier contaminates the very statistic used to detect it — and, more importantly for the lookahead point, would have required already knowing position 6's value to build the check applied at position 5.

🎯
A real-world trading scenario

The Tokyo-lunch fat-finger that a full-sample filter missed for two years

A research desk maintains a multi-year daily FX dataset and runs an annual cleaning pass: compute the full-sample mean and standard deviation of daily returns for each pair, flag anything beyond 5 standard deviations as a fat finger, and manually review the flagged days. A one-off fat-finger print during thin Tokyo-lunch liquidity in a minor cross sits at roughly 4.2 standard deviations under this full-sample check — just inside the threshold — and passes through uncaught for two years, quietly inflating that single day's realized volatility in every downstream calculation that touches it.

Rebuilding the same check as a rolling, only-past MAD filter (as this lesson recommends) flags the same day immediately: measured against the handful of days immediately preceding it — the only context a live system would have had on that actual date — the same print is enormously more extreme in local, contemporaneous terms than it is against two years of full-sample statistics, which include plenty of other volatile days elsewhere in the sample that quietly widen the full-sample standard deviation and mask this one. The full-sample check wasn't just less precise; it was structurally the wrong tool, because it could only ever have been run with two years of hindsight — not the way a live cleaning pipeline actually has to operate, one new tick at a time.

Section 05

It actually happened: the fat finger the tape was right about

Failure mode 1 above describes the classic fat-finger print: a manual entry error produces one wild tick, the price snaps back, and the cleaning job is to flag it and not let it poison anything downstream. The most famous fat finger in market history did not fit that picture, because the error was in the order, not in the data.

It actually happened

8 December 2005: 610,000 shares at ¥1

J-Com, a Japanese recruitment company, listed on the Tokyo Stock Exchange's Mothers market on 8 December 2005. A trader at Mizuho Securities meant to sell 1 share at ¥610,000. The order went in as 610,000 shares at ¥1. J-Com had only about 14,500 shares in issue, so the order offered more than forty times every share that existed. A warning was reported to have appeared and been overridden.

The price collapsed to the exchange's daily limit and the order kept filling. Mizuho tried to cancel it and could not: a flaw in the exchange's own trading system stopped the cancellation going through. Other firms and individual traders bought the shares, and the firm was left short far more stock than could ever be delivered. The trades stood; the settlement of shares that did not exist was resolved in cash. Mizuho's loss was roughly ¥40 billion. The exchange's president resigned within weeks, several securities firms that had profited later agreed under official pressure to give up those gains, and in 2009 a Tokyo court ordered the exchange to pay Mizuho about ¥10.7 billion for the failed cancellation.

For a data cleaner, the uncomfortable fact is this: every print on the J-Com tape that morning was accurate. An outlier filter would have been right to flag the session as extreme. A cleaning rule that then replaced those prints as "bad ticks" would have erased trades that really happened, losses that were really booked, and the profits other traders really made.

If you later tidied up the sales book by crossing out the "weird" $1 sales, the book would look normal, but it would be lying: those sales really happened and somebody really lost a lot of money.

Where the textbook version breaks

The fat-finger signature in this lesson assumes the mistake lives in the data: one isolated tick, miles from its neighbours, then a snap back. J-Com broke that assumption. The mistake lived in the order, the data recorded it faithfully, and the extreme session was a real market event closer to the lesson's "halt and gap" case than to a bad tick. An outlier filter answers "is this unusual?"; only the venue's trade record, including any trades it later cancels, answers "did this happen?" A plausibility bound on size (shares traded against shares in issue) would have caught the order before the fact; no price filter applied afterwards can tell you whether to delete it.

A fair test would re-run each validated study with the outlier filter on and off, and with forward-fill against flag-and-exclude for gaps, then report whether any verdict moves; and check every print the filter removes against the venue's record of cancelled trades, so that only prints the venue itself voided are dropped.
Section 06

How practitioners actually use this

Production data-cleaning pipelines run these checks as streaming, one-tick-at-a-time computations — or as batch code that replays exactly that only-past logic, never a whole-file pass that peeks ahead — so the exact same logic that cleans a live feed in real time is what generates the historical, "already cleaned" dataset used for research. Rolling-window outlier detectors, per-instrument plausible-return bounds, and explicit gap/halt flags are standard, well-understood tools; the real engineering discipline is less about inventing a cleverer filter and more about making sure the filter used to build historical research data is provably the same filter (same window, same only-past discipline) that would run on a live tick arriving right now.

📏

Robust, not mean-based

Median/MAD resist being distorted by the very outliers they're meant to catch — a mean/std-based filter can be fooled by its own targets.

🕰️

Only-past, always

Every rolling statistic used for cleaning must be recomputed fresh at each point using only prior data — the same discipline a feature needs.

🧩

Match the fix to the failure

A fat finger, a stale quote, and a genuine halt are different events and usually need different handling, not one blanket rule.

🗒️

Log what was changed

Every cleaning decision (flagged, filled how, or excluded) should be recorded — an unrecorded fix is indistinguishable from silent corruption later.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where cleaning quietly goes wrong

  • Using full-sample mean/standard deviation for outlier detection. The single most damaging version of this lesson's mandatory pitfall — it's both statistically fragile (a mean/std pair is distorted by the outliers it's hunting) and a lookahead-bias bug.
  • Forward-filling across a genuine market event. Manufactures a fake flat/zero-return period that a volatility or trend feature will read as real, calm information.
  • Interpolating across a long or consequential gap. Invents a smooth path through data that, in reality, likely resumed at a sharp discontinuity rather than anywhere near the interpolated average.
  • Excluding too liberally on a feed with routine short micro-gaps. Shrinks the usable sample for no real benefit if the gaps are genuinely inconsequential.
  • Confusing a halt with a bad tick. A halt is a real event with a real (if wide) price jump either side of it — it should be represented as missing, not "corrected" toward some assumed continuous path.
  • Treating a duplicate/out-of-order timestamp as a data quality footnote. Left unhandled it silently double-counts a move or computes a return between two prices that were never actually adjacent in time.
Scenario drill · 8 December 2005

Your filter lights up on a brand-new Tokyo listing

You run the cleaning pipeline for Japanese equities. J-Com listed on the Tokyo Stock Exchange's Mothers market this morning. A sell order for 610,000 shares at ¥1 has hit the market, with only about 14,500 shares in issue, and the price has collapsed to the daily limit. Your rolling MAD filter flags the whole session. What do you do with those prints?

  1. Delete them as fat-finger ticks and forward-fill the last sensible price.
    The error was in the order, not in the data. The prints record trades that happened. Deleting them, and forward-filling a calm price over a real crash, erases a real event and manufactures fake calm.
  2. Keep the flag, keep the prints, and check them against the exchange's record of which trades stood or were cancelled.
    Detection said "unusual"; only the venue's record says "did this happen". Keep what the venue kept, drop only what it voided, and log the decision.
  3. Exclude the whole session from the research sample.
    Honest, because it invents nothing. But it removes a real extreme event from the history your models learn from, and a strategy's behaviour on days like this is exactly what you need to see.
  4. Interpolate between yesterday's close and tomorrow's open across the session.
    It invents a smooth path through a session that was anything but, and it uses tomorrow's open, a value no live system would have had yet.

Section 07

Key Takeaways

✓Four distinct failure modes, four distinct signatures. Fat-finger prints, stale quotes, halts, and duplicate/out-of-order timestamps each need their own detection and handling — not one blanket rule.
✓Median/MAD is the robust default for outlier detection. It resists being distorted by the very extreme values it's meant to catch, unlike a mean/standard-deviation pair.
✗Cleaning with full-sample statistics is a lookahead-bias bug. The single sharpest, most easily missed mistake in this lesson — it must never happen, in cleaning any more than in feature construction.
⚠Every missing-data strategy has a real, concrete failure case. Forward-fill manufactures fake calm; interpolation invents data; flagging-and-excluding shrinks the sample. There is no universally-correct default.
✓Detection and handling are separate decisions. Flagging a bad value is a different question from deciding what, if anything, replaces it — conflating the two hides the tradeoff.
✓Log every cleaning decision. An unrecorded fix is indistinguishable from silent corruption once enough time has passed.
⚠An outlier is not the same as an error. J-Com's 2005 fat finger was a real, standing trade, recorded accurately. Flag extremes, but remove only what the venue says never happened, and log the decision.
Section 08

Self-test

Q1. Why is a stale quote often more dangerous to catch than an outright feed disconnect?
A disconnect is visible in the connection status itself — something downstream can check "is this feed even up right now" and get an honest answer. A stale quote leaves the connection technically live while the actual prices stop refreshing, so nothing in the connection metadata signals a problem; a consuming system sees a continuous stream of valid-looking messages that simply aren't reflecting the real market, and only a check on the content (is the price actually changing) rather than the connection status can catch it.
Q2. Why is median/MAD preferred over mean/standard deviation for outlier detection?
A mean and standard deviation are themselves heavily influenced by extreme values — a single huge outlier can drag the mean toward it and inflate the standard deviation, making the very outlier being hunted for look less extreme relative to a yardstick it has already distorted. The median and median absolute deviation are robust statistics: a handful of extreme values barely moves either one, so the detector stays sensitive to genuinely extreme points instead of being fooled by them.
Q3. Give a concrete case where forward-fill is the wrong missing-data strategy, and explain why.
Forward-filling across a genuine halt or a stale-quote period during a real news event manufactures a flat, zero-return stretch that looks exactly like a calm, information-free period to anything downstream. A volatility estimator or a trend-following feature built on top of that filled data will read the manufactured flatness as authentic market quiet, when in reality the feed simply stopped reporting during a period that may have been anything but calm — the fill has invented information (that nothing happened) rather than honestly representing an absence of information.
Q4. What exactly makes "remove anything more than 5 standard deviations from the full-sample mean" a lookahead-bias bug, and which other lesson's failure mode does it mirror?
Computing the mean and standard deviation over the entire sample uses every future data point, relative to whichever row is currently being checked, to decide whether that row looks normal or extreme. A live cleaning pipeline processing ticks one at a time as they arrive could never reproduce this — it has no way to know the full-sample statistics until the sample is complete, which by definition is after every row in it has already occurred. This is the identical failure mode Feature Engineering, Leakage & Reproducibility describes for a feature z-scored using full-sample mean/standard deviation instead of only-past statistics — the same underlying rule (never let time index \(i\)'s computation see data from \(> i\)) applied one stage earlier, to cleaning the raw series rather than to a feature built from it.
Q5. Why can a maximum-plausible-return-per-tick bound catch something a rolling MAD filter might miss?
A rolling MAD filter's own reference window can itself become contaminated — for example, by a cluster of several bad prints occurring close together, which drags the rolling median and MAD toward the contamination and makes a subsequent bad print look less extreme relative to an already-distorted local baseline. An absolute, instrument-specific plausible-return bound doesn't depend on any recent window at all, so it still flags an implausible move even during a stretch where the rolling statistics have temporarily been thrown off by recent bad data.

  • Christophe Leys, Christophe Ley, Olivier Klein, Philippe Bernard & Laurent Licata, "Detecting outliers: Do not use standard deviation around the mean, use absolute deviation around the median," Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, 49(4), 2013 — the standard, widely-cited case for median/MAD over mean/standard-deviation for robust outlier detection, applicable well beyond its original field.
  • Tim N. Falkenberry, "High Frequency Data Filtering," Tick Data Inc. technical paper, 2002 — a practitioner-oriented treatment of exactly the failure modes named in this lesson (bad ticks, out-of-sequence records, exchange-specific quirks) in real historical tick data.
  • Christian T. Brownlees & Giampiero M. Gallo, "Financial econometric analysis at ultra-high frequency: Data handling concerns," Computational Statistics & Data Analysis, 51(4), 2006 — an academic treatment of cleaning and filtering ultra-high-frequency data before any econometric modeling is applied to it.
