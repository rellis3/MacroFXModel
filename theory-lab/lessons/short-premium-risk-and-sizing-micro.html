Lesson 7 · Options in Practice

Short Premium, or Why the Broker's Margin Is Not Your Risk

Selling options pays small amounts often and loses large amounts rarely. This guide is about the large amounts: how to measure them before they arrive, and how to size a short-premium book so one bad night is survivable. Educational, not advice: sold options can lose far more than the premium collected.

The whole idea
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

I sell index options every month. I collect a credit, it expires worthless, and I do it again. My win rate is over ninety percent. What is the catch?

The catch is in the size of the losses, not how often they come. Take one strangle on an index at one hundred, forty-five days out. You sell a put at ninety-five and a call at one hundred and five, and you collect about two hundred dollars.

And the worst case?

Put the index through a grid. Move it up or down twenty percent, and jump volatility by up to thirty points. The worst square on that grid costs about sixteen hundred and fifty dollars. That is more than eight times the two hundred you collected. One bad night erases about eight cycles of income.

But my broker only asks for about seventeen hundred dollars of margin per strangle. Isn't margin the limit?

Margin is a collateral rule. It says what the broker wants posted today. It is not your worst case, and brokers can raise it in a crisis, which is when you can least afford it. Say you have a hundred thousand dollars and use half of it as margin. That buys twenty-nine strangles. In the worst square they lose almost half the account.

So how many should I sell?

Pick a loss you can survive in the worst square. Say two percent of the account, which is two thousand dollars. Divide by the worst case per contract. Sixteen fifty goes into two thousand once. One strangle.

One! Against twenty-nine?

And it earns about a twenty-ninth of the premium. That is the real price of survival, and nobody advertises it. Defined risk changes the arithmetic. An iron condor has long wings that cap the loss at about three hundred and fifty dollars, so the same budget buys five of them.

So defined risk means safe.

It means each contract has a ceiling. It says nothing about how many contracts you open. A hundred and forty-one condors at half margin lose about half the account just the same. Small loss per contract, huge number of contracts.

What about the Greeks? I thought risk reports use delta, gamma and vega.

They are a local map. A twenty percent fall with volatility up twenty points: the Greek estimate says about two thousand dollars lost on one strangle, and a full re-pricing says about thirteen hundred and fifty. Greeks are good warning lights. Put the limit on the full scenario loss.

And a book of ten different stocks? That is diversified.

On a calm day. In a crash, correlations jump. Ten positions with mild correlation behave like three independent bets. When the correlation goes to eighty percent, they behave like barely more than one.

Has any of this really happened?

The fifteenth of January, twenty fifteen. The Swiss central bank had held a floor under the euro against the franc for more than three years. Then it dropped it, with immediate effect. At one point the franc was up about thirty percent against the euro. Stop orders were filled far away from their levels, a London broker went into insolvency, and a big American broker needed a three hundred million dollar rescue.

But that was spot, not options.

Yes, and that is why it matters. Take a put struck four percent below the floor, sold for a thousand francs on a million euros. After the gap it owes a hundred and ten thousand francs. A hundred and ten times the premium. A promise from a central bank is not a cap on your payoff.

So the one-line version?

Size by what you can lose in the worst square, not by what the broker will lend you. And remember the grid is a floor on the damage, not a ceiling.

Section 1

Small wins, rare big loss

A short option is a small insurance policy written on a price. The premium is the credit. The storm is a big move, a jump in volatility, or both.

🚪

The toll booth

Most months the option expires worthless and you keep the credit. Win rate high, curve smooth. That is what the payoff looks like on almost every day.

💥

The lump

The gain is capped at the credit and the loss is not. One short strangle collects $198 and its worst square on the grid loses $1,651, about 8.4× the credit.

🎲

The win rate hides it

If every loser lost that much, you would need to win 89.3% of cycles just to break even. A 90% win rate can sit beside a losing strategy.

🏠

The insurer's lesson

A flood insurer fails in the storm year, not the average year. It sets how many policies to write by the worst storm, not by the cash a regulator allows.

Break-even win rate: p = L / (L + c). With L = $1,651 and c = $198, p = 89.3%.
Section 2

Defined risk and undefined risk

What each structure can lose at most, per share, with credit c and spread width w.

Undefined risk
  • Short put: loses K − c if the stock goes to zero. Large, but capped
  • Short call: unlimited, the price has no ceiling
  • Short strangle: put side large, call side unlimited
  • The worst case has to come from a grid of scenarios
Defined risk
  • Put credit spread: w − c. Here $4.33, or $433 a contract
  • Iron condor: widest wing minus total credit. Here $353 a contract
  • The cap is the worst case, whatever the market does
  • Caps the loss per contract, not the number of contracts
Defined risk limits the loss per contract. Only your contract count limits the total.

Section 4

Margin is a rule, not a bound

The strangle needs about $1,698 of margin on a common equity-option rule. Its worst square loses $1,651.

What margin answers
  • "What collateral must I post today?"
  • Set by the broker or clearing house, differs by product
  • Can be raised after a shock, when you can least afford it
  • Portfolio-style margin shocks spot a few percent and can be far lower
What it does not answer
  • "What can I lose in the worst square?"
  • Half the account as margin buys 29 strangles. They lose 47.9% of the account in the worst square
  • Portfolio-style margin lets the same account hold 220
Where this needs qualifying

Margin rules differ by broker and exchange and change over time. The 20% rule here is a common US equity-option rule of thumb, used for illustration. Check yours.

Section 5

Size by the worst case

Contracts = risk budget ÷ worst-case loss per contract, rounded down. Compare it with sizing by margin.

Try it: size a position two ways
Per contract: credit $198 · margin $1,698 · worst case $1,651
Sized by margin: 29 contracts, premium $5,730. Worst square loses $47,866 = 47.9% of equity
Sized by worst case: 1 contracts, premium $198. Worst square loses $1,651 = 1.7% of equity

The second sizing is smaller and earns less. That premium is the price of taking the worst square and still trading next month. Switch to the iron condor: its cap is small, but 50% margin still buys so many that the loss is about half the account.

Where the textbook version breaks

The estimate is close for small moves and wrong for large ones: here too gloomy by half at −20% and +20 points, because gamma and vega fade once an option is deep in the money. In other books the error runs the other way. Use Greeks as warning lights and put the limit on the full scenario loss.

Section 7

Gamma, assignment and gaps

⏳

Gamma grows near expiry

One at-the-money short straddle: gamma per share rises from 0.0566 at 45 days to 0.3811 at one day, about 6.7 times. Theta pays for a move of only about 1.05% a day. Bigger moves lose, and the loss grows with the square of the move.

📬

Early assignment

American options can be exercised any time. A deep in-the-money call is often exercised before ex-dividend when the dividend exceeds its time value. Example: a 90 call with 10 days left has about $0.08 of time value, against a $1.00 dividend.

📌

Pin risk

If the underlying finishes right on your strike you do not know whether you will be assigned, and a weekend can move the price before you find out. SPX options are European and cash-settled, so early assignment does not arise.

🕳

Gaps

A stop and a delta hedge need a path. A gap removes it. On 15 January 2015 EUR/CHF fell from about 1.20 to a low near 0.85 within minutes when the SNB dropped its minimum, before recovering to just above parity.

Ten positions, how many independent bets?

For N equal positions with average correlation ρ: N_eff = N / (1 + (N − 1) ρ). Drag ρ and watch the effective number of bets.

Section 9 · It actually happened

15 January 2015: a promise that capped nothing

It actually happened

In September 2011 the Swiss National Bank set a minimum of CHF 1.20 per euro and defended it by buying foreign currency. For over three years EUR/CHF could barely trade below it. On 15 January 2015 the bank discontinued the minimum "with immediate effect", stopped the purchases and cut the sight-deposit rate to −0.75%. At one point the franc was up about 30% against the euro, and later EUR/CHF was down about 13%, near 1.04. Stops and other resting orders were often filled far from their levels. Alpari UK, a London retail broker, entered insolvency, Global Brokers NZ could not resume business, and FXCM took a $300 million financing from Leucadia National on 16 January.

Where the textbook version breaks

A scenario grid assumes you can trade at model prices once the shock arrives, and Black-Scholes assumes the price moves continuously. A gap is a jump, a stop is filled at the next price, and a policy floor is an exposure to a decision, not a cap on the payoff. The real loss is larger than the grid says, so keep the budget small. For a short-volatility note such as XIV, which lost about 96% on 5 February 2018 and was terminated, the loss is the allocation times the move.

Section 11

Quick check

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

A strangle collects $198 and loses $1,651 in its worst square. About what win rate does it need to break even if every loss were that large?

p = L / (L + c) = 1,651 / 1,849 = 89.3%. Most losers are smaller, but the example shows why a win rate alone says little.
Question 2

Why is "use half the account as margin" a poor sizing rule for short strangles?

Margin answers what you must post today. At $1,698 each, half of $100,000 buys 29 strangles, which lose about $47,866 in the worst square. Brokers can raise margin after a shock.
Question 3

An iron condor can lose at most $353 a contract. Is a book of 141 of them safe?

Defined risk caps the loss per contract, not the number of contracts. Dividing a 2% budget of $2,000 by $353 gives 5 condors.
Question 4

For a 20% fall with volatility up 20 points, the Greek estimate is $2,056 and a full re-pricing is $1,347. What should a loss limit use?

Greeks are a local expansion. Gamma and vega change as the option moves, so for big moves the estimate can be off by half, or in the other direction in other books. Re-price each scenario in full.
Question 5

EUR/CHF is at 1.20 behind a central-bank floor. You sell a put at 1.15 on EUR 1,000,000 for CHF 1,000, and the floor is dropped with EUR/CHF at 1.04. What do you owe?

1,000,000 × (1.15 − 1.04) = 110,000. A policy floor is an exposure to a decision, not a cap on the payoff, and a stop is filled at the next price after a gap.
Your call
Scenario drill · Wednesday 14 January 2015, evening

A floor has held for three years, and it pays to sell what is under it

EUR/CHF has traded at or above 1.20 since the Swiss National Bank set that minimum in September 2011, and the bank has defended it by buying foreign currency. You run a small FX account. You sell one-month EUR/CHF puts struck below 1.20, which pay little but almost always expire worthless, and you also hold a leveraged long EUR/CHF position. Your broker lets you use most of the account as margin. Tonight a colleague says the floor cannot be broken because the bank has promised to defend it. What do you do with the size?

  1. Hold the size. The central bank has promised to defend the floor, so the downside is limited.
    A promise is an exposure to a decision, not a cap on the payoff. The floor was a policy, and policies can end. The puts and the long position are both short that decision, and a model with a floor in it will never price a world where the floor is gone.
  2. Sell more puts. They pay so little that you need more of them to make the income worthwhile.
    Low premium on a large notional is leverage on the one scenario you cannot price. The worst loss grows with every contract, and the premium barely moves. Chasing a premium target by adding size is how a quiet position becomes the largest risk in the account.
  3. Put the floor-removed scenario into the grid: a fall of 15% to 30% in minutes, no fills in between. Cut the notional until that loss fits a small budget, and treat the long position as having no stop.
    This is the sizing rule applied to a can't-happen row. Divide the budget by the loss in the worst cell and round down. You give up income on the quiet days and keep the account on the day the market skips the stop.
  4. Keep the size but place stop-loss orders just below 1.20 on the long position.
    A stop is an order, not a guarantee. Once triggered it is filled at the next available price, and in a gap that can be far below the stop. It helps in an orderly decline, but it does not answer the sizing question for a jump, and it does nothing for the short puts.

What happened. On Thursday 15 January 2015 the SNB discontinued the minimum rate "with immediate effect", stopped its foreign-currency purchases and cut the rate on sight deposits to −0.75%. At one point on the day the franc was up about 30% against the euro, and EUR/CHF was later down about 13%, near 1.04. Alpari UK, a London retail broker, entered insolvency, Global Brokers NZ said it could not resume business, and FXCM took a $300 million financing from Leucadia National on 16 January. This drill is a simplified position built on documented events, not anyone's actual account.

Section 12

What to carry forward

01

A short option has a gain capped at the credit and a loss several times larger. The strangle's worst square is about 8.4× its credit, so a high win rate says little.

02

Defined risk caps the loss per contract at width minus credit. It does not cap how many contracts you open.

03

Margin is a collateral rule that can change. It is not your worst case.

04

Size by the worst square: contracts = budget ÷ worst-case loss per contract, rounded down. A 2% budget on $100,000 is one strangle or five iron condors.

05

Greeks are a local map and fail for big moves. Gamma rises about 6.7 times between 45 days and one day, and assignment and gaps skip your stops.

06

Diversification fades in a crash, and a promise is not a cap. On 15 January 2015 the franc was up about 30% against the euro at one point and stops skipped. Size so one bad night is survivable.

What this now lets you do

Re-price any short-premium position on a grid of spot and volatility shocks, read off the worst square, and turn a loss budget into a number of contracts. Tell margin from risk, defined risk from small risk, and a calm sample from a safe one.
