Five Ways to Trade Options, and How Each One Fails

Buying direction, selling premium, trading volatility, hedging and relative value: what each one is really a bet on, how often it wins, and the way it usually goes wrong.

CONCEPT — a map of the styles and their maths, not a recommendation ~18 min read Prereqs: The Four Option Positions
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
Risk: this is education, not advice. An option you buy can lose the whole premium. An option you sell can lose far more than you collected. Every number here comes from stated assumptions, not from a forecast.
TL;DR
  • People use options in five different ways: buying direction, selling premium, trading volatility, hedging and relative value. They share a contract but not a bet. Each has its own win rate, its own payoff shape and its own way of failing.
  • Win rate and payoff shape are one fact seen twice. Take a stock at 100 with 30-day implied volatility of 20%. The 105 call wins 15.8% of the time and, when it wins, pays 4.5 times its average loss. The 95 put wins 82.9% of the time and, when it loses, loses 6.1 times its average win. At fair prices both average zero before costs.
  • Averaging zero means edge is the whole game. Your edge is what the position is worth on your forecast, minus what you pay, minus costs: \(\text{edge} = V_{you} - P - c\). In the style lab, costs of 10 cents a leg make every style average a loss when implied and realised volatility match.
  • How each one fails: buyers need size and timing as well as direction, and bleed time value. Sellers hide one large loss behind many small wins. Volatility traders are right and still lose because the move was priced in. Hedgers pay for protection and drop it just before they need it. Relative-value traders watch a spread widen before it closes.
  • Evidence: studies of retail option traders in the Netherlands (Bauer, Cosemans and Eichholtz, 2009) and the United States (Bryzgalova, Pavlova and Sikorskaya, 2023) both find that they lose money on average. The first points to timing errors and trading costs. The second reports an average bid-ask spread of 12.6% on the weekly options retail traders prefer.
  • Real-world episode: by the end of 2008 Berkshire Hathaway had sold $37.1 billion of 15 and 20 year equity index puts and collected $4.9 billion. It marked them at a $10 billion liability, a $5.1 billion paper loss, and survived because the contracts could not be settled early and almost none needed collateral. A retail seller has neither protection.

People keep telling me options are dangerous. But they are just contracts. Where does the danger actually come from?

From how you use them. There are five common ways. You can buy for direction, sell premium, trade volatility, hedge, or trade one option against another. Same contract, five different bets.

Take the first two. A stock is at one hundred, and the market expects a normal swing of about six percent over the next month.

Buying a call struck at one hundred and five costs about sixty-four cents. It only pays if the stock finishes above one hundred and five, plus the premium. That happens about sixteen times in a hundred. When it does, the average win is about three dollars twenty-six, roughly four and a half times the average loss.

A low win rate, with big wins. And selling?

Sell a put struck at ninety-five and you collect fifty-seven cents. You keep it about eighty-three times in a hundred. But the average win is forty-six cents and the average loss is two dollars eighty-two. The loss is six times the win.

So the seller looks like a genius for months.

Until the month that matters. And here is the key point. If volatility is priced fairly, both traders average the same result before costs, which is zero. A high win rate and a low win rate are two ways of slicing the same average.

Then how does anyone make money?

Only by holding a view that differs from the price, and being right. A seller needs implied volatility to run above what is later realised. A buyer needs a bigger move than the price assumes. Without that, costs turn it into a small, steady loss.

Do costs really matter that much?

Researchers who studied retail option traders, in the Netherlands and in the United States, found they lose money on average. The American study found they favour cheap weekly options, with an average bid-ask spread of twelve point six percent. Paying that on every trade is a heavy toll.

What about the other three styles?

Volatility traders bet on how far the price travels, not which way, and fail when the move is already in the price. Hedgers pay to cap a risk they already hold, so their option usually expires unused. That is the point. Relative-value traders bet that two options are mispriced against each other, and fail when the gap widens before it closes.

Has anyone sold insurance at scale and survived?

Berkshire Hathaway sold about thirty-seven billion dollars of long-dated index puts and collected four point nine billion. At the end of two thousand and eight it valued them as a ten billion dollar liability, a five point one billion dollar paper loss. But the contracts could only be settled at the end, and almost none needed collateral, so nobody could force it to sell.

So a style can survive, with the right funding.

Right. Before you trade any of the five, finish the sentence: I get paid because. Then know exactly how you lose, and whether you can hold through it.

Section 01

Why this matters

"I trade options" describes almost nothing. One person is paying a few pounds for a lottery ticket on a takeover. Another is a dealer quoting both sides of a thousand strikes and hedging the result. A third is a company protecting next year's euro receipts. They hold the same four positions you met in The Four Option Positions, and they are making completely different bets.

The difference matters because the style decides how you lose. A buyer loses slowly and often. A seller loses rarely and heavily. A hedger "loses" the premium on purpose. If you hear that someone made money from options, or lost everything, it tells you little until you know which style they ran, how big they ran it, and who funded the risk.

This lesson is the practical layer above the contract and the Greeks. For each style it asks four questions: what are you really betting on, how often does it win and by how much, what edge would you need, and how does it typically fail. Later in this series the same ground is covered in more detail: contract mechanics, spreads and structures, trade selection, the variance risk premium, delta hedging, the risk of short premium and hedging with options.

💡
Plain-English intuition

An insurance shop, and which side of the counter you stand on

Picture an insurance shop. Customers buy cover and the shop sells it. The price list is set so that, if the shop's guess about the risk is right, neither side makes money on average. Everyone who walks in is standing on one side of the counter for a reason.

The customer who wants to win big on a rare event is buying a lottery ticket (style 1). The customer who owns a house and wants cover is hedging (style 4). The shop itself is selling premium (style 2). A customer who thinks the weather will be wilder than the shop assumes is trading volatility (style 3). Someone who buys cover at one shop and sells a near-identical policy at another is trading relative value (style 5). The prices are fair on average, so the only ways to win are to know something the price does not, to be paid for carrying a risk you can really carry, or to need the cover. The rest of the lesson makes that precise.

The five bets

The figures below come from the style lab in Section 02: a stock at 100, 30 days to expiry, implied volatility 20%, costs of 10 cents per option leg, and realised volatility equal to implied. Win rates and averages include those costs.

🎯

1. Buy direction

Betting on: direction, size and timing, all at once. A long 105 call needs the price above the strike plus the premium before it runs out.

Shape: it wins 15.8% of the time, and the average win is 4.5 times the average loss.

Edge needed: a forecast that a large move is likelier than the option price assumes.

Typical failure: right about direction, wrong about size or time. Event buyers also lose when implied volatility drops after the news.

🧾

2. Sell premium

Betting on: a quieter market than the price implies. You are the insurer.

Shape: the 95 put wins 82.9% of the time. The average win is $0.46 and the average loss is $2.82, so one loss is worth 6.1 wins.

Edge needed: implied volatility that runs above what is realised by more than the tail losses and costs, plus a size you can survive.

Typical failure: one loss undoes many wins, and a long winning run invites bigger size.

🌊

3. Trade volatility

Betting on: how far the price travels, not which way. A long straddle profits from a big move either way.

Shape: the unhedged 100 straddle wins 40.5% of the time, with an average win of $3.21 against an average loss of $2.52. Professionals usually delta-hedge so that direction drops out.

Edge needed: a better forecast of realised volatility than the market's implied number.

Typical failure: the move comes but is already priced in. Long volatility also pays time decay every day.

🛡️

4. Hedge

Betting on: nothing. You already own a risk and pay to cap it. Success is a bad outcome that hurt less.

Shape: the option usually expires unused, so win rate is the wrong measure. Own 100 shares plus the 95 put and your worst case is a loss of $5.67 a share, for a premium of $0.57.

Edge needed: not a forecast but a reason: a mandate, a limit, a budget or a business exposure, and a fair price.

Typical failure: hedging the wrong exposure, buying protection after the shock at peak prices, or dropping it after a long bleed.

⚖️

5. Relative value

Betting on: one option being rich or cheap against another, with direction mostly hedged out: term structure, skew, strike against strike, index against members.

Shape: selling the 30-day and buying the 90-day call at 100 wins 42.7% of the time. The best case is $1.36 and the worst case is $1.87.

Edge needed: a view of fair value, tight execution on every leg and the funding to wait.

Typical failure: the gap widens before it closes, the hedge is imperfect, liquidity vanishes in stress. See the convergence trades in The Kelly Criterion & Position Sizing.

🔀

Real positions blend them

A covered call is a hedge-like income trade and also sells premium. A collar sells upside to pay for downside cover. A calendar mixes volatility and relative value. Name the main bet, then name the second bet you did not mean to take. Spreads and structures are built later in this series.

Section 02

The math: win rate, payoff shape and edge

Watch the formula build
Where "edge = what it is worth to you, minus what you pay, minus costs" comes from
\[ \text{average result} = \color{#fbbf24}{p\,W - (1-p)\,L} \]

Every trade has a win rate \(p\), an average win \(W\) and an average loss \(L\) (a positive number). Weight each by how often it happens. This is the average result of a trade, before costs.

\[ \text{edge} = p\,W - (1-p)\,L \;\color{#fbbf24}{-\; c} \]

Take off costs: the bid-ask spread you cross and the commission, on every option leg, in and out. Costs are paid whether you win or lose.

\[ \text{edge} = \color{#fbbf24}{V_{you} - P} - c \]

The same number from the pricing side. \(V_{you}\) is what the position is worth at the end on average, if your forecast of drift and volatility is right. \(P\) is what you pay to open it (negative if you collect a credit). Win rate and payoff shape are just the two halves of \(V_{you} - P\).

\[ V_{you} = P \;\Rightarrow\; \text{edge} = \color{#fbbf24}{-c} \]

If your forecast matches the market's, every style averages minus its costs. The styles still differ a lot in how it feels: a buyer loses small amounts often, a seller wins small amounts often and loses a lot sometimes.

\[ \text{edge} \approx \color{#fbbf24}{\nu\,(\sigma_{you} - \sigma_{mkt})} - c \]

If the only difference is volatility, the gap in value is vega times the gap in volatility. Vega \(\nu\) is the value change per volatility point, with a plus sign if you are long options and a minus sign if you are short.

\[ p^{*} = \color{#10b981}{\frac{L + c}{W + L}} \]

Set the edge to zero and solve for the win rate that just pays for itself, given a payoff shape. A seller with a big average loss needs a win rate near 100%. A buyer with big average wins can survive a win rate of 20%.

Win rate and shape are one number. When the market prices an option fairly, its average payoff equals its price, so \(p\,W = (1-p)\,L\) before costs. A trade that wins nine times in ten must lose about nine times as much when it loses. A trade that wins one time in five must win about four times as much when it wins. Quoting a win rate without the average win and the average loss is quoting half a fact.

\[ \text{edge} = p\,W - (1-p)\,L - c = V_{you} - P - c \]

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine a game where the shop sets the prices so nobody wins on average. You can pick a game that pays a little almost every time and sometimes takes a lot, or one that pays a lot rarely and takes a little most times. Both games are fair, so on average you walk out with nothing, minus the small fee at the door. The only way to win is to know something the shop does not.

The trap: a high win rate is not an edge. Selling premium wins most of the time by construction, because the price you collect is set so that the rare large loss pays for it. A seller who looks only at the win rate sees skill that is really just the shape of the bet.

Worked example

The same fair month, a buyer and a seller

A stock is at 100, implied volatility is 20%, and the option expires in 30 days. One implied expected move is \(100 \times 0.20 \times \sqrt{30/365} = 5.73\). Costs are 10 cents per leg. Realised volatility turns out to be exactly 20%.

The buyer pays $0.64 for the 105 call. Before costs it wins 16.2% of the time, the average win is $3.28 and the average loss is $0.63:

\[ p\,W - (1-p)\,L = 0.162 \times 3.28 - 0.838 \times 0.63 \approx 0.00 \]
0.162win rate before costs
$3.28average win
$0.63average loss

Fair price, so zero. Now take off the 10 cent cost: the edge is \(0.00 - 0.10 = -0.10\) per share. With the cost included, the win rate falls to 15.8% and the average loss rises to $0.73, but the average result is still \(-\$0.10\).

The seller of the 95 put collects $0.57. Before costs it wins 83.4% of the time, the average win is $0.56 and the average loss is $2.81:

\[ 0.834 \times 0.56 - 0.166 \times 2.81 \approx 0.00 \]

The same zero, wearing a completely different face: a win rate five times higher, an average win more than five times smaller, and an average loss more than four times bigger than the buyer's. After the same 10 cents the seller's edge is also \(-0.10\).

Now give someone an edge. If realised volatility is 25% while the options were priced at 20%, the straddle's value rises by about \(\nu \times 5\) points. The straddle's vega is \(\nu = $0.2287\) per volatility point, so \(0.2287 \times 5 = 1.14\), which matches the full repricing, $1.143. After the 20 cents of costs on its two legs, the straddle buyer averages +$0.94 a share, the call buyer +$0.34 and the put seller −$0.50. At 15% realised volatility the picture flips: the put seller averages +$0.23, the call buyer −$0.46 and the straddle buyer −$1.34. Volatility, not the style, decides who gets paid.

Real-world trading scenario

Same view, different style, different result

You think a stock at 100 will rise to about 108 over the next month. The 30-day options are priced at 20% implied volatility, costs are 10 cents a leg, and you hold them to expiry. Two styles that both "express bullishness":

Stock at expiryLong 105 callShort 95 put
108 (your view)+$2.26+$0.47
103 (up 3%)−$0.74+$0.47
90 (view wrong)−$0.74−$4.53

If the view is exactly right, the call turns a $0.64 premium into a profit of $2.26 after costs, and the put earns only its credit however far the stock rises. If the stock rises 3%, you were right about direction and the call still loses its whole premium. If you were wrong, the call loses $0.74 and the put loses $4.53. A view about direction does not choose the style. What you want to be paid for, how big a move you expect and how much you can afford to lose does.

Section 03

It actually happened: Berkshire Hathaway's index puts, 2008

The trap above says a seller's win rate hides a large tail. The cleanest large-scale example of someone selling that tail, and of why the same style can survive when the funding is different, is in Berkshire Hathaway's 2008 shareholder letter.

It actually happened

Selling long-dated index puts, with no margin calls

Berkshire Hathaway sold put options on four stock indices: the S&P 500, the FTSE 100, the Euro Stoxx 50 and the Nikkei 225. In his letter for 2008 Warren Buffett reports contracts that expire after 15 or 20 years, with the first coming due on 9 September 2019 and the last on 24 January 2028. The contracts pay out only if the index is below its starting level on the final day. The total the company could be asked to pay, if every index went to zero, was $37.1 billion. Berkshire had been paid $4.9 billion up front for taking that risk.

The letter says that neither side can settle early: "it's only the price on the final day that counts." It also says that only a small percentage of Berkshire's derivative contracts required it to post collateral when markets moved against it, and that even in the chaotic fourth quarter of 2008 it had to post less than 1% of its securities portfolio. At the end of 2008 Berkshire valued the puts at a $10 billion liability using Black-Scholes, against the $4.9 billion received. That is a reported mark-to-market loss of $5.1 billion, with nothing yet paid. Buffett's own example: if all four indices fell 25% from their starting levels, the company would owe about $9 billion, spread between 2019 and 2028.

This is style 2 at industrial scale: take a premium now, carry a large tail payment later. The 2008 marks show what it looks like in a crisis, deeply under water on paper. What made it survivable was not skill in picking the month. It was the structure: a fixed final settlement date, almost no collateral calls, and 15 to 20 years in which to be wrong.

⚠️
Where the textbook version breaks

"Sell premium and wait" assumes you are allowed to wait

The maths of style 2 assumes you keep the position to the end. A broker's margin system does not. It reprices your account every day and can close you out when your equity falls below its requirement, which turns an unrealised loss into a realised one. Berkshire's contracts were arranged so that this could not happen. Yours almost certainly were not. The same logic applies to every style: the maths gives you the average, and funding decides whether you are still there to collect it.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Berkshire sold a lot of very long-term insurance and took the money first. In 2008 the books said it would probably owe a lot, but it did not have to pay for years, and nobody could make it hand over cash early. A small trader who sells the same sort of insurance can be told to pay up at once when prices fall, so the same game can be safe for a giant and dangerous for you.

Section 04

How practitioners actually use this

Who tends to use which

These are tendencies, not rules. Most participants use more than one style.

🙋

Retail traders

Mostly style 1 (buying calls and puts) and style 2 (covered calls, cash-secured puts, simple credit spreads). The US data in Bryzgalova, Pavlova and Sikorskaya show retail preferring cheap, short-dated weekly options.

🏦

Dealers and market makers

They quote both sides and earn the spread, then hedge the delta and vega they are left with. Their book is the other side of everyone else's trade, which is why dealer hedging shows up in Gamma Exposure & Dealer Hedging Flows. Relative value is a large part of how they manage the book.

📈

Funds

Volatility and relative-value funds run styles 3 and 5, usually delta-hedged and sized by a risk model. Macro and equity funds also buy options for convex payoffs (style 1) and sell them for income (style 2).

🏭

Corporates, asset managers, insurers

Mostly style 4. A company hedges a currency or commodity exposure; a fund buys puts or collars on holdings. Asset managers also sell calls against holdings for income, which blends styles 2 and 4. See FX Options Quoting for how currency options are quoted.

What the retail data say

Two peer-reviewed studies are the most cited evidence on how individual traders fare. Neither says that every retail trader loses, or that nobody can make money from options. Both describe averages in particular samples.

  • Bauer, Cosemans and Eichholtz (2009), Journal of Banking & Finance, studied individual investors' option trading. Most investors lost substantially on their option positions, losses much larger than on their equity trading. The authors attribute this to poor timing, driven by overreaction to past stock returns, with high trading costs adding to it. Gambling and entertainment appeared to matter far more than hedging as a motive, and performance persisted, so past losers tended to keep losing.
  • Bryzgalova, Pavlova and Sikorskaya (2023), Journal of Finance, studied retail options trading in the United States after the rise of payment for order flow. Retail investors favoured cheaper, weekly options, with an average bid-ask spread of 12.6%, and lost money on average.

For scale: a 12.6% spread on the lab's $0.64 call is about 8 cents wide. Crossing it in and out costs far more than the 10 cents per leg the lab assumes, and it is paid on every trade.

Common pitfalls

Where each style quietly goes wrong

Reading a win rate as skill. A seller's high win rate comes from the shape of the bet, and a long run of wins is exactly what the shape predicts. It says nothing about whether the tail is paid for.

Choosing the style from the view, not from the price. "I am bullish, so I buy a call" skips the questions that matter: how big a move, by when, and what the market already assumes. In the scenario above the right direction still lost money.

Mistaking defined risk for small risk. A spread that caps the loss at $500 can still lose $500 on every trade you take. Defined risk limits the worst case. It does not make a bad edge good.

Ignoring costs. Costs are certain and an edge is not. With a spread of a few percent on the option price and a few legs per trade, costs can exceed a small edge.

Style drift. A buyer who starts selling "to fund the buying", or a hedger who starts adding to the hedge when it feels right, has changed styles without changing risk limits. Write down which bet you are making before the trade and check afterwards that it is the bet you actually took.

Sizing from margin. The broker's margin requirement is a funding number, not a risk number. Short options can lose several times the margin held against them. Size by the worst case you can name.

Scenario drill · an illustrative earnings week

Everyone expects a big move. Which side do you take?

This is a hypothetical situation, not a real company, with round numbers chosen to keep the arithmetic clear. A stock is at $100 and reports results tomorrow after the close. The nearest at-the-money straddle costs $6.40, so the market is pricing a move of roughly 6% either way. You also expect a big move, perhaps 6%. You have no view on direction, and costs are $0.10 per option leg. What do you do?

  1. Buy the straddle. A big move is coming.
    The price already contains your forecast. If the move is 6% as priced, the straddle returns about what it cost before costs, and the 20 cents of costs on two legs makes the average result negative. You need the move to be larger than 6.4% plus costs, and you have no reason to think it will be.
  2. Sell the straddle. The price is high, so collect it.
    A price that matches a fair forecast is not high. If the move is 6% you roughly break even before costs. A single 12% move erases several quiet quarters of credit, and you have no reason to think the move will be smaller than priced.
  3. Pass, unless you can say why you expect a move bigger or smaller than 6.4%.
    Edge is your forecast against the price. Where the two agree there is none, and costs make the average trade a loss on either side. A trade is worth considering only when you have a reason for a different number, and then you size it for the case where you are wrong.
  4. Buy a small straddle for the fun of it, sized so that losing the premium is trivial.
    Defensible as a bet you treat as entertainment, with the loss capped at an amount you do not mind. It is not an edge: the expected result is minus the costs. The research on retail option traders found entertainment was a bigger motive than hedging, and it found large average losses.

This is a hypothetical, so there is no outcome to report. The arithmetic is the point. If the typical move really is the priced 6.4%, a straddle buyer's average result is zero before costs and −$0.20 after them, and the seller's is also −$0.20. Both sides lose the toll to the market maker. Someone can only do better than that with a forecast that differs from the price and is right.

Section 05

Key Takeaways

✓There are five styles, and each is a different bet. Buy direction, sell premium, trade volatility, hedge and relative value share contracts but not risks. Name which one you are making before you trade.
✓Win rate and payoff shape are one fact. At fair prices \(pW = (1-p)L\). A high win rate means a large average loss, and a low win rate means a large average win.
✓Edge is your forecast against the price, minus costs. \(\text{edge} = V_{you} - P - c\). If you cannot say where your forecast differs from the price, you have no edge, and every style averages minus its costs.
✓For a volatility view, edge is vega times the gap in volatility. Realised above implied pays long options and costs short ones, whichever style you called it.
✓A hedge is judged by the floor it buys. Its option usually expires unused, so win rate is the wrong measure and the price relative to the protection is the right one.
⚠The same style can be safe or fatal depending on funding. Berkshire's index puts could not be settled early and almost none needed collateral. A retail seller faces daily margin and can be closed out at the worst moment.
⚠Direction alone does not choose the style. A right view on direction still lost money on the long call when the move was smaller than the break-even. Size, timing and what you want to be paid for decide.
✗The average retail option trader has lost money. Two peer-reviewed studies, in the Netherlands and the United States, find large average losses, with timing and costs among the reasons. Treat a high win rate and a tight spread as things to check, not to assume.
Section 06

Self-test

Q1. A short put wins 83% of the time and still averages a loss. How?
Its average win is small and its average loss is large. In the lab the 95 put wins $0.46 on average and loses $2.82 on average, so \(0.829 \times 0.46 - 0.171 \times 2.82 = -0.10\) after costs. At a fair price the win rate and the loss-to-win ratio are two sides of one fact, \(pW = (1-p)L\), and the 10 cent cost pushes the result below zero. A high win rate is not an edge.
Q2. The long 105 call wins only 16% of the time. Does that make it a bad trade?
No. A low win rate is the other half of a high payoff ratio: the average win is about 4.5 times the average loss. Whether it is a good trade depends on edge, not win rate: whether your forecast of a large move is better than the price and large enough to beat costs and time decay. With implied equal to realised, it averages minus the cost like every other style.
Q3. If implied volatility equals realised volatility, what does each style average before costs, and what do costs do?
Zero. When an option is priced at the volatility that is later realised, its average payoff equals its price, so every style averages zero before costs. Costs are then subtracted on every leg of every trade, so every style averages minus its costs: 10 cents a leg in the lab, which makes the straddle and calendar (two legs) average −$0.20 and the single-leg styles −$0.10.
Q4. Why is win rate the wrong way to judge a hedge?
A hedge is bought to cap a risk you already hold, so in most months the option expires unused and the premium is "lost" by design. The measures that matter are the worst case it buys and what the protection costs. In the lab, 100 shares plus the 95 put cap the loss at $5.67 a share for a premium of $0.57. A hedge that never paid out can still have been good value.
Q5. What made Berkshire's short index puts survivable, and what does a retail seller lack?
The contracts expired in 15 and 20 years, neither side could settle early, and only a small percentage of its derivative contracts required collateral. Even with a $5.1 billion paper loss at the end of 2008, nobody could force it to close. A retail seller faces daily margin, can be closed out by the broker as the price falls, and so may turn an unrealised loss into a realised one before expiry.
Q6. A calendar spread sells a 30-day call and buys a 90-day call at the same strike. What is it a bet on, and how does it fail?
It is a relative-value and volatility bet: that the near option is rich compared with the far one, so a quiet month earns the difference. Profit is capped near the strike. It fails when the price moves far from the strike (the lab's worst case is the whole debit, $1.87 after costs), or when the far option's volatility falls more than the near one's. It also needs tight spreads on both legs, because each leg pays costs.
