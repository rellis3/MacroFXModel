Bipower Variation, Splitting Variance Into Two Stories

Realized variance adds up every squared intraday return and calls the total "today's volatility." It has no way to say how much of that total was ordinary diffusion versus one violent, discrete jump — a single scheduled-news spike and forty quiet ticks that happen to sum to the same number look identical to it. Bipower variation asks a sharper question of the exact same data and splits the number back into its two pieces.

Section 1

One number, two very different things added together

Σ

RV sums squared returns

Realized variance, RV = Σr², converges to the price path's true quadratic variation as sampling gets finer — a real, useful result, and the building block behind HAR-RV.

⚡

Quadratic variation has two sources

Ordinary continuous diffusion, running all the time — and discrete jumps, firing rarely and violently. Both contribute squared returns to the same RV total.

🙈

RV can't tell them apart

One 40-pip, one-minute NFP spike and forty ordinary 1-pip wiggles that happen to sum to the same total sum-of-squares are, to plain RV, the exact same number.

💰

Why the split matters

Continuous variance is the risk you size a position around holding through ordinary noise. Jump variance is a genuinely different risk — the "event vol" desks price separately (see the Merton jump-diffusion lesson).

Imagine measuring a hiker's day only by total elevation gain. One hiker climbed a long, steady, moderate slope; another mostly walked flat ground but fell into one deep, sudden ravine. Same total number, completely different day. RV is that total-elevation number for a return series — and bipower variation is the trick that tells the two stories apart using only the same data RV already has.

Same mechanism throughout: RV treats a jump quadratically (r² of the jump itself); BV treats it linearly (the jump times an ordinary neighbor). That asymmetry — not a fitted parameter, not a threshold — is the entire reason BV stays robust to jumps while RV does not.

Section 4

The π/2 isn't decoration — it's an exact correction

For a standard normal Z, E[|Z|] = √(2/π). That one identity is the entire reason the constant is there.

Without the correction

An ordinary diffusive return is r = σ√Δ·Z. Two adjacent absolute returns have E[|rᵢ||rᵢ₋₁|] = σ²Δ·E[|Z|]² = σ²Δ·(2/π) — short of the true σ²Δ by a factor of 2/π ≈ 0.637. Left uncorrected, the raw sum would understate continuous variance by roughly 36%, even on a session with zero jumps.

With π/2

Multiplying by π/2 exactly cancels that shortfall: (π/2)·(2/π) = 1. So E[BV] = σ²T = E[RV] in the no-jump case — BV is unbiased for precisely the same continuous variance RV would estimate if there were no jumps at all. Not a tuning knob; the unique constant that makes the two estimators agree when there's nothing for them to disagree about.

Where this needs a number

E[|Z|] = √(2/π) ≈ 0.7979 for a standard normal. Square it: E[|Z|]² = 2/π ≈ 0.6366. Take its reciprocal: π/2 ≈ 1.5708 — exactly the constant in the BV formula. Every step is an identity, not an approximation.

Section 7

What J tells you — and what it doesn't

A whole-session ratio, not a timestamp

J = max(RV−BV, 0) — and the normalized jump fraction J/RV — answer "of this session's total realized variance, how much came from jump activity?" That's a single aggregate number per period. It does not say which specific one-minute return was the jump, or exactly when it happened.

Section 8

A different fix, for a different contaminant

🧩

Bipower variation — robust to jumps

Adjacent absolute-return products, scaled by π/2. Fixes RV's conflation of continuous and discrete-jump variance. This deck's subject.

📶

Realized kernels — robust to microstructure noise

Barndorff-Nielsen, Hansen, Lunde & Shephard (2008): a kernel-weighted sum of realized autocovariances, built to stay consistent when bid-ask bounce contaminates very-high-frequency returns — a genuinely different problem, not derived here.

The two contaminants can show up in the same dataset at once — sampling fast enough to resolve jumps precisely also picks up more microstructure noise. Serious realized-vol work usually checks more than one sampling frequency rather than trusting a single number blindly.
Section 9

Measured on real data — and honestly reported when it didn't pay off

Verified against this repo's actual code

The exact RV/BV/J formulas here are implemented as bipower() and jump_fraction() in volatilityExhaustion/vol_exhaustion_lib.py, run across ~70,000 (pair, date) rows of real FX/gold 1-minute data. The measurement passed a real out-of-sample test: days carrying a Major scheduled-news release are measurably jumpier than quiet days, both halves of an IS/OOS split, all 3 asset classes, both 1-min and 5-min sampling (t +2.1 to +14.0, 12/12 cells) — the day's single largest move lands within ±5min of the release 35-41% of the time vs a ~1% random baseline.

But using it to improve a forecast tested NULL

A diffusion-only volatility forecast, scaled by the trailing diffusive share of variance, needed to beat the raw forecast by ≥2% OOS QLIKE on days after a high-jump day. Actual result: −0.24% (FX majors), +0.26% (crosses), −0.16% (gold) — an order of magnitude short, sign disagreeing across classes. The underlying premise checked out as genuinely true (jump variance predicts lower future variance, β = −0.33, t = −10.3, replicating on 23/26 instruments) — the effect is real, just too small to survive as a forecast improvement. Per the repo's own pre-registered stop rule, this was never wired into the live forecaster.

Section 10

The clean theory needs help in small, real samples

Where plain BV can be fooled
  • Two jumps landing in consecutive intervals — each one's "ordinary" neighbor is actually the other jump
  • Only a few hundred bars per session means real finite-sample noise, not the clean asymptotic limit
  • Lag-1-only pairing can run slightly biased low in short samples, independent of any jump at all
Why it isn't fatal
  • Truncated and multipower variation variants use more than two returns per term, or cap suspiciously large returns before pairing
  • Built specifically to stay robust even when jumps cluster or samples are short
  • Plain bipower variation, as derived here, is the clean foundation those variants build on — not a discarded first draft
Section 11

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

What does realized variance (RV) fail to distinguish?

RV sums r² for every return with no regard for what caused it — a large squared return from ordinary diffusion and one from a genuine jump add the identical amount.
Question 2

Why is BV robust to a jump while RV is not?

A jump's neighbor is still an ordinary diffusive return, so pairing them gives "large × ordinary," not "large × large" — a much smaller, shrinking share of the total as sampling gets finer.
Question 3

Where does the π/2 constant in the BV formula come from?

Adjacent absolute normal returns have expectation scaled by 2/π short of the true variance; multiplying by π/2 exactly cancels that shortfall.
Question 4

A session has RV = 0.00004 and BV = 0.000031. What does J = max(RV−BV, 0) tell you?

J/RV = 0.000009/0.00004 = 22.5%. It's a whole-session aggregate split — identifying which specific return was the jump needs a per-return detector (next lesson).
Question 5

In this repo, did using the RV/BV jump split actually improve the live volatility forecaster?

Phase 2 (validating jump share against known news events) passed cleanly. Phase 3 (using the split to beat the raw forecast OOS) missed its pre-registered bar by roughly an order of magnitude — a real but too-small effect, reported honestly as null rather than wired in.
Section 12

What to carry forward

01

RV estimates total quadratic variation — continuous plus jump, conflated — with no way to tell the two apart on its own.

02

BV replaces squares with adjacent absolute-return products: a jump inflates RV quadratically but BV only linearly, through its ordinary neighbor.

03

The π/2 constant is an exact correction for E[|Z|]² = 2/π, not a fitted or arbitrary number.

04

J = max(RV−BV, 0) is a whole-session, aggregate jump share — not a timestamp for any individual return.

05

Realized kernels solve a related but different problem — microstructure noise, not jumps.

06

This repo validated the measurement on real data against known news events, then honestly reported that using it to improve a forecast tested null.

What this now lets you do

Read a realized-variance number knowing it might be hiding a jump inside it, and know exactly which second formula to reach for to check. Understand precisely why pairing neighboring returns — not squaring, not deleting outliers — is what makes an estimator jump-robust. And keep "the measurement is validated" and "the measurement improves a forecast" as two separate claims, the way this repo's own honest null result insists on.
