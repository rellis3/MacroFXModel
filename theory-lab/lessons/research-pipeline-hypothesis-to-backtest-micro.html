Research Process 1 · Quantitative Research Process

The Research Pipeline, From Idea to Honest Evidence

Every strategy that ever gets traded starts as an idea, and every idea eventually has to survive the same five-stage gauntlet: state why it should work before looking at the data, test the plainest possible version, add complexity deliberately and count every attempt, validate on data it never touched, and only then — skeptically — call it a candidate. This is the first lesson in a brand-new category, and it's the connective tissue between two lessons already in this curriculum: it's the reason the Multiple Testing math matters, and the reason the Walk-Forward & Purged Cross-Validation split has to be genuine. Get the order wrong and both of those lessons quietly become the explanation for why a great-looking backtest wasn't.

Section 1

A bag of a thousand keys will always open something

Hand someone a lock and a thousand keys, and tell them to find the one that fits. Try enough keys and, on attempt 640, one turns. Have they learned that key 640 is special — cut for that lock? Not really. Given a large enough bag, something was always going to turn eventually, whether or not any key was ever meant for that lock. A disciplined locksmith works the other way: reason about the lock first, try a specific motivated guess, and if it fails, say so — rather than quietly reaching for key 641, 642, 643… and reporting only the one that clicked.

Every stage in this lesson exists to stop a research process from quietly becoming "try enough keys."

🔍

Look first, hypothesize after

A pattern found by scanning the data and explained afterward isn't a hypothesis — it's a description of one sample, dressed up as a prediction.

🎛

Tune before you baseline

Adding parameters before measuring the plain version leaves no honest reference point for how much of the result is the idea versus the tuning.

🕳

Validate a data-mined idea

A technically flawless out-of-sample test on a hypothesis mined from the same data is still worthless — the leak happened upstream.

🎯

Set the bar after the result

Deciding what "worked" means only after seeing the number is indistinguishable from moving the goalposts to wherever the ball landed.

Section 3

One shortcut undoes all five stages

The dashed red loop is the failure mode: a disappointing result at Stage 3 or 4 quietly sends you back to try a new variant on the same data.

The loop isn't wrong because looking for a better idea is wrong — it's wrong because it turns a disciplined pipeline back into an undisclosed search, quietly re-opening the exact problem Stage 1 and Stage 5 exist to close.
Section 4

A mechanism is falsifiable. A pattern is just a memory.

Stage 1, done right versus done backwards.

Pattern first, story after
  • "This 14-day crossover made money in the data I looked at"
  • The story is invented to fit whatever already turned up
  • Guaranteed to look good in-sample — that's how it was found
  • No independent reason to expect it continues
Mechanism first, data after
  • "Central banks defend a target, so a widening rate gap should pressure the currency"
  • Stated before any return series is pulled
  • Can plausibly fail to show up — that failure would be informative
  • A real claim about why, not a description of what already happened
Confusing the two is the single most common way a research process turns into curve-fitting without anyone intending it — every individual step afterward can be executed correctly, and the result is still worthless, because the leak happened here, before any test began.
Section 5

Taste the soup before you add the spices

Stages 2 and 3: measure the plain version, then add complexity as a tracked, deliberate purchase.

🥄

Zero parameters, nothing to fit

A bare sign or zero-crossing rule has no lookback, threshold, or smoothing constant to tune — so there's nothing available to accidentally flatter one historical sample.

📏

The baseline is the reference point

Measured and recorded honestly before anything is added, it's the number every more complex version has to beat — and its modesty is itself informative.

🧂

Every parameter is a purchase

It buys the chance of a genuinely better fit, and in the same transaction, a fresh chance that fit is noise rather than signal. Neither is free.

📋

Log every trial, kept or discarded

The parameters you tried and threw out still count — leaving them out understates N for the correction the next stage needs.

This isn't just theory — it's this repo's own house rule

This project's own working agreement (CLAUDE.md) says it almost word for word: "Start with the minimal-DOF version of the signal… Get that number honestly first; only add tunable parameters after, knowing each one you add is overfit risk you're buying." Stages 2–3 of this lesson are that rule, generalized.

Section 6

A correct test on the wrong data is still wrong

Stage 4 needs two things at once: real costs, and a split that doesn't quietly leak.

Looks like validation
  • Frictionless fills, no slippage
  • A chronological split date — feels clean
  • Rolling-window features built across the boundary
  • Result: an optimistic number with a hidden leak
Actually is validation
  • Realistic transaction costs and slippage, always on
  • A genuine train/test boundary the tuning never touched
  • Purge + embargo around the boundary for overlapping windows
  • Result: a number that means what it claims to mean
A plain chronological split date is necessary but not sufficient — features and labels built from rolling windows can straddle the boundary and leak test-period information backward into training even when the date itself looks clean. This is the entire subject of Walk-Forward & Purged Cross-Validation: read it before trusting any Stage 4 number.

Trying variation after variation until one backtests well is functionally the same failure the Multiple Testing lesson's math describes. Drag the slider.

This is exactly the Multiple Testing lesson's family-wise error rate, aimed at a research loop instead of a batch of pre-planned tests. Reporting only the variation that happened to clear the bar — without saying how many were tried — is the same failure mode as reporting only the best of 70 pre-sliced tests.

Section 9

Write the bar down before you see the number

Pre-registration, Stage 5 — testing first and rationalizing after is a genuinely different, less honest practice.

Test first, rationalize after
  • Run the OOS test, see Sharpe = 0.30
  • Decide, only now, that "0.30 is actually pretty good for this asset class"
  • The bar bends to fit whatever number came back
  • A null result can never survive this process — there's always a story
Write the hypothesis down first
  • Before Stage 4 runs: "OOS Sharpe above 0.30 with ≥30 trades = supported"
  • The bar is fixed, in writing, before the number exists
  • A result below the bar is allowed to just mean "no"
  • This project's own working agreement states the same rule: pre-register both outcomes before running a test
The trap

Setting "what counts as working" only after the out-of-sample number is already in hand is functionally indistinguishable from moving the goalposts. Pre-registration is the only thing standing between an honest "this didn't work" and a comfortable excuse — and it costs nothing but writing one sentence down first.

Section 10

The crossover that "worked" after the eighth tweak

Same effort, run through the pipeline — three of five stages had already broken before any out-of-sample test ran.

✗

Stage 1 — skipped entirely

There was never a mechanism, only a pattern that "looked right" a few times, with a story invented afterward.

✗

Stage 2 — never isolated

The first two flat attempts were treated as failures to fix, not recorded as the honest zero-edge baseline this idea actually has.

✗

Stage 3 — trial count uncounted

Eight rounds of tuning, unlogged — the eventual "Sharpe of 1.4" understates how many variations were actually searched.

✗

Stage 5 — no bar, ever

No pass/fail criteria were set in advance, so there was no way for the process to produce an honest null even if the idea truly had nothing.

Whatever the eventual out-of-sample test shows, this idea entered it having already broken most of the stages that keep noise from disguising itself as signal — and each individual re-run, taken alone, looked like ordinary, reasonable iteration.

Section 11

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Why does a hypothesis need a stated mechanism, rather than just a pattern that performed well historically?

A pattern-first "hypothesis" has no independent test besides "did it look good in the data used to find it" — true by construction for whatever eventually got selected. A mechanism can plausibly fail to hold, which is what makes it informative.
Question 2

Why measure a zero-parameter version of a rule before adding any tunable parameters?

Every parameter added afterward is a new way to accidentally flatter the specific sample it was tuned on. The untunable baseline is the honest reference point every later version has to beat.
Question 3

Why is a technically correct out-of-sample test still worthless if the hypothesis was found by searching that same broad dataset?

If the rule was already selected because it looked good on data related to the test set, the leak happened upstream, before any split existed. No amount of correctness at the validation stage fixes a leak that occurred earlier.
Question 4

What specifically does pre-registering the verdict criteria prevent?

Without a bar fixed in advance, there's no way for a test to produce a genuine null verdict, even when the honest answer is that the idea has nothing — the goalposts simply move to wherever the result landed.
Question 5

20 independent parameter variations are tried, each with a 5% chance of looking good by pure chance alone. Roughly what's the probability at least one clears the bar?

1 − (1 − 0.05)²⁰ ≈ 1 − 0.358 ≈ 64.2% — the same formula the Multiple Testing lesson applies to 70 pre-sliced tests, aimed here at 20 tried-and-tuned variations instead.
Section 12

What to carry forward

01

Form the hypothesis before touching the data — a pattern explained after the fact is a description of one sample, not a falsifiable prediction.

02

Test the minimal-degrees-of-freedom version first — a zero-parameter rule is close to impossible to overfit, and its honest performance is the baseline.

03

Every added parameter is overfitting risk being purchased — add deliberately, one at a time, and log every trial, including the ones discarded.

04

Out-of-sample validation needs real costs and a genuinely leak-free split — a clean chronological date isn't automatically enough (see Walk-Forward & Purged Cross-Validation).

05

Pre-register what "worked" and "didn't work" mean before the test runs — otherwise a null quietly gets re-narrated into "promising."

06

Trying many variations until one backtests well is the Multiple Testing lesson's math in different clothes — a survivor has to clear the chance-expected ceiling, not just beat zero.

What this now lets you do

Recognize the moment a research process quietly stops testing a hypothesis and starts searching for one — whether it's your own work or someone else's write-up. Ask the three questions that matter before trusting any backtest: was the mechanism stated before the data was searched, was the baseline measured honestly, and was the pass/fail bar written down before the result came back. Read a headline Sharpe correctly by asking how many variations it survived, using the same arithmetic as Multiple Testing, and demand the genuine holdout discipline of Walk-Forward & Purged Cross-Validation before believing the out-of-sample number at all.
