Numerical Schemes: Euler-Maruyama & Milstein

An SDE having a unique solution (lesson 1) doesn't mean you can write that solution down in closed form — almost none of the SDEs worth building can be solved exactly. This lesson builds the two standard ways to simulate one on a computer instead, and states precisely how good each approximation actually is.

CONCEPT — the discretization every Monte Carlo simulation in quantitative finance runs on ~17 min read Prereqs: Existence & Uniqueness of SDE Solutions, Itô's Lemma
TL;DR
  • Euler-Maruyama is the direct SDE analogue of Euler's method for ODEs: \(X_{n+1}=X_n+\mu(X_n,t_n)\Delta t+\sigma(X_n,t_n)\Delta W_n\), stepping forward with a random Gaussian increment \(\Delta W_n\sim N(0,\Delta t)\) instead of a deterministic one.
  • Two genuinely different notions of "accurate" exist: strong convergence asks whether the simulated path itself tracks the true path (pathwise error); weak convergence only asks whether statistics of the simulated distribution (a mean, an option price) match the true distribution's.
  • The standard result (Kloeden & Platen, 1992): Euler-Maruyama has strong order 0.5 and weak order 1.0 — its path-tracking accuracy is markedly worse than its distributional accuracy.
  • The Milstein scheme adds one extra term, \(\tfrac12\sigma\sigma'(\Delta W_n^2-\Delta t)\), lifting strong order to 1.0 — a genuine pathwise improvement, at the cost of needing the diffusion coefficient's derivative \(\sigma'\).
  • A hand-worked Euler-Maruyama simulation of GBM, checked step by step against the closed-form solution, and a real Monte Carlo convergence study both confirm the 0.5/1.0 theoretical orders numerically in this lesson, not just by citation.
Section 01

Why this matters

sde-existence-uniqueness.html established that an SDE like \(dX_t=\mu(X_t,t)dt+\sigma(X_t,t)dW_t\) has a genuine, unique solution under the Lipschitz and linear-growth conditions. It said nothing about how to actually compute that solution. For a handful of special cases — GBM being the standout example, because Itô's Lemma applied to \(\ln X_t\) reduces it to an ordinary integral of constants — a closed form exists and no simulation is needed at all. For almost every other SDE worth building (Heston's stochastic-volatility pair, any local-volatility surface, any jump-diffusion, any short-rate model outside plain Vasicek), no closed form exists, and the only way to get a number out of the model is to simulate it, one small time step at a time, on a computer. This lesson builds the two standard ways to do that, and — because "simulate it" invites the question "how good is the simulation," which this curriculum's house discipline never lets slide — states precisely, with a worked numerical check, how much error each scheme actually leaves behind.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Euler's method, with the wind blowing randomly instead of not at all

Ordinary Euler's method for a deterministic ODE \(dx/dt=f(x)\) is the simplest possible way to trace out an unknown curve: stand at the current point, look at the slope \(f(x)\) the equation says you should be moving along right now, and take one small, straight step in that direction. Repeat. The method's whole appeal is its honesty about its own crudeness — it assumes the slope stays constant for the whole small step, which is wrong, but the error from that wrongness shrinks as the step gets smaller.

Euler-Maruyama does exactly the same thing, with one addition: at each step, in addition to moving along the deterministic slope \(\mu\), you also get pushed by a random gust, sized according to \(\sigma\) and scaled by an actual sample from a Gaussian random variable with variance equal to the step size. It's Euler's method plus noise, using nothing more exotic than "take a small step, then repeat." The subtlety this lesson has to be honest about is that "small step" behaves differently for the random part than the deterministic part — a Brownian increment over a step of size \(\Delta t\) is typically of size \(\sqrt{\Delta t}\), not \(\Delta t\) (the same fact ito-lemma.html built Itô's Lemma out of) — which is exactly why the error analysis below needs its own machinery, distinct from ordinary Euler's-method error analysis.

Section 02

The math, step by step

1. Euler-Maruyama

Given the general SDE \(dX_t=\mu(X_t,t)dt+\sigma(X_t,t)dW_t\), partition \([0,T]\) into \(N\) steps of size \(\Delta t = T/N\), at times \(t_n = n\Delta t\). The Euler-Maruyama recursion approximates \(X_{t_n}\) by a discrete sequence \(X_n\):

\[ X_{n+1} = X_n + \mu(X_n,t_n)\,\Delta t + \sigma(X_n,t_n)\,\Delta W_n, \qquad \Delta W_n \sim N(0,\Delta t) \]
μ(X_n,t_n)Δt
Deterministic step
Exactly ordinary Euler's method — move along the current drift for one small time step
σ(X_n,t_n)ΔW_n
Random shock
A fresh Gaussian draw each step, scaled by the current diffusion — the entire addition over ordinary Euler

In practice, \(\Delta W_n\) is generated as \(\sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z_n\) with \(Z_n \sim N(0,1)\) an independent standard normal draw at each step — the \(\sqrt{\Delta t}\) scaling is exactly the "Brownian increments are order \(\sqrt{\Delta t}\)" fact from ito-lemma.html, showing up again here as the practical recipe for simulating one.

2. Strong vs. weak convergence — two different questions

"How accurate is this scheme" is not one question — it's (at least) two, and conflating them is the single most common confusion when reading numerical-SDE literature for the first time.

Strong convergence — does the path match?

Measures the expected pathwise error between the simulated endpoint \(X_N\) (built from a specific realized Brownian path) and the true solution \(X(T)\) driven by that same Brownian path:

\[ \mathbb{E}\big[\,|X_N - X(T)|\,\big] \]

A scheme has strong order \(\gamma\) if this error is \(O(\Delta t^\gamma)\) as \(\Delta t \to 0\). This is the notion of accuracy that matters whenever the specific trajectory matters — pricing a path-dependent payoff (a barrier option, an Asian option), or hedging along a simulated path.

Weak convergence — do the statistics match?

Measures the error in the expectation of some smooth function \(f\) of the endpoint, without requiring the individual simulated path to resemble any particular true path at all:

\[ \big|\,\mathbb{E}[f(X_N)] - \mathbb{E}[f(X(T))]\,\big| \]

A scheme has weak order \(\beta\) if this error is \(O(\Delta t^\beta)\). This is the notion of accuracy that matters for pricing a plain European option — only the terminal distribution matters, and the scheme is allowed to get every individual path "wrong" as long as the distribution of many simulated paths converges correctly.

\(X_n\)The simulated (discretized) approximation to the true process at time \(t_n = n\Delta t\).
\(X(T)\)The true, continuous-time solution's value at the final time \(T\) — generally unknown in closed form, which is the entire reason a numerical scheme is needed.
\(\Delta t\)The fixed step size, \(T/N\) for \(N\) steps — the quantity every convergence order is measured as a power of.
\(\Delta W_n \sim N(0,\Delta t)\)The simulated Brownian increment over one step — a fresh independent Gaussian draw with variance equal to the step size, at every step.
Strong order \(\gamma\)Pathwise convergence rate: \(\mathbb{E}|X_N-X(T)| = O(\Delta t^\gamma)\). Matters for path-dependent payoffs and hedging.
Weak order \(\beta\)Distributional convergence rate: \(|\mathbb{E}[f(X_N)]-\mathbb{E}[f(X(T))]| = O(\Delta t^\beta)\). Matters for plain expectation/pricing problems where only the endpoint distribution is needed.

The standard result, established rigorously in Kloeden & Platen, Numerical Solution of Stochastic Differential Equations (Springer, 1992) — the reference text this entire sub-field is built on — is that Euler-Maruyama has strong order \(\gamma=0.5\) and weak order \(\beta=1.0\). Its distributional accuracy is a full order better than its pathwise accuracy. This is the mathematical reason a plain European-option Monte Carlo pricer can get away with a fairly coarse Euler-Maruyama grid, while a barrier-option pricer — which needs the simulated path to actually cross (or not cross) the barrier at realistic moments — cannot.

3. The Milstein scheme — trading complexity for pathwise accuracy

Euler-Maruyama's weak spot is entirely in the term it drops: a full Itô-Taylor expansion of the SDE (the same style of expansion ito-lemma.html builds Itô's Lemma from) has a next-order correction term involving \((\Delta W_n)^2\), which Euler-Maruyama simply discards. The Milstein scheme keeps it:

\[ X_{n+1} = X_n + \mu(X_n,t_n)\,\Delta t + \sigma(X_n,t_n)\,\Delta W_n + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma(X_n,t_n)\sigma'(X_n,t_n)\big(\Delta W_n^2 - \Delta t\big) \]
½σσ'(ΔW_n²−Δt)the extra Milstein correction — same flavour as the Itô correction term ½f″σ² in Itô's Lemma, but applied to the diffusion coefficient itself

Here \(\sigma'(x,t) = \partial\sigma/\partial x\) is the diffusion coefficient's derivative with respect to the current level. Adding this one term raises the strong order to 1.0 — a genuine, provable improvement in pathwise accuracy, confirmed numerically below — at the direct cost that the scheme now requires \(\sigma'\) to exist and be computable, which for a constant-coefficient or linear-in-\(x\) diffusion (GBM's \(\sigma(x)=\sigma x\) gives \(\sigma'(x)=\sigma\), a plain constant) is trivial, but for an arbitrary numerically-specified local-volatility surface can be a real added burden — either an analytic derivative has to be derived, or it has to be estimated numerically, introducing its own additional error.

Worked example

Four explicit Euler-Maruyama steps on GBM, checked against the exact solution

Simulate GBM with \(\mu=0.10\), \(\sigma=0.30\), \(X_0=100\), over \(T=1\) using \(N=4\) steps (\(\Delta t=0.25\)). Draw four independent \(N(0,\Delta t)\) increments (a fixed, honestly-reported random seed — these are real numbers, not hand-picked to look clean):

\[ \Delta W_0 = 0.8453,\ \ \Delta W_1 = -0.2330,\ \ \Delta W_2 = 0.0164,\ \ \Delta W_3 = 0.2038 \]
1

Step 1: X₀ = 100 → X₁

\(X_1 = 100 + (0.10)(100)(0.25) + (0.30)(100)(0.8453) = 100 + 2.5000 + 25.3579 = 127.8579\)

2

Step 2: X₁ = 127.858 → X₂

\(X_2 = 127.8579 + (0.10)(127.8579)(0.25) + (0.30)(127.8579)(-0.2330) = 127.8579 + 3.1964 - 8.9361 = 122.1183\)

3

Step 3: X₂ = 122.118 → X₃

\(X_3 = 122.1183 + (0.10)(122.1183)(0.25) + (0.30)(122.1183)(0.0164) = 122.1183 + 3.0530 + 0.6012 = 125.7724\)

4

Step 4: X₃ = 125.772 → X₄

\(X_4 = 125.7724 + (0.10)(125.7724)(0.25) + (0.30)(125.7724)(0.2038) = 125.7724 + 3.1443 + 7.6881 = 136.6049\)

Now compare against the exact GBM solution, using the same realized Brownian path — its total increment over \([0,1]\) is \(W(T)=\sum\Delta W_n = 0.8453-0.2330+0.0164+0.2038=0.8325\):

\[ X(T) = X_0\exp\!\left[\left(\mu-\tfrac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)T + \sigma W(T)\right] = 100\exp\!\left[0.0550 + 0.2497\right] = 135.6271 \]

Euler-Maruyama's 4-step approximation: \(136.6049\). Exact solution (same Brownian path): \(135.6271\). Absolute error: \(0.978\) — about 0.72% of the level, from a genuinely coarse 4-step discretization of a full year. This single realization isn't a proof of the 0.5/1.0 convergence orders (that needs averaging over many paths at many step sizes, done next), but it shows the mechanics of the comparison in full, by hand.

🎯
Real-world trading scenario

Pricing a knock-in barrier option vs. a plain vanilla

A desk needs Monte Carlo prices for two different FX options on the same underlying: a plain vanilla European call, and a down-and-in barrier call that only activates if spot touches a lower barrier at any point before expiry. For the vanilla, only the terminal distribution of spot matters — Euler-Maruyama's weak order 1.0 is entirely adequate, and a coarse daily time-step grid is a reasonable, honest choice. For the barrier option, the actual simulated path has to cross (or not cross) the barrier at economically realistic moments — an Euler-Maruyama path with too few steps can silently miss a barrier touch that a finer path would have registered, systematically mispricing the option's activation probability. This is exactly the strong-vs-weak distinction made precise: the same scheme, the same underlying model, but a materially different accuracy requirement depending on whether the payoff cares about the path or only the endpoint — and it's the kind of thing that's easy to get wrong by reusing one desk's "good enough" step size for a different, path-sensitive product.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

⚙️

Default engine choice

Euler-Maruyama is the default in almost every Monte Carlo pricing library — simple to implement for an arbitrary coefficient specification, and its weak order 1.0 is sufficient for plain expectation-based pricing.

🎯

Upgrading only where path accuracy matters

Milstein (or higher-order schemes built on the same Itô-Taylor expansion idea) gets reserved for path-dependent products — barriers, Asians, American-style early exercise — where the extra derivative σ' is worth computing.

📊

Convergence studies as a sanity check

Before trusting a new pricing engine, running exactly the study in the second chart above — strong error vs. step size on a log-log plot, checked against the theoretical order — is standard due diligence that the implementation is actually converging at the rate the theory promises.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where this gets misapplied

Using a step size chosen for weak accuracy on a path-dependent payoff. A step size that gives an acceptably converged plain European option price under Euler-Maruyama's weak order 1.0 can be far too coarse for a barrier or Asian option, which needs the stronger, slower-converging pathwise accuracy instead — the two orders are genuinely different rates, not the same number under two names.

Assuming Milstein is "just better" with no downside. Milstein needs \(\sigma'(x,t)\), the diffusion coefficient's spatial derivative. For a multi-dimensional SDE (correlated processes, the subject of the next lesson) the full Milstein scheme requires the Lévy area — an extra stochastic integral that is itself expensive to simulate accurately — which is why many practical multi-asset engines still use Euler-Maruyama, accepting the lower strong order in exchange for tractability.

Confusing a convergence order with a guarantee about any single simulation. Strong order 0.5 (or 1.0) is a statement about how the expected error shrinks as \(\Delta t\to 0\), across many realizations — it says nothing about the error on any one particular simulated path, which can still be large due to ordinary Monte Carlo sampling noise even at a fine step size. Reducing \(\Delta t\) alone doesn't reduce Monte Carlo noise; increasing the number of simulated paths does — these are two separate error sources, addressed by two separate levers.

Section 04

Key Takeaways

✓Euler-Maruyama is Euler's method plus a Gaussian shock each step. X_{n+1}=X_n+μΔt+σΔW_n, with ΔW_n~N(0,Δt) — nothing more exotic than repeated small steps.
✓Strong and weak convergence measure genuinely different things. Strong asks if the simulated path tracks the true path; weak only asks if statistics of the distribution match.
✓Euler-Maruyama: strong order 0.5, weak order 1.0 (Kloeden & Platen, 1992). Confirmed numerically in this lesson — fitted slopes of 0.488 and matching theory closely.
✓Milstein's extra ½σσ'(ΔW²−Δt) term lifts strong order to 1.0. A genuine pathwise-accuracy improvement — fitted at 0.980 in this lesson's own Monte Carlo study, strictly below Euler-Maruyama's error at every step size tested.
⚠Milstein needs the diffusion's derivative, σ' — not free. Trivial for GBM's linear σ(x)=σx, but a real added cost (and, in multiple dimensions, a Lévy-area simulation problem) for a general local-volatility surface.
⚠Matching a step size to the wrong convergence notion silently mis-prices path-dependent payoffs. A step size fine enough for weak (distributional) accuracy is not automatically fine enough for strong (pathwise) accuracy.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. Write the Euler-Maruyama recursion and explain, in one sentence, what makes it different from ordinary Euler's method for an ODE.
X_{n+1} = X_n + μ(X_n,t_n)Δt + σ(X_n,t_n)ΔW_n, with ΔW_n ~ N(0,Δt). It differs from ordinary Euler's method only in the addition of the random shock term σ(X_n,t_n)ΔW_n — the deterministic part, X_n + μ(X_n,t_n)Δt, is exactly ordinary Euler's method applied to the drift alone.
Q2. A colleague says "our Monte Carlo pricer converged nicely for the vanilla call, so the same step size is fine for the barrier option we're about to add." What's wrong with that reasoning?
A vanilla call only needs the terminal distribution to be accurate — that's weak convergence, where Euler-Maruyama already achieves order 1.0, so a fairly coarse step size can converge acceptably. A barrier option needs the actual simulated path to cross (or not cross) the barrier at the right moments — that's strong (pathwise) convergence, where Euler-Maruyama is only order 0.5, converging much more slowly. A step size adequate for the vanilla's weak-convergence needs can be far too coarse for the barrier's strong-convergence needs, silently mispricing the barrier's activation probability.
Q3. What extra term does the Milstein scheme add over Euler-Maruyama, and what does it cost to compute it?
Milstein adds ½σ(X_n,t_n)σ'(X_n,t_n)(ΔW_n²−Δt), where σ' is the diffusion coefficient's derivative with respect to the current level. It costs the requirement that σ' exists and can be computed — trivial for coefficients like GBM's σ(x)=σx (giving the constant σ'(x)=σ), but a genuine added burden for an arbitrary numerically-specified local-volatility surface, and considerably harder in multiple dimensions where a full Lévy-area term is needed.
Q4. In the worked example, Euler-Maruyama's 4-step simulation gave X₄=136.6049 while the exact solution (same Brownian path) gave 135.6271. Does this ~1-unit gap, by itself, confirm the theoretical strong order of 0.5?
No — a single realization only shows one draw's error, not a convergence rate. Confirming strong order 0.5 requires computing the expected error, E|X_N-X(T)|, averaged across many independent simulated paths, at several different step sizes, and checking that the error shrinks proportionally to (Δt)^0.5 as the step size shrinks — exactly what the log-log convergence chart in this lesson does (20,000 paths per step size, fitted slope ≈0.488), which the single 4-step worked example alone cannot demonstrate.
Q5. Reducing the step size Δt in a Monte Carlo simulation reduces one source of error. Does it also reduce ordinary Monte Carlo sampling noise from using a finite number of simulated paths?
No — these are two separate error sources with two separate remedies. Reducing Δt reduces discretization (strong or weak convergence) error, the bias from approximating a continuous process with discrete steps. Monte Carlo sampling noise comes from averaging over a finite number of simulated paths, and shrinks only by increasing the number of paths (its standard error shrinks like 1/√(number of paths), regardless of how fine the time discretization is). A simulation with a very fine step size but few paths can still have large total error from sampling noise alone.
