Point-in-Time Data & Survivorship Bias, What the Backtest Never Saw

Two data problems that both make a backtest look better than the live strategy it's supposed to represent: using a number as of a date before it was actually knowable (because the "real" number arrived later, revised), and evaluating a universe that quietly excludes everything that failed along the way. Neither shows up as an error — both just make the past look calmer and the winners look more common than they really were.

The rule underneath both panels: a revision should always add a new dated record, never mutate the old one. The moment a store only holds "the current value," the historical vintage is unrecoverable.
Think of a newspaper archive where every correction quietly rewrites yesterday's front page in place, with no trace of what it originally said. A properly built archive keeps the original edition and each dated correction as a separate layer on top — so "what did this page say on the day it was printed" and "what do we now believe was true" stay two different, honestly answerable questions.
Section 2

Revisions: the number you see today isn't the number that existed then

One "March GDP" fact, published then revised twice, at three different dates with three different values.

A backtest that grabs today's fully-revised 1.4% and treats it as what a trader saw on April 28th has handed itself information from the future — relative to that date. It's an easy leak to miss precisely because the revised number doesn't look like a leak; it looks like an ordinary historical data point.

Feature Engineering, Leakage & Reproducibility already flags this from one level up — a feature has to be computable from only what was genuinely knowable on its date. This deck goes one level deeper: what the data store itself has to do, mechanically, for that to even be possible. One real piece of that already exists in this repo: js/laborMarketEngine.js's revisionScore() fetches both the current, fully-revised payrolls series and BLS's original first-published vintage, and scores the gap between them — a genuine, working revision-vintage check, not a hypothetical.

Vintage

The specific version of a data value as it stood on a given historical date, before any later revision.

📅

Reference period

The period a data point describes — "March" — distinct from when it was actually published.

📣

Publication date

The date a value first became publicly knowable. An "as of" query keys off this, never the reference period.

⏳

Embargo / lag

The gap between when an event occurred and when data describing it became available to trade on.

A month's inflation reading describes conditions during that month, but isn't published until weeks into the next one. Dating that record to "the last day of the month" instead of its true release date makes it look tradeable for weeks before it actually existed.

Section 4

Survivorship bias: the database that only remembers the winners

A different failure mode from revisions — this one is about which rows are missing, not whether a row's value is stale.

🗂

Definition

A dataset that only includes entities still active, listed, or reporting today — silently dropping anything that failed, delisted, defaulted, or merged away along the way.

🎯

Why it isn't random

Failure correlates with poor performance — the entities that disappear are disproportionately the weak ones, not a random cross-section.

↗

Direction of the bias

Always optimistic. It looks like "the average result of everything ever tried," when it's really "the average of whatever happened to still be around later."

🌐

Not just stock-picking

Any cross-sectional universe — funds, currencies, bonds, counterparties — is vulnerable if it's built from "what still exists today."

Judging how good a school is by only interviewing students still enrolled — never the ones who dropped out — makes the school look great, because struggling students are exactly the ones most likely to have already left. A database built from "what's still around today" makes the identical mistake with funds, companies, or currencies.
Section 5

1996: when someone actually measured it

Survivorship bias went from a plausible-sounding worry to a quantified, documented distortion.

The classic study

Edwin J. Elton, Martin J. Gruber & Christopher R. Blake, "Survivorship Bias and Mutual Fund Performance," Review of Financial Studies, 9(4), 1996. They compared mutual fund performance computed from a survivors-only database against a more complete one that also captured funds that had since disappeared.

Complete database (failures included)
Result: the survivors-only version meaningfully overstated true average historical fund performance — not by an edge case, but as a systematic, measured effect.
Why "measured" matters

Turning survivorship bias from a plausible argument into a demonstrated, quantified distortion made it impossible to dismiss as hypothetical — which is exactly why it became the standard reference on the topic, and why point-in-time, failure-inclusive databases became standard practice in serious fund-performance research afterward.

A point-in-time universe is genuinely harder and more expensive to build and maintain than a "current universe, backfilled" one — it has to track membership as of each historical date, not just today's roster. That extra cost is the entire difference between the two panels above.

Section 7

The gap compounds the further back you look

Same basket, cumulative return over 10 years, computed two ways.

Both lines start at the same point, because at year 0 nothing has failed yet — there's nothing to exclude. Every year that passes gives another instrument the chance to fail and drop out of "what's active today," so the survivors-only line's overstatement keeps compounding. The gap isn't a rounding error — it's nearly triple.

Section 11

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Why is a single mutable "current value" table incompatible with point-in-time correctness?

A revision has to add a new dated record, not mutate the old one — otherwise the historical vintage is unrecoverable, forever.
Question 2

An "as of" query should key off which date?

A live system only ever knows what's already been published. Keying off the reference period instead hands a backtest information from the future relative to the query date.
Question 3

Why does survivorship bias produce a systematically optimistic result, not a random error?

The dropped entities aren't a random cross-section — they're disproportionately the failures, so removing them pulls the reported average up.
Question 4

80 survivors averaging 9%, 20 failures averaging −15% — what's the true, all-funds average?

(80 × 9% + 20 × −15%) ÷ 100 = (720 − 300) ÷ 100 = 4.2%.
Question 5

What did Elton, Gruber & Blake (1996) actually add to the survivorship-bias argument?

Comparing a survivors-only mutual fund database against a more complete one, they found the survivors-only version meaningfully overstated true average performance — a measured result, not just an argument.
Section 12

What to carry forward

01

Point-in-time correctness is an operational property of the data store — immutable dated snapshots, never one mutable "current" table overwritten in place.

02

Revisions are common, not rare — using today's fully-revised figure as if it were known on the original release date is a real, easy-to-miss lookahead bug.

03

An "as of" query must key off the publication date, never the reference period the data describes — those two dates are routinely far apart.

04

Survivorship bias silently excludes failures from a "what exists today" universe, and it's always optimistic — never a random, harmless omission.

05

Elton, Gruber & Blake (1996) made this a measured fact, not an argument — a survivors-only mutual fund database meaningfully overstated true average performance.

06

A point-in-time universe fixes it, but it's genuinely harder and costlier to build than a "current universe, backfilled" database — it has to track membership as of each historical date.

What this now lets you do

Spot the difference between "a number that's stale" and "a number that never should have been visible yet" — and treat revision history and universe completeness as data-infrastructure requirements, not backtest afterthoughts. Ask, of any historical universe: does this include what failed, or only what's still here to ask about?
