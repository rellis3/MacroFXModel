Point-in-Time Data & Survivorship Bias, What the Backtest Never Saw

Two data problems that both make a backtest look better than the live strategy it's supposed to represent: using a number as of a date before it was actually knowable (because the "real" number arrived later, revised), and evaluating a universe that quietly excludes everything that failed along the way. Neither shows up as an error — both just make the past look calmer and the winners look more common than they really were.

The whole idea
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

My backtest uses the official history for every number. How can that leak the future?

Because the official history today is not the history you would have seen back then. Take a March GDP figure. It comes out at the end of April as one point nine percent.

And that's the number?

For now. A month later it is revised to one point six. A month after that, to one point four. Three numbers, all called March GDP.

So which one belongs in my backtest?

The one that was published on or before the day you are pretending to trade. On the thirtieth of May, that is one point six. Never the one point four. Nobody had that yet.

And on the fifteenth of April?

Nothing at all. March had ended, but the figure had not been published. The month a number describes and the day it became known are two different dates. Your queries have to use the second one.

My database just keeps the latest value for each month.

Then the first revision already wiped out what was known at the time. A store you can trust never overwrites. It adds each revision as a new dated record, so every old version is still there.

Fine. What's the other trap?

Missing rows. Say a hundred funds start a decade. Twenty do so badly they close, averaging minus fifteen percent a year. The eighty survivors average nine percent.

And a database of funds open today only has the eighty.

So it reports nine percent. Put the twenty back and the true average is four point two. More than half the apparent return came from forgetting the losers. Elton, Gruber and Blake measured this bias in real fund data in 1996.

That's a level being off. Can it do worse?

Yes. In 1992 Brown, Goetzmann, Ibbotson and Ross simulated managers with no skill at all, and closed the funds that did badly. The standard test then found that winners kept winning. The filter made the pattern, not the managers.

So a clean-looking dataset can invent a whole strategy.

It can. The one-line version: ask what was known on the day, and who was still in the room, before you trust any backtest.

Think of a newspaper archive where every correction quietly rewrites yesterday's front page in place, with no trace of what it originally said. A properly built archive keeps the original edition and each dated correction as a separate layer on top — so "what did this page say on the day it was printed" and "what do we now believe was true" stay two different, honestly answerable questions.
Section 2

Revisions: the number you see today isn't the number that existed then

One "March GDP" fact, published then revised twice, at three different dates with three different values.

A backtest that grabs today's fully-revised 1.4% and treats it as what a trader saw on April 28th has handed itself information from the future — relative to that date. It's an easy leak to miss precisely because the revised number doesn't look like a leak; it looks like an ordinary historical data point.

Feature Engineering, Leakage & Reproducibility already flags this from one level up — a feature has to be computable from only what was genuinely knowable on its date. This deck goes one level deeper: what the data store itself has to do, mechanically, for that to even be possible. One real piece of that already exists in this repo: js/laborMarketEngine.js's revisionScore() fetches both the current, fully-revised payrolls series and BLS's original first-published vintage, and scores the gap between them — a genuine, working revision-vintage check, not a hypothetical.

The specific version of a data value as it stood on a given historical date, before any later revision.

Reference period

The period a data point describes — "March" — distinct from when it was actually published.

Publication date

The date a value first became publicly knowable. An "as of" query keys off this, never the reference period.

⏳

Embargo / lag

The gap between when an event occurred and when data describing it became available to trade on.

A month's inflation reading describes conditions during that month, but isn't published until weeks into the next one. Dating that record to "the last day of the month" instead of its true release date makes it look tradeable for weeks before it actually existed.

Section 4

Survivorship bias: the database that only remembers the winners

A different failure mode from revisions — this one is about which rows are missing, not whether a row's value is stale.

🗂

Definition

A dataset that only includes entities still active, listed, or reporting today — silently dropping anything that failed, delisted, defaulted, or merged away along the way.

🎯

Why it isn't random

Failure correlates with poor performance — the entities that disappear are disproportionately the weak ones, not a random cross-section.

↗

Direction of the bias

Optimistic, whenever exits are driven by poor results (the usual case). It looks like "the average result of everything ever tried," when it's really "the average of whatever happened to still be around later."

🌐

Not just stock-picking

Any cross-sectional universe — funds, currencies, bonds, counterparties — is vulnerable if it's built from "what still exists today."

Judging how good a school is by only interviewing students still enrolled — never the ones who dropped out — makes the school look great, because struggling students are exactly the ones most likely to have already left. A database built from "what's still around today" makes the identical mistake with funds, companies, or currencies.
Section 5

1996: when someone actually measured it

Survivorship bias went from a plausible-sounding worry to a quantified, documented distortion.

The classic study

Edwin J. Elton, Martin J. Gruber & Christopher R. Blake, "Survivorship Bias and Mutual Fund Performance," Review of Financial Studies, 9(4), 1996. They compared mutual fund performance computed from a survivors-only database against a more complete one that also captured funds that had since disappeared.

Result: the survivors-only version meaningfully overstated true average historical fund performance — not by an edge case, but as a systematic, measured effect.
Why "measured" matters

Turning survivorship bias from a plausible argument into a demonstrated, quantified distortion made it impossible to dismiss as hypothetical — which is exactly why it became the standard reference on the topic, and why point-in-time, failure-inclusive databases became standard practice in serious fund-performance research afterward.

Section 11 · It actually happened

1992: persistence with no skill in it

It actually happened

Early-1990s studies reported that mutual funds which beat their peers tended to do it again: the "hot hand". Brown, Goetzmann, Ibbotson & Ross (Review of Financial Studies, 1992) simulated managers with no skill, only different risk levels, and closed any fund whose cumulative results fell far enough. The persistence test on the survivors still found persistence. High-risk managers who drew bad results were gone; the ones left had mostly drawn good ones. Later work on databases that kept dead funds (notably Carhart, 1997) found much of the winners' persistence was explained by common factors and costs.

Section 12

Where the textbook breaks, and what this desk found

Desk test: nothing found level-touch, the desk's own survivorship episode: the original Sharpe 5.36 came from a survivorship filter (only touches that later "held" were counted). Barrier-free re-test with paired controls (since audited against the same lines shifted to the wrong price): the level increment over a random price is zero or negative.
Where the textbook version breaks

The usual telling treats survivorship as an optimistic shift in an average, fixed by adding the failures back. But survival depends on cumulative results, so the filter also builds a fake link between past and future performance. Persistence, momentum and "levels that hold" tests on a filtered sample inherit it.

Section 13

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Why is a single mutable "current value" table incompatible with point-in-time correctness?

A revision has to add a new dated record, not mutate the old one — otherwise the historical vintage is unrecoverable, forever.
Question 2

An "as of" query should key off which date?

A live system only ever knows what's already been published. Keying off the reference period instead hands a backtest information from the future relative to the query date.
Question 3

Why does survivorship bias produce a systematically optimistic result, not a random error?

The dropped entities aren't a random cross-section — they're disproportionately the failures, so removing them pulls the reported average up.
Question 4

80 survivors averaging 9%, 20 failures averaging −15% — what's the true, all-funds average?

(80 × 9% + 20 × −15%) ÷ 100 = (720 − 300) ÷ 100 = 4.2%.
Question 5

What did Elton, Gruber & Blake (1996) actually add to the survivorship-bias argument?

Comparing a survivors-only mutual fund database against a more complete one, they found the survivors-only version meaningfully overstated true average performance — a measured result, not just an argument.
Your call
Scenario drill · 21 August 2024

The payroll history under your backtest just changed

You are testing a strategy that trades the US dollar on the monthly payrolls report. Your backtest reads payroll figures from a table that keeps one current value per month. Today the Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes its preliminary annual benchmark revision: job growth in the twelve months to March 2024 was likely overstated by about 818,000. Your data vendor will fold the revision into the history. What do you do with the backtest?

  1. Re-run it on the updated table. Better data makes a better backtest.
    Better data about the economy, worse data about what traders saw. The strategy traded the first-print number on release day. The revised figure did not exist then, so feeding it in is lookahead that looks like ordinary history.
  2. Drop the twelve revised months and keep everything else.
    It removes this one leak, but payroll figures are revised routinely, month after month and again each year. The rest of the history has the same problem, just less visibly.
  3. Rebuild the history from first-release vintages, each tagged with the date it was published, and test on those.
    This is what a live system could have known on each day. Public archives that keep every vintage, such as the St. Louis Fed's ALFRED, exist for exactly this reason. Store the revisions as new dated records, never as overwrites.
  4. Freeze the table as it stands today so nothing changes again.
    Freezing keeps every revision that was already folded in place before today. You still cannot tell which numbers were first prints. What you need is the publication date on every value, not a frozen copy.

What happened. On 21 August 2024 the Bureau of Labor Statistics published a preliminary benchmark revision cutting payroll growth for the year to March 2024 by about 818,000 jobs. Any backtest run afterwards on a current-value table would quietly trade on weaker numbers that nobody had seen when those reports came out. The prices moved on the first prints, months earlier. Only a vintage-aware history can tell the two apart. The final benchmark, published in February 2025, came in smaller, at about −589,000 for March 2024, which is itself a reminder that the history keeps moving.

Sources: Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2024 preliminary benchmark revision notice, 21 Aug 2024 (−818,000, −0.5% of total nonfarm employment, for March 2024); BLS, CES benchmark revision 2024 (final revision); ALFRED, Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis, for data vintages.

Section 14

What to carry forward

01

Point-in-time correctness is an operational property of the data store — immutable dated snapshots, never one mutable "current" table overwritten in place.

02

Revisions are common, not rare — using today's fully-revised figure as if it were known on the original release date is a real, easy-to-miss lookahead bug.

03

An "as of" query must key off the publication date, never the reference period the data describes — those two dates are routinely far apart.

04

Survivorship bias silently excludes failures from a "what exists today" universe, and because exits are usually driven by poor results it's optimistic — never a random, harmless omission.

05

Elton, Gruber & Blake (1996) made this a measured fact, not an argument — a survivors-only mutual fund database meaningfully overstated true average performance.

06

A point-in-time universe fixes it, but it's genuinely harder and costlier to build than a "current universe, backfilled" database — it has to track membership as of each historical date.

07

Survivorship can invent a pattern, not just lift an average: a survivors-only fund database shows "hot hands" with no skill in it (1992). Desk verdict, NULL: a Sharpe 5.36 level strategy was a survivorship filter; honestly re-tested, the level adds zero or less over a random price.

What this now lets you do

Spot the difference between "a number that's stale" and "a number that never should have been visible yet" — and treat revision history and universe completeness as data-infrastructure requirements, not backtest afterthoughts. Ask, of any historical universe: does this include what failed, or only what's still here to ask about?
