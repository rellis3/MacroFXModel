Principal Component Analysis & Factor Regimes

A basket of currency pairs looks like many separate bets, but most days it's really one bet — "risk-on or risk-off" — wearing several costumes; PCA is the tool that measures exactly how true that is, and when.

CONCEPT — partially built (eigenvalues only) in this repo ~16 min read Prereqs: Cointegration & Statistical Arbitrage
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • PCA finds the direction (eigenvector) through a basket's return data along which it varies the most — the "eigenvalue" measures how much variance lies along that direction, and the explained variance ratio (\(\lambda_i / \sum_j \lambda_j\)) turns that into a headline percentage, e.g. "PC1 explains 72% of the basket's variance."
  • Because eigenvectors of a covariance/correlation matrix are mutually perpendicular, the principal components they define are uncorrelated by construction, not by empirical coincidence — PC1 captures the most shared variance, PC2 the next-most orthogonal to it, and so on.
  • A component's loadings (the entries of its eigenvector, one per instrument) show which pairs actually make up that shared factor — a large loading means that instrument drives the component; a near-zero loading means it barely participates.
  • This repo is partially built here already: this project already computes the eigenvalues of a correlation matrix (feeding an "effective number of bets" metric in this project's own diversification research), but it deliberately doesn't return the eigenvectors/loadings — the "which instruments drive PC1" piece this lesson covers is not yet built.
  • PCA is a descriptive/risk-visibility tool, not a trading signal by itself — correlation structure (and PC1 dominance) shifts across regimes, famously spiking toward 1 in a crisis, so any rule built on top of it needs its own OOS validation.
  • Real-world episode: March 2020's dash for cash. Firms and funds sold whatever was liquid to raise dollars, even Treasuries and gold, and almost every currency fell against the dollar at once. The basket collapsed onto one factor just when diversification was needed. This desk's PCA audit of its spread sleeves (spread-sleeve-factor-audit) found ~55% of raw risk was unpaid factor risk, with a correction that leaves the relative-value reading unconfirmed.
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

I've got positions in six currency pairs. That's six separate views, so I'm diversified. Right?

Look at a day when the dollar moves. Euro up, sterling up, Aussie up, yen and franc pairs down. Six pairs all telling you the same thing. You may hold one big dollar bet in six costumes.

How do I measure that, rather than eyeball it?

Principal component analysis. Imagine filming a swarm of fireflies. From most angles it's a tangle. From one angle, almost all the motion lines up. PCA finds that angle in your returns.

What does it actually compute?

Start with the grid of correlations between every pair. PCA finds the special directions in that grid, called eigenvectors. Each one comes with a number, its eigenvalue, which says how much of the movement lies along it.

And the first one is the big one.

Yes. The first principal component is the direction with the most variance. The second is the best direction at right angles to it, and so on. Because they're at right angles, they're uncorrelated by construction.

Give me a number.

Take euro-dollar, sterling-dollar and Aussie-yen. Euro and sterling are correlated at point seven five. Aussie-yen only about point two with either. The eigenvalues come out around one point eight two, point nine three and a quarter. They add to three. So the first component holds about sixty-one percent of everything that moves.

And which pairs are in it?

That's the loadings. Euro and sterling load heavily. Aussie-yen loads in the same direction, but less. It's partly doing its own thing.

Does that structure stay put?

No, and that's the danger. In March 2020's dash for cash, everyone needed dollars at once. They sold whatever was liquid, even Treasuries and gold, and the dollar rose against almost every currency. A basket with several stories collapsed onto one factor.

Right when I needed the diversification.

Exactly. A PCA fitted on calm months told you that you had several bets. In the crisis you had one. Correlations go towards one in a crash.

So it's a forecast of nothing?

It's a description of the past window. Great for seeing your real concentration. Not a promise about tomorrow, and not a trading signal on its own.

One line?

Count your bets by factors, not by positions, and assume a crisis will merge them into one.

Section 01

Why this matters

Open any multi-pair FX dashboard on a day the market moves and a familiar pattern shows up: EURUSD up, GBPUSD up, AUDUSD up, USDJPY down, USDCHF down — six or seven "different" pairs that all just told you the same thing, dollar weakness (or strength), at the same time. If you hold positions in all of them thinking you have seven independent views, you don't; you have one large, leveraged bet on a single common factor and some small residual noise around it. The opposite can also be true: most days the basket looks coordinated, and then one pair — say, a pair with an idiosyncratic domestic story, a central bank surprise, a commodity shock — decouples and moves on its own reasons. Knowing which situation you're in, quantitatively rather than by eyeballing a heatmap, is the entire point of this lesson.

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is the standard statistical tool for exactly this question: given a basket of correlated series, how many genuinely independent sources of variation are actually driving them, and how much of today's move is the shared one versus the pair-specific one? This repo already has a foot in this door — this project already computes the eigenvalues of a correlation matrix to build an "effective number of bets" metric in this project's own diversification research. This lesson explains the full machinery those eigenvalues come from, including the piece that isn't built yet: the eigenvectors, which tell you not just "how concentrated is the basket" but "concentrated around what — which pairs, in which direction, make up the dominant factor."

💡
Plain-English intuition

Finding the camera angle that shows the most motion

Imagine filming a swarm of fireflies at night from many possible camera angles. Most angles show a confusing tangle of dots moving in every direction. But there's usually one specific angle — one specific line of sight — from which the swarm's motion looks the biggest and clearest: maybe the fireflies are mostly drifting left-to-right together, and if you point your camera straight down that left-right axis, almost all of the apparent motion is captured in that single dimension. Point the camera 90 degrees off that axis and you'd barely see anything move at all — you'd have picked the angle with the least information.

PCA is the mathematical procedure for finding that best camera angle automatically, except instead of fireflies in physical space it works on a cloud of data points — in our case, each point is one day's return across several currency pairs, so a 3-pair basket produces a cloud of points in 3-dimensional space (one axis per pair). PCA finds the direction through that cloud along which the data is most spread out (the "first principal component," PC1), then the next-best direction that's perpendicular to the first and captures most of what's left (PC2), and so on. "Spread out" here is a direct stand-in for "variance" — a direction along which the cloud is spread out wide is a direction that explains a lot of the day-to-day movement; a direction along which the cloud is thin is a direction that barely matters. If most of the cloud's spread lives along one single direction, that means most of what's "moving" in your basket is really one underlying thing, viewed from several angles (several currency pairs) at once.

Start from data you already know how to build: daily (or hourly, or weekly — PCA doesn't care about the horizon) returns for \(N\) instruments over \(T\) time periods. Arrange that as a table with \(T\) rows (one per day) and \(N\) columns (one per instrument) — call this matrix \(R\). Everything below builds up from that table.

Step 1 — the covariance matrix

Before you can ask "what's the shared factor," you need a precise way to describe how every pair of instruments in the basket co-moves. That's the covariance matrix. For two return series \(x\) and \(y\), covariance is the average product of their simultaneous deviations from their own means:

\[ \text{Cov}(x,y) = \frac{1}{T-1}\sum_{t=1}^{T} (x_t - \bar{x})(y_t - \bar{y}) \]
xₜ − x̄, yₜ − ȳeach series' deviation from its own mean, on the same day
Cov(x,y)positive if they tend to co-move, negative if they move opposite, ≈0 if unrelated

Read this as: on each day \(t\), take how far \(x\) was from its own average that day, take how far \(y\) was from its own average that day, and multiply the two. If \(x\) and \(y\) tend to be above-average on the same days and below-average on the same days, most of those products are positive (positive times positive, or negative times negative), so the sum — and the covariance — comes out positive: they co-move. If one tends to be above-average exactly when the other is below-average, the products are mostly negative: they move opposite each other. If there's no consistent relationship, positive and negative products roughly cancel and covariance comes out near zero. (Covariance's units are messy — return-of-\(x\) times return-of-\(y\) — which is why the more familiar correlation, covariance divided by the product of the two standard deviations, is usually reported instead; correlation is the same idea rescaled to always sit in \([-1, 1]\). PCA can be run on either the covariance matrix or the correlation matrix — on a correlation matrix every instrument is automatically put on the same footing regardless of how volatile it is individually, which is usually what you want across a mixed FX basket where, say, GBPJPY is naturally noisier day-to-day than EURCHF.)

For an \(N\)-instrument basket, you compute this pairwise number for every combination and arrange the results in an \(N \times N\) grid — the covariance matrix \(\Sigma\) (capital sigma). Its diagonal entries are each instrument's own variance (covariance of a series with itself); its off-diagonal entries are the pairwise covariances. This is exactly the same object this project's own pair-ranking tooling already renders as a heatmap for you (using correlation instead of raw covariance) — PCA is the next step after you have that grid, not a replacement for it.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Covariance is a simple two-step check repeated every day: was pair A above or below its own normal that day, and was pair B above or below its own normal that same day? If they're usually both above-normal together (or both below-normal together), covariance comes out positive — they're moving as a team. If one's up exactly when the other is down, covariance comes out negative — they're pulling in opposite directions.

Step 2 — what an eigenvector and eigenvalue actually are

This is the one piece of linear algebra this lesson needs, and it's worth building from scratch rather than waving at "diagonalize the matrix." A matrix like \(\Sigma\) can be thought of as a machine that takes any direction (a vector) and transforms it — stretching it, squishing it, rotating it. Feed the matrix almost any starting direction and the output direction usually points somewhere different from the input.

An eigenvector of \(\Sigma\) is special: it's one of the (very few) directions where the matrix's transformation doesn't rotate the vector at all — it only stretches or shrinks it, leaving it pointing along the exact same line. The amount of stretching is the corresponding eigenvalue. Formally, \(v\) is an eigenvector of \(\Sigma\) with eigenvalue \(\lambda\) (lambda) if:

\[ \Sigma v = \lambda v \]

Read this literally: multiplying the matrix \(\Sigma\) by the vector \(v\) gives back the same vector \(v\), just scaled by the number \(\lambda\) — nothing about \(v\)'s direction changed, only its length. For a covariance matrix specifically (which is always symmetric and, for real data, "positive semi-definite" — a technical guarantee that its eigenvalues are never negative), there's a beautiful, guaranteed result: an \(N \times N\) covariance matrix always has exactly \(N\) eigenvectors, and — this is the key fact for everything below — those \(N\) eigenvectors are mutually perpendicular (technically: orthogonal) to each other. They form a complete, non-overlapping set of directions that together span the whole space the data lives in.

That last property is what makes eigenvectors of a covariance matrix so useful as "factors": because they're mutually perpendicular by construction, the amount the data varies along one eigenvector is completely uninformative about how much it varies along another. They are, by the geometry alone, uncorrelated with each other — not because you asked for that, but because perpendicular directions in the data cloud can't share variance with each other by definition. This is exactly the "best camera angle" from the intuition box, generalized: instead of one best angle, a covariance matrix gives you a whole set of mutually perpendicular best angles, ranked by how much of the data's spread each one captures.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Think of the covariance matrix as a machine that grabs any arrow and yanks it around. Almost any arrow you feed it gets bent to point somewhere new. But there are a couple of special arrows that the machine only stretches or shrinks — it never bends them off their own line. Those special arrows are eigenvectors, and how much they got stretched is the eigenvalue — the bigger the stretch, the more that direction matters.

Step 3 — principal components, ranked by eigenvalue

Each eigenvector \(v_i\) of \(\Sigma\), paired with its eigenvalue \(\lambda_i\), defines one principal component. Sort them by eigenvalue from largest to smallest: \(\lambda_1 \geq \lambda_2 \geq \dots \geq \lambda_N\). The eigenvector with the largest eigenvalue is PC1 — the single direction along which the basket's daily returns vary the most, i.e. the direction that best explains "what happened today" in the fewest dimensions. PC2 is the next-largest eigenvalue's eigenvector — the direction, perpendicular to PC1, that best explains whatever variance PC1 didn't. And so on down to PC\(_N\), which by the time you get there is usually mopping up scraps of noise.

Each day's actual return vector can then be re-expressed not as "3.2 pips on EURUSD, -1.1 pips on GBPUSD, ..." but as "this many units along PC1, this many along PC2, ..." — a re-description of the exact same information in a rotated coordinate system chosen so that the first few axes carry as much of the story as possible. A day's score on PC1 (its coordinate along that eigenvector) is called its PC1 score; it is a single number that stands in for "how much did the common factor move today."

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Once you've found all those special stretch-only arrows, you line them up from "stretched the most" to "stretched the least." The one stretched the most (PC1) is the single direction that captures the biggest chunk of how the whole basket actually moves — like finding the one camera angle that shows the most action, then the next-best angle perpendicular to it, and so on.

Step 4 — explained variance ratio

The eigenvalues themselves tell you exactly how much of the basket's total variance each component accounts for, because the sum of all \(N\) eigenvalues equals the total variance in the system (the sum of the diagonal of \(\Sigma\), a fact called the trace being preserved). The explained variance ratio of component \(i\) is simply its eigenvalue's share of that total:

\[ \text{EVR}_i = \frac{\lambda_i}{\sum_{j=1}^{N} \lambda_j} \]
λᵢcomponent i's own eigenvalue
Σⱼλⱼthe total variance across all N components
EVRᵢcomponent i's share of the total — sums to 1 across all components

If \(\text{EVR}_1 = 0.72\), that means 72% of the total variance across every instrument in the basket, every day, is captured by a single direction — PC1. The remaining 28% is spread across PC2 through PC\(_N\), the more idiosyncratic structure. This single number is the headline output of a PCA-on-a-basket exercise: it directly answers "how much of what moves in this basket is one thing."

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

If you add up how much every special direction got stretched, that total is the whole basket's total wobble. Explained variance ratio just asks what fraction of that total wobble belongs to one particular direction — if PC1's share is 72%, that means almost three-quarters of everything moving in the basket, on any given day, boils down to one shared story.

Step 5 — loadings

An eigenvector \(v_i\) is itself a list of \(N\) numbers — one per instrument in the basket — because it's a direction in the same \(N\)-dimensional space the return data lives in. Those numbers are called the component's loadings: how much, and in which direction (sign), each original instrument contributes to that component. A loading close to zero means that instrument barely participates in this component; a large-magnitude loading means it's a dominant contributor.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

A loading is just "how much does this one instrument show up in this particular shared story." If EURUSD has a big loading on PC1 and some obscure pair has a loading near zero, it means EURUSD is a starring character in whatever PC1 represents, while that other pair is basically an extra who barely appears in that scene at all.

\(R\)The \(T \times N\) return matrix — \(T\) days of history down the rows, \(N\) instruments across the columns. The raw data everything below is computed from.

\(\lambda_i\)(lambda) The eigenvalue paired with \(v_i\) — how much variance in the data lies along that direction. Always \(\geq 0\) for a covariance/correlation matrix. Components are ordered so \(\lambda_1 \geq \lambda_2 \geq \dots\), i.e. PC1 has the largest eigenvalue.
PC1, PC2, …The principal components themselves, i.e. the eigenvectors ranked by eigenvalue. PC1 is "the direction that explains the most variance"; each subsequent PC explains the most of what's left, subject to being perpendicular to every PC before it.

Read across a component's loadings to see which instruments define it; read down a single instrument's loadings across PC1, PC2, … to see which components that instrument shows up in.

One sign-convention wrinkle worth flagging before the worked example: eigenvectors are only defined up to an overall sign flip — \(v\) and \(-v\) satisfy \(\Sigma v = \lambda v\) equally well, since \(\Sigma(-v) = -\Sigma v = -\lambda v = \lambda(-v)\). Different PCA implementations may hand you PC1 with all-positive loadings or all-negative loadings for the exact same underlying factor — the direction is the same, only the labeling of "which end is positive" is arbitrary. What's never arbitrary is the relative sign and magnitude between instruments within one component — that's the actual content.

🧮
Worked example

Three FX pairs, one common driver and one holdout

Suppose you take daily returns on three pairs — EURUSD, GBPUSD, and AUDJPY — over some historical window, and their pairwise correlation matrix (a stand-in for a normalized covariance matrix, so PCA can be run directly on it) comes back looking like this — plausible numbers, not pulled from real data, but internally consistent with a market where the first two pairs share a strong dollar-driven common factor and the third is more independent:

\[ \Sigma \approx \begin{pmatrix} 1.00 & 0.75 & 0.20 \\ 0.75 & 1.00 & 0.15 \\ 0.20 & 0.15 & 1.00 \end{pmatrix} \quad \begin{array}{l} \text{row/col 1: EURUSD} \\ \text{row/col 2: GBPUSD} \\ \text{row/col 3: AUDJPY} \end{array} \]

Read the off-diagonal entries as ordinary correlations: EURUSD and GBPUSD move together strongly (0.75) — unsurprising, both are "USD on one side" majors that share a lot of dollar-flow-driven variance. AUDJPY is only weakly linked to either (0.20 and 0.15) — it has its own commodity/risk-sentiment/carry drivers that don't fully overlap with the EUR/GBP dollar-bloc story.

Running PCA on this matrix (the eigen-decomposition itself is mechanical — this is exactly what this project already computes via a Jacobi rotation, described below) would produce three eigenvalues summing to 3 (the trace of a 3×3 correlation matrix, since every diagonal entry is 1). Given the structure above, expect something like \(\lambda_1 \approx 1.82\), \(\lambda_2 \approx 0.93\), \(\lambda_3 \approx 0.25\) — so \(\text{EVR}_1 \approx 1.82/3 \approx 61\%\). PC1's loadings would come out roughly \((0.68,\ 0.67,\ 0.29)\) — EURUSD and GBPUSD loading almost equally and strongly (they're the pair driving the shared factor), AUDJPY loading in the same direction but noticeably smaller, since some, but not all, of its variance is shared with the other two.

Contrast that with a basket where AUDJPY were fully idiosyncratic (correlations of 0.02 and -0.03 instead of 0.20 and 0.15): PC1 would then load almost entirely on EURUSD and GBPUSD alone — something like \((0.71, 0.71, -0.01)\) — with \(\text{EVR}_1\) a little lower, about 58% (almost all of it the two pairs' shared variance, out of three pairs' worth of total variance) rather than 61%, and a separate component (likely PC2 or PC3) would load almost entirely on AUDJPY by itself, since a fully idiosyncratic instrument can't be "explained" by any shared direction — it needs its own dimension. That contrast is the whole diagnostic in miniature: a high loading on PC1 for every instrument, with a high \(\text{EVR}_1\), says "this basket is really one trade in several costumes"; a pair sitting near-zero on PC1 and picking up its own later component says "this one is actually doing something different."

Section 03

It actually happened: March 2020, when correlations went to one

The caveat later in this lesson, that correlations "tend to spike toward 1 in a crisis", is easy to nod along to. In March 2020 it showed up across almost every market in the space of two weeks.

It actually happened

March 2020: the dash for cash

As COVID-19 spread and lockdowns began, the S&P 500 fell about 34% from its 19 February peak to its 23 March low, and the VIX closed at a record 82.69 on 16 March. What made the middle of March unusual was what got sold. Firms, funds and foreign borrowers all needed dollars at once, to meet margin calls, redemptions and dollar debts. They sold whatever they could sell quickly, including assets that normally rally in a panic. For several days long-dated US Treasury yields rose during an equity crash, and gold fell too.

In FX, the dollar rose sharply against almost every other currency at the same time. In the language of this lesson, a basket of pairs that normally carries several distinct stories (a euro story, a commodity-bloc story, a yen story) collapsed onto one factor: the dollar. That is what a dominant PC1 looks like. A large \(\lambda_1\) share, and an "effective number of bets" close to one, arrived exactly when diversification was supposed to help.

The Federal Reserve cut rates to zero on 15 March and widened its dollar swap lines with other central banks on 15 and 19 March, so they could lend dollars on to their own banks. On 23 March it announced open-ended asset purchases and new credit facilities. The dollar squeeze eased after that, and with it the one-factor market.

Where the textbook version breaks. PCA summarises one covariance matrix for the whole window, as if the structure inside it were constant. A PCA fitted on the calm months before March 2020 described a market with several factors, and that market disappeared in days. Correlation is also an average linear co-movement. Assets tend to fall together more tightly than they rise together, so a calm-period eigenvalue spectrum overstates how many independent bets you hold in the tail, which is when it matters most.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Most days the fireflies drift in a few different directions, so you need a few camera angles to see them all. In March 2020 something scared all of them at once and they flew the same way. One camera angle showed everything. If you'd thought you were watching several separate swarms, you found out you were really watching one.

The tempting trading claim: strip out the dominant factors with PCA, and what's left is a cleaner, diversified bet. This desk ran exactly that audit on its spread sleeves, and separately tested what follows a day when everything falls together.

Desk test: held up spread-sleeve-factor-audit: are the yield-spread sleeves a disguised dollar bet? Currency-space PCA, K=2 by noise band, a factor-neutral book, costs on every leg including hedges. Neutralising raised out-of-sample Sharpe (2Y 1.02→1.33, 10Y 0.58→1.01, combined 0.98→1.36) while cutting OOS vol about 60–65% (2Y 14.1%→5.1%). About 55% of the raw book's risk was factor risk that did not pay. Read it with its own caveats: in-sample is weak for every version, the OOS strength sits in the 2022–24 rate-divergence cycle, and a correction dated 2026-09-22 notes the PC-only hedge can leave net USD in the book. Until a hedge that also zeroes net USD is run, the relative-value claim is unconfirmed.
Desk test: held up breadth-all-down: the nearest thing this desk has tested to a one-factor day. All six indices close down on one session (n=1,002 since 2008): the next five sessions run wider, SPX500 +0.31 ATR [+0.17, +0.45], NAS100 +0.26 [+0.15, +0.38]. Two in a row (n=243): +0.55 and +0.35. Direction five sessions later is 58–61% up, the ordinary drift. A range fact, not a bounce call.

Section 04

How practitioners actually use this

In FX and rates specifically, running PCA across the G10 majors (or across a curve of interest-rate tenors, where it's an even older and more standard tool) very reliably produces a PC1 that is intuitively readable as a broad "dollar factor" or "risk sentiment factor" — most major pairs load onto it with the same sign relative to USD strength/weakness, because a large share of daily FX variance really is driven by shared macro inputs: global risk appetite, US rate expectations, broad dollar flows. PC2 and beyond typically pick up more regional or idiosyncratic structure — a euro-area-specific factor, a commodity-bloc factor, and so on. On rate curves, the classic and extremely well-documented result (going back decades in the fixed-income literature) is that the first three principal components of a yield curve's covariance matrix map almost exactly onto "level" (parallel shifts), "slope" (steepening/flattening), and "curvature" (butterfly) — a result stable enough that many trading desks quote curve risk directly in level/slope/curvature units rather than raw tenor-by-tenor exposure.

Two concrete uses fall out of this:

Risk aggregation

A portfolio across many correlated instruments can have its total risk decomposed into "how much exposure do I have to PC1 (the common factor) versus PC2, PC3, …" — a cleaner view of true concentration than a large pairwise correlation grid, because a grid full of medium-to-high numbers doesn't tell you whether that's one dominant factor or several moderate ones; the eigenvalue spectrum does. This is exactly the motivation behind an "effective number of bets" metric.

Anomaly / regime detection

Watching each day's PC1 score, and how much of that day's variance PC1 alone explained, flags unusual days — an instrument that normally loads heavily on PC1 suddenly barely moving with it, or a normally-idiosyncratic instrument suddenly co-moving tightly with the pack, is often a sign something instrument-specific happened rather than a shared macro move.

The caveat: PCA is a descriptive/summarization tool, not a forecast — it tells you cleanly what the historical covariance structure looked like over the window you fed it, not what tomorrow's structure will be, and that structure can and does shift across regimes (calm markets versus crisis markets famously show very different PC1 dominance — correlations, and therefore \(\text{EVR}_1\), tend to spike toward 1 in a crisis, the well-known "correlations go to 1 in a crash" phenomenon).
⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where PCA on a market basket goes wrong in practice

Treating the loadings/eigenvalues as fixed. A covariance matrix — and therefore its eigenvectors and eigenvalues — is estimated over whatever window of history you feed it, and market correlation structure genuinely changes across regimes (the crisis-correlations-go- to-1 effect mentioned above is the sharpest version of this). A PCA run on the last two years might describe a materially different factor structure than one run on the last two months. Report the estimation window explicitly, and treat PC1/EVR as regime-dependent readings, not permanent properties of a basket.

Not rescaling instruments with very different volatility before running PCA on raw covariance. Covariance (unlike correlation) is scale-dependent — an instrument with much larger absolute daily moves (say, a volatile emerging-market cross next to a stable G10 pair) mechanically dominates a raw-covariance-based PCA regardless of how correlated it actually is with anything else, simply because its variance term is bigger. Running PCA on the correlation matrix (equivalent to standardizing every series to unit variance first) is the standard fix, and the one used throughout this lesson and in this project's own implementation.

Reading "uncorrelated by construction" as "economically independent." PC2, PC3, … are mathematically guaranteed to be uncorrelated with PC1 over the exact sample used to fit them — that's a property of eigenvectors, not evidence about the real world. It does not mean PC2 represents some clean, interpretable economic story ("the euro- specific factor") — later components increasingly mix genuine idiosyncratic signal with plain estimation noise, especially past the first two or three when the basket isn't huge. Don't over-interpret every component's loadings as meaningful; PC1 (and often PC2, on a large liquid basket) is usually the only one worth naming.

Small \(N\), unstable eigenvectors. With only 3-5 instruments and a modest sample size, eigenvector directions — especially for the smaller, later components — can be noisy and sensitive to which exact days are in the sample. Eigenvalues are comparatively more stable than eigenvectors under sampling noise, particularly when two eigenvalues are close together (the corresponding eigenvector directions become poorly determined and can rotate into each other with small data perturbations — a classic numerical-linear-algebra caveat, not specific to finance). Don't over-read a precise-looking loading number to two decimal places from a small basket over a short window.

PCA is not causal. A high loading tells you an instrument co-moves strongly with the dominant statistical direction — it does not tell you why, and doesn't distinguish "this pair is driven by the same macro cause as the others" from "this pair happens to be correlated with the others over this window for reasons that won't persist." Same caution as correlation itself, one layer up.

Scenario drill · 16 March 2020

Six currency pairs, and they are all moving as one

Last night the Federal Reserve cut rates to zero and widened its dollar swap lines. Firms and funds are scrambling for dollars and selling whatever is liquid, even Treasuries and gold. The dollar is rising against almost every currency at once. You hold six FX positions, sized as six separate bets because a PCA fitted on the calm months before showed several distinct factors. What do you do?

  1. Keep the sizes. The PCA says you hold several independent bets.
    That PCA describes a market that has just disappeared. It was fitted on calm months, and a calm-period spectrum overstates how many independent bets you hold in the tail, which is now.
  2. Add two more pairs to spread the risk further.
    In a one-factor market the new pairs load on the same dollar factor. You add size to the one bet you already have too much of.
  3. Re-run the PCA on the last few weeks, treat the book as one dollar bet, and cut the gross size to what you would hold in a single position.
    This is what the lesson's warning means in practice. Count bets by factors, not by positions. With one factor dominating, the honest size is the size of one trade.
  4. Hold for now. The Fed acted last night, so the dollar squeeze should ease.
    It did ease eventually, but you cannot know when. Holding a book sized for several bets while it behaves as one is a bet on the timing of policy, not risk management.

What happened. That day the VIX closed at a record 82.69. The Fed widened its dollar swap lines again on 19 March and, on 23 March, announced open-ended asset purchases and new credit facilities. The S&P 500 hit its low that day, about 34% below its 19 February peak. The dollar squeeze eased after that, and with it the one-factor market.

Section 05

Key Takeaways

✓A basket of correlated pairs often really is one bet. If PC1 explains 72% of variance, most of what moves your book on any given day is a single shared factor wearing several costumes.
✓Eigenvectors of a covariance matrix are perpendicular by construction. Principal components are uncorrelated because of the geometry, not because you found something — PC1 captures the most shared variance, PC2 the next-most orthogonal to it, and so on.
✓Loadings tell you which instruments make up a factor. A large loading means that instrument drives the component; a near-zero loading means it barely participates — the diagnostic eigenvalues alone can't give you.
✓This repo has eigenvalues, not eigenvectors, yet. This project's own eigenvalue computation feeds "effective number of bets" but deliberately doesn't return loadings — the "which instruments drive PC1" piece is a documented, unbuilt extension.
⚠Factor structure shifts across regimes. Correlations — and PC1 dominance — famously spike toward 1 in a crisis; a PCA fit on calm markets can describe a very different structure than one fit during stress.
⚠PCA is descriptive, not causal, and not a forecast. High loadings tell you co-movement, not why, and don't predict tomorrow's structure — any trading rule built on top still needs its own honest OOS validation.
⚠Diversification measured in calm markets disappears when it's needed. In March 2020's dash for cash, currencies collapsed onto one dollar factor and even Treasuries and gold were sold. On this desk, PCA-neutralising the spread sleeves raised OOS Sharpe and showed ~55% of raw risk was unpaid factor risk, but a 2026-09-22 correction leaves the relative-value reading unconfirmed until net USD is also hedged.
Section 06

Self-test

Q1. If PC1 explains 70% of the variance across your FX basket, what does that imply about the diversification benefit of holding all those pairs at once?
It implies the diversification benefit is much smaller than the number of positions suggests. Holding, say, 6 pairs where PC1 explains 70% of variance means roughly 70% of what moves your combined book on any given day is really one common factor moving through all 6 positions at once — not 6 independent sources of risk. The "effective number of bets" is much closer to 1-2 than to 6. This doesn't mean the positions are literally the same trade, but it does mean a shock to that common factor (e.g. a broad dollar move) will hit most of the book in the same direction simultaneously, which is exactly the concentration a naive position count hides.
Q2. Why are principal components always uncorrelated with each other, by construction, rather than as an empirical finding?
Because principal components are the eigenvectors of a covariance (or correlation) matrix, and for a symmetric matrix like that, distinct eigenvectors are mathematically guaranteed to be mutually perpendicular (orthogonal) to each other. Perpendicular directions in a data cloud cannot share variance with each other by geometric definition — the amount the data spreads along one such direction says nothing about how much it spreads along a direction at 90 degrees to it. So "uncorrelated" here isn't a pattern you discovered in the data; it's a structural consequence of how the components were constructed in the first place.
Q3. What is the difference between a loading and an eigenvalue — what question does each one answer?
An eigenvalue (\(\lambda_i\)) answers "how much total variance does this component capture" — it's a single number per component, describing the component's overall importance across the whole basket. A loading is a per-instrument number within a component — one entry of the eigenvector \(v_i\) — answering "how much, and in which direction, does this specific instrument contribute to this specific component." A component can have a large eigenvalue (it explains a lot of variance overall) while a particular instrument still has a small loading on it (that instrument barely participates in that particular shared factor, even though the factor itself is important for the basket as a whole).
Q4. In the worked example, why did AUDJPY's PC1 loading shrink (and a new component pick it up) when its correlations to EURUSD/GBPUSD were lowered from 0.20/0.15 to 0.02/-0.03?
PC1 is the direction of maximum shared variance — it's built to capture whatever moves multiple instruments together. When AUDJPY's correlation with the other two pairs dropped toward zero, there was less shared movement left for PC1 to capture from that instrument, so PC1's "camera angle" tilted to point more purely along the EURUSD/GBPUSD shared direction, and AUDJPY's loading on it shrank correspondingly. But AUDJPY's own variance didn't disappear — it still moves, just not in sync with the others — so that variance has to show up somewhere in the eigen-decomposition (the total must still sum correctly). It shows up as its own, separate component, because a fully idiosyncratic instrument's variance can't be explained by any direction shared with something else; it needs a dimension of its own.
Q5. Why does running PCA on the raw covariance matrix (instead of the correlation matrix) risk giving misleading results across a mixed basket of, say, a stable G10 pair and a volatile EM cross?
Covariance carries the units and scale of the underlying series — an instrument with much larger absolute daily moves has a mechanically larger variance term, regardless of how correlated it actually is with the rest of the basket. PCA on raw covariance will tend to let that high-variance instrument dominate the top component simply because it contributes the most raw variance, not because it shares the most structure with the others. Running PCA on the correlation matrix instead (equivalent to standardizing every series to unit variance first) removes that scale effect, so the components reflect actual co-movement structure rather than which instrument happens to be loudest.
Q6. This project already computes eigenvalues from a correlation matrix, feeding an "effective number of bets" metric. What specifically would need to be added to get from that to a usable "what does PC1 look like today" diagnostic, and why isn't the eigenvalue number alone enough?
The eigenvalues alone tell you how concentrated the basket's variance is overall (a single summary number — the effective number of bets) but not around what. To answer "what does PC1 look like," you need the eigenvectors — the loadings — which the current implementation deliberately doesn't return (it only needed the eigenvalue spectrum for the effective-bets formulas). The eigenvectors fall out of the same Jacobi-rotation algorithm as the accumulated rotation matrix, so extracting them is an extension of existing code rather than a new algorithm — but until that's built, the existing tooling can tell you "your book is concentrated" without being able to tell you "concentrated in EURUSD and GBPUSD specifically."

Further reading
  • Karl Pearson, "On Lines and Planes of Closest Fit to Systems of Points in Space," Philosophical Magazine, 1901 — the original geometric formulation PCA is built on (finding the best-fitting line/plane through a cloud of points), predating its statistical/finance use by decades.
  • Harold Hotelling, "Analysis of a Complex of Statistical Variables into Principal Components," Journal of Educational Psychology, 1933 — the paper that formalized PCA in its modern statistical form and coined "principal components."
  • Ruey S. Tsay, Analysis of Financial Time Series, Wiley — the standard financial-econometrics textbook treatment of PCA/factor models applied to asset returns and yield curves, including the level/slope/curvature result referenced above.
  • Barra (now part of MSCI) multi-factor risk model methodology — the commercial template for statistical/fundamental factor risk models built on this exact eigen-decomposition idea, widely used across equity and multi-asset risk management; general methodology overviews are publicly available from MSCI.
  • Robert Litterman & José Scheinkman, "Common Factors Affecting Bond Returns," Journal of Fixed Income, 1991 — the classic empirical demonstration of the level/slope/curvature PCA decomposition on yield curves referenced in the practitioner-use section.
