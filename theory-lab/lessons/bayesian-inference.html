Bayesian Inference & Updating

A rule for turning "here is what I believed" plus "here is what I just observed" into "here is what I should believe now" — and the reason a positive signal is almost never as strong as it feels.

CONCEPT — a reasoning framework, not a signal ~14 min read Prereqs: Efficient Market Hypothesis (helpful, not required)

Why this matters

Every strategy in this repo eventually asks the same question in disguise: "given what just happened, how much should I believe X?" X might be "this is a trend day, not a reversion day," or "this level will hold," or "this regime classifier's BUY flag is real." The honest answer to that question is never just "yes" or "no" — it is a number between 0 and 1, and that number depends on two separate things: how rare X was before you saw the new evidence, and how strong the new evidence actually is. Bayesian inference is the formal, provably-correct procedure for combining those two things. It is not a trading indicator; it is the logic that should sit underneath any indicator that claims to update your belief in something.

The reason this is lesson 2 and not lesson 12 is that almost every mistake described later in this series — p-hacking, overfitting a classifier, trusting a backtest's win rate at face value — is, underneath, a failure to account for a prior. If you only remember one thing from this page, make it this: a piece of evidence is not the same thing as the probability it implies. How much a piece of evidence should move your belief depends on how likely the thing was to begin with.

Plain-English intuition

Updating a belief, not replacing it

Imagine you think there's a 20% chance it will rain today (your prior — what you believed before checking anything). You glance outside and see dark clouds. Dark clouds are more common on days it rains than on days it doesn't, so seeing them should raise your belief in rain. But it shouldn't send it straight to 100% — dark clouds sometimes pass without rain, and it's still true that most days, of any cloud cover, it doesn't rain in most climates. Bayesian inference is the arithmetic for exactly how much to move your belief: not all the way to certainty, and not by some vague gut-feel amount, but by a precise quantity that depends on (a) how rare rain was to start with and (b) how much more common dark clouds are on rainy days than on dry ones. Do this again tomorrow — today's updated belief becomes tomorrow's starting point — and you have sequential Bayesian updating, which is what a well-built classifier or filter is implicitly supposed to be doing every time new data arrives.

The math, step by step

Everything here is built from one definition: the definition of conditional probability. Nothing is assumed beyond that — Bayes' theorem is not a separate axiom, it is an algebraic rearrangement of this one definition, discovered by noticing that a shared quantity can be written two different ways.

Let H stand for a hypothesis (a claim you're not sure is true — "this is a trend day") and E stand for evidence (something you actually observed — "the classifier fired BUY"). P(H) means "the probability that H is true," a number between 0 (definitely false) and 1 (definitely true). P(H \cap E) means "the probability that both H and E are true" (∩ is set-intersection notation: "and"). The conditional probability P(H \mid E) — read "the probability of H, given E" — is defined as the probability of both happening, divided by the probability of the condition happening at all:

\[ P(H \mid E) = \frac{P(H \cap E)}{P(E)} \]

This is just restricting your universe: out of all the times E happens, what fraction of those times does H also happen? Rearranging this definition (multiply both sides by P(E)) gives the "multiplication rule":

\[ P(H \cap E) = P(H \mid E)\, P(E) \]

But P(H \cap E) is symmetric — "H and E" is the same event as "E and H" — so by the exact same definition applied the other way around:

\[ P(H \cap E) = P(E \mid H)\, P(H) \]

Both expressions equal the same quantity, P(H \cap E), so they equal each other:

\[ P(H \mid E)\, P(E) = P(E \mid H)\, P(H) \]

Divide both sides by P(E) and you have derived Bayes' theorem — not assumed it, derived it, from nothing but "and" being symmetric:

\[ P(H \mid E) = \frac{P(E \mid H)\, P(H)}{P(E)} \]

Every term in that equation has a name, because in practice you compute them from different sources and it matters which is which:

\(H\)The hypothesis — the thing you want to know the truth of (e.g. "today is a trend day").
\(E\)The evidence — what you actually observed (e.g. "the classifier flagged trend-day").
\(P(H)\)The prior — your belief in H before seeing this evidence. Usually the base rate: how often is H true in general?
\(P(E \mid H)\)The likelihood — if H were true, how probable is this exact evidence? (Not "how probable is H" — a common confusion, addressed below.)
\(P(E)\)The evidence / marginal likelihood — the overall probability of observing E at all, averaged over every way H could be true or false.
\(P(H \mid E)\)The posterior — your updated belief in H after seeing E. This is the answer.

P(E) is the term people usually get stuck computing, because it isn't handed to you directly — it has to be built from every way E could happen. If H is binary (true or false), the law of total probability expands it as:

\[ P(E) = P(E \mid H)\,P(H) \;+\; P(E \mid \lnot H)\,P(\lnot H) \]

where ¬H ("not H") is the complement of H, and P(\lnot H) = 1 - P(H). In words: the total chance of seeing the evidence is the chance of seeing it when H is true, weighted by how often H is true, plus the chance of seeing it when H is false, weighted by how often H is false. This is the step that most intuitive, non-Bayesian reasoning skips entirely — people anchor on P(E \mid H) (how good the test is) and quietly forget P(E \mid \lnot H) (how often you'd see the same evidence for the wrong reason). The worked example below makes that skip concrete and shows exactly how much it costs you.

Sequential updating. Bayes' theorem doesn't care whether P(H) came from a textbook base rate or from yesterday's posterior. That's the whole trick of doing this repeatedly: today's posterior P(H \mid E_1) becomes tomorrow's prior when new evidence E_2 arrives, and you apply the theorem again:

\[ P(H \mid E_1, E_2) = \frac{P(E_2 \mid H, E_1)\, P(H \mid E_1)}{P(E_2 \mid E_1)} \]

If E_2 is independent of E_1 given H (a common, simplifying but not automatic assumption), this collapses to just plugging the old posterior in as the new prior and multiplying by the new likelihood ratio. This is what "the market updates on news" or "a classifier updates on each new bar" formally means: not "throw out the old belief and start over," but "multiply the old belief by how much more (or less) likely the new data makes it."

Bayes factors. Sometimes you don't want the raw posterior probability — you want to compare two competing hypotheses directly (e.g. "trend day" vs. "reversion day") and ask how strongly the evidence favors one over the other, independent of how you felt about them beforehand. Take the ratio of the two posteriors:

\[ \frac{P(H_1 \mid E)}{P(H_2 \mid E)} = \underbrace{\frac{P(E \mid H_1)}{P(E \mid H_2)}}_{\text{Bayes factor, } K} \times \underbrace{\frac{P(H_1)}{P(H_2)}}_{\text{prior odds}} \]

The term K is the Bayes factor: it says exactly how many times more likely the evidence was under \(H_1\) than under \(H_2\), with the prior completely factored out. That separation is the point — K tells you what the evidence itself is worth, so two people with different priors can agree on K and still (correctly) land on different posteriors. A rough, widely used convention (Harold Jeffreys, 1961) for interpreting K: 1–3 is "barely worth mentioning," 3–10 is "substantial" evidence, 10–30 is "strong," and above 100 is "decisive." A Bayes factor of 3 means the data is 3× more consistent with \(H_1\) than \(H_2\) — worth noting, not worth betting the account on. A Bayes factor of 30 is a different order of claim entirely.

Worked example

A trend-day classifier's "80% accurate" is not the probability it's right

Suppose a regime classifier is built to flag "trend day" vs. "reversion day," and its validation report says it's 80% accurate: when a day really is a trend day, it fires the trend-day flag 80% of the time (and correspondingly, when a day really is a reversion day, it correctly stays quiet 80% of the time — treat both error rates as equal for simplicity). Separately, suppose only 20% of days are actually trend days in this market — most days revert. The classifier fires the flag today. What is the actual probability that today really is a trend day?

The intuitive, wrong answer is "80%," because that's the number that got quoted. The correct answer requires the prior. Define:

\(H\) = "today is a trend day," so \(P(H) = 0.20\) (the base rate) and \(P(\lnot H) = 0.80\).
\(E\) = "the classifier fires the trend-day flag."
\(P(E \mid H) = 0.80\) — true positive rate (fires correctly when it really is a trend day).
\(P(E \mid \lnot H) = 0.20\) — false positive rate (fires anyway when it's actually a reversion day, since accuracy is symmetric at 80%).

First, compute the total probability of the flag firing at all, over both cases:

\[ P(E) = P(E\mid H)P(H) + P(E\mid\lnot H)P(\lnot H) = (0.80)(0.20) + (0.20)(0.80) = 0.16 + 0.16 = 0.32 \]

Now apply Bayes' theorem:

\[ P(H \mid E) = \frac{P(E\mid H)\,P(H)}{P(E)} = \frac{0.16}{0.32} = 0.50 \]

When the classifier flags trend-day, the real probability it's right is only 50% — a coin flip — not 80%. This is the base-rate fallacy: the classifier's 80% accuracy applies to each class separately, but because trend days are rare (20% of days) relative to reversion days (80% of days), the pool of false positives generated from the much larger reversion-day population (0.20 × 0.80 = 0.16) is exactly as large as the pool of true positives from the small trend-day population (0.80 × 0.20 = 0.16). The flag alone can't distinguish which pool you're in. Note what drove the answer: when \(P(E\mid H)\) and \(P(E\mid\lnot H)\) happen to be complements of each other (0.80 and 0.20) the posterior collapses to exactly \(P(H\mid E)=P(H)/[P(H)+P(\lnot H)P(E\mid\lnot H)/P(E\mid H)]\) — plug in the numbers and see for yourself that a rarer prior always pulls the posterior further from the quoted accuracy. If trend days were 50% of days instead of 20%, the same 80%-accurate classifier would yield a posterior of 80% — matching the quoted accuracy exactly, because a 50/50 prior contributes no distortion. The lesson is not "the classifier is useless" — a jump from a 20% prior to a 50% posterior is real evidence, a Bayes factor of \(0.80/0.20 = 4\), squarely in Jeffreys' "substantial" band. The lesson is that the flag moved your belief, it didn't resolve it, and reporting "80% accurate" without the base rate badly overstates how confident you should be in any single flagged day.

How practitioners actually use this

Three places this shows up constantly, outside of pure statistics:

Medical and diagnostic testing is the textbook version of the example above, and it's not academic — real screening-test debates (mammography, HIV testing, COVID testing) turn on exactly this arithmetic, because a rare condition plus an imperfect test almost always means a positive result is less certain than it feels. Spam filters and fraud detection are built as literal Bayesian classifiers (naive Bayes): each word or feature is evidence that updates the posterior probability of "spam" versus "not spam," multiplicatively, one feature at a time — which is precisely the sequential-updating idea above. A/B testing and Bayesian statistics more broadly use this to report "there is an 87% probability variant B is better" instead of a p-value, because that statement is what people actually want to know and a frequentist p-value does not say it (more on that distinction directly below). In quantitative finance specifically, Bayesian updating underlies Kalman filters (used for rolling estimates of parameters like beta or volatility that drift over time), and it is the formal justification for "don't trust a signal in isolation — weight it by how rare/common the regime it's claiming to detect actually is."

Bayesian vs. frequentist — the actual philosophical difference. This is not just two toolkits for the same job; the two schools disagree about what the word "probability" means.

  • Frequentist: probability is a long-run frequency — "if I repeated this exact experiment infinitely many times, what fraction of trials would show this outcome?" Under this view a hypothesis is either true or false (a fixed, unknown fact about the world) — it doesn't have a "probability" at all, only data generated under it does. A frequentist confidence interval or p-value is a statement about the procedure's long-run behavior, not a direct probability statement about your specific hypothesis. This is why "there's a 95% chance the true mean is in this interval" is, strictly, a misstatement of what a 95% confidence interval means — the true mean is fixed and either is or isn't in the interval; the 95% refers to how often the procedure would capture it across repeated experiments.
  • Bayesian: probability is a degree of belief — a number that quantifies your uncertainty about a specific claim, and it is perfectly coherent to say "there is an 87% probability today is a trend day" about a single, particular, never-to-be-repeated day. A hypothesis can have a probability, updated as evidence arrives, because probability here describes the state of your knowledge, not a frequency over an ensemble of repeated trials.

Practically, this cashes out as a difference in what question each approach is built to answer. A frequentist significance test answers "if the null hypothesis were true, how surprising is data this extreme?" (the p-value) — it never directly states the probability the null is true. A Bayesian posterior answers "given this data, what should I believe about the hypothesis?" directly — but the price is that it requires you to state a prior, which is an extra, sometimes contestable input a frequentist test doesn't need. Neither approach is "more rigorous" in the abstract; they are answering different questions, and a lot of confusion in casual data analysis comes from silently treating a p-value as if it were a posterior probability, which — as the worked example above shows for a classifier's "accuracy" — is exactly the base-rate fallacy in a different costume.

Where this could plug into MacroFXModel

Confluence scoring is currently additive, not Bayesian — and that's a genuine, unbuilt design question

js/levelSources.js emits a Level[] from several independent sources (daily open, prior hi/lo, pivots, volume profile, swing S&R, round numbers, VWAP), and collectLevels / clusterLevels group nearby levels from different sources into a confluence cluster. Separately, js/entryGradeCore.js's computeSignalScore combines several component scores (hmmScore, momScore, rbScore, structScore, optionally macroScore) into a single 0–100 signal score via a fixed weighted sum — 25/25/20/20/10 with macro data on, 38/25/25/12 without.

A weighted sum treats each confirming signal as contributing a fixed, independent slice of "confidence," regardless of how correlated that signal is with the others already counted, and regardless of how common or rare the situation it's flagging actually is. A Bayesian alternative is a genuinely different design: treat "fade" vs. "follow" as two competing hypotheses with a prior (e.g. the historical base rate of fade vs. follow outcomes at this kind of level, the way the worked example above used the 20% base rate of trend days), and treat each additional confirming signal (structural confluence, momentum alignment, range-bias conviction, macro alignment) as evidence that updates the posterior via its own likelihood ratio — sequential updating exactly as derived above, each new signal multiplying in a Bayes factor rather than adding a fixed point weight. The structural appeal, in principle, is that this can naturally down-weight correlated signals (two features that are mostly saying the same thing shouldn't each buy a full, independent slice of confidence the way a linear sum lets them) and it would produce an actual calibrated probability ("62% likely to fade") instead of an unitless 0–100 score.

To be explicit about where this stands: this is not built, not prototyped, and not validated — it is a candidate reframing of an existing brick, described honestly as a hypothesis about design, not a claim of improvement. Building it would require estimating real likelihood ratios per signal from historical data (not guessed weights), handling the correlation-between-signals problem explicitly rather than just asserting Bayes handles it gracefully, and — per this repo's Lego Principle #5 — proving it beats the incumbent additive computeSignalScore on true out-of-sample Sharpe with a non-trivial OOS trade count before it would replace anything live. A more "principled-sounding" formula is not evidence by itself; only the OOS comparison is.

Common pitfalls

Where Bayesian reasoning quietly goes wrong

Confusing \(P(E \mid H)\) with \(P(H \mid E)\). "The classifier is 80% accurate" is a statement about \(P(E \mid H)\) — how likely the flag is, given the truth. It is routinely misread as \(P(H \mid E)\) — how likely the truth is, given the flag. These are only equal when \(P(H) = P(E)\), which is essentially never the case for a rare event. This single confusion is the entire mechanism behind the worked example above (this error even has a name: the "prosecutor's fallacy," from its history in courtroom misuse of forensic-match statistics).

Ignoring the prior entirely, or picking one that quietly does all the work. A prior that's too confident makes new evidence nearly irrelevant to the posterior (you'll barely update no matter what you observe); a "flat" or uninformative prior can hide the fact that you're implicitly still asserting something — there's no such thing as an assumption-free starting point. State the prior and where it came from (a real historical base rate, ideally) rather than letting a default sit there unexamined.

Chaining updates on evidence that isn't actually independent. The simplified sequential-updating formula above assumed each new piece of evidence is conditionally independent of the others given H. Two correlated signals — e.g. two level sources that are both really just re-detecting the same daily pivot — will get double-counted as if they were two separate confirmations, inflating the posterior further than the evidence actually warrants. This is precisely the concern raised in the repo box above about a naive multi-signal Bayesian score.

Treating a Bayes factor as a posterior probability. A Bayes factor of 30 is strong evidence in favor of \(H_1\) over \(H_2\) — it is not a statement that \(P(H_1 \mid E) = 30/31\). The final posterior still depends on the prior odds; a large Bayes factor applied to a very unlikely prior can still land at a modest posterior.

Self-test

Q1. Why does a positive test result not mean what most people intuitively think it means?
Because people read a test's accuracy figure — \(P(E \mid H)\), the chance of a positive result given the condition is real — as if it were the answer to a different question: \(P(H \mid E)\), the chance the condition is real given a positive result. Those are only the same number when the base rate of the condition equals the base rate of a positive test overall, which is essentially never true for anything rare. The true posterior also depends on the prior \(P(H)\) and the false-positive rate \(P(E \mid \lnot H)\), both of which get ignored by the intuitive reading.
Q2. What's the difference between "updating" a belief and "re-deriving it from scratch"?
Updating means using your current posterior as the new prior and multiplying in the new evidence's likelihood ratio — the old belief is preserved and adjusted, not discarded. Re-deriving from scratch would mean throwing away everything you'd learned and computing a fresh posterior using only the newest piece of evidence and some generic default prior. Sequential Bayesian updating is specifically the mathematical justification for why it's correct to keep carrying forward accumulated belief rather than resetting on every new observation — as long as each new piece of evidence is properly conditioned on what came before.
Q3. What does a Bayes factor of 3 mean, versus a Bayes factor of 30?
A Bayes factor of 3 means the observed evidence is 3 times more probable under \(H_1\) than under \(H_2\) — on Jeffreys' scale that's "barely worth mentioning" to "substantial," a modest nudge. A Bayes factor of 30 means the evidence is 30 times more probable under \(H_1\) — "strong" evidence, a much bigger nudge to the posterior odds. Neither number alone tells you the final posterior probability — you still have to multiply by the prior odds \(P(H_1)/P(H_2)\) to get there.
Q4. In the trend-day classifier example, why did an "80% accurate" classifier only give a 50% posterior?
Because trend days were rare to begin with (20% prior), the pool of false alarms generated from the much larger population of reversion days (80% of days × 20% false-positive rate = 0.16) ended up exactly as large as the pool of correct alarms generated from the small population of real trend days (20% of days × 80% true-positive rate = 0.16). The flag alone can't tell you which pool a given alarm came from, so the posterior split 50/50 between them. A rarer prior always pulls the posterior further away from the raw accuracy figure.
Q5. What's the core philosophical disagreement between Bayesian and frequentist statistics — not just the different formulas, but what "probability" means to each?
Frequentists define probability as a long-run frequency over repeated trials — under that definition, a fixed but unknown hypothesis doesn't itself have a probability, only the data generated under repeated experiments does. Bayesians define probability as a degree of belief, a number that can meaningfully attach to a single, non-repeatable claim ("this specific day is a trend day"), updated coherently as evidence arrives. This is why a frequentist p-value answers "how surprising is this data if the null were true?" while a Bayesian posterior answers "what should I now believe about the hypothesis?" — genuinely different questions, not two routes to the same number.
Q6. Why is a weighted additive score (like computeSignalScore's 25/25/20/20/10 blend) not automatically "wrong" just because a Bayesian alternative exists?
Because "more principled-sounding" is not the same as "better" — per the Lego Principle in this repo's CLAUDE.md, an idea only earns adoption by beating the incumbent on true out-of-sample Sharpe with a non-trivial OOS trade count, not by sounding more theoretically justified. A naive Bayesian version also introduces its own failure mode — treating correlated signals as independent evidence and double-counting them — that a fixed weighted sum with hand-tuned weights doesn't automatically have. Neither approach is assumption-free; both need to be tested, not asserted.

Further reading

  • Thomas Bayes (posthumously, via Richard Price), "An Essay towards solving a Problem in the Doctrine of Chances", Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society, 1763 — the original source, on inferring an unknown probability from observed trials.
  • Pierre-Simon Laplace, Théorie analytique des probabilités, 1812 — developed the general theorem (independently of, and in more usable form than, Bayes' original essay) and applied it broadly; much of what's taught as "Bayes' theorem" today owes as much to Laplace.
  • E.T. Jaynes, Probability Theory: The Logic of Science, Cambridge University Press, 2003 — the rigorous case for probability as extended logic / degree of belief, and the deepest single treatment of why the Bayesian framework is the consistent one.
  • Nate Silver, The Signal and the Noise, Penguin Press, 2012 — an accessible, practitioner-facing tour of Bayesian reasoning applied to forecasting (weather, elections, poker, markets), a good bridge from the math here to real-world judgment calls.
  • Harold Jeffreys, Theory of Probability, 3rd ed., Oxford University Press, 1961 — origin of the Bayes-factor interpretation scale referenced above.
