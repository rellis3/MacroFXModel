Garman-Klass & Yang-Zhang, Reading the Whole Bar

A closing price is a snapshot of where the day ended up. It says nothing about how far price actually traveled to get there. This deck opens a brand-new category by asking a simple question with a genuinely useful answer: what happens once a volatility estimator is allowed to read the open, high, and low too — not just the close?

Section 1 · How it reached trading

More volatility out of the same daily bar

Range-based volatility · Parkinson; Garman & Klass, 1980 → Yang & Zhang, 2000

How it reached trading

Close-to-close vol uses one of a daily bar's four prices. Parkinson (1980) showed the high–low range is several times more efficient; Garman & Klass (1980) added the open and close.

Rogers & Satchell (1991) removed drift bias and Yang & Zhang (2000) added the overnight gap. They are now standard wherever a desk sizes or checks vol from bars rather than ticks.

Section 2

One number a day is a wasteful way to measure a day

Two very different days can produce an identical closing price.

📷

Close-to-close sees a snapshot

Every historical-vol calculation built on log returns uses exactly one price per day — wherever the bar happened to end up.

🌪

A wild day and a dead day can look identical

Open 1.0820, spike to 1.0900, collapse to 1.0790, close 1.0825 — versus a flat day that opens 1.0820 and closes 1.0825. Close-to-close can't tell them apart.

📊

The high and low were sitting right there

Every daily bar already carries four prices, not one. Range-based (OHLC) estimators use all four.

⚡

The payoff: far less data needed

Reading the whole bar reaches the same statistical precision with a fraction of the trading days a close-only estimate would need.

Think of a closing price as a single 6pm photo of someone's day. It can't tell you if that day was chaotic or calm — only how things happened to settle by evening. The high and low are the missing record of how much actually happened in between.
Section 3

Parkinson (1980) — just the range

The simplest range-based estimator: look only at how wide the day swung.

The formula
σ²_P
=
ln(H/L)²
÷
4·ln2
Under a continuous, driftless price model, this single ratio is an unbiased estimate of that day's variance — using only the high and the low, nothing else.
About 5x more statistically efficient than close-to-close under that model. The cost: it can't see drift, and it can't see the open or close at all — a day that swings wide and snaps back looks identical to one that swings wide and stays there.
Section 4

Garman-Klass (1980) — range plus open-close

Adds back exactly the information Parkinson throws away: where the bar started and ended.

↕

0.5 · ln(H/L)²

The range term — captures every bit of within-bar movement, however wide the swing.

−

(2ln2−1) · ln(C/O)²

Subtracted back out, weighted by ≈0.3863 — corrects for the fact that a close-to-close comparison only cares where the bar started and ended, not how far it wandered.

About 7.4x more efficient than close-to-close under the same driftless model — better than Parkinson alone, for one extra term already sitting in the same daily bar.
Where this breaks

Garman-Klass assumes continuous trading with no gap between one bar's close and the next bar's open. Whatever happens in that gap — a weekend rollover, a scheduled-release jump — is completely invisible to it.

Section 5

Three estimators, side by side

What each one uses, what each one assumes, and where each one breaks.

Compare
Uses: high, low only. Assumes: continuous, driftless motion. Efficiency vs. close-close: ≈5x. Blind to: drift, the open/close, and any gap between bars.
Uses: high, low, open, close. Assumes: driftless motion, no gap between bars. Efficiency vs. close-close: ≈7.4x. Blind to: the overnight/weekend gap specifically — its single biggest real-world limitation for FX.
Uses: high, low, open, close, and the prior close (for the overnight gap). Assumes: nothing about drift; explicitly models the overnight gap. Efficiency vs. close-close: up to ≈14x under drift+jump scenarios in the original paper. Blind to: essentially nothing this deck covers — the most complete of the four.

Each estimator in this progression fixes exactly one limitation of the one before it — the range term, then the open-close correction, then (next) drift-independence, then the overnight gap.

Section 6

Rogers-Satchell — fixing the zero-drift assumption

Parkinson and Garman-Klass both quietly assume the price never trends. A steadily trending day fools both of them a little.

Parkinson & Garman-Klass
  • Derived assuming zero drift
  • A steadily trending day picks up a small systematic bias
  • Can't distinguish "wandered" from "walked somewhere"
Rogers-Satchell (1991)
  • σ²_RS = ln(H/C)ln(H/O) + ln(L/C)ln(L/O)
  • Drift-independent by construction — stays unbiased on a trending day
  • Rarely used alone — its real job is as a building block inside Yang-Zhang, next
Rogers-Satchell is the ingredient, not usually the finished dish. What it fixes is exactly the property Yang-Zhang needs from its own intraday-range term.
Section 7

Yang-Zhang (2000) — the complete combination

Three variance sources, weighted to minimize the total.

σ²_YZ
=
σ²_overnight
+
k·σ²_open-close
+
(1−k)·σ²_RS
🌙

σ²_overnight = Var[ln(Oₜ/Cₜ₋₁)]

The exact close-to-open gap Garman-Klass can never see — measured directly, not inferred.

📐

k = 0.34 / (1.34 + (n+1)/(n−1))

Not a guess — derived to minimize the combined estimator's total variance for a sample of n days.

Overnight fixes Garman-Klass's blind spot. Rogers-Satchell fixes the zero-drift assumption. k blends them at the mathematically optimal weight. That's the whole design in one sentence.

Section 11

A quiet week, then a weekend gap

A desk sizes stops off a 30-day Garman-Klass figure all week — fine, because nothing gapped. Then a Friday-evening headline moves rates expectations, and price gaps meaningfully over the weekend rollover. Garman-Klass's rolling estimate barely reacts: the gap happened entirely between two bars, in the exact blind spot its no-gap assumption creates. Yang-Zhang's estimate jumps immediately, because its overnight term is built specifically to measure a move like this one.

A desk sizing risk off Garman-Klass alone that week would be working from a volatility figure that quietly understated exactly the kind of move that just happened — the dollars-and-stops version of "GK assumes no gap," not just an abstract limitation.

Section 13

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

What does Parkinson's estimator use that close-to-close volatility never looks at?

Parkinson uses only H and L from the current bar — the exact information a close-to-close calculation never touches.
Question 2

What is Garman-Klass's real limitation?

GK's formula only ever looks at one bar's own OHLC — whatever happens in the gap to the next bar's open (a weekend rollover, a news jump) is invisible to it.
Question 3

What property does Rogers-Satchell have that Parkinson and Garman-Klass both lack?

Parkinson and Garman-Klass are both derived under a zero-drift assumption; Rogers-Satchell's formula is specifically constructed to stay unbiased regardless of drift.
Question 4

What does Yang-Zhang's overnight term specifically fix?

Var[ln(Oₜ/Cₜ₋₁)] is a dedicated term measuring exactly the overnight gap Garman-Klass has no way to see — Rogers-Satchell (already inside Yang-Zhang) is what fixes the drift assumption.
Question 5

Is Yang-Zhang actually used in this repo's production code, or just explained here?

js/volForecast.js's computeForecast() calls yangZhangVolSeries() as its primary σ input for FX and gold; a second, independent Python implementation in forge/vol.py is walk-forward validated and tested.
Section 14

What to carry forward

01

Close-to-close volatility uses one price a day. Range-based (OHLC) estimators use all four a daily bar already provides.

02

Parkinson (range only) → Garman-Klass (+ open-close) → Rogers-Satchell (drift-independent) → Yang-Zhang (+ overnight gap) is a ladder of increasingly complete assumptions.

03

Garman-Klass's real limitation is the no-gap assumption — a genuine issue for FX around weekend rollovers, fixed specifically by Yang-Zhang's overnight term.

04

This deck's own Monte Carlo check recovered ≈5x (Parkinson) and ≈7.8x (Garman-Klass) efficiency gains over close-close vol — matching the textbook figures, not just citing them.

05

All four estimators are genuinely implemented in this repo — Yang-Zhang is the live production estimator, not a concept sitting unused.

06

On a desk: Parkinson → Garman-Klass (1980) → Yang-Zhang (2000) are the standard bar-based vol inputs for sizing, stops and realised-vs-implied checks.

What this now lets you do

Recognize when a close-only volatility figure is quietly throwing away real information, and reach for an OHLC estimator instead. Know exactly which failure mode each estimator fixes — drift, the open/close, or the overnight gap — instead of treating "range-based" as one interchangeable idea. Read this repo's own volatility code with the actual formulas in hand, not just the estimator names.
