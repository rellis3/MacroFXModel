Bond Pricing & Yield to Maturity, as Present Value

A bond's price is nothing more than the present value of a known stream of future cash — the same discounting arithmetic behind any other asset, applied to coupons and a face-value repayment instead of dividends. Yield to maturity flips the question around: given the price the market is actually charging, what single discount rate explains it? Everything a rates desk does — quoting, comparing, and repricing bonds — sits on top of these two ideas.

Four ordinary coupons, then a fifth payment that bundles the last coupon together with the full face-value repayment. That's the entire promise — every bond pricing question is about what this stack of dated cash is worth today.
C

The coupon

A fixed payment, set at issuance, paid every period until maturity. It never changes for the life of the bond, no matter what happens to market rates.

F

The face value

Also called par value or principal — repaid exactly once, alongside the final coupon, at maturity. Every bond redeems at exactly F, regardless of what it traded for along the way.

Whether $1,000 today is a fair price for that promise depends on one thing: what else you could do with your money instead. If the going rate elsewhere is 5%, a bond only paying a 4% coupon is a worse deal than the alternative — so it shouldn't cost a full $1,000. Bond pricing is exactly that comparison, made precise.

Section 2

The pricing formula, piece by piece

P = Σ C/(1+y)ᵗ + F/(1+y)ⁿ — discount every coupon, discount the face value, add them together.

Σ

The coupon annuity — Σ C/(1+y)ᵗ

Every coupon, discounted back to today from the exact period it lands in. A coupon five years out is discounted more than one arriving next year.

$

The face-value lump sum — F/(1+y)ⁿ

The one-time repayment of principal, discounted only from maturity — it isn't spread across every period the way the coupons are.

P

The price — everything added together

What the entire promise is worth today, given a required discount rate y. Change y, and every term in the sum changes with it.

Each term is ordinary present-value discounting: a dollar received t periods from now is worth 1/(1+y)ᵗ dollars today, because a dollar today could instead be invested at rate y and grow into (1+y)ᵗ dollars by then. The bond formula is that discounting applied once per coupon, plus once more for the face value — nothing more exotic than that.

Section 4

Yield up, price down — no exceptions

Every cashflow in the formula is divided by (1+y) raised to some positive power.

Raise the yield

Every denominator (1+y)ᵗ grows. Every single term in the sum — every discounted coupon, and the discounted face value — shrinks. There is no term in a plain fixed-coupon bond's formula that a higher y can make bigger.

Lower the yield

Every denominator shrinks instead, so every term grows, and the sum — the price — rises. Same logic, opposite direction, with the same "no exceptions" guarantee.

This isn't an empirical tendency that occasionally breaks — it falls directly out of the algebra of discounting a fixed cashflow stream. It holds for every plain-vanilla fixed-coupon bond, always. It's also not a straight line: the curve connecting price to yield bends, an effect called convexity that the next slide makes visible.
Why this is the first thing a desk checks

When a headline says "the 10-year yield jumped 15 basis points," no one needs to re-run a pricing model to know the direction bond prices just moved — the inverse relationship says it immediately. The actual work starts one level deeper: how much did the price move, which is a duration-and-convexity question, not this slide's.

Section 6

Why YTM has to be searched for, not looked up

YTM is the same pricing formula, run backwards: given the market price, solve for y.

1 or 2 periods

A single-payment bond rearranges directly: P=(C+F)/(1+y) gives y=(C+F)/P − 1, plain algebra. Two periods is a quadratic in y — still solvable by a closed-form formula, just a messier one.

3+ periods — every real bond

y is raised to three or more different powers in the same equation simultaneously. There's no general algebraic formula — analogous to the quadratic formula, but for a degree-n polynomial — that isolates it. An iterative numerical search is the only option.

The search itself is simple in spirit: guess a yield, price the bond, compare that price to the observed market price, adjust the guess in the direction that closes the gap, repeat. Newton-Raphson is the standard version of exactly this — start near the coupon rate, use the pricing formula's local slope to take a smarter step each time, and converge in a handful of iterations.
Where this needs qualifying

Newton-Raphson can fail to converge, oscillate, or land on an economically meaningless root if the initial guess is poor or the local slope is unusual. Real pricing libraries bound the search to a sensible yield range and fall back to a slower but more robust method — bisection — if Newton-Raphson misbehaves.

Section 9

Two footnotes worth knowing

✂

Clean price vs. dirty price

The formula above assumes valuation exactly on a coupon date. Between coupon dates, the dirty price (what a buyer actually pays) adds accrued interest to the theoretical present value; the clean price — what trading screens quote — strips it back out, so quotes move smoothly instead of jumping by the coupon amount on every coupon date.

↘

Pull to par

As maturity approaches, holding yield fixed, a premium bond's price decays smoothly toward face value and a discount bond's price rises smoothly toward it — because every bond redeems at exactly F regardless of what it traded for along the way. Traders holding premium bonds near maturity plan for this decay; it isn't a market move.

↻

YTM assumes coupons reinvest at YTM itself

The realized return an investor actually earns depends on the rate each coupon gets reinvested at when it's paid — which may not match the original YTM. That gap is called reinvestment risk, and it grows for longer, higher-coupon bonds where more of the total return comes from reinvested coupons.

Section 10

How a rates desk actually uses this

🗣

Quoted in yield, not price

A trader says "it's trading at 4.32%," not "it's trading at $978.40" — yield is what's comparable across different coupons, maturities, and issuers, which is exactly why it's the quoted convention.

⚖

Relative value & spread trades

A 3% coupon bond and a 6% coupon bond of the same maturity aren't directly comparable by price, but their YTMs are, on the same scale — much like a z-score makes otherwise-unrelated series comparable.

🧮

Production systems solve for YTM iteratively

Every pricing library and terminal runs Newton-Raphson (or bisection/secant) against the exact pricing equation from Section 6 — never by hand, and never via a closed form, because none exists beyond a couple of periods.

➡

Sets up the next two lessons directly

Duration and convexity are, respectively, the first- and second-order sensitivity of this exact pricing formula to a change in yield — the slope and the bend of the curve drawn in Section 5.

A stronger-than-expected inflation print hits the wire; headlines say "the 10-year yield jumped 15 basis points." No one re-runs a pricing model to know the direction — Section 4's inverse relationship says it instantly. The desk's actual work is how much the price moved, which is precisely the job duration and convexity do next.
Section 11

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

What single discount rate makes the present value of every future cashflow equal a bond's observed market price?

That's exactly how YTM is defined — it's solved backwards from an observed price, not read off a table or fixed at issuance like the coupon rate.
Question 2

Market yields rise 25 basis points overnight. What happens to a plain fixed-coupon bond's price?

Every cashflow is discounted at a higher rate, so every term in the present-value sum shrinks — no exceptions for a plain fixed-coupon bond.
Question 3

A bond's coupon rate is 6% and its YTM is 4.5%. Does it trade above, at, or below face value?

Coupon rate > YTM ⇒ premium. Investors bid the price up until the richer coupon's effective return matches the market's 4.5%.
Question 4

Why is there no closed-form algebraic formula for YTM once a bond has 3 or more coupon periods?

n=1 is linear (solvable directly), n=2 is a quadratic (still closed-form), but n≥3 has no general algebraic solution — only an iterative numerical search, like Newton-Raphson.
Question 5

On the price-yield curve, why does it bend instead of running as a straight line?

That curvature is convexity — the second-order effect the next lesson, Duration & Convexity, quantifies precisely.
Section 12

What to carry forward

01

A bond's price is nothing more than the present value of its coupons plus its face value, both discounted at the same required yield: P = Σ C/(1+y)ᵗ + F/(1+y)ⁿ.

02

Yield to maturity is that same formula solved backwards — the single rate that makes the present value of every cashflow equal the bond's observed market price.

03

Price and yield always move in opposite directions — a fixed cashflow discounted at a higher rate is worth less, with no exceptions for a plain fixed-coupon bond.

04

Beyond one or two coupon periods, there is no closed-form solution for YTM — every real bond's yield is found by an iterative numerical search, such as Newton-Raphson.

05

Comparing a bond's coupon rate to its YTM tells you immediately whether it trades at a discount, at par, or at a premium to face value.

06

The price-yield relationship curves rather than running straight — that curvature (convexity) is exactly what the next lesson quantifies.

What this now lets you do

Price any plain fixed-coupon bond from its coupon, face value, yield, and maturity. Read "yield up, price down" instantly off any headline, without re-running a model. Classify a bond as trading at a discount, at par, or at a premium just by comparing its coupon rate to its YTM. And recognize why a "YTM" field on any terminal is running an iterative solver under the hood, not a table lookup.
