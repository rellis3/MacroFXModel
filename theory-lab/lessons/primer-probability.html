Probability & Random Variables

The formal machinery for talking about uncertain numbers — and the reason so many things you sum or average, in finance or anywhere else, end up looking approximately bell-shaped even when none of the ingredients are.

PRIMER — foundational background ~14 min read Prereqs: Descriptive Statistics & the Normal Distribution
TL;DR
  • A random variable maps outcomes to numbers; expectation \(E[X]=\sum_i x_i P(X=x_i)\) is its probability-weighted average, and variance \(Var(X)=E[X^2]-(E[X])^2\) measures the typical squared spread around that average (worked example: a fair die has \(E[X]=3.5\), \(Var(X)\approx2.92\), \(\sigma\approx1.71\)).
  • Two events are independent when \(P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)\) — knowing one tells you nothing about the other. Consecutive coin flips qualify; consecutive days of market volatility generally do not (volatility clustering).
  • The Law of Large Numbers guarantees a sample average converges to the true \(E[X]\) as \(n\to\infty\), but says nothing about how close it is at any small, fixed \(n\) — the reasoning behind this repo's "≥30 OOS trades" floor.
  • The Central Limit Theorem: the average of many independent copies of any random variable with finite mean/variance approaches a normal distribution as \(n\) grows — even a flat, uniform die roll's average becomes bell-shaped by \(n=30\) — which is why so many aggregated financial quantities look roughly normal even when no individual ingredient is.
  • That CLT justification depends on independence, and daily financial returns visibly violate it via volatility clustering — so "assume returns are approximately normal" is a defensible starting point that gets corrected (e.g. by GARCH), not a settled fact.
Section 01

Why this matters

The Bayesian Inference lesson in this series talks about "the probability that today is a trend day" and updates that probability as evidence arrives. That lesson never stops to define what a probability formally is, or what it means to attach one to a number that changes from day to day — it assumes you already have the vocabulary. This primer supplies that vocabulary. It is the layer underneath almost everything else in this repo: a return, a P&L outcome, a volatility estimate, and a classifier's flag are all random variables — numbers whose value isn't known in advance but whose long-run behavior can still be described precisely. Expectation and variance are the two numbers used constantly to summarize that behavior (they are, respectively, the formal versions of "average" and "spread" you already met informally in the Descriptive Statistics primer). Independence, the Law of Large Numbers, and the Central Limit Theorem are the three ideas that explain why averaging and summing behave the way they do — and why "assume returns are roughly normal, then correct for fat tails" is a defensible starting point rather than an arbitrary one.

None of this is trading-specific. It is the same mathematics used in insurance, physics, polling, and quality control. Reading it as general literacy — not as a repo module — is the right frame; the payoff is that every later lesson that says "assume normality" or "the sample mean converges" is now a claim you can evaluate instead of a phrase you take on faith.

💡
Plain-English intuition

A random variable is just a number you don't know yet, attached to rules you do

Before you roll a die, you don't know what number will come up — but you know a lot about it anyway: it will be 1 through 6, each equally likely, and if you rolled it a thousand times the average would land close to 3.5. That's the whole idea of a random variable in one sentence: an unknown value with a known set of rules governing how it behaves. Tomorrow's EUR/USD daily return is the same kind of object — you don't know its exact value today, but you can describe its rules (roughly, typically small, centered near zero, occasionally large). Expectation is the formal name for "the average you'd see if you could repeat this many times." Variance is the formal name for "how spread out those repeats typically are around that average." The Law of Large Numbers is the promise that if you actually do repeat something many times, the observed average gets arbitrarily close to the true expectation — it's why a casino is confident in its edge even though any single hand is pure luck. The Central Limit Theorem is a stranger, more useful fact: if you add up (or average) a large number of roughly independent random quantities, the sum itself starts looking normally distributed — bell-shaped — almost no matter what the individual pieces looked like. That is why bell curves show up constantly in nature and in markets: not because every underlying process is secretly normal, but because so many observed quantities are themselves sums of many smaller, mostly-independent pieces.

Section 02

The math, step by step

Start from the ground up. A sample space, written \(\Omega\) (the Greek letter omega), is the set of every possible outcome of some uncertain process — for a single die roll, \(\Omega = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}\); for a coin flip, \(\Omega = \{\text{heads}, \text{tails}\}\). An event is any subset of the sample space you care about — "the die shows an even number" is the event \(\{2, 4, 6\}\). A probability \(P(\cdot)\) is a function that assigns each event a number between 0 and 1, following three rules (Kolmogorov's axioms, discussed in Further Reading): every probability is non-negative, the probability of the whole sample space is exactly 1 (something in \(\Omega\) always happens), and the probability of two non-overlapping events happening is the sum of their individual probabilities. Everything in probability theory, including Bayes' theorem in the previous lesson, is built from those three rules and nothing more.

A random variable, conventionally written with a capital letter like \(X\), is a function that assigns a number to every outcome in the sample space. For a die roll, the simplest random variable just returns the face value itself: \(X(\omega) = \omega\). But a random variable doesn't have to be that direct — you could define \(X\) as "1 if the die shows an even number, 0 otherwise," or, in a trading context, "the close-to-close percentage return of EUR/USD tomorrow." The random variable is the bridge between an abstract outcome (which day-type occurs, which face lands up) and an actual number you can do arithmetic on (a return, a payoff, a P&L). Random variables come in two flavors that matter for which formulas apply: discrete (a countable list of possible values, like a die face or a win/loss outcome) and continuous (any value in a range, like a percentage return). This primer works mostly with the discrete case because the arithmetic is transparent; the ideas carry over to the continuous case by replacing sums with integrals.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

A random variable is just a number-generating machine hooked up to something uncertain, like a die. Before you roll, nobody knows what number will pop out — but you already know all the rules: it'll be 1 through 6, and every number is equally likely to show up.

Expectation. The expectation (also called the expected value, or the mean of the random variable) is a weighted average of every possible value \(X\) can take, where each value is weighted by how likely it is:

\[ E[X] = \sum_i x_i \, P(X = x_i) \]

Read the right-hand side literally: for every possible outcome \(x_i\), multiply it by the probability of that outcome, then add up all those products. This is exactly the same operation as a weighted average you'd compute in any other context — a student's weighted GPA, a portfolio's weighted return — except here the "weights" are probabilities instead of credit-hours or position sizes, and they are guaranteed by the axioms above to sum to exactly 1. If every outcome is equally likely (probability \(1/n\) each, for \(n\) outcomes), \(E[X]\) collapses to the ordinary arithmetic mean \(\frac{1}{n}\sum_i x_i\) — the sample mean from the Descriptive Statistics primer is the special, equally-weighted case of this more general definition.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Expectation answers "what would the average look like if I did this a jillion times?" Roll a die forever and average all those rolls, and you'll land on 3.5 — even though you can never actually roll a 3.5 on any single roll.

Variance of a random variable. Expectation tells you the center; variance tells you the spread — specifically, the expected value of the squared distance between \(X\) and its own mean:

\[ Var(X) = E\big[(X - E[X])^2\big] \]
X − E[X]a value's distance from the true mean
E[·]weight each squared distance by its probability
Var(X)the resulting typical squared spread

This is the direct population-level counterpart of the sample-variance formula from the Descriptive Statistics primer — \(\frac{1}{n}\sum_i (x_i - \bar{x})^2\) (or \(\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2\) with Bessel's correction). The sample formula estimates variance from a finite, observed batch of data by averaging squared deviations from the sample mean \(\bar{x}\); the definition here is the same idea applied to the full, theoretical probability distribution, averaging squared deviations from the true mean \(E[X]\), weighted by probability instead of by \(1/n\). One is what you compute from a dataset; the other is the fact about the underlying random variable that the dataset is trying to estimate. A useful algebraic shortcut, derived by expanding the square, is:

\[ Var(X) = E[X^2] - \big(E[X]\big)^2 \]
E[X²]the expectation of the squared value
(E[X])²the square of the plain expectation

"The expectation of the square, minus the square of the expectation." This is usually the faster way to compute variance by hand, and it's the formula the worked example below uses. As with the Descriptive Statistics primer, the standard deviation \(\sigma = \sqrt{Var(X)}\) is the square root of variance, brought back into the original units (a variance of "return-percent squared" isn't directly interpretable; its square root, in return-percent, is).

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Variance answers a different question than expectation: not "where's the middle," but "how far do things usually wander away from the middle?" A die that always landed on the same number would have zero variance; a die that jumps wildly between 1 and 6 would have a big variance.

Independence. Two events \(A\) and \(B\) are independent if knowing one happened tells you nothing about whether the other happened — formally, if:

\[ P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B) \]
A ∩ Bthe event "A and B both happen"
P(A)P(B)their individual probabilities, simply multiplied

Compare this to the conditional-probability definition from the Bayesian Inference lesson, \(P(A \mid B) = P(A \cap B)/P(B)\). Substitute the independence condition in and you get \(P(A \mid B) = P(A)\) — observing \(B\) leaves your belief about \(A\) completely unchanged, which is the plain-English meaning of "independent." Two successive fair coin flips are independent (the first flip has zero bearing on the second); two consecutive days of market volatility, importantly, generally are not — a point the pitfalls section below returns to, because it is exactly where naive use of the ideas on this page breaks down for financial data.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Two things are independent when knowing about one tells you nothing new about the other — like flipping a coin twice. The first flip landing heads doesn't make the second flip any more or less likely to be heads too. But a hot, sunny day today does make tomorrow more likely to be hot and sunny too — that's not independent.

The trap: the Law of Large Numbers and the Central Limit Theorem below both quietly assume independence. Volatility clustering means financial returns routinely violate that assumption — so borrowing CLT-flavored "returns are approximately normal" reasoning is a defensible starting point, not a guarantee, and it breaks down worst exactly during the volatility spikes where getting it right matters most.

The Law of Large Numbers (LLN). Suppose you draw \(n\) independent copies of a random variable \(X\) (\(n\) independent die rolls, \(n\) independent trades from the same underlying process) and compute their sample average \(\bar{X}_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i\). The Law of Large Numbers states that as \(n\) grows, this sample average converges to the true expectation:

\[ \bar{X}_n \xrightarrow{\;n \to \infty\;} E[X] \]
X̄ₙthe sample average of n independent draws
n → ∞as the number of draws grows without bound
E[X]the true, theoretical mean it converges to

In plain words: the more times you repeat an uncertain process and average the results, the closer that observed average gets to the true, theoretical mean — and the gap shrinks reliably, not just optimistically. This is the formal justification for "the house always wins in the long run" and for why a backtest's average trade outcome is meaningful only once it's built from enough trades — a five-trade sample average can sit far from the true expectation; the LLN says nothing about how fast convergence happens at any particular \(n\), only that it happens eventually (a point returned to in the pitfalls section).

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

The Law of Large Numbers is why a casino isn't nervous about any one single hand of cards — they know that if you play a game enough times, the actual average result gets closer and closer to the true expected average, even though any one single game is totally unpredictable.

The Central Limit Theorem (CLT). The LLN says the sample average converges to a single number, \(E[X]\). The CLT goes further and describes the shape of the sample average's own distribution along the way. Take \(n\) independent copies of a random variable \(X\) (any distribution — it does not need to be normal, or even close to it — provided it has a finite mean and finite variance), and look at their average \(\bar{X}_n\). As \(n\) grows, the distribution of \(\bar{X}_n\) itself approaches a normal distribution, centered at \(E[X]\) with variance \(Var(X)/n\):

\[ \bar{X}_n \;\approx\; \mathcal{N}\!\left(E[X],\; \frac{Var(X)}{n}\right) \quad \text{for large } n \]
N(·,·)a normal distribution, regardless of X's own shape
E[X]where the resulting bell curve centers
Var(X)/nits variance — shrinking as n grows

This is a genuinely surprising claim: the individual \(X_i\) can be lopsided, skewed, bounded, discrete, or shaped nothing like a bell curve — a single die roll is flat, not bell-shaped — and yet the average of many independent copies of it converges toward a bell curve anyway. The chart below makes this concrete with the simplest possible example: a single die roll. Note the variance of the average shrinks as \(1/n\) — averaging more independent draws doesn't just make the shape more normal, it also tightens the spread around the true mean, which is the CLT and the LLN reinforcing each other from two different angles (one converges the center, the other converges the shape and narrows the spread around that center).

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Here's a strange but true fact: even something totally uneven, like a die where every number is equally likely, starts looking like a smooth, even bell shape once you average lots and lots of rolls together. It's like mixing lots of different-colored paints — no matter how weird each individual color is, mix enough of them together and you tend to get a similar, smooth kind of gray.

🧮
Worked example

Expectation and variance of a fair six-sided die, computed by hand

Let \(X\) be the outcome of one roll of a fair six-sided die, so \(X \in \{1,2,3,4,5,6\}\), each with probability \(1/6\). By the definition of expectation:

\[ E[X] = \frac{1}{6}(1) + \frac{1}{6}(2) + \frac{1}{6}(3) + \frac{1}{6}(4) + \frac{1}{6}(5) + \frac{1}{6}(6) = \frac{1+2+3+4+5+6}{6} = \frac{21}{6} = 3.5 \]

The expected value of a single die roll is 3.5 — notice this is not itself a possible outcome of any single roll (you can never actually roll a 3.5), which is a useful reminder that "expectation" means long-run average, not "most likely single result."

Now compute the variance using the shortcut formula \(Var(X) = E[X^2] - (E[X])^2\). First, \(E[X^2]\), the expectation of the squared outcome:

\[ E[X^2] = \frac{1}{6}(1^2) + \frac{1}{6}(2^2) + \frac{1}{6}(3^2) + \frac{1}{6}(4^2) + \frac{1}{6}(5^2) + \frac{1}{6}(6^2) = \frac{1+4+9+16+25+36}{6} = \frac{91}{6} \approx 15.1667 \]

Now subtract \((E[X])^2 = 3.5^2 = 12.25\):

\[ Var(X) = \frac{91}{6} - 3.5^2 \approx 15.1667 - 12.25 = 2.9167 \]

So \(Var(X) \approx 2.9167\) (in "die-face-squared" units, which is why variance alone is awkward to interpret directly). Taking the square root gives the standard deviation:

\[ \sigma = \sqrt{2.9167} \approx 1.71 \]

A single die roll has expected value 3.5 and typically deviates from that expected value by about 1.71. This is the exact random variable used in the chart above: the "n = 1" panel is this flat distribution over \(\{1,...,6\}\); the "n = 5" and "n = 30" panels are the distributions of the average of 5 and 30 independent draws of this same \(X\), each of which (by the CLT formula above) has expectation still 3.5 but variance shrinking to \(2.9167/5 \approx 0.583\) and \(2.9167/30 \approx 0.097\) respectively — narrower and more normal-shaped as \(n\) grows, exactly as pictured.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

Expectation, variance, the LLN and the CLT are not abstractions confined to a textbook — they're the working vocabulary behind four entirely different fields, each leaning on a different piece of the same machinery:

🏦

Insurance & actuarial pricing

Prices a policy near the expected payout plus a margin, relying on the LLN across thousands of independent policyholders to make the average payout predictable in aggregate.

🏭

Quality control & manufacturing

Uses the CLT directly: a sample of n parts is measured, and the sample average is assumed approximately normal regardless of any single part's error distribution.

🗳️

Polling & survey statistics

A poll's margin of error is a CLT-derived confidence interval around a sample proportion — itself an average of many independent 0/1 random variables.

📈

Quantitative finance

Expectation and variance are the literal inputs to mean-variance optimization; the CLT justifies treating an aggregated return as approximately normal before GARCH-style corrections.

Insurance and actuarial pricing is the oldest applied use of expectation: an insurer prices a policy near the expected payout (a probability-weighted average of claim sizes) plus a margin, and relies on the Law of Large Numbers across thousands of independent policyholders to make that average payout highly predictable in aggregate, even though any single policyholder's outcome is not. Quality control and manufacturing use the CLT directly: a sample of \(n\) parts is measured, the sample average is assumed approximately normal by the CLT regardless of how any single part's measurement error is distributed, and that normality is what makes standard control-chart thresholds (like "flag anything more than 3 standard deviations from target") statistically meaningful. Polling and survey statistics lean on the same fact — a poll's margin of error is a CLT-derived confidence interval around a sample proportion, which is itself an average of many independent 0/1 random variables (one per respondent). Quantitative finance uses expectation and variance as the literal building blocks of portfolio theory (expected return and variance of return are the two inputs to mean-variance optimization) and leans on the CLT as the justification for treating an aggregated return — a day's return built from many intraday trades, a portfolio's return built from many positions — as approximately normal as a starting assumption, before layering on corrections (fat tails, skew, volatility clustering) that account for where the CLT's assumptions don't fully hold. GARCH models, covered elsewhere in this series, exist specifically because that "approximately normal" starting point needs those corrections.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where these ideas quietly get misapplied

Assuming financial returns are independent because the CLT needs independence and you want to use the CLT. The CLT's guarantee is conditional on its assumptions — roughly independent inputs with finite variance — holding. Daily and intraday financial returns are well documented to exhibit volatility clustering: a large move today makes a large move tomorrow more likely, which is a direct violation of independence (this is the entire motivation for GARCH-family models, covered elsewhere in this series). Applying CLT-based normality assumptions to a return series that violates independence doesn't make the math wrong in the abstract — it makes the application wrong, and it breaks down worst exactly where it matters most: during volatility spikes and crisis periods, which is when tail risk is actually being priced.

Confusing "the average of many draws looks normal" with "the underlying process is normal." The die-roll example above is the clearest possible illustration of the difference: a single die roll is uniformly flat, not remotely bell-shaped, yet the average of many independent die rolls is close to normal. The CLT is a statement about sums and averages, not a statement that the original random variable itself must be, or even resembles, a normal distribution. A single day's return being roughly normal-shaped (if it even is) is a separate empirical question from whether the microstructure events that produced it are individually normal — they almost certainly aren't.

Expecting Law of Large Numbers convergence at a small, fixed \(n\). The LLN promises the sample average converges to the true expectation as \(n \to \infty\) — it makes no promise about how close \(\bar{X}_n\) is to \(E[X]\) at any specific, finite \(n\) you happen to have. A 20-trade backtest's average return can sit meaningfully far from the strategy's true expected return; treating a small-sample average as if it already equaled the true mean is a direct misreading of what the LLN guarantees. This is the same concern behind this repo's Lego Principle requirement of a "non-trivial OOS trade count (≥30)" before trusting an out-of-sample Sharpe number — 30 is not a magic number that suddenly invokes the LLN or CLT, it's a practical floor meant to keep the sample average from being dominated by a handful of lucky or unlucky trades.

Treating variance as risk without asking whether the distribution is symmetric. \(Var(X)\) penalizes upside and downside deviations from the mean identically. For a return distribution with meaningful skew — many option payoffs, trend-following strategies with rare large wins, or reversion strategies with rare large losses — the variance can understate the risk that actually matters (the size and likelihood of the bad tail specifically). Skewness and kurtosis, covered in the Descriptive Statistics primer and used directly in this project's own performance-metrics code, exist precisely to capture what variance alone misses.

Section 04

Key Takeaways

✓A random variable is a rule-governed unknown. It maps outcomes in a sample space to numbers — a return, a P&L, a day-type flag are all random variables in this precise sense.
✓Expectation is a probability-weighted average. \(E[X]=\sum_i x_i P(X=x_i)\) generalizes the plain sample mean to the case where outcomes aren't equally likely.
✓Variance is the same spread idea, at the population level. \(Var(X)=E[X^2]-(E[X])^2\) is the theoretical counterpart of the sample-variance formula from the Descriptive Statistics primer.
✓The Law of Large Numbers guarantees convergence, eventually. A sample average approaches the true \(E[X]\) as \(n\to\infty\) — but says nothing about how close it is at any small, fixed \(n\), which is exactly why this repo requires ≥30 OOS trades before trusting a Sharpe number.
✓The CLT explains why sums look normal. The average of many independent copies of almost any random variable — even a flat die roll — approaches a normal distribution as \(n\) grows, which is why so many aggregated financial quantities look roughly bell-shaped.
⚠The CLT's guarantee depends on independence. Daily financial returns violate it via volatility clustering — big moves cluster in time rather than scattering independently — which is exactly why "assume normal, then correct" (GARCH) is the honest framing, not "assume normal, done."
⚠Variance alone hides skew. It penalizes upside and downside deviations identically, which understates the risk that actually matters for a strategy with a lopsided return distribution.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. What is a random variable, formally — and why is "the outcome of a die roll" itself not quite a precise enough answer?
A random variable is a function that assigns a numeric value to every outcome in a sample space. "The outcome of a die roll" describes the sample space \(\Omega = \{1,...,6\}\) — the set of possible outcomes — but a random variable is the mapping from those outcomes to numbers you can compute with. For a plain die roll the mapping is the identity function (the outcome and the number happen to be the same), but a random variable can just as easily be a derived quantity, like "1 if the roll is even, 0 otherwise," or "tomorrow's percentage return of EUR/USD" — the underlying outcome (which physical event occurs) and the random variable (the number you assign to it) are conceptually distinct even when, in the simplest examples, they look identical.
Q2. Why does the Central Limit Theorem matter even if you never explicitly compute a sum of random variables?
Because many quantities you observe directly — a day's return, a portfolio's P&L, a sample average in a backtest report — are themselves, implicitly, sums or averages of many smaller, roughly independent contributions (individual trades, individual positions, individual price ticks), even though you never see or compute those smaller pieces separately. The CLT is the reason those aggregated quantities tend to look approximately normal on inspection, without anyone having deliberately summed anything — the aggregation already happened upstream, in how the quantity was generated.
Q3. What's a concrete example of financial data violating the CLT's independence assumption?
Volatility clustering: large price moves tend to be followed by more large price moves, and calm periods tend to be followed by more calm periods, rather than each day's volatility being an independent draw unrelated to the previous day's. This is a direct violation of the independence the CLT requires between the pieces being aggregated, and it's the empirical fact that GARCH-family models are built specifically to capture — modeling today's variance as depending on recent variance and recent shocks, rather than assuming each day's return is an independent draw from a fixed, unchanging distribution.
Q4. What's the difference between the Law of Large Numbers and the Central Limit Theorem — what does each one actually promise?
The Law of Large Numbers promises that a sample average converges to a single number, the true expectation \(E[X]\), as the sample size grows — it's a statement about where the average ends up. The Central Limit Theorem promises something about the shape of the sample average's distribution along the way — that it approaches a normal distribution, centered at \(E[X]\) with variance \(Var(X)/n\), regardless of the original distribution's shape. The LLN tells you convergence happens; the CLT tells you approximately what the remaining uncertainty around that convergence looks like at a given, finite sample size. Neither promises anything about how close you are at any one specific, small \(n\) — both are statements about the limit as \(n\) grows.
Q5. In the fair-die worked example, why is Var(X) computed as E[X²] − (E[X])² instead of directly from E[(X − E[X])²]?
Both formulas are mathematically identical — E[X²] − (E[X])² is derived by algebraically expanding the square in the definition E[(X − E[X])²] and simplifying, so they always give the same answer. The shortcut is used because it's usually faster to compute by hand or in code: you only need two separate expectations (of X and of X²) rather than first computing E[X], then re-looping through every value to compute the squared deviation from it. For the die example, this meant computing E[X²] = 91/6 once, then a single subtraction, rather than computing (1−3.5)², (2−3.5)², and so on individually.

Further reading
  • Andrey Kolmogorov, Grundbegriffe der Wahrscheinlichkeitsrechnung ("Foundations of the Theory of Probability"), 1933 — the axiomatic foundation (the three rules referenced above) that put probability theory on rigorous mathematical footing for the first time.
  • Jacob Bernoulli, Ars Conjectandi ("The Art of Conjecturing"), published posthumously 1713 — contains the first proof of a Law of Large Numbers (Bernoulli's "Golden Theorem"), establishing that observed frequencies converge to true probabilities as trials increase.
  • Pierre-Simon Laplace, Théorie analytique des probabilités, 1812, and Aleksandr Lyapunov's rigorous convergence conditions (1901) — the historical development of the Central Limit Theorem from Laplace's early, more restrictive versions to the general conditions used today.
  • Sheldon Ross, A First Course in Probability, 10th ed., Pearson, 2019 — the standard university textbook covering sample spaces, random variables, expectation, variance, and both limit theorems in far more depth and rigor than this primer.
  • Nassim Nicholas Taleb, The Black Swan, Random House, 2007 — an accessible, deliberately provocative critique of over-relying on CLT-flavored normality assumptions in finance, useful as a counterweight to reading this primer as "returns are basically normal, done."
