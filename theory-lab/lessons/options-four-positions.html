The Four Option Positions

Two yes/no choices — did you buy or sell, is it a call or a put — give four positions, and from those two choices alone you can read off who holds the right, who carries the obligation, the worst case, and the sign of every Greek.

CONCEPT — explained here; desk verdicts quoted where tested ~17 min read Prereqs: none — this is the first page. Goes deeper in Black-Scholes
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • Long = you bought it, short = you sold it. Call = the right to buy, put = the right to sell. Every option position is one of four cells: long call, short call, long put, short put.
  • The buyer holds a right and pays the premium; the seller collects the premium and carries an obligation. A right can be walked away from; an obligation cannot.
  • Max loss of any long option is the premium paid. Max gain of any short option is the premium collected. The other end is strike − premium for puts and unlimited for calls.
  • The Greek signs follow from the cell: long options are long gamma, short theta, long vega; short options are the mirror. Delta's sign is the only one that depends on call vs put.
  • Delta is N(d₁), not the probability of finishing in the money. That probability (under the pricing model's risk-neutral measure) is N(d₂). At-the-money and short-dated they nearly match (0.52 vs 0.50); one year at 60% vol they are 0.63 vs 0.39.
  • Premium = intrinsic value (what exercising right now is worth) + extrinsic value (everything else — time and volatility). Only extrinsic value decays.
Section 01

Why this matters

The Black-Scholes, Theta Decay, Gamma Exposure and Second-Order Greeks lessons all assume you already know what a call and a put are, and which side of the trade you are on. This page is the missing first page underneath them. It is also the vocabulary every options number on a trading screen is written in: a put wall, a call wall, a put/call ratio and a dealer gamma number are all statements about who bought and who sold which of these four positions.

The single most useful habit this lesson builds: whenever someone says "puts" or "calls", ask "bought or sold?" The same contract means the opposite thing depending on which side you hold. A put is bearish for the person who bought it and bullish for the person who sold it — and both of them are holding the identical piece of paper.

💡
Plain-English intuition

An option is an insurance policy — and someone has to be the insurer

Think of a put as house insurance on a stock. The buyer pays a small premium up front and gets the right to sell at the strike price if things go badly. If nothing goes wrong, they lose the premium and walk away — that's the whole downside. The seller is the insurance company. They pocket the premium on day one, and in exchange they carry the obligation to pay out if the bad thing happens — however bad it turns out to be.

A call is the same deal pointed the other way: the right to buy at the strike, useful if the price rockets. The buyer's worst case is the premium. The seller's worst case is whatever the rocket does. Most of what follows is just being precise about that asymmetry: buyers rent upside, sellers rent out their balance sheet.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

You pay your friend £1 for a ticket that says "you must sell me your bike for £50 any time this month if I ask." If bikes get pricey, you use the ticket. If not, you throw it away and you've lost £1. Your friend got the £1 for sure — but if bikes become worth £500, they still have to hand theirs over for £50. You had a choice. They had a promise.

The trap: "put = bearish" is only true for the person who bought the put. A put that someone sold is a bullish position. Every option contract in existence has one buyer and one seller, so "there are a lot of puts out there" on its own says nothing about which way anyone is leaning.

Section 03

The math, step by step

1. Payoff at expiry — the hockey sticks

At expiry an option has no time left, so its value is just what exercising it is worth. With \(S_T\) the underlying price at expiry and \(K\) the strike:

\[ \text{Call payoff} = \max(S_T - K,\,0) \qquad \text{Put payoff} = \max(K - S_T,\,0) \]
S_T − Kwhat a call is worth to exercise: buy at K, sell at the market
K − S_Twhat a put is worth to exercise: buy at the market, sell at K
max(·, 0)the right part — the holder never exercises at a loss

The P&L of each of the four positions is that payoff, minus or plus the premium \(p\), multiplied by the side \(s\) (\(+1\) if you bought, \(-1\) if you sold):

\[ \text{P\&L}_{\text{expiry}} = s\,\big(\text{payoff}(S_T) - p\big), \qquad s\in\{+1,-1\} \]
s
The side
+1 bought (a right), −1 sold (an obligation). Flipping it mirrors the whole picture about zero
payoff(ST)
The contract
Call or put — which way the hockey stick points
p
The premium
Paid by the buyer, collected by the seller, on day one

Because the payoff is never below zero, the buyer's worst case is \(-p\). Because the seller's P&L is the exact negative of the buyer's, the seller's best case is \(+p\) and the seller's worst case is minus the buyer's best case: \(-(K-p)\) for a put (the price can fall only to zero) and \(-\infty\) for a call (it can rise without limit). Options are a zero-sum transfer between the two sides before costs: every pound one side makes, the other side loses.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Whatever the buyer wins, the seller loses, and the other way round — like two people on a see-saw. The buyer can only ever lose the ticket price. So the seller can only ever win the ticket price. All the big numbers live on the buyer's winning side and the seller's losing side.

2. Premium = intrinsic + extrinsic

Before expiry, an option is worth more than its exercise value, because there is still time for the price to move in the holder's favour. Split the premium into two pieces:

\[ p = \underbrace{\max(S-K,0)}_{\text{intrinsic (call)}} + \underbrace{\big(p - \max(S-K,0)\big)}_{\text{extrinsic}} \]
intrinsicwhat you would get by exercising right now — zero unless in the money
extrinsictime value — the price of the chance that things improve before expiry

An option whose strike is better than the current price is in the money (ITM) and has intrinsic value. One at the current price is at the money (ATM); one that would be pointless to exercise today is out of the money (OTM) and is pure extrinsic value. Only the extrinsic part decays with time — that decay is theta, the subject of Theta Decay. At expiry extrinsic value is zero and the premium is all intrinsic.

3. Delta — and why it is not a probability

Delta is how much the option's price moves for a one-unit move in the underlying. In the Black-Scholes model, for a European option on a non-dividend-paying underlying:

\[ \Delta_{\text{call}} = N(d_1), \qquad \Delta_{\text{put}} = N(d_1) - 1, \qquad d_1 = \frac{\ln(S/K) + (r + \tfrac12\sigma^2)\tau}{\sigma\sqrt{\tau}} \]
N(d₁)between 0 and 1 for a call; the put's delta sits between −1 and 0
ln(S/K)moneyness — how far spot is from the strike, in log terms
σ√τhow far price can plausibly travel before expiry

A widely repeated shortcut says "a 0.50 delta means a 50% chance of finishing in the money." That is an approximation, not an identity. Under the same model's risk-neutral measure, the probability that a call finishes in the money is a different number:

\[ \mathbb{Q}(S_T > K) = N(d_2), \qquad d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{\tau} \]
N(d₂)risk-neutral probability of expiring in the money
d₁ − d₂ = σ√τthe gap between them grows with volatility and with time

Since \(d_1\) always exceeds \(d_2\) by \(\sigma\sqrt{\tau}\), delta is always larger than the in-the-money probability. When \(\sigma\sqrt{\tau}\) is small — a short-dated, low-vol option — the two are close and the shortcut is harmless. When it is large, they come apart badly. And even N(d₂) is a probability under the pricing measure, which builds in the risk-free drift rather than anyone's real-world forecast — so neither number is "the chance this trade works".

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Delta tells you how much the option's price wiggles when the stock wiggles. People like to pretend it's also "the chance of winning", and for short, calm options it nearly is. But for long, jumpy options, the "wiggle number" and the "winning chance" drift apart — like a car's speedometer and its fuel gauge both pointing at the middle on a short trip, but not on a long one.

4. Why the other three signs follow from the side alone

Put-call parity says a call minus a put at the same strike and expiry is a forward contract:

\[ C - P = S - Ke^{-r\tau} \]
C − Plong call plus short put, same strike
S − Ke⁻ʳᵗa forward: straight line in S, no curvature, no volatility exposure

The right-hand side is a straight line in \(S\) with no \(\sigma\) in it. Differentiate twice with respect to \(S\), or once with respect to \(\sigma\), and it vanishes. So a call and a put at the same strike have identical gamma and identical vega — both positive for the holder. Their deltas differ by exactly 1, and their thetas differ only by the small financing term \(rKe^{-r\tau}\). That is why the buyer side of either contract is long gamma, long vega and (almost always) short theta, and the seller side is the exact mirror. Direction is the only Greek that cares whether it is a call or a put.

One strike, four positions, three moneyness levels

Inputs: spot \(S = 100\), \(r = 2\%\), \(\sigma = 20\%\), three months to expiry (\(\tau = 0.25\)). All prices from the Black-Scholes closed form.

The at-the-money premiums (K = 100)

Call \(C = 4.23\), put \(P = 3.73\). Check parity: \(C - P = 0.50\) and \(S - Ke^{-r\tau} = 100 - 99.50 = 0.50\). ✓

Short call: gain at most 4.23; breakeven 104.23; loss unlimited.

Short put: gain at most 3.73; breakeven 96.27; loss at most 96.27.

3

Intrinsic vs extrinsic across strikes (calls)

K = 90 (ITM): premium 11.09 = intrinsic 10.00 + extrinsic 1.09.
K = 100 (ATM): premium 4.23 = intrinsic 0 + extrinsic 4.23 — all time value, the most of the three.
K = 110 (OTM): premium 1.04 = intrinsic 0 + extrinsic 1.04.
Extrinsic value peaks at the money: that's where the outcome is most uncertain.

4

Delta vs in-the-money probability, two cases

30 days, σ = 20%, ATM: \(d_1 = 0.0573\), \(d_2 = 0.0000\) → Δ = 0.5229, N(d₂) = 0.5000. Close enough that nobody gets hurt.
1 year, σ = 60%, ATM: \(d_1 = 0.3333\), \(d_2 = -0.2667\) → Δ = 0.6306, N(d₂) = 0.3949. Reading this delta as "63% chance" overstates the model's own in-the-money probability by 24 percentage points.

Section 04

When the obligation came due

On paper, the seller's position is "collect premium, usually keep it." The column the seller signs is the one that says what happens when "usually" fails. Here is the chain a short option runs through when the price gaps against it:

Option sellerSells a put (or call) far from spot, collects a small premium — the "can't happen" strike
→
ShockPrice gaps through the strike with no trading in between
→
Broker / clearing houseMarks the short option at its new, much higher value and calls for margin — immediately
→
SellerCannot meet the call, is forced to buy back or is closed out at whatever price exists
→
OutcomeLoss many times the premium; any shortfall beyond the account lands on the broker
It actually happened

15 January 2015: the Swiss franc floor goes

In September 2011 the Swiss National Bank set a minimum exchange rate of 1.20 francs per euro and said it would buy foreign currency in unlimited quantities to defend it. For more than three years EUR/CHF sat just above that line. The floor made a sharp franc rally look close to impossible, and the market priced it that way: selling options that paid out on a big franc rise looked like collecting premium for an event the central bank had promised would not happen.

On the morning of 15 January 2015 the SNB abandoned the floor without warning and cut its deposit rate to −0.75%. Within minutes the franc surged; EUR/CHF traded well below parity at the extreme before settling near it — a move of the kind almost never seen in a major currency pair. Prices did not trade smoothly through the levels in between, so stop-loss orders could not be filled where they were set.

Anyone short the franc's upside held the obligation side of the trade at that moment. The damage was visible at retail FX brokers whose clients had leveraged positions: client losses exceeded account balances, the brokers were left holding the shortfall, Alpari's UK arm entered insolvency, and FXCM needed a rescue loan of about $300 million from Leucadia to stay within its capital requirements. A right lets you walk away. An obligation, it turned out, does not even let you leave at the price you planned.

The same lesson arrived in equity volatility three years later. On 5 February 2018 the VIX had one of the largest one-day jumps in its history, and exchange-traded notes built to be short volatility — the insurance-seller position in packaged form — lost most of their value in a single session; Credit Suisse terminated its XIV note shortly afterwards.

🎯
Real-world trading scenario

Reading "put open interest is rising" correctly

A screen shows put open interest in an index climbing fast into a data release. The naive read is "traders are bearish." The 2×2 says: every one of those new contracts has a buyer (long put — bearish or hedging) and a seller (short put — bullish or income-seeking). Open interest counts the contracts, not the side that initiated them. If the buyers are pension funds hedging equity they already own, the "bearish" flow is really a long-equity investor paying for insurance. If the sellers are dealers, the dealers are now short gamma — which matters for how they hedge (the subject of the Gamma Exposure lesson), not for which way the index goes next.

The honest translation of "put OI is rising" is: more insurance has changed hands. Who bought it, who sold it, and why, are separate questions the open-interest number cannot answer by itself.

Section 05

How practitioners actually use this

🧭

"Bought or sold?" first

Before interpreting any options flow, desks ask which side initiated. Exchange data that flags buyer- vs seller-initiated trades exists precisely because raw volume and open interest cannot answer this.

📏

Sizing by worst case, not by premium

A buyer sizes by premium at risk — it is the worst case. A seller sizes by the loss at a stress price (strike minus premium for a put; a chosen shock for a call), never by the premium collected.

∑

Net delta across a book

Signed deltas add: long 100 shares (Δ = 1 each) plus a short call of delta 0.2 nets to 0.8 per share. Book-level delta is the directional exposure a desk hedges; it says nothing about how big the tails are.

⏳

Delta as a rough odds gauge — with a footnote

Traders use "the 25-delta strike" as shorthand for "roughly a one-in-four outcome." That is fine for short-dated options; for long-dated or high-vol ones, check N(d₂) instead.

Not tested here Folk claim: a put-heavy market (high put/call ratio) is a bearish market. The desk's evidence ledger holds no entry scoring the put/call ratio, and the thresholds the desk uses to label it bullish or bearish have no recorded provenance. This lesson's 2×2 is the reason to doubt the claim on its face: open interest cannot tell a bought put from a sold one. Treat the ratio as a description of how much insurance exists, not a forecast.
Desk test: nothing found Adjacent claim, tested here: "price pins to options max pain into expiry." Tested properly on 2026-09-10 and null — with the open-interest publish-lag timing, DTE, gamma, sign convention and spot basis all verified. Max pain is a level to know about, not a magnet.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where the 2×2 gets misread

"Puts are bearish." Only for the buyer. A short put is a bullish position, and a book of sold puts is long the market.

"Delta is the probability of finishing in the money." Delta is N(d₁); the model's risk-neutral ITM probability is N(d₂), always smaller for a call, and the gap is σ√τ inside the normal function. Fine as a shortcut for short-dated options, wrong for long-dated or volatile ones — and neither is a real-world forecast.

"Short options always have positive theta." Almost always. A deep in-the-money European put can have slightly positive theta for its holder (the interactive above shows it near spot 70), because waiting delays receiving the strike. The textbook signs on the card are the rule, not a law.

"The seller's risk is small because the premium is small." The premium is the seller's best case. The premium being small usually means the market thinks the bad case is unlikely — not that it is small.

"Same row, same risk." Short put and short call share three of four Greek signs but not their worst case: one is bounded, one is not. That is the whole of the next lesson.

Section 06

Key Takeaways

✓Two choices, four positions. Long/short (bought or sold) × call/put (right to buy or sell). Buyers hold rights and pay premium; sellers collect premium and carry obligations.
✓Buyers' worst case is the premium; sellers' best case is the premium. The other end is strike − premium for puts and unlimited for calls.
✓Gamma, theta and vega signs come from the side; delta's sign comes from the row. Put-call parity makes a call and put at one strike share gamma and vega exactly.
✓Premium = intrinsic + extrinsic. Only the extrinsic part decays, and it peaks at the money.
⚠Delta is N(d₁), not the ITM probability N(d₂). 0.52 vs 0.50 at 30 days and 20% vol; 0.63 vs 0.39 at one year and 60%.
⚠Untested here: put/call ratio as a direction signal. No ledger entry; open interest cannot tell bought from sold.
✗Tested null: max pain as a magnet. Price does not pin to max pain into expiry on this desk's data.
Section 07

Self-test

Q1. Which two of the four positions are bullish, and why is one of them a put?
Long call and short put. A short put means you sold someone the right to sell to you at the strike; you keep the premium if the price stays above the strike, so you want the price to hold or rise. The contract is a put, but the side makes the position bullish.
Q2. K = 100, put premium 3.73. What are the short put's max gain, max loss and breakeven?
Max gain 3.73 (keep the premium if the put expires worthless). Max loss \(100 - 3.73 = 96.27\), reached if the underlying goes to zero. Breakeven at expiry 96.27.
Q3. Why do a call and a put at the same strike and expiry have the same gamma and vega?
Put-call parity: \(C - P = S - Ke^{-r\tau}\). The right side is linear in \(S\) and contains no \(\sigma\), so its second derivative in \(S\) and its derivative in \(\sigma\) are both zero. Therefore \(\Gamma_C = \Gamma_P\) and \(\nu_C = \nu_P\).
Q4. A one-year at-the-money call at 60% vol has delta 0.63. Is there a 63% chance it finishes in the money?
No. Delta is N(d₁) = 0.6306. The model's risk-neutral probability of finishing in the money is N(d₂) = N(0.3333 − 0.6) = N(−0.2667) = 0.3949. The gap is driven by σ√τ = 0.6, which is large here. And N(d₂) is itself a pricing-measure probability, not a real-world forecast.
Q5. A 90-strike call trades at 11.09 with spot at 100. Split it into intrinsic and extrinsic value. Which part will be gone at expiry?
Intrinsic = 100 − 90 = 10.00; extrinsic = 1.09. The extrinsic 1.09 decays to zero by expiry (that decay is theta). The intrinsic part changes only if spot moves.
Q6. Put open interest doubles. What can you conclude about market direction from that alone?
Nothing directional. Each new contract has a buyer (long put — bearish or hedging) and a seller (short put — bullish). Open interest records that more insurance exists, not which side initiated. The desk has no tested result supporting the put/call ratio as a direction signal.
