Lesson 05 · Foundation Mathematics

Optimization Basics: Where the Slope Goes Flat

Every "find the best X" question in quantitative finance turns out to be one of two more precise questions: find the input that makes something as large or small as possible with no restrictions at all, or find the best input subject to some condition that has to hold exactly. This lesson builds both from nothing — a zero derivative, then a Lagrange multiplier — and ends at the exact math underneath minimizing a portfolio's risk for a given target return.

Section 1

The top of a hill has no uphill left

"Optimize" has one precise meaning: keep moving as long as some direction still improves things, and stop exactly when none does.

⛰

Flat tangent = no better move

For a single-variable function, the optimum sits where the tangent line is perfectly flat — the derivative is exactly zero. This is the first-order condition: f'(x*) = 0.

∇

Same idea, more variables

With several inputs, every partial derivative has to be zero at once — the entire gradient vector vanishes: ∇f(x*) = 0. No direction, in any variable, still helps.

⚑

"Stationary" isn't the same word as "optimal"

A flat tangent is equally consistent with a minimum, a maximum, or a saddle. The first-order condition narrows the search — it doesn't finish it. More on that two slides from now.

This exact stopping condition — the gradient hitting zero — is what a numerical optimizer is silently checking for on every iteration, whether it's calibrating a model to market prices or rebalancing a portfolio in production. "Gradient descent" is just walking downhill until this condition is met.
Section 2

Watch the slope hit exactly zero

Drag the point along the curve. The tangent line's slope is the first-order condition, live — it should read zero only at the very bottom.

Try it
Position, xf'(x) = 0.00
Not at the minimum yet — the tangent still tilts, so there's a direction that still improves f.

On the left half of the curve the slope is negative — f is still falling as x increases, so moving right helps. On the right half it's positive — f is rising, so moving left helps. Only at x = 0, the vertex, does the tangent go perfectly flat: f'(x*) = 0, the first-order condition, satisfied exactly where the curve stops falling and before it starts rising.

Section 3

Zero slope alone doesn't say which kind of flat

Three shapes, all perfectly flat at their center. Only the second derivative tells them apart.

Try it
f''(x*) > 0 — curves upward on both sides, like a bowl. This flat point is a genuine local minimum.
f''(x*) < 0 — curves downward on both sides, like a dome. This flat point is a genuine local maximum.
Mixed signs — curves upward in one direction (green), downward in another (red, dashed). Only possible with more than one variable: a saddle point, which the first-order condition alone can't tell apart from a true optimum.

In several variables, the Hessian matrix (built from every second partial derivative) plays this exact role: check the signs of its eigenvalues at the stationary point. All positive is a bowl in every direction — a genuine minimum. All negative is a dome — a genuine maximum. A mix is a saddle. When a function's Hessian has all-positive eigenvalues everywhere, not just at one point, it's called convex — and for a convex function, any stationary point found is guaranteed to be the true global optimum, not just a local one.

Section 4

Now put a fence around the hill

You're only allowed to stand on one specific path — not anywhere on the hillside. The unrestricted summit might not even be on it.

The picture
Dashed rings: points where f has equal value, rising toward the blue peak. Anywhere else on the amber fence, the two arrows would point in different directions — and sliding along the fence would still improve f.

Plain ∇f = 0 is now the wrong thing to check — the unrestricted peak may not even sit on the fence, and even where the fence is feasible, walking along it might still improve f. The best point on the fence is instead where walking further in either direction along it stops helping. At that exact point, something striking is always true: the objective's gradient and the fence's gradient point along the exact same line. If they didn't, there'd be a leftover direction along the fence that still climbs — meaning that point wasn't the best one on the fence yet.

Section 5

The Lagrangian folds the fence into the hill

One new function, one new variable λ, and the fence's gradient condition falls straight out of ordinary calculus.

Try it
ℒ(x, λ)
=
f(x)
−
λ (g(x) − k)
The objective, f(x), minus the multiplier λ times "how far the constraint is from being satisfied." λ is a brand-new variable, solved for alongside x.
f'(x)
=
λ g'(x)
Read plainly: the objective's gradient and the constraint's gradient must be parallel — exact scalar multiples of one another, with λ as the scaling factor. This is the fence picture, made algebraic.
g(x)
=
k
The original constraint, recovered exactly. ℒ is built so that differentiating with respect to λ hands the constraint straight back — feasibility is enforced by the same machinery, not checked separately.

λ itself is not just algebra bookkeeping: its value measures how much the optimal objective would change per unit change in the constraint's target k — a genuine shadow price for the constraint. A related idea, the KKT conditions, extend this exact logic to constraints that only need to hold as ≤ or ≥ rather than exactly — an inequality constraint is either "active" (binding, behaving just like the equality case above, λ ≠ 0) or "slack" (not limiting anything, λ = 0).

Where this needs qualifying

A stationary point of ℒ is still only a candidate — exactly as with the unconstrained case, a Lagrangian stationary point could be a saddle rather than a genuine constrained optimum unless a second-order check (or convexity) confirms it.

Section 6

Minimizing portfolio risk is this exact problem

Two made-up but plausible assets. Find the mix that hits a 6% target return with the smallest possible variance.

Minimizing a portfolio's variance subject to hitting a target return is a Lagrange-multiplier problem wearing a trading costume. "Best portfolio subject to a return target" is exactly "best point on the fence" from the last two slides.
Worked by hand

Asset 1: μ₁ = 8%, σ₁ = 20%. Asset 2: μ₂ = 4%, σ₂ = 10%. Correlation ρ = 0.30. With only two assets and w₂ = 1 − w₁, the 6% return target alone pins down the one free weight:

w₁(8) + (1−w₁)(4)
=
6
Solves to w₁ = 0.5, w₂ = 0.5 — an even split.
σₚ²
=
0.25(0.04) + 0.25(0.01) + 0.5(0.30)(0.02)
σₚ² = 0.0155, so σₚ = √0.0155 ≈ 12.4% — below Asset 1's own 20% volatility, and even below a naive 50/50 average of the two (15%), because the correlation is well under 1 and diversification genuinely helps.
With exactly two assets and one return target, the return equation alone solves for w₁ — no multiplier needed to arrive at the number. λ becomes indispensable once a third asset (or a second constraint, like "weights sum to 1," handled separately) enters and there's an actual trade-off to balance rather than one equation with one unknown.

Section 8

Nobody solves a 50-asset Lagrangian by hand

A numerical optimizer does the algebra. What survives into daily use is the vocabulary and the intuition.

📈

The efficient frontier

Solving this exact Lagrangian for many different target returns at once traces out the whole frontier — one λ value per point on the curve.

💰

Shadow prices

A Lagrange multiplier's value is routinely read as a shadow price — how much the optimal objective would improve per unit of relaxing a binding constraint (a risk budget, a leverage cap, a sector limit).

🧮

Numerical solvers, not hand algebra

Real portfolio and risk-parity optimizers use quadratic/convex-programming software implementing KKT conditions internally — this lesson's hand-derivation is what to check the output against, not a step performed live.

🚧

Binding vs. slack, in practice

Practitioners routinely check which constraints in a large optimization are actually binding (nonzero multiplier) versus slack (multiplier zero, not limiting anything) — the active/inactive distinction from Section 5.

Whenever a portfolio, risk, or calibration problem is described as finding the "best" something "subject to" some condition, the Lagrangian/KKT machinery is running underneath — whether or not the report ever uses the word.
Section 9

Eyeballing the turn vs. checking the rule

"Where does this curve stop falling and start rising" can be answered two ways.

Eyeballing it
  • Squint at a chart or a table of values and guess where the turn looks like it happens
  • No way to confirm it's a minimum rather than a saddle or a max
  • Falls apart the moment there's more than one variable, or a constraint bolted on
  • Can't be checked or automated — it's a judgment call, not a method
Checking the rule
  • Set the derivative (or gradient) to zero — first-order condition
  • Check the second derivative's sign, or the Hessian's eigenvalues — second-order condition
  • Add a fence: build ℒ, take its own first-order conditions with respect to x and λ
  • Same method whether it's one variable or fifty, unconstrained or not
Where this needs qualifying

Two common practical mistakes go the other direction from "eyeballing": treating any stationary point as automatically optimal without a second-order check, and assuming every stated inequality constraint must be binding when the true optimum may sit comfortably inside it. And a mathematically exact optimum is only as good as its inputs — Markowitz-style optimization is well known to be highly sensitive to estimation error in expected returns, μ, especially.

Section 10

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Why isn't f'(x*) = 0 alone enough to conclude x* is a minimum?

The first-order condition only says the function is momentarily flat. The sign of f''(x*), or the Hessian's eigenvalues in several variables, resolves which kind of flat point it actually is.
Question 2

At a constrained optimum, what must be true of the objective's gradient ∇f and the constraint's gradient ∇g?

If they weren't parallel, the objective's gradient would have a leftover component running along the constraint — meaning a small move along the fence would still improve things, so the point couldn't have been optimal yet.
Question 3

Two assets: μ₁ = 8%, μ₂ = 4%. What weight on Asset 1 hits a 6% target return?

w₁(8) + (1−w₁)(4) = 6 → 4 + 4w₁ = 6 → w₁ = 0.5.
Question 4

A constraint's Lagrange multiplier comes out to exactly zero at the optimum. What does that mean?

A zero multiplier means the constraint has no marginal effect on the optimal objective — the true best solution doesn't need to push up against that limit at all.
Question 5

What do KKT conditions add on top of ordinary Lagrange multipliers?

Lagrange multipliers as built in this lesson assume an exact equality constraint. KKT conditions generalize the same parallel-gradients logic to constraints that only need to hold as an inequality.
Section 11

What to carry forward

01

An unconstrained optimum has a flat gradient — f'(x*) = 0, or ∇f(x*) = 0 with several variables — meaning no direction still improves the objective.

02

The second-order condition tells minimum from maximum from saddle — the sign of f''(x*), or the Hessian's eigenvalue signs, resolves what the first-order condition leaves ambiguous.

03

The Lagrangian, ℒ = f(x) − λ(g(x) − k), folds a constraint into the objective — its own first-order conditions recover the constraint automatically and force the two gradients to be parallel at the optimum.

04

λ is a shadow price, not just algebra — it measures how much the optimal objective would change per unit change in the constraint's target.

05

Minimizing portfolio variance for a target return is exactly this Lagrangian problem — the two-asset worked example is the smallest possible instance of full Markowitz portfolio theory.

06

KKT conditions extend the same logic to inequality constraints, and real solvers use them numerically — the hand-derivation here is for checking output, not for large problems.

What this now lets you do

Recognise any "find the best X subject to Y" problem as a Lagrangian in disguise, and know what its multiplier actually means economically. Tell a genuine optimum apart from a merely stationary point. See a portfolio manager's minimum-variance calculation for what it is — this lesson's fence-and-hill picture, run at institutional scale.
