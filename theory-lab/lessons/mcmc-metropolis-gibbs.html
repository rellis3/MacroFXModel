MCMC: Metropolis-Hastings & Gibbs Sampling

When no conjugate prior exists, the posterior can't be written down in closed form — but you can still build a random walk that visits every point in proportion to how likely it is, and let the visit frequencies stand in for the distribution itself.

CONCEPT — explained here, not a claim this repo has built or validated it ~17 min read Prereqs: Conjugate Priors & Bayesian Updating in Practice
TL;DR
  • Conjugate updating (previous lesson) only works for a short list of matched prior/likelihood pairs. Almost every realistic posterior has no closed form at all — MCMC computes it anyway, by simulation.
  • Metropolis-Hastings: propose \(\theta' \sim q(\theta'\mid\theta)\), accept with probability \(\alpha=\min\!\big(1,\ \tfrac{p(\theta')q(\theta\mid\theta')}{p(\theta)q(\theta'\mid\theta)}\big)\); repeat. The resulting chain's long-run visit frequencies converge to the target distribution \(p(\theta)\), because the accept/reject rule is built to satisfy detailed balance.
  • Gibbs sampling is the special case where you always propose a parameter directly from its true full conditional distribution — the acceptance probability algebraically collapses to exactly 1, so every proposal is accepted.
  • Worked example: five explicit steps of a 1-D random-walk Metropolis chain sampling from a bimodal (double-well) target, computed by hand — two accepts, one reject, two automatic uphill accepts.
  • A chain isn't trustworthy until it's diagnosed: trace plots, burn-in, effective sample size, and the Gelman-Rubin \(\hat{R}\) statistic are the standard checks, and the interactive chart below lets you watch mixing quality change with proposal step size.
Section 01

Why this matters

The Conjugate Priors & Bayesian Updating in Practice lesson showed that Beta-Binomial and Normal-Normal updates have a clean closed form — the posterior is a known distribution family, computed with a few lines of algebra. That's the exception, not the rule. The moment a model has more than one parameter interacting nonlinearly, or a likelihood that isn't matched to any standard prior family, the posterior distribution can still be written down as "prior times likelihood, renormalized" — but the renormalizing constant (the denominator \(P(E)\) from Bayesian Inference & Updating's derivation) becomes a high-dimensional integral with no closed-form answer. You can't evaluate the posterior's density directly, and you often can't even compute it up to a normalizing constant in a form you can integrate.

Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sidesteps the integral entirely. Instead of computing the posterior's shape analytically, it builds a random walk — a sequence of proposed and accepted/rejected parameter values — engineered so that, after enough steps, the *frequency* with which the walk visits each region of parameter space matches the posterior's actual probability there. You never compute the normalizing constant; you only ever need the posterior's shape up to that constant, because the constant cancels out of every ratio the algorithm computes. This is the single idea that unlocked Bayesian statistics for realistic, multi-parameter models — hierarchical models (next lesson), Hidden Markov Models, and essentially all modern Bayesian machine learning depend on it.

💡
Plain-English intuition

A random walk that prefers high ground, but doesn't refuse to go downhill

Imagine the posterior distribution as a landscape — hills where the parameter is plausible, valleys where it isn't. You're a hiker dropped somewhere on this landscape who wants to spend time at each spot in proportion to its height (its plausibility), without ever being handed a map. The strategy: from where you stand, propose a random nearby step. If it's uphill (more plausible), always take it. If it's downhill (less plausible), take it anyway sometimes — specifically, with probability equal to how much less plausible the new spot is compared to the current one. Never refusing all downhill moves is essential: without it, you'd walk straight to the nearest hilltop and get stuck there forever, never exploring the rest of the landscape or discovering a second, separate hill somewhere else. Take enough steps this way, and a histogram of everywhere you've stood becomes an accurate picture of the entire landscape's shape — even though you never once computed its exact height at any point, only ever compared two heights to each other.

Section 02

The math, step by step

Metropolis-Hastings

Let \(p(\theta)\) denote the target distribution — the posterior you want samples from, known only up to a constant (write \(p(\theta) \propto f(\theta)\) where \(f\) is computable but the true normalizing integral isn't). At each step, given the chain's current position \(\theta\), a proposal distribution \(q(\theta' \mid \theta)\) generates a candidate next point \(\theta'\) — commonly a Normal distribution centered at \(\theta\) (a "random walk" proposal). Accept the candidate with probability:

\[ \alpha(\theta,\theta') = \min\!\left(1,\ \frac{p(\theta')\,q(\theta \mid \theta')}{p(\theta)\,q(\theta' \mid \theta)}\right) \]
p(θ')/p(θ)how much more (or less) plausible the proposed point is
q(θ∣θ')/q(θ'∣θ)correction for an asymmetric proposal — how easy it is to propose the reverse move

If accepted, the chain moves to \(\theta'\); if rejected, the chain stays at \(\theta\) (this counts as a repeat of the current value — it is not discarded). Because \(p\) only appears as a ratio \(p(\theta')/p(\theta)\), any normalizing constant in \(p\) cancels exactly — the algorithm only ever needs the unnormalized \(f(\theta') / f(\theta)\), never the true \(p(\theta)\) itself. When the proposal is symmetric (\(q(\theta'\mid\theta) = q(\theta\mid\theta')\), as for a Normal random walk centered at the current point), the \(q\) terms cancel too, and the rule collapses to the original 1953 Metropolis algorithm's simpler acceptance probability, \(\alpha = \min(1,\ p(\theta')/p(\theta))\) — exactly the ratio used in the worked example below. Hastings' 1970 generalization keeps the \(q\) correction term, allowing asymmetric proposals.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Think of exploring a dark room by randomly wandering: at each step you try a random nearby spot. If it feels more interesting than where you're standing, you always move there. If it feels less interesting, you still sometimes move there anyway — how often depends on just how much less interesting it is. That "sometimes wander somewhere worse" habit is what stops you from getting stuck pacing back and forth on the single most interesting spot you happen to find first, and it's the entire accept/reject rule above, in one sentence.

u ~ Uniform(0,1)

θ₁, θ₂, …
The chain
After enough steps, its visit frequencies approximate p(θ)

Why this converges to the target — detailed balance, conceptually. A Markov chain has \(p(\theta)\) as its long-run, stationary distribution if the rate of probability flowing from any state \(\theta\) to any other state \(\theta'\) exactly equals the rate flowing back — a symmetry condition called detailed balance: \(p(\theta)\,T(\theta \to \theta') = p(\theta')\,T(\theta' \to \theta)\), where \(T\) is the chain's transition probability (proposal times acceptance). The Metropolis-Hastings acceptance rule is deliberately constructed so this equation holds automatically for the specific \(\alpha\) formula above — plug the acceptance rule into both sides and the proposal densities and the min() terms cancel out to leave an identity. Detailed balance is a sufficient condition for \(p(\theta)\) to be a stationary distribution of the chain (not the only possible one, but the one MCMC is built around): once you're sampling from \(p\), one more accept/reject step keeps you sampling from \(p\). Combined with the chain being able to eventually reach any region of positive probability (a separate technical condition called irreducibility), this guarantees that regardless of where the chain starts, its distribution converges toward \(p(\theta)\) as the number of steps grows — which is precisely why a histogram of a long enough chain approximates the target distribution, without ever computing its normalizing constant.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Detailed balance just means "as much traffic flows from A to B as flows back from B to A." Picture two neighboring towns connected by a road, with people moving back and forth every day. If the flow in each direction is perfectly balanced, the populations of both towns stay steady over time, even though individual people keep moving. The accept/reject rule is designed so that kind of two-way balance holds between every pair of spots in parameter space — which is exactly why the chain settles into a stable, unchanging pattern of visit frequencies that matches the target distribution.

Gibbs sampling — the special case with 100% acceptance

Gibbs sampling applies when the target has multiple parameters, \(\theta = (\theta_1,\dots,\theta_k)\), and each parameter's full conditional distribution — its distribution with every other parameter held fixed at their current values, \(p(\theta_j \mid \theta_{-j}, \text{data})\) — is itself something you can sample from directly (often because it's conjugate, one parameter at a time). Gibbs sampling cycles through the parameters, updating one at a time by drawing directly from its full conditional:

\[ \theta_j^{(t+1)} \sim p\big(\theta_j \mid \theta_1^{(t+1)},\dots,\theta_{j-1}^{(t+1)},\theta_{j+1}^{(t)},\dots,\theta_k^{(t)},\ \text{data}\big) \quad \text{for } j=1,\dots,k \]

This is Metropolis-Hastings with the proposal distribution \(q\) chosen to equal the true full conditional itself. Substitute \(q(\theta_j' \mid \theta) = p(\theta_j' \mid \theta_{-j})\) into the general acceptance ratio: \(p(\theta') = p(\theta_j'\mid\theta_{-j})\,p(\theta_{-j})\), and \(q(\theta_j \mid \theta') = p(\theta_j \mid \theta_{-j})\) (the reverse move's proposal density, at the same fixed \(\theta_{-j}\)); the \(p(\theta_{-j})\) and full-conditional terms all cancel exactly, leaving the ratio identically equal to 1 — the proposal is always accepted. This is why Gibbs sampling is normally described separately from "generic" Metropolis-Hastings even though it's mathematically a special case: there's no accept/reject step to tune at all, only the choice of which full conditional to derive and sample from next.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Regular Metropolis-Hastings is like guessing where to step next and then checking whether that guess was any good. Gibbs sampling skips the guessing part entirely — for each parameter in turn, it draws directly from the exact right answer for "given everything else is fixed, what should this one be." There's nothing to double-check, so nothing ever gets rejected; it's the difference between guessing a friend's exact address and simply being handed it.

Worked example

Five explicit steps of random-walk Metropolis, by hand

Target (unnormalized): \(f(\theta) = \exp(-\theta^4/4 + \theta^2/2)\), the same bimodal shape used in the chart above. Start at \(\theta_0 = 0\), where \(f(0) = e^0 = 1\). At each step, a proposed increment \(\Delta\) is given (standing in for a draw from the proposal), and a uniform draw \(u\) decides accept/reject whenever the move is downhill.

1

Propose θ′ = 0 + 0.8 = 0.8 (uphill)

\(f(0.8) = \exp(-0.8^4/4 + 0.8^2/2) = \exp(-0.1024+0.32) = \exp(0.2176) = 1.2431\). Ratio \(=1.2431/1=1.2431 > 1\), so \(\alpha=\min(1,1.2431)=1\) — automatic accept, no coin flip needed. New state: \(\theta_1 = 0.8\).

2

Propose θ′ = 0.8 − 1.5 = −0.7 (downhill)

\(f(-0.7) = \exp(-0.7^4/4+0.7^2/2) = \exp(-0.060025+0.245) = \exp(0.184975) = 1.2032\). Ratio \(=1.2032/1.2431=0.9679\), so \(\alpha=0.9679\). Draw \(u=0.55\): since \(u<\alpha\), accept. New state: \(\theta_2=-0.7\).

3

Propose θ′ = −0.7 + 0.3 = −0.4 (downhill)

\(f(-0.4) = \exp(-0.4^4/4+0.4^2/2) = \exp(-0.0064+0.08) = \exp(0.0736) = 1.0764\). Ratio \(=1.0764/1.2032=0.8946\), so \(\alpha=0.8946\). Draw \(u=0.93\): since \(u>\alpha\), reject — the chain repeats its current value. New state: \(\theta_3=-0.7\) (unchanged).

4

Propose θ′ = −0.7 − 0.9 = −1.6 (downhill)

\(f(-1.6) = \exp(-1.6^4/4+1.6^2/2) = \exp(-1.6384+1.28) = \exp(-0.3584) = 0.6988\). Ratio \(=0.6988/1.2032=0.5808\), so \(\alpha=0.5808\). Draw \(u=0.21\): since \(u<\alpha\), accept. New state: \(\theta_4=-1.6\).

5

Propose θ′ = −1.6 + 0.5 = −1.1 (uphill)

\(f(-1.1) = \exp(-1.1^4/4+1.1^2/2) = \exp(-0.366025+0.605) = \exp(0.238975) = 1.2699\). Ratio \(=1.2699/0.6988=1.8175 > 1\), so \(\alpha=1\) — automatic accept. New state: \(\theta_5=-1.1\).

After five steps: two automatic uphill accepts, one downhill accept, one rejection, and the chain has drifted from \(\theta_0=0\) (the dip between the two modes) toward \(\theta_5=-1.1\), right next to the left mode at \(\theta=-1\). Notice it never "knew" the modes were at \(\pm1\) — it only ever compared \(f\) at two nearby points, over and over, and that alone was enough to pull it toward high ground. A genuine risk visible even in this tiny run: the chain has now spent its time near the left mode only — it hasn't yet demonstrated it can also visit the right mode near \(\theta=+1\), which is exactly the kind of mixing problem the diagnostics below exist to catch.

🎯
Real-world scenario

A researcher fitting a nonlinear volatility model

Suppose a researcher wants a Bayesian posterior over the parameters of a nonlinear stochastic-volatility model — say a mean-reversion speed, a long-run variance level, and a vol-of-vol parameter, all interacting multiplicatively inside a likelihood that has no algebraic closed form. There is no conjugate prior for this likelihood — the Beta-Binomial and Normal-Normal tricks from the previous lesson simply don't apply here. The researcher instead writes code that can evaluate the unnormalized posterior density (prior × likelihood) at any candidate parameter vector, builds a Metropolis-Hastings sampler with a multivariate Normal random-walk proposal, runs several independent chains from different starting points for tens of thousands of iterations, discards an initial burn-in period, and checks trace plots and \(\hat{R}\) before trusting the output as a genuine sample from the posterior over the three parameters jointly — including their correlations with each other, which a simpler method fitting each parameter separately would have missed entirely. This describes the mechanics of the method, not a claim that such a model has been built, fit, or validated anywhere in this repo.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

Running an MCMC sampler is only step one — a chain that hasn't been diagnosed is not yet a trustworthy sample from the posterior. Four checks are standard practice:

📉

Trace plots

A plot of the sampled value against iteration number, like the chart above. A healthy chain looks like noisy static around a stable band ("hairy caterpillar"); a chain that drifts, gets stuck flat for long stretches, or shows an obvious trend hasn't converged or is mixing poorly.

✂️

Burn-in

The first chunk of iterations, taken before the chain has settled into the target distribution's typical region, is discarded — those early samples reflect the (arbitrary) starting point more than the posterior itself.

🔗

Effective sample size (ESS)

MCMC samples are autocorrelated — each draw resembles the one before it, unlike truly independent samples. ESS estimates how many independent draws the chain's correlated samples are actually "worth" for estimating a quantity like the posterior mean; a chain of 10,000 correlated draws might carry the information of only a few hundred independent ones.

📐

Gelman-Rubin R̂

Run several chains from different, deliberately over-dispersed starting points. If they've all converged to the same target, the variance between chains should be no larger than the variance within each one; R̂ formalizes that ratio and should sit close to 1.00 (commonly, below about 1.01) before the chains are trusted.

The Gelman-Rubin statistic, precisely: with \(m\) chains each of length \(n\) (after discarding burn-in), let \(\bar{\theta}_j\) be chain \(j\)'s mean and \(\bar{\theta}\) the grand mean across all chains. The between-chain variance is \(B = \frac{n}{m-1}\sum_j(\bar{\theta}_j-\bar{\theta})^2\); the within-chain variance is \(W = \frac{1}{m}\sum_j s_j^2\) (the average of each chain's own sample variance \(s_j^2\)). A pooled variance estimate, \(\widehat{\text{Var}}^+ = \frac{n-1}{n}W + \frac{1}{n}B\), is compared to \(W\) alone:

\[ \hat{R} = \sqrt{\frac{\widehat{\text{Var}}^+}{W}} \]
Waverage within-chain variance — how much each chain wiggles on its own
Bbetween-chain variance — how much the chains' overall levels disagree with each other

If every chain has converged to the same target distribution, \(B\) is small relative to \(W\), and \(\hat{R}\to 1\). If chains are still stuck exploring different regions (e.g. one chain trapped near \(\theta=+1\) and another near \(\theta=-1\) in the bimodal example above, neither having mixed across), \(B\) is large, inflating \(\widehat{\text{Var}}^+\) well above \(W\), and \(\hat{R}\) sits noticeably above 1 — a direct, quantitative flag that the chains disagree about where the posterior actually is.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

R̂ is a sanity check that asks several independent chains, "did you all end up describing the same place?" It's like sending several search parties out from different starting points to map the same forest — if they all come back with roughly the same map, you can trust it. If one party keeps describing a totally different forest, something went wrong, and R̂ well above 1.00 is the number that tells you so.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where MCMC quietly goes wrong

Mistaking "it ran without error" for "it converged." A sampler with a badly tuned step size, a multimodal target, or too few iterations will happily produce ten thousand numbers that look like a posterior sample and are actually garbage — biased toward whichever region the chain happened to wander into first. The worked example above is a small, deliberately visible instance: five steps is nowhere near enough to have explored both modes of the target.

Tuning the proposal step size wrong in either direction. Too small, and the chain crawls, taking a very long time to traverse the full distribution (high autocorrelation, low ESS). Too large, and almost every downhill proposal gets rejected, so the chain barely moves at all — also high autocorrelation, for the opposite reason. Both look like "it's running" without producing a useful sample; a moderate acceptance rate (often targeted around 20-50% depending on dimensionality) is the standard rule of thumb, not a coincidence.

Running only one chain. A single chain can look perfectly well-behaved in its own trace plot while having converged to the wrong mode entirely, in a multimodal posterior — there's nothing inside that one chain's history to reveal the mistake. This is precisely why R̂ requires multiple, independently started chains: it's a check that different starting points end up agreeing.

Treating R̂≈1 as proof, not evidence. R̂ close to 1 means the chains agree with each other — it does not prove they've found the true posterior if all of them share the same blind spot (a region of parameter space none of them was ever likely to propose a move into). Diagnostics reduce risk; they don't eliminate it.

Section 04

Key Takeaways

✓MCMC replaces an intractable integral with a random walk. The chain's long-run visit frequencies approximate the target distribution — the normalizing constant is never computed, only ratios of the unnormalized density.
✓Detailed balance is why it works. The Metropolis-Hastings accept/reject rule is built so probability flow between any two states balances exactly, which makes the target distribution stationary for the chain.
✓Gibbs sampling always accepts because proposing directly from a parameter's true full conditional makes the Metropolis-Hastings ratio algebraically equal to 1 — no tuning, but it requires those conditionals to be sampleable in closed form.
⚠A chain that runs is not automatically a chain that converged. Trace plots, burn-in, ESS, and R̂ across multiple chains are the standard, non-optional checks before trusting any MCMC output.
⚠Proposal step size is a real tuning problem. Too small or too large both produce poor mixing, just for opposite reasons — the interactive chart above makes this failure mode directly visible.
✗This lesson explains a computational method, not a trading signal. MCMC is a way to compute a posterior that has no closed form — whatever model it's applied to still has to earn its keep OOS, exactly like every other idea in this curriculum.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. Why doesn't Metropolis-Hastings need to know the posterior's normalizing constant?
Because the acceptance probability only ever uses the ratio p(θ')/p(θ) (times a proposal-density correction). Any normalizing constant that divides both p(θ') and p(θ) cancels exactly in that ratio, so the algorithm only needs the unnormalized density — prior times likelihood, without ever computing the integral that would normalize it into a true probability distribution.
Q2. In the worked example, why was the move from θ=0 to θ′=0.8 accepted automatically, with no coin flip?
Because f(0.8)=1.2431 is larger than f(0)=1 — the proposed point is uphill (more plausible than the current point), so the ratio f(θ')/f(θ) exceeds 1, and α=min(1, ratio)=1. An acceptance probability of exactly 1 means the move is always taken; there's nothing for a uniform draw u to compare against, since u≤1 always holds.
Q3. Why is Gibbs sampling's acceptance probability always exactly 1?
Because Gibbs sampling's proposal distribution is chosen to be the parameter's own true full conditional distribution. Substituting that choice into the general Metropolis-Hastings ratio makes the forward and reverse move probabilities cancel algebraically against the target density's own conditional structure, collapsing the ratio to exactly 1 — so every proposed move is accepted, with no separate accept/reject step needed.
Q4. What does a Gelman-Rubin R̂ well above 1 (e.g. 1.3) tell you, and what should you do about it?
It means the between-chain variance B is large relative to the within-chain variance W — the independently started chains disagree about where the posterior's mass actually is, which is a strong signal they have not converged (e.g. each may be stuck near a different mode of a multimodal target). The standard response is to run the chains much longer, improve the proposal (e.g. tune its step size or use a smarter proposal shape), or reconsider the model — not to report the current samples as a trustworthy posterior.
Q5. Why can a proposal step size that's too large produce a poorly mixing chain, given that large steps sound like they'd explore more?
Because a large step is very likely to land somewhere with much lower target density than the current point — the acceptance ratio p(θ')/p(θ) becomes small, so most proposals get rejected and the chain repeats its current value over and over. The chain is technically "capable" of large jumps, but in practice accepts so few of them that it barely moves at all — the opposite failure mode from a too-small step size (which moves constantly but only by tiny amounts), yet with the same symptom: high autocorrelation and low effective sample size.
