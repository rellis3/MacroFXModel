Granger Causality & Lead-Lag Analysis

The Vector Error Correction Model told you, for one already-cointegrated pair, which side does the correcting. That's a narrow question about one specific kind of relationship. This lesson asks something broader and more precisely defined: for any two series at all, does one's past genuinely sharpen a forecast of the other's future — beyond what the target's own history already gives you — and can that claim be pinned down with an exact F-test rather than a hunch about which line moved first on the chart?

CONCEPT — no Granger-causality F-test or VAR-based causality regression exists anywhere in this repo (grepped js/, DecisionEngine/, every root .py file, and server.js); every hit for "Granger" is Engle-Granger's cointegration test (a different contribution by the same author, already covered in the Cointegration lesson) or an unrelated Granger-Ramanathan regression weighting scheme in js/volHorseRaceEngine.js — see "How practitioners actually use this" for the simpler cross-correlation cousin that genuinely does exist, in several places ~18 min read Prereqs: ARIMA & Vector Autoregression (VAR), Cointegration & Statistical Arbitrage, Vector Error Correction Models
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • \(X\) Granger-causes \(Y\) means one precise, narrow thing: adding \(X\)'s lagged values to a regression that already contains \(Y\)'s own lags significantly improves the forecast of \(Y\). Nothing more, nothing metaphysical — a statistical, incremental-predictive-power claim, not a structural one.
  • The test is a nested-regression F-test: fit a restricted model (\(Y_t\) on its own lags only) and an unrestricted model (\(Y_t\) on its own lags plus \(X\)'s lags), then test whether the \(X\) coefficients are jointly zero via \(F = \frac{(RSS_r - RSS_u)/q}{RSS_u/(n-k)}\), where \(q\) is the number of dropped \(X\) lags and \(k\) is the unrestricted model's parameter count.
  • Two mandatory pre-checks, both skippable in ways that silently break the test: pick the lag order \(p\) with AIC/BIC before testing (the Model Selection: AIC & BIC lesson derives both criteria), and confirm both series are stationary first (test on returns, not price levels — unless the pair is cointegrated, in which case the VECM lesson's error-correction framework is the correct home for this question, not a plain differenced VAR).
  • A worked toy example below fits both regressions on 12 observations and gets \(F \approx 38.6\) against \(q=1\), \(n-k=9\) degrees of freedom — comfortably past even the 1% critical value of 10.56, a clean reject-the-null result computed end to end and hand-checkable.
  • Run the test in both directions. \(X\) Granger-causing \(Y\) and \(Y\) Granger-causing \(X\) are two separate hypotheses on two separate regressions; both can be significant at once (genuine feedback), one can be, or neither.
  • Cross-correlation of returns at various lags — \(\text{corr}(X_t, Y_{t+k})\) for a range of \(k\) — is the simpler, non-regression cousin of the identical lead-lag question, and unlike Granger causality it does not control for \(Y\)'s own persistence. This repo genuinely runs several versions of that simpler tool in production; grep results are in "How practitioners actually use this" below. No Granger F-test exists anywhere in the codebase.
Section 01

Why this matters

The Vector Error Correction Models lesson answered a narrow, specific question about a narrow, specific situation: given two series already proven cointegrated, which one does most of the work correcting back toward equilibrium when the spread stretches too far? That question only even makes sense for a cointegrated pair — it presupposes a stable long-run relationship exists at all. This lesson asks a different, more general question that applies to any two time series, cointegrated or not, related or not: does one series' recent history genuinely help forecast the other's next move, beyond what the target series' own history already tells you? That is the literal content of Granger causality, and it is the formal machinery behind every "does the yield spread lead FX" or "does this cross lead that one" question this curriculum and this repo's own research code keep raising informally.

This ground is not entirely new. The ARIMA & Vector Autoregression (VAR) lesson already introduces Granger causality as the natural diagnostic to run on a fitted VAR — it states the restricted-vs-unrestricted F-test in one paragraph, gives the rooster-and-sunrise analogy for why "Granger-causes" isn't real causation, and walks through a narrated (but explicitly not yet fitted) example asking whether a DE–US yield spread leads EUR/USD. That lesson is the right place for VAR itself: how a multi-series regression system is built and estimated, lag by lag. What it deliberately does not do — flagged by its own "no such fit has actually been run" caveat — is write out the F-statistic's exact formula, walk a worked numeric example all the way through, build a live calculator, or work through the pre-checks and pitfalls in depth. That is the entire content of this lesson: the same diagnostic, taken from a one-paragraph mention to a fully worked, testable procedure.

💡
Plain-English intuition

A narrower, more honest way to ask "does X move first?"

Staring at a chart and noticing "the spread always seems to dip a day before FX sells off" is a real observation, but it's an observation about one dataset, filtered through one person's eye for patterns — exactly the kind of thing that's easy to convince yourself of after the fact. Granger's actual proposal, from his 1969 paper, is a specific test that replaces that impression with a number: build the best possible forecast of \(Y\) using only \(Y\)'s own past, then build a second forecast that also gets to see \(X\)'s past, and check — formally, with an F-test — whether the second forecast is significantly better. If \(X\)'s history buys nothing once \(Y\)'s own history is already accounted for, \(X\) doesn't Granger-cause \(Y\), full stop, no matter how compelling a handful of chart-reading instances looked.

The name is famously misleading, and Granger himself was careful about this: "Granger-causes" is not a claim that \(X\) makes \(Y\) happen. It is a claim that \(X\)'s history carries genuine, incremental forecasting content for \(Y\) — which a shared, unobserved driver hitting both series on a slightly different clock can produce just as easily as a real causal channel can. Treat every result this lesson's test produces as "X's past has predictive value for Y," never as "X causes Y" — the distinction is not pedantic, it's the entire content of what the test can and can't tell you.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine trying to guess tomorrow's temperature. First you guess using only the last week's temperatures. Then you guess again, but this time you're also allowed to look at the last week's ice cream sales. If peeking at ice cream sales genuinely makes your temperature guess better — not just different, actually more accurate — then ice cream sales "Granger-cause" temperature by this test's narrow definition. Obviously ice cream doesn't make the weather happen; both are driven by the actual sun, which the test never sees directly. That's exactly the trap: the test can only tell you "this extra information helped predict," never "this is the true reason."

Section 02

The math, step by step

This section states Granger causality's precise statistical definition, the two nested regressions the test actually compares, the F-statistic formula in full, and the two pre-checks (lag order, stationarity) that have to happen before any of it is meaningful.

\(Y_t, X_t\)The two series under test — is \(X\)'s past useful for forecasting \(Y\)? (The reverse direction, does \(Y\)'s past forecast \(X\), is a separate test with the roles swapped — see the bidirectional discussion below.)
\(p\)The lag order — how many past periods of each series enter the regression. Chosen by AIC/BIC before the causality test, not tuned to make the test come out significant.
\(a_i, b_j\)Coefficients on \(Y\)'s own lags and on \(X\)'s lags, respectively, in the unrestricted model.
\(RSS_r, RSS_u\)Residual sum of squares from the restricted (no \(X\) lags) and unrestricted (with \(X\) lags) regressions.
\(q\)The number of restrictions being tested — equal to \(p\), the number of \(X\)-lag coefficients set to zero in the restricted model.
\(k\)The total number of estimated parameters in the unrestricted model (intercept plus all \(Y\)-lag and \(X\)-lag coefficients).
\(n\)The number of observations actually used in the regression (the full sample minus \(p\) lost to lagging).
\(F\)The test statistic, compared against the \(F(q, n-k)\) distribution's critical value.

1. The precise definition. This is a statistical definition, not a philosophical one, and it is defined entirely in terms of one specific regression comparison:

\(X\) Granger-causes \(Y\) if, and only if, including past values of \(X\) in a regression that already contains past values of \(Y\) produces a statistically significant improvement in forecasting \(Y\)'s current value — measured by whether the added \(X\)-lag coefficients are jointly, significantly different from zero. That's the whole definition. It says nothing about mechanism, nothing about which series "really" drives the other in some deeper sense, and nothing that survives outside this specific VAR/regression framework.

2. The two nested regressions. Everything rests on comparing a restricted model against an unrestricted one that nests it — the unrestricted model reduces exactly to the restricted model when every \(b_j\) is set to zero:

\[ \textbf{Restricted:}\quad Y_t = c + \sum_{i=1}^{p} a_i\, Y_{t-i} + e_t \]
\[ \textbf{Unrestricted:}\quad Y_t = c + \sum_{i=1}^{p} a_i\, Y_{t-i} + \sum_{j=1}^{p} b_j\, X_{t-j} + \epsilon_t \]
Σ a_i Y_{t-i}Y's own momentum — present in both models, untouched by the test
Σ b_j X_{t-j}the new content — dropped entirely in the restricted model, the only thing the test asks about
e_t, ε_teach model's own residual — its own RSS is what the test compares

The null hypothesis is exactly \(H_0: b_1 = b_2 = \dots = b_p = 0\) — every added \(X\)-lag coefficient is zero, meaning \(X\)'s history adds nothing. Fit both regressions by ordinary least squares on the same sample (this lesson takes OLS fitting itself as given, exactly as the Cointegration and VECM lessons treat their own regression coefficients as already estimated inputs), record each one's residual sum of squares, and compare.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Two students take the same test to predict tomorrow's price. Student A (the restricted model) only gets to study the price's own past. Student B (the unrestricted model) gets to study the price's own past and another series' past. If Student B's answer sheet has way less total error than Student A's — more than could plausibly happen just from Student B having extra, useless scratch paper to fidget with — then that other series genuinely helped, and it Granger-causes the price. If Student B does barely any better despite the extra material, the other series wasn't carrying real information.

3. The F-statistic, in full. A larger unrestricted model can never have higher RSS than its nested restricted version — adding regressors can only help or do nothing, since OLS could always set every \(b_j = 0\) and recover the restricted fit exactly if that were truly optimal. The F-test asks whether the RSS drop is bigger than pure chance would produce from adding \(q\) meaningless extra regressors:

\[ F = \frac{\left(RSS_r - RSS_u\right)/q}{RSS_u/(n-k)} \]
(RSS_r − RSS_u)/q
Numerator
The average RSS improvement per restriction removed — how much better the fit gets, per added X-lag coefficient
RSS_u/(n−k)
Denominator
The unrestricted model's own residual variance estimate — the yardstick "how much RSS improvement is unremarkable, just from noise"
F ~ F(q, n−k)
Reference distribution
Under H₀ (all bⱼ = 0), F follows an F-distribution with q and n−k degrees of freedom

A large \(F\) means the \(X\) lags bought a fit improvement well beyond what \(q\) extra, genuinely useless regressors would be expected to buy by chance alone — reject \(H_0\), and call the result "\(X\) Granger-causes \(Y\)" at whatever significance level the chosen critical value corresponds to. A small \(F\) means the improvement is indistinguishable from noise — fail to reject, and there is no evidence \(X\)'s history adds anything once \(Y\)'s own history is already in the model.

📏
Pre-check 1 — lag order, chosen before testing

Pick \(p\) with AIC/BIC first, then run the test at that \(p\)

Both regressions above share the same lag order \(p\) — that's what makes them nested and the F-test valid. \(p\) itself is not a free knob to tune until the test comes out significant; it has to be chosen by an independent, principled criterion first, exactly the way the Model Selection: AIC & BIC lesson derives \(AIC = 2k - 2\ln\hat L\) and \(BIC = k\ln(n) - 2\ln\hat L\) (equivalently, for an OLS regression's Gaussian likelihood, \(-2\ln\hat L\) reduces to \(n\ln(RSS/n)\) plus a constant that cancels when comparing models on the same data) for penalizing added parameters. The standard practice: fit the (restricted, \(Y\)-only) VAR-style autoregression at several candidate lag orders \(p = 1, 2, \dots, p_{\max}\), pick whichever minimizes AIC or BIC, and use that same \(p\) for both the restricted and unrestricted Granger regressions. Searching over many lag orders and reporting only the one that produces the smallest p-value for the causality test itself — rather than the one AIC/BIC actually selected — is a data-mining shortcut that inflates the false-positive rate, precisely the multiple-testing problem this curriculum's own Multiple Testing lesson exists to catch elsewhere.

📐
Pre-check 2 — stationarity

Test on stationary series, or use a VECM instead

The F-test's reference distribution assumes standard regression conditions that a non-stationary, trending series violates outright — fitting this test directly on raw price levels risks exactly the classic Granger & Newbold (1974) spurious-regression failure mode already flagged in the ARIMA & VAR lesson's own pitfalls: impressive-looking significance that reflects two series both trending over time, not any genuine predictive link. The fix in the ordinary case is the same fix ARIMA's own "I" step and VAR's pre-check both use: difference the series first (test on returns, not price levels) so both inputs are stationary before any lag structure is fit.

There is exactly one legitimate exception, and it needs its own name rather than being silently ignored: if \(Y\) and \(X\) are cointegrated, naively differencing both before testing throws away the long-run equilibrium relationship between them — the same warning the ARIMA & VAR lesson gives about plain differenced VARs on cointegrated pairs. The correct home for a Granger-style test on a genuinely cointegrated pair is inside the Vector Error Correction Model framework: test the short-run lagged-difference terms and the error-correction coefficient (\(\alpha_Y\) or \(\alpha_X\) in that lesson's own notation) jointly, rather than testing a plain differenced VAR that has quietly deleted the one term — \(Z_{t-1}\) — carrying the pair's actual long-run linkage. This lesson does not re-derive that VECM-based joint test in full; it flags the exception and points to the VECM lesson's own error-correction term as exactly the piece a plain differenced-VAR Granger test would otherwise be missing.

4. Bidirectional causality and feedback. Nothing about the setup above is symmetric in \(X\) and \(Y\) — the test above only asks whether \(X\)'s past helps forecast \(Y\). The mirror-image test, with \(Y\)'s lags added to a regression of \(X\) on \(X\)'s own lags, is a completely separate hypothesis on a separate pair of regressions:

➡️

One-directional

\(X\) Granger-causes \(Y\) (test 1 significant), but \(Y\) does not Granger-cause \(X\) (test 2 not significant). \(X\) behaves like a genuine leading indicator for \(Y\) within this framework, with no evidence of the reverse.

🔁

Bidirectional — a feedback loop

Both tests significant: \(X\)'s past helps forecast \(Y\), and \(Y\)'s past helps forecast \(X\). Neither series is a pure leader — the two feed into each other, and treating either one as "the" driver misreads the fitted result.

A feedback loop is not a contradiction or a sign one of the two tests is broken — it is a perfectly coherent, common empirical outcome, especially for two closely related instruments (a currency pair and a highly correlated cross, or spot and a related futures contract) where genuine information plausibly flows both ways at different, overlapping timescales. Running only one direction and silently assuming "no evidence \(X\) causes \(Y\)" also rules out \(Y\) causing \(X\) is a common and entirely avoidable mistake — the two tests cost twice the regressions and answer a question the single-direction result cannot.

Adding \(X_{t-1}\) drops the RSS from 0.4260 to 0.0806 — a large apparent improvement, but "large" only means something once it's weighed against how much RSS a single extra, genuinely useless regressor would be expected to shave off by chance. That's exactly what the F-statistic weighs:

q = 1one restriction dropped — the single X-lag coefficient set to zero
k = 3unrestricted model's parameters: intercept, Y-lag, X-lag
n = 12observations in the regression sample
n − k = 9denominator degrees of freedom
\[ F = \frac{(0.4260 - 0.0806)/1}{0.0806/9} = \frac{0.3454}{0.008956} \approx 38.57 \]

In this toy sample, \(X\) Granger-causes \(Y\) at lag 1, well past the 1% significance level. Nothing here claims this specific dataset reflects any real traded instrument; the arithmetic is exact and reproducible, the series are illustrative.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

The FX use case: does one correlated series lead another? The natural application is exactly the lead-lag question this whole curriculum category is organized around: pick two related instruments — a currency pair and a highly correlated cross, spot and a related interest-rate future, or the yield-spread/EUR-USD pairing the ARIMA & VAR lesson raises but leaves untested — difference both to returns, select a lag order by AIC/BIC, and run the Granger F-test in both directions. A clean asymmetric result (one direction significant, the reverse not) is directly actionable: it says the leading series' recent moves carry genuine forecasting content for the lagging series, at a specific, testable lag — not just "seems to move first" from eyeballing a chart.

🗂️
What's actually verified against this repo's code

No Granger regression exists — but the simpler cross-correlation cousin genuinely does, in several independent places

A direct grep for granger/Granger across js/, DecisionEngine/, every root .py file, and server.js turns up real hits — but every one of them is either Engle-Granger's cointegration test (portfolioBacktest/portfolio_backtest.py, js/hedgeSignalV2Engine.js, js/goldMinerArbEngine.js — a different Granger contribution entirely, covered in the Cointegration lesson) or an unrelated Granger-Ramanathan regression-weighting scheme in js/volHorseRaceEngine.js. Nothing in this repo fits a restricted/unrestricted VAR pair or computes the F-statistic this lesson derives.

What genuinely does exist, in production, is this lesson's "simpler cousin" — plain cross-correlation of one series against another at a range of lags, with no regression underneath it at all. js/nasdaqResearch.js's leadLagCorrelation(driverSeries, targetSeries, opts) computes Pearson or Spearman correlation between the driver at time \(t\) and the target at time \(t+\text{lag}\) across a configurable lag range, with a t-stat from corrTStat — its own comment states the convention this lesson's chart above reuses: "lag > 0 means the driver LEADS the target." js/creditLeadLagEngine.js's leadLagTable(driver, target, maxLag) does the identical Pearson-correlation-at-each-lag computation for the credit-spread/NQ realized-vol relationship. js/yieldCouplingCore.js's computeDailyLeadLag(fx, spread, opts) runs the same idea on daily DE–US yield-spread and FX returns, explicitly to test "at what lag does the spread lead price." None of these three tools controls for the target series' own autocorrelation the way the Granger F-test's restricted model does — they answer the simpler, cheaper question this lesson's cross-correlation chart illustrates, not the regression-based one. Separately, js/compass.js's compositeSpreadZSeries feeds a rolling-regression residual (price regressed on a composite yield-spread z-score) that its own comment calls "the real lead-lag metric" — a genuine regression, but a single-equation divergence residual, not a restricted-vs- unrestricted Granger comparison either.

🎯
Real-world trading scenario

A correlated cross and a rate-futures contract: which one actually leads?

Suppose a desk trades two highly correlated pairs — say a commodity currency against USD, and a second commodity currency against USD, both exposed to the same broad risk-sentiment factor — and wants to know whether one systematically leads the other by a lag short enough to trade. The naive approach is exactly the cross-correlation tools grepped above: compute \(\text{corr}(X_t, Y_{t+k})\) for a range of \(k\) and look for a peak away from zero. That's a real, useful first pass — and it's what this repo's own lead-lag tooling already runs. But a peak at lag \(k=2\) in a raw cross-correlation could just as easily reflect \(Y\)'s own momentum (yesterday's move predicting today's, with no help from \(X\) at all) coinciding with \(X\) and \(Y\) simply being correlated in level. The Granger F-test is the version of the same question that actually controls for that: it only credits \(X\) with "leading" \(Y\) if \(X\)'s lags improve the forecast after \(Y\)'s own momentum is already in the model. A desk that found a cross-correlation peak worth investigating further would run the Granger F-test next, precisely to separate "X's own lagged moves add real information" from "Y is just autocorrelated, and X happens to be correlated with Y in general."

The same logic applies directly to spot versus a related interest-rate future: a futures contract pricing in a rate move ahead of spot FX catching up is a textbook lead-lag candidate, and exactly the kind of relationship where distinguishing "the future genuinely leads" from "both instruments share the same slow-moving driver" is the entire point of running the regression-based test rather than stopping at a correlation peak.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where Granger causality testing quietly goes wrong

Spurious Granger causality from non-stationarity. Skipping the stationarity pre-check and fitting on trending price levels is the single most common way this test produces a confident-looking but meaningless result — the classic Granger & Newbold (1974) spurious-regression failure mode, restated here in its most direct form: two independently trending series can produce a textbook-looking "significant" F-statistic with zero genuine predictive link. Test on returns.

A common confounding factor. The test cannot distinguish "X's history genuinely helps forecast Y" from "an unobserved third factor drives both X and Y, hitting X's data feed slightly earlier." Two FX pairs both moved by the same broad risk-sentiment shock, where one pair's price feed simply updates or gets quoted a few seconds before the other's, can produce a clean, significant one-directional Granger result with no genuine causal or even informational link between the two series themselves — the shared shock, not either series, is doing all the work.

Measurement-timing and synchronicity effects. A subtler version of the same trap: two instruments that trade in different time zones, or close at different times of day, can show an apparent "lead-lag" relationship that is really just an artifact of when each series' price was last updated relative to when new information hit the market — not one series genuinely anticipating the other. An asset whose daily close is measured several hours before a second asset's close will mechanically look like it "leads," purely because its recorded price is older and hasn't yet absorbed news that later moves the second asset. This is a data-construction artifact, not a predictive relationship, and it does not show up as an obvious error in the F-statistic itself — it has to be ruled out by checking the actual timestamps and market hours behind each series before trusting a lead-lag finding.

Low power with short samples or too many lags. Every lag added to \(p\) costs a parameter in \(k\), which shrinks \(n-k\) and eats into the denominator degrees of freedom driving the test's power. On the kind of sample sizes common in daily FX/macro research — a few hundred to a few thousand observations — a lag order chosen too generously, or a sample cut too short, can fail to detect a real, economically meaningful lead-lag relationship simply because there isn't enough data left to estimate it precisely. This cuts the opposite direction from the spurious-significance pitfalls above: it's a risk of false negatives, not false positives, and the AIC/BIC lag-selection pre-check exists partly to avoid it by not fitting more lags than the sample can actually support.

Section 04

Key Takeaways

✓Granger causality has one precise, statistical definition. X Granger-causes Y if adding X's lags to a regression already containing Y's own lags significantly improves the forecast of Y — an incremental-predictive-power claim, nothing more.
✓The test is a nested F-test. \(F = \frac{(RSS_r-RSS_u)/q}{RSS_u/(n-k)}\), comparing a restricted (Y-lags only) and unrestricted (Y-lags plus X-lags) regression, referenced against \(F(q, n-k)\).
⚠Two pre-checks are mandatory, not optional. Choose the lag order with AIC/BIC before testing, and confirm stationarity (test returns, not levels) — or route a genuinely cointegrated pair through the VECM's error-correction framework instead of a plain differenced VAR.
✓Test both directions. X-causes-Y and Y-causes-X are separate hypotheses on separate regressions; both, either, or neither can be significant, and a bidirectional result (a feedback loop) is a coherent, common finding, not a broken test.
✗"Granger-causes" is not "causes." It is a statistical, predictive claim vulnerable to confounding, non-stationarity, and measurement-timing artifacts — never read a significant result as proof of a real mechanistic link.
⚠This repo runs the simpler cousin, not the regression test itself. No Granger F-test exists anywhere in the codebase; genuine cross-correlation-at-various-lags tools do (js/nasdaqResearch.js, js/creditLeadLagEngine.js, js/yieldCouplingCore.js), and they don't control for the target's own autocorrelation the way this test does.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. State the precise, statistical definition of "X Granger-causes Y" — not the informal or philosophical version.
X Granger-causes Y if including past values of X in a regression that already contains Y's own past values produces a statistically significant improvement in forecasting Y — formally, if the coefficients on X's lagged terms are jointly, significantly different from zero in the unrestricted model versus the restricted (Y-lags-only) model. It is a claim about incremental predictive power within this specific regression framework, not a claim about true, structural, or mechanistic causation.
Q2. Write the restricted and unrestricted regressions the Granger F-test compares, and identify q and k in terms of the lag order p.
Restricted: \(Y_t = c + \sum_{i=1}^{p} a_i Y_{t-i} + e_t\). Unrestricted: \(Y_t = c + \sum_{i=1}^{p} a_i Y_{t-i} + \sum_{j=1}^{p} b_j X_{t-j} + \epsilon_t\). \(q = p\) — the number of X-lag coefficients set to zero in the restricted model (the number of restrictions under \(H_0\)). \(k = 2p+1\) — the unrestricted model's total parameter count: one intercept, \(p\) Y-lag coefficients, and \(p\) X-lag coefficients.
Q3. Given RSS_r = 0.60, RSS_u = 0.20, q = 2, n = 30, k = 5, compute the F-statistic.
\(F = \frac{(0.60 - 0.20)/2}{0.20/(30-5)} = \frac{0.20}{0.20/25} = \frac{0.20}{0.008} = 25\). With df₁ = 2 and df₂ = 25, this F is far above any standard critical value at conventional significance levels (roughly 3.4 at the 5% level for these degrees of freedom) — reject \(H_0\); X Granger-causes Y in this sample.
Q4. Why must the lag order p be chosen with AIC/BIC before running the Granger test, rather than searched over until the test itself looks most significant?
The restricted and unrestricted models must share the same, fixed lag order for the F-test's nested-model comparison and its reference \(F(q, n-k)\) distribution to be valid. Choosing p by AIC/BIC first (an independent criterion balancing fit against parameter count) fixes that lag order on principled grounds before the causality question is even asked. Searching over many lag orders and reporting whichever one produces the smallest causality p-value, instead of the one AIC/BIC actually selected, is a multiple-testing shortcut that inflates the false-positive rate — the test's stated significance level no longer means what it claims to mean once the lag order itself was chosen to maximize significance.
Q5. Two series are non-stationary but genuinely cointegrated. What goes wrong if you difference both and run a plain Granger F-test, and what should be done instead?
Differencing a cointegrated pair before testing discards the long-run equilibrium relationship between them — the same information a plain differenced VAR loses, per the ARIMA & VAR lesson's own pitfall. The correct approach is to test within a Vector Error Correction Model framework instead: include the error-correction term (last period's cointegrating residual, \(Z_{t-1}\) in the VECM lesson's own notation) alongside the short-run lagged-difference terms, and test the X-lag coefficients and/or the error-correction coefficient jointly, rather than silently dropping the one term that carries the pair's long-run linkage.
Q6. A Granger test finds that a broad risk-sentiment proxy Granger-causes both currency pair A and currency pair B, each with a slightly different lag. Someone concludes "A Granger-causes B." What's the flaw, and what pitfall does this illustrate?
This is the confounding-factor pitfall: if a shared risk-sentiment shock genuinely drives both A and B, but reaches each pair's price feed with a slightly different delay, a Granger test run only on A and B directly (without controlling for the shared driver) can produce a clean, significant "A Granger-causes B" result even though neither pair has any real informational or causal link to the other — the third factor is doing all the work. The fix is to test for or control for the suspected common driver (here, the risk-sentiment proxy itself) rather than treating a bivariate A-vs-B result as evidence of a direct relationship between the two.
