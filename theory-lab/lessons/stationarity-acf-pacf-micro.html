Deeper Foundations 3

Stationarity, ACF & PACF — Read Before You Trust Any Time-Series Model

Every rolling-window feature, every AR/ARIMA fit, every GARCH forecast, and the Cointegration lesson's residual test quietly assumes something about how a series behaves through time — that its statistical personality doesn't itself drift as the calendar moves forward. This lesson makes that assumption explicit, calls it by its name, and hands you the two diagnostic plots that check whether it actually holds: the autocorrelation function (ACF) and the partial autocorrelation function (PACF).

Section 1

A series that behaves the same way, no matter when you look

A time series is (weakly) stationary if three things hold constant through time — depending only on how far apart two observations are, never on the calendar date itself.

μ

A fixed mean

E[Xt] = μ — the series fluctuates around one long-run level, not a level that keeps sliding to somewhere new.

σ²

A fixed variance

Var(Xt) = σ² — how far the series typically strays from its mean doesn't grow or shrink as time passes.

γ

Autocovariance depends only on lag

Cov(Xt, Xt+k) = γ(k) — how related two points are depends only on how many steps apart they are (k), never on which date you happen to check.

Picture a bouncy ball dropped in the same spot every day — it always settles around the same height, bounces about the same amount, and how today's bounce relates to yesterday's is always roughly the same story, whether you check on a Monday or a Friday. A series that keeps drifting to new heights, or gets wilder and calmer at random over the years, is the opposite — the rules of the game are quietly changing underneath you.
Why this gets skipped over

Almost nothing states this assumption out loud before using it. A "20-day rolling average" is, whether anyone says so or not, an implicit claim that the last 20 days behave like any other 20-day stretch. If that's false, the average is quietly blending together several different regimes as if they were one.

Section 2

A leash, versus no leash at all

Two processes already seen elsewhere in this Theory Lab sit at opposite ends of this idea.

Ornstein-Uhlenbeck — stationary

dXt = θ(μ − Xt)dt + σ dWt has a pull-back-to-μ term built directly into its drift — the further it strays, the harder it gets pulled back. That gives it a fixed mean and a variance that settles to a constant σ²/2θ. Like a dog on a leash tied to a post: it can roam, but it always gets pulled back toward home.

Pure random walk — non-stationary

Xt = Xt−1 + εt has no pull-back term at all. Its variance grows linearly with time — Var(Xt) = tσ² — so there is no fixed "normal level" to revert to. Like a dog that's wandered off with no leash: it just keeps drifting farther away, with nowhere it's likely to circle back to.

Every raw FX rate, equity index, or bond-yield level behaves like the random-walk case. Most well-specified spreads, and most return series, behave much closer to the mean-reverting case.

The single most important trading consequence

Regress one non-stationary price level on another and you can get a high R² and "significant"-looking coefficients that are pure statistical illusion — a spurious regression, not a real relationship. That's precisely why the Cointegration lesson checks its residual for stationarity before trusting anything else about the fit.

Section 4

How correlated is a series with its own past self?

For a stationary series, the ACF at lag k is the correlation between the series and a copy of itself shifted back k steps.

γ(k) is the autocovariance — how related Xt is to Xt−k, in raw units. γ(0) is just the series' own variance — autocovariance at lag zero — used to normalize. The result is a pure correlation between −1 and 1.

ACF(0) is always exactly 1 — any series is perfectly correlated with itself at zero lag. Plotting ACF(k) against k for a range of lags (typically the first 10-40) produces what's called a correlogram, and its shape is genuinely diagnostic, not decorative.

Three shapes, three stories

≈0

White noise — no memory

Each value is an independent draw. The correlogram sits near zero at every lag beyond zero, scattered randomly inside a thin significance band, with no discernible pattern.

↘

AR(1)-like — decays gradually

High at lag 1, smaller at lag 2, smaller still at lag 3 — shrinking roughly geometrically toward zero, eventually disappearing into the noise band at higher lags.

⚠

Trending / non-stationary — decays very slowly

Still meaningfully elevated even 20-30 lags out. This is the classic visual tell-tale of a unit root — today's value literally contains the sum of every shock since the series began.

Practitioners often eyeball a correlogram before ever running a formal test like ADF — a slowly-decaying ACF is the cheap first-pass signal that a proper stationarity test is worth running.
Section 6

What lag k adds, once you already know what's in between

The ACF at lag k mixes together the direct relationship between Xt and Xt−k with everything carried indirectly through the intervening lags 1, …, k−1. The PACF at lag k is that same relationship after removing (partialling out) the linear effect of every lag in between — conceptually, the coefficient on Xt−k in a regression of Xt on Xt−1, Xt−2, …, Xt−k all at once.

Picture dropping a pebble in a pond. The ripple — the ACF — keeps spreading outward, fainter and fainter, but never really stops all at once. But if you ask "did the pebble itself splash again a moment ago" — the PACF — the answer is a clean yes right after the drop, and a flat no forever after. The single splash is the whole cause, even though its ripple keeps echoing outward for a while.
Where this needs qualifying

In practice the PACF is computed efficiently via the Durbin-Levinson recursion, not one large regression per lag — but the regression framing above is the right way to understand what the number actually means: whatever Xt−k still explains about Xt once every closer lag has already had its say.

Section 9

ACF decays, PACF cuts off — the AR(p) signature

This is the payoff of defining both functions rather than just one.

ACF — decays gradually

It never cleanly stops, because the direct dependence at lag p still echoes forward through every later lag via the chained-correlation mechanism. An AR(1) process, for instance, has ACF(k) = φk — technically nonzero at every lag, however small.

PACF — cuts off sharply after lag p

Once lags 1 through p are partialled out, there's genuinely nothing left for lag p+1 to explain. For AR(1) specifically: large at lag 1, then dropping abruptly to near-zero from lag 2 onward — even though the ACF at lag 2, 3, 4… is still visibly nonzero.

That contrast is the classic Box-Jenkins signature of an autoregressive process — reading where the PACF cuts off is the standard way to identify p, the order of an AR model, directly from data instead of guessing a lookback window.
The mirror-image rule holds for a moving-average MA(q) process — ACF cuts off after lag q, PACF decays gradually — which is why the two plots together, not either alone, are the classical starting point for choosing an ARIMA model's (p,q).
Section 10

The desk-relevant payoff

🔢

Model order identification

Box-Jenkins: a PACF cutoff plus a decaying ACF picks the AR order p directly off the plots, before ever running a fit.

✅

Pre-model sanity check

GARCH, VAR, and the Cointegration residual test all assume stationarity — a correlogram is the cheapest check before trusting any of them.

📏

ADF lag length

The PACF of a differenced residual is a principled way to choose how many lagged-difference terms a Dickey-Fuller regression needs.

🪟

Lookback-window design

Any "last N periods" feature is an implicit autocorrelation claim — ACF/PACF is the checkable version of that assumption.

Where reading a correlogram quietly goes wrong

Compute an ACF/PACF on a raw, trending price level instead of a return or a properly-tested spread, and the slow decay mostly just reflects the trend/unit-root behavior — not genuine short-memory structure. Difference or detrend first, then check the transformed series' correlogram.

Checking 20 or 40 lags at once and treating every bar that pokes outside the ±1.96/√N significance band as "real" ignores that testing many lags simultaneously is a multiple-comparisons problem — roughly 1 in 20 lags clears a 5% band by chance alone, even for genuine white noise. A single isolated spike is far weaker evidence than a clean, sustained decay or cutoff across several lags.

Section 11

Quick check

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Why does a random walk's ACF decay so much more slowly than a stationary AR(1) process's ACF?

A random walk's value is X₀ plus the sum of every shock so far — it never truly "forgets." An AR(1) process's shocks fade geometrically (ACF(k) = φᵏ) precisely because |φ| < 1 pulls it back toward its mean.
Question 2

For a stationary AR(1) process with φ = 0.6, what is ACF(2)?

ACF(k) = φᵏ, so ACF(2) = 0.6² = 0.36 — the same formula from the calculator above.
Question 3

A PACF that's large at lags 1 and 2, then drops into the noise band from lag 3 onward, most likely signals:

A PACF that cuts off after lag p is the AR(p) signature — here p = 2. An MA(2) process would show the opposite pattern: ACF cuts off, PACF decays gradually.
Question 4

What does "partial" mean in "partial autocorrelation"?

PACF at lag k measures the relationship between X_t and X_{t−k} after removing the linear effect of every lag in between — what lag k adds beyond what the closer lags already told you.
Question 5

Why is regressing one non-stationary price series on another dangerous?

Two independently trending non-stationary series can look strongly related in a regression with no real relationship at all. This is exactly why the Cointegration lesson checks the residual for stationarity before trusting the fit.
Section 12

What to carry forward

01

Stationarity means constant mean, variance, and autocovariance — all three depend only on lag k, never on the calendar date.

02

Raw price levels are usually non-stationary and random-walk-like; returns and well-specified spreads are usually much closer to stationary.

03

The ACF measures the total relationship at each lag — direct dependence plus everything carried through the chain of intervening lags.

04

The PACF strips that chain out, measuring only what lag k adds beyond what the closer lags already explain.

05

ACF decays gradually, PACF cuts off sharply after lag p — the Box-Jenkins signature that lets you read an AR model's order straight off the two plots.

06

Never trust a regression, an ARIMA fit, or a Cointegration test on a series you haven't checked for stationarity first — the spurious-regression risk is real, not theoretical.

What this now lets you do

Read a correlogram and tell, at a glance, whether a series looks stationary or is dragging a unit root behind it. Pick an AR model's order — or size a lookback window — from evidence instead of a guessed round number. Recognise when a regression's strong-looking relationship might be a statistical mirage rather than a real one, before trusting it with capital.
