Lesson 16 · Macro & FX Theory

Purchasing Power Parity, or Why a Big Mac Should Cost the Same Everywhere

Interest rate parity pins the forward FX rate down almost mechanically, enforced by riskless arbitrage that closes gaps in seconds. Purchasing Power Parity is the other half of classical international finance, and it could not behave more differently: it's a real, well-documented anchor for where a currency "should" sit over years — and a famously bad clock for when it actually gets there. Holding both of those facts at once, without collapsing one into the other, is the entire point of this lesson.

Section 1

If a coffee costs $3 in New York and £3 in London, something has to give

Same coffee, same beans, same size. GBP/USD = 1.50.

The setup
London coffee: £3
×1.50
= $4.50
$4.50 versus New York's $3.00 — 50% more, for a genuinely identical good.

That's the Law of One Price: once you strip out transport costs, tariffs, and taxes, an identical, freely tradeable good should cost the same everywhere once priced in a common currency. If it doesn't, someone can buy it where it's cheap and sell it where it's dear until the gap closes. Purchasing Power Parity (PPP) is just this idea scaled up from one coffee to an entire economy's basket of goods — compare the general price level (something like a CPI basket) in each country instead of one product.

Why this is harder at economy scale

You can't physically arbitrage a haircut or a hospital visit across a border the way you can a tradeable commodity. Scaling the Law of One Price from a coffee to a whole economy is the same logic — but it takes a lot longer to actually play out.

Section 2

Absolute PPP: the strict, "in theory" version

The exchange rate should exactly equal the ratio of the two countries' price levels.

The formula
S
=
P_domestic ÷ P_foreign
Read it plainly: S is whatever number makes the same basket cost the same amount in either currency.
S

Nominal spot rate

The exchange rate you'd actually see quoted, expressed consistently as one currency per unit of the other.

Pd

Domestic price level

Cost, in domestic currency, of a representative basket of goods and services — conceptually, a CPI index level.

Pf

Foreign price level

Cost, in foreign currency, of the same representative basket.

This is the "one price for one basket" idea taken completely literally. It's also, empirically, close to never exactly true.

Section 3

Why absolute PPP almost never holds exactly

Three well-understood, not-particularly-mysterious reasons.

🧺

Baskets differ

A Japanese basket and an Argentine basket don't contain the same goods in the same proportions — there's no single "basket" to compare.

🏠

Non-tradeable goods

A haircut, rent, a restaurant meal, most healthcare and construction — a large share of any real economy simply cannot be shipped across a border to arbitrage a price gap.

🛃

Friction

Tariffs, transport costs, and taxes drive a further wedge between "priced the same" and "priced the same once you account for friction."

Absolute PPP is the clean textbook statement, but it's best treated as a limiting case — a useful reference point, not something you'd expect to hold to the decimal in real data.
Section 4

Relative PPP: the version that's actually useful

Take absolute PPP, convert levels into rates of change.

The formula
%ΔS
≈
π_domestic − π_foreign
The currency of the higher-inflation country should depreciate by roughly the size of the inflation gap.

If domestic prices rise 8% a year and foreign prices rise 2%, and the exchange rate doesn't move, domestic goods get steadily, mechanically more expensive relative to foreign goods every year — a widening Law-of-One-Price violation. Relative PPP says the currency should depreciate by roughly the 6-point gap to keep relative competitiveness unchanged. This is weaker than absolute PPP — it says nothing about whether S currently equals P_domestic/P_foreign, only about the direction and rough size of future changes — which is exactly why it survives contact with real data better.

Section 6

The real exchange rate — a "cheap-o-meter"

If PPP held exactly at every moment, this number would never move.

The formula
Real FX
=
S × P_foreign ÷ P_domestic
Constant if PPP held exactly. In practice, it wanders — sometimes for years — above or below its own average.

Overvalued means the real rate is unusually high relative to its own history (domestic goods look expensive to foreigners on a PPP basis); undervalued means the opposite. Notice the shape: a handful of long, slow cycles across 24 years — not fast, jittery oscillation.

Section 7

Short-run clock vs. long-run pull

The exact same PPP relationship, viewed on two different horizons.

Try it
No mechanism forces a quick close — the deviation can widen just as easily as narrow, on any given day. PPP says almost nothing about this window.
Over a multi-year sample, the wandering does show a slow pull back toward fair value — the reversion Rogoff documented. Real, but glacial.

This is the single hardest thing about PPP to hold in your head at once: "the theory is basically right over the long run" and "the theory is nearly useless for timing a trade" are both true, simultaneously, about the exact same relationship.

Section 8

The Big Mac Index

The most famous informal illustration of absolute PPP, published by The Economist since 1986.

A Big Mac is a near-identical, standardized product assembled from broadly similar inputs (beef, bread, cheese, labor, rent) in dozens of countries — comparing its price, converted to a common currency, gives a rough, easy-to-communicate absolute-PPP check. If a Big Mac costs $5.50 in the US and the equivalent of $3.20 elsewhere at the current exchange rate, that currency looks "cheap" by roughly 42% on a Big Mac basis.

🍔

One good, not a basket

A single standardized product isn't a representative sample of an entire economy's consumption.

🏠

Non-tradeable inputs

Rent and labor are baked into the price and have nothing to do with currency valuation.

⏱️

Says nothing about timing

Even a genuine reading gives no clock for when — or whether — the gap actually closes.

Where this needs qualifying

Richer countries tend to have pricier haircuts and Big Macs for a structural reason too — the Balassa-Samuelson effect: higher wages in the productive tradeable sector pull up wages, and therefore prices, in the non-tradeable sector as well. A currency that looks "PPP-rich" may partly just reflect this, not a bubble waiting to pop.

Section 9

The PPP puzzle — why it holds so slowly

Kenneth Rogoff's widely-cited 1996 review named this problem outright.

Real exchange rates do appear to mean-revert over long enough samples — but the estimated half-life of a PPP deviation is typically found to be roughly three to five years. That's remarkably slow compared to how fast standard macro models suggest prices and nominal exchange rates ought to adjust. The puzzle isn't "does PPP hold" — the reversion shows up reasonably robustly given a long enough sample — it's "why does it take so absurdly long."

The trap

PPP deviations can widen before they narrow. Nothing in the literature says a currently-undervalued currency is at or near its turning point — a "20% undervalued" reading has historically stretched wider, for years, before ever closing.

Section 10

"This currency looks 20% undervalued" — what does that license you to do?

The same reading, judged against two very different decision horizons.

Trading horizon — days to months
  • Almost nothing — a 20-30% deviation has historically persisted for years
  • No mechanism stops it from widening further next month or next quarter
  • Being "right" on a 3-5 year half-life is cold comfort to a book sized for a multi-week trade
  • Mark-to-market drawdown along the way can exceed what most books can absorb
Strategic horizon — years
  • A corporate treasurer hedging a five-year FX liability: the slow reversion is real information
  • A pension or sovereign fund setting a strategic (not tactical) hedge ratio
  • A long-horizon allocator deciding how much currency risk to run passively
  • Same reading, same math — just matched to a horizon PPP's clock can plausibly serve

The lesson isn't "PPP is useless" — it's that PPP's usefulness is horizon-conditional. Confusing a multi-year anchor for a trading signal is a well-known way to lose money while being directionally correct.

Section 11

Quick check

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Why is relative PPP more useful in practice than absolute PPP?

Relative PPP is a claim about rates of change — a weaker, differenced statement that survives contact with real, imperfectly comparable data far better than a levels-based claim about matching absolute price levels across two different consumption baskets.
Question 2

Domestic inflation is 8%/yr, foreign inflation is 2%/yr. What does relative PPP say the exchange rate should do?

%ΔS ≈ π_domestic − π_foreign = 8% − 2% = 6%. The higher-inflation currency should depreciate roughly by the inflation gap to keep relative competitiveness unchanged.
Question 3

What does Rogoff's "PPP puzzle" actually find?

The puzzle isn't whether PPP holds — reversion does show up given a long enough sample — it's why the half-life is so absurdly slow (3-5 years) compared to what standard price-stickiness arguments predict.
Question 4

Is the Big Mac Index a rigorous test of PPP?

It's genuinely useful as popular education and a transparent cross-check on the Law of One Price — not for precisely quantifying misvaluation. Its price embeds non-tradeable local costs like rent and labor that have little to do with currency valuation.
Question 5

A short-term trader shorts a currency purely because it looks "20% PPP-rich." What's the realistic risk?

Unlike covered interest parity, nothing forces a PPP gap to close quickly. A trader sized for a multi-week horizon can face drawdowns larger than the book can absorb long before — if ever — the multi-year reversion shows up.
Section 12

What to carry forward

01

PPP is the Law of One Price, scaled up — an identical basket should cost the same in every currency, or the exchange rate should move until it does.

02

Absolute PPP (S = P_domestic/P_foreign) is the strict, rarely-true version; non-tradeable goods, differing baskets, and trade friction all keep it from holding exactly.

03

Relative PPP (%ΔS ≈ π_domestic − π_foreign) is the weaker, more empirically useful version — it only needs inflation rates and the FX move to track each other.

04

The real exchange rate is the practical "cheap or expensive" gauge — constant if PPP held exactly, but in practice it wanders for years above or below its own average.

05

PPP closes glacially — Rogoff's "PPP puzzle" finds half-lives of three to five years, and a deviation can widen before it narrows.

06

PPP's usefulness is horizon-conditional — nearly useless for a trading-horizon decision, a genuinely relevant input for a multi-year hedge or strategic-allocation decision.

What this now lets you do

Read a "currency X% undervalued on PPP" headline correctly — as a multi-year fair-value observation, not a trading signal. Tell absolute PPP apart from relative PPP, and know which one any given claim is actually making. Recognise the real exchange rate as the number that would be constant if PPP held exactly, and judge a currency's "cheapness" against its own history rather than an absolute standard.
