Attention & Transformers, Without the Relay Race

The previous lesson's gated recurrent networks — LSTM and GRU — solved most of the vanishing-gradient problem, but they never solved a second, structural limitation: every hidden state still has to be computed one step after the last, in order, with the entire relevant history squeezed into one fixed-size vector along the way. This lesson introduces the mechanism that removes that constraint entirely — attention — and the architecture built around it, the Transformer, plus a named variant purpose-built for forecasting: the Temporal Fusion Transformer.

Section 1

Computing h₄ requires h₃ to already exist, which requires h₂, which requires h₁. By the time information from step 1 reaches h₄, it has been compressed and re-compressed three times over — and step 4 cannot even begin until step 3 has finished, no matter how much compute is sitting idle.
LSTM and GRU gates control what survives each compression step. They do nothing to remove the sequential dependency itself.
📦

The compression bottleneck

Every relevant fact about the whole history so far — 60 days, 6 months, whatever the lookback is — has to be squeezed into one fixed-size hidden state. Some of it necessarily gets lost.

🐌

The training bottleneck

Step 500 cannot start until step 499 finishes. That dependency chain cannot be parallelized across time during training — a hard architectural ceiling, not a slow implementation.

Think of it as a game of telephone: a message whispered person to person down a line arrives diluted by every retelling in between, and nobody can start relaying their part until the person before them has finished.
Section 2

Attention's core idea: look directly, weigh by relevance

Instead of compressing history into one hidden state, let every prediction look back and directly score every past step.

❓

Query — "what am I looking for"

The position currently making a prediction holds up a question: given what I need right now, which past steps actually matter?

🏷

Key — "what do I contain"

Every past step offers a label describing its content, compared against the query to produce a relevance score.

📤

Value — "what I'd actually contribute"

The content itself, blended into the output in proportion to how relevant its key turned out to be.

Compare the query against every key by dot product, scale the scores down (this keeps the next step's gradient well-behaved), turn them into weights that are non-negative and sum to one, then blend the values using those weights. Every past step gets scored and combined in one computation — no relay, no compounding compression, and crucially, every position's score can be computed in parallel with every other position's, which is exactly what a strictly sequential RNN cannot offer.

Where this needs qualifying

"Look at everyone at once" isn't free — comparing every position against every other position produces an n×n matrix of scores. For a short lookback that's trivial; for a long financial series it becomes a real cost, covered on Section 9.

Section 6

Self-attention has no sense of order — so one gets added

Self-attention

Queries, keys, and values all come from the same sequence — every position attends to every position in that same sequence, including itself. This is the core mechanism a Transformer stacks over and over.

Positional encoding

QKᵀ is a set of pairwise dot products — swap the rows of the input and the set of scores is unchanged, just relabeled. A fixed or learned vector unique to each position is added onto each input embedding before attention runs, restoring the order information an RNN gets for free from its own step-by-step structure.

A pitfall worth naming here

Forgetting positional encoding produces a model that is structurally blind to whether a large move happened yesterday or three months ago — order stops mattering at all, which defeats the entire point of applying this to a time series. And for forecasting specifically, attention also needs a causal mask so a given step can only attend to earlier steps, never later ones — without it, a model can trivially "attend to the future," producing backtest results that are pure look-ahead leakage, not a real forecast.

Section 7

Multi-head attention: several relevance judgments at once

One attention computation learns one notion of "relevant." Multi-head attention runs several, in parallel, each with its own learned projections.

1

Head 1 might specialize in recency

Learning to weigh whatever happened most recently most heavily — the closest thing to what a plain recurrent state already does.

2

Head 2 might specialize in seasonality

Learning to weigh the same weekday, or the same point in a macro cycle, regardless of how far back it sits.

3

Head 3 might specialize in event relevance

Learning to weigh a scheduled macro release or earnings date heavily, independent of its distance from the query — the pattern from Section 4's slider.

＋

Nothing forces this split explicitly

Each head is independently initialized and projected, then trained side by side — specialization emerges from whatever reduces the loss most, not from an instruction to divide the labor this way.

One friend reading a room notices who's standing close together; another notices eye contact; another notices the topic of conversation. Multi-head attention is asking several such readings for the same room at once, then combining all of them.

Section 9

The catch: O(n²), and a reputation that doesn't automatically transfer

"Everyone compares against everyone" has a real, named cost — and a real, named temptation to misuse the model's reputation.

n positions

For a few dozen steps this is trivial. For years of daily bars, or months of intraday bars, n can be large enough that the quadratic cost becomes a genuine practical constraint — the specific problem time-series-focused variants like the Temporal Fusion Transformer and Informer exist to address.

Mandatory honest framing

Transformers are, today, genuinely the dominant architecture for large-scale sequence modeling — a well-evidenced statement about the large text and vision corpora where the architecture was developed and most thoroughly tested. It is not, on its own, evidence that a transformer will outperform a well-tuned classical model or a well-tuned LSTM on a typical individual market series, which is usually far shorter and far noisier than the datasets that made the architecture's reputation. Transformers need substantially more data and compute to realize their advantage — without enough data, a transformer's larger capacity can just as easily produce more convincing overfitting rather than a real accuracy gain. Whether a transformer earns its complexity on a specific series is an empirical question, answered only by honest, leakage-free out-of-sample comparison — never assumed from reputation in an unrelated domain.

Section 10

A named example, built for forecasting: the Temporal Fusion Transformer

Lim, Arık, Loeff & Pfister, International Journal of Forecasting, 2021 — attention adapted specifically for time-series forecasting, not repurposed from language modeling.

🎛

Variable-selection networks

Dedicated components that learn which input variables matter most for a given forecast — static features, known-future inputs, and observed-past inputs are all handled through separate, purpose-built gates.

📊

Full predictive distributions, not just a point forecast

Outputs quantiles across multiple horizons at once — a range of plausible outcomes with an explicit sense of uncertainty, rather than a single number pretending to be certain.

🔍

Built-in interpretability

Because attention weights are a readable artifact of the computation, the TFT exposes — deliberately, through its own dedicated components — which past time steps and which input variables a given forecast actually relied on most.

A plain LSTM's final hidden state compresses 60 days of history into one vector with no direct record of which days mattered. An attention-based forecast's weights can say, explicitly: "day 41 and day 58 dominated this prediction, day 12 barely registered." That is genuinely useful for understanding a trained model — it is not, on its own, proof that what the model weighted constitutes real predictive edge, which stays a separate empirical question, exactly as it does for every other model in this curriculum.
Section 11

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

What structural limitation does even a gated LSTM or GRU still have, that has nothing to do with vanishing gradients?

Computing h_t requires h_{t-1} to already exist, all the way back to the start — a dependency chain no amount of compute can parallelize, gated or not.
Question 2

What replaces the step-by-step relay in an attention-based model?

Attention(Q,K,V) = softmax(QKᵀ/√dₖ)V — every query is scored against every key in one computation, with no step waiting on any other step's result.
Question 3

Why does a Transformer need positional encoding when an RNN never did?

An RNN's step-by-step structure encodes order for free. Attention compares positions directly and in parallel, so order has to be added back in explicitly, before the first attention layer runs.
Question 4

What does the softmax step in scaled dot-product attention guarantee?

Softmax turns any vector of raw scores into a proper weighting — never negative, always summing to one — which is exactly what lets the next step blend the value vectors as a weighted average.
Question 5

What does the Temporal Fusion Transformer add specifically for forecasting, beyond plain self-attention?

The TFT (Lim et al., 2021) combines attention with recurrent layers and purpose-built variable-selection gating for interpretable, multi-horizon, distributional forecasts. Reducing the O(n²) cost is Informer's contribution (Zhou et al., 2021), a different named variant.
Section 12

What to carry forward

01

LSTM and GRU still process a sequence strictly step by step, compressing all relevant history into one fixed-size hidden state — a genuine bottleneck, and training can't parallelize across time steps.

02

Attention(Q,K,V) = softmax(QKᵀ/√dₖ)V lets every position directly compare against and weigh every other position at once — no compression, and every comparison can run in parallel during training.

03

Self-attention applies this within one sequence; multi-head attention runs several independently-projected comparisons in parallel so different heads can specialize.

04

Attention has no built-in sense of order — positional encoding restores it, and forecasting specifically also requires a causal mask so a step can't attend to the future.

05

The direct cost of "everyone compares against everyone" is O(n²) in sequence length — the reason time-series-specific variants like the Temporal Fusion Transformer and Informer exist.

06

A transformer's dominance in NLP and vision needs far more data and compute to earn that edge — never assume it transfers to a short, noisy market series without testing it, out-of-sample, against a well-tuned simpler baseline.

What this now lets you do

Read a causal, attention-based forecast's weights the way the Temporal Fusion Transformer is built to expose them — which past days or features a prediction actually relied on, not just what it predicted. Recognize when the O(n²) cost of full attention is a real constraint, and know that time-series-specific architectures exist precisely to address it. And most importantly, treat "transformers are state of the art" as a fact about NLP and vision benchmarks, not a substitute for testing the architecture, honestly and out-of-sample, on the specific series in front of you.
