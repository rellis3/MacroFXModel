Stress Testing, Scenario Analysis & Reverse Stress Tests

Asking "what happens if X" only gets you scenarios you thought to ask about — asking "what would have to happen for me to lose $Y" can surface the concentration risk you didn't know to look for.

CONCEPT — explains a risk-management framework; no stress-testing pipeline is built in this repo ~14 min read Prereqs: Coherent Risk Measures and Extreme Value Theory help but aren't required
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • Historical scenario replay applies a real past shock (e.g. a 2008-style quarter) to today's current portfolio weights — internally consistent, because the co-movements actually happened together.
  • Hypothetical / Monte Carlo scenario stress simulates a shock from an assumed distribution or correlation regime (e.g. "what if every correlation goes to 1") — it can explore scenarios that have never actually occurred.
  • Reverse stress testing flips the question: instead of "what's my loss under scenario X," it asks "what scenario would cause an unacceptable loss of $Y," solving backward from the loss threshold to the shock.
  • Reverse stress tests are especially good at surfacing hidden concentration — positions that look diversified on the surface (different asset classes, different desks) but load on the same underlying factor once you solve for what would actually break the portfolio.
  • Both forward-looking methods carry an honest limitation: historical scenarios are anchored to what already happened, and the next crisis rarely repeats the last one's exact shape; Monte Carlo scenarios are only as good as the correlation/distribution assumptions feeding them.
  • Real-world episode: in September 2022 UK pension schemes' LDI funds held collateral buffers sized to past gilt moves. The mini-budget produced a bigger one, forced selling made it bigger still, and the Bank of England had to buy long gilts to stop the loop. On this desk, reading history forward as a forecast tested null (analogue-weeks).
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

We already run stress tests. We replay 2008 on the book every quarter and we survive it. Isn't that enough?

It answers one question: what if that exact storm hit the house you live in today. It's useful, because the moves really did happen together. But it only covers storms you thought to ask about.

So I add made-up storms. Simulations where every correlation goes to one.

Good, that's the second tool. It can explore crises that never happened. But it's only as good as the rule you feed it. Assume a mild storm and the house looks safe for the wrong reason.

And the third?

The third flips the question. Don't pick a storm and measure the damage. Pick the damage you cannot survive, and solve backward for the storm. A structural engineer asks the same thing: what is the smallest failure that brings the house down?

Walk me through it with numbers.

A ten million dollar book. Six million in a short-volatility equity carry strategy, four million in high-yield credit carry. Different desks, different asset classes. Looks diversified.

And each desk survives its own bad quarter.

Easily. The equity desk loses thirty percent of its six million, one point eight. The credit desk loses fifteen percent of its four, point six. But both are really driven by the same thing: a broad collapse in risk appetite. One bad quarter on that one factor costs two point four million together.

Twenty-four percent of the book. Painful, but survivable.

Now go backward. The committee says losing five million, half the book, is unacceptable. Five divided by two point four is about two point oh eight. A shock only about twice an ordinary bad quarter, on one factor, takes out half the book.

So the two desks were one bet.

Exactly. That's what a reverse test is for: finding the one beam the whole house rests on.

Has a forward-only buffer ever failed like that?

UK pension funds, September 2022. Their leveraged hedging funds kept collateral buffers sized to past gilt moves. On the twenty-third the mini-budget hit, and long yields rose further and faster than the buffers had been set for. Funds sold gilts to meet margin calls, which pushed yields up again. On the twenty-eighth the Bank of England had to buy long gilts to break the loop.

So what's the one-line version?

Forward tests tell you what the storms you imagined would cost; a reverse test tells you which storm breaks you, and it's usually smaller than you'd think.

Section 01

Why this matters

A portfolio's day-to-day risk statistics — volatility, VaR, CVaR — are built from the ordinary, everyday range of outcomes a return series has actually produced. Stress testing exists precisely because the outcomes that matter most for survival are, almost by definition, the ones a routine statistical summary is least equipped to describe: the specific, coordinated, often correlation-breaking shock that doesn't look like an ordinary bad day, it looks like a different regime entirely. Three complementary techniques have developed to address this, each answering a differently-shaped question, and the third — reverse stress testing — asks the question in the direction that's easiest to overlook: not "how would I do under this scenario," but "what scenario would actually break me," which is frequently a more useful question precisely because nobody had to think of it first.

💡
Plain-English intuition

A homeowner checks the roof, then asks what it would actually take to bring the house down

Most home risk assessment works forward: "what happens to this house in a Category 3 hurricane," "what happens in a magnitude-6 earthquake" — you pick a scenario, and evaluate the damage. That's useful, but it only ever tells you about the scenarios you thought to ask about. A structural engineer doing a genuinely thorough safety review asks the question the other way around: "what is the smallest combination of failures that would actually bring this house down?" That question sometimes reveals something a forward checklist never would — maybe it isn't the roof or the foundation individually, but one specific load-bearing beam that, if compromised, cascades into everything else failing at once, even under conditions milder than either the hurricane or the earthquake scenario alone. Reverse stress testing is exactly that engineer's question applied to a portfolio: instead of grading survivability against scenarios you already imagined, you solve backward from "what's the smallest, most plausible thing that would cause the loss I truly cannot tolerate," which routinely uncovers a single hidden point of failure that a list of pre-picked scenarios would never have surfaced, because nobody thought to test for it directly.

The math, step by step

\[ \color{#10b981}{s^{*} = \frac{\$5\text{M}}{\$2.4\text{M}} \approx 2.08} \]

We'll define each of the three approaches precisely, in the order a risk team typically layers them: replay what actually happened, then explore what could hypothetically happen, then solve backward from the loss level that would actually matter.

1. Historical scenario replay

Let \(w\) be the vector of current portfolio weights (or dollar exposures) across \(n\) assets or risk factors, and let \(r^{\text{hist}}\) be the vector of realized returns for those same factors observed during some specific past stress episode (a particular week, month, or quarter). Historical scenario replay computes:

\[ \text{PnL}^{\text{hist}} = w^{\top} r^{\text{hist}} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i \, r_i^{\text{hist}} \]
wtoday's actual portfolio weights/exposures — not the weights held during the historical episode
r^histthe realized returns of each factor during the chosen past shock
w⊤r^histtoday's portfolio, marked against yesterday's shock

The key design choice is applying today's weights to a past shock — the point isn't to ask "how did this portfolio actually do in 2008" (it may not have existed then, or held completely different positions), it's to ask "if that exact historical shock happened again tomorrow, starting from the book I actually hold right now, what would happen." Because \(r^{\text{hist}}\) is a real, jointly observed set of returns, every correlation and co-movement between factors is automatically correct — you don't need to separately model how equities, credit spreads, and currencies moved together, because they already did, and the history captures the true joint behavior.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Historical scenario replay is asking "if that exact storm from a few years ago hit again tomorrow, but landed on the house I actually live in right now, what would happen?" You don't have to guess how the wind, rain, and flooding interacted with each other, because they really did happen together, exactly that way, once already — you're just pointing the real storm at your current house instead of the old one.

2. Hypothetical / Monte Carlo scenario stress

Rather than replaying an observed episode, this approach specifies a stress distribution — typically a shocked covariance matrix \(\Sigma^{\text{stress}}\) reflecting an assumed crisis regime (a common stylized assumption: correlations across risk factors move toward 1, since diversifying assets have repeatedly been observed to move together far more than usual during real crises) — and draws many simulated return vectors from it:

\[ r^{(k)} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu^{\text{stress}}, \Sigma^{\text{stress}}), \qquad k = 1, \ldots, N \]
Σ^stressan assumed stress-regime covariance matrix — not necessarily the ordinary one
μ^stressthe assumed mean shock vector under the stress regime
Nthe number of Monte Carlo draws — the more, the smoother the resulting loss distribution's estimate

Each draw \(r^{(k)}\) is combined with today's weights, \(\text{PnL}^{(k)} = w^{\top} r^{(k)}\), producing a full simulated distribution of stressed outcomes rather than a single number — from which a stressed VaR, CVaR, or full distribution shape can be read off. The entire exercise is only as good as \(\Sigma^{\text{stress}}\) and \(\mu^{\text{stress}}\): if the assumed correlation regime or shock size doesn't resemble whatever a real future crisis actually looks like, the simulated loss distribution won't either, no matter how many draws \(N\) you run.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Instead of replaying one real storm, Monte Carlo stress testing is imagining thousands of made-up storms based on a rule like "in a real disaster, wind and rain tend to get much worse together than usual" — and then running the house through all of them to see how bad it could get. It's more flexible than replaying just one real storm, but it's only as trustworthy as the made-up rule you fed it; a rule that quietly assumes calmer weather than reality gives will make the house look safer than it actually is.

3. Reverse stress testing — solving backward

Both methods above start from a scenario and compute a loss. Reverse stress testing inverts the direction: start from an unacceptable loss threshold \(Y^{*}\) (e.g. the loss that would trigger insolvency, breach a regulatory capital floor, or simply be judged intolerable by the risk committee), and solve for the scenario, or set of scenarios, that would produce it. In the simplest single-factor case, if portfolio loss is modeled as a function of a shock size \(s\) along some risk factor, \(\text{Loss}(s)\), reverse stress testing solves:

\[ s^{*} : \text{Loss}(s^{*}) = Y^{*} \]
Y*the unacceptable loss threshold you start from — chosen by the risk committee, not derived
s*the shock size that would produce exactly that loss — solved for, not assumed

With multiple risk factors, this becomes a search over the space of possible shocks for the smallest, most plausible combination that reaches \(Y^{*}\) — and the practically important output isn't just the number \(s^{*}\), it's which combination of factor moves gets there. A portfolio whose only route to the unacceptable loss threshold requires several independent, large, and implausible shocks to occur together is structurally safer than one where a single, modest, entirely plausible shock along one specific factor gets there on its own — and the latter is exactly the hidden-concentration finding the worked example below illustrates.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Instead of asking "what does this storm do to my house," reverse stress testing asks "how big would a storm actually have to be to knock my house down — and what would that storm need to hit exactly?" If it turns out only a fairly ordinary gust aimed at one specific weak spot is enough, that's a much scarier and more useful finding than knowing you'd survive a hypothetical monster hurricane you were never really at risk of anyway.

\(w\)Today's actual portfolio weights or dollar exposures across risk factors — held fixed while the scenario changes.
\(r^{\text{hist}}\)The realized returns of each risk factor during a chosen historical stress episode.
\(\Sigma^{\text{stress}}\), \(\mu^{\text{stress}}\)An assumed stress-regime covariance matrix and mean shock, used to simulate hypothetical (not historically observed) scenarios.
\(r^{(k)}\)One simulated draw of returns under the stress distribution; \(k=1,\ldots,N\) indexes the Monte Carlo sample.
\(\text{Loss}(s)\)The portfolio's loss as a function of a shock size \(s\) along one or more risk factors.
\(Y^{*}\)The unacceptable loss threshold chosen up front — the starting point of a reverse stress test.
\(s^{*}\)The shock size solved for such that \(\text{Loss}(s^{*}) = Y^{*}\) — the output of a reverse stress test.

Worked example

Solving backward reveals two "different" strategies share one real risk factor

A $10,000,000 book holds two positions that look diversified on paper: $6,000,000 in a short-volatility equity carry strategy (Strategy A) and $4,000,000 in a high-yield credit carry strategy (Strategy B) — different asset classes, different desks, different instruments. Suppose both are actually driven by the same underlying latent "risk-off" factor \(s\) (a broad collapse in risk appetite), with loss sensitivities \(\beta_A = 30\%\) of notional per unit of \(s\) (Strategy A's short-vol convexity makes it lose disproportionately) and \(\beta_B = 15\%\) of notional per unit of \(s\) (Strategy B's credit spreads widen less sharply per unit of the same shock):

\[ \text{Loss}(s) = \$6\text{M} \times 0.30 \times s + \$4\text{M} \times 0.15 \times s = \$2.4\text{M} \times s \]

Forward historical scenario (\(s=1\), "a 2008-style quarter"): Loss(1) = $2.4M, or 24% of the book. Viewed desk by desk, Strategy A alone loses 30% of its $6M (a survivable, well-understood equity-vol drawdown) and Strategy B alone loses 15% of its $4M (a survivable credit widening) — each individually looks like a manageable, ordinary bad quarter for that specific desk.

Reverse stress test: the risk committee sets an unacceptable-loss threshold of $5M (50% of the book). Solving \(\$2.4\text{M} \times s^{*} = \$5\text{M}\) gives \(s^{*} \approx 2.08\) — a shock only about twice the size of an ordinary "2008-style quarter" along the same single factor already identified above.

The concentration reveal: a forward-looking review that stress-tested "equity volatility spike" and "credit spread widening" as two separate scenarios, each hitting only its own desk, would have reported the book as diversified — each desk survives its own shock comfortably. The reverse stress test shows the book actually has only one real risk factor, not two: a single, moderate risk-off shock (roughly twice an ordinary bad quarter, not an exotic or extreme one) hits both desks simultaneously and takes out half the book. What looked like diversification across asset classes was, underneath, concentration in a single latent factor.
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

It's like two friends who seem totally independent — one drives to work, one bikes — until you realize they both cross the same one bridge every single day. Check each of them separately and everything looks fine and diversified. Ask instead "what's the single smallest thing that could stop both of them at once," and the answer turns out to be "that one bridge closes" — a much smaller, more ordinary event than it looked like two people needed two separate disasters.

Real-world trading scenario

Stress-testing an EM local-rates carry book ahead of an FOMC decision

A macro fund holds a book of emerging-market local-rates carry positions across several currencies, funded in dollars, ahead of a Federal Reserve policy decision that markets consider a genuine two-way risk event. The risk team layers all three techniques rather than picking just one. First, a historical scenario replay applies the actual realized cross-asset return pattern from a well-known past episode of sudden EM stress (a taper-tantrum-style repricing) to today's current book, producing a concrete, internally consistent P&L estimate built from real historical co-movements. Second, a Monte Carlo overlay stresses the same book under a hypothetical "correlations go to 1" regime — since the historical episode, however severe, is still only one specific realized path, and the team wants to see the range of outcomes if the various EM currencies sell off in a more uniformly correlated way than they did historically, capturing scenarios the single historical path might understate. Finally, a reverse stress test asks the sharper question directly: what size of dollar-funding-cost shock, combined with what size of EM currency move, would be required to breach the fund's actual capital floor? Solving backward reveals whether the book's real vulnerability is the currency exposure everyone is already watching, or the funding-cost sensitivity that's easy to overlook because it doesn't show up as a headline "EM risk" line item on the standard risk report.

Section 03

It actually happened: UK gilts and LDI, September 2022

It actually happened

A stress buffer sized to history meets a move that history hadn't produced

Many UK defined-benefit pension schemes hedged their long-dated liabilities through liability-driven investment (LDI) funds. Those funds held gilts and used leverage, through gilt repo and interest-rate swaps, to get more hedge per pound of the scheme's money. The structure works as long as yields drift. If long yields rise sharply, the hedges lose value and the funds have to post collateral quickly. Each fund kept a collateral buffer for this, sized to cover a yield rise of a chosen size.

On 23 September 2022 the UK government's "mini-budget" announced large tax cuts without saying how they would be paid for. Investors demanded far more to hold long gilts, and long-dated yields rose over a few days by more, and faster, than anything the funds' buffers had been set for. The Bank of England said afterwards that the move went beyond what LDI funds had been prepared for. The collateral calls came in. To meet them, funds and schemes sold gilts into a market with few buyers, which pushed yields up again and set off the next round of calls.

On 28 September the Bank of England stepped in with temporary purchases of long-dated gilts, explicitly to protect financial stability rather than as monetary policy, and ended them on 14 October. Within weeks the Chancellor had been replaced and the Prime Minister had resigned. UK authorities then set a higher minimum level of resilience for LDI funds.

In this lesson's terms, the buffer was a forward stress test. Pick a shock size \(s\) from the history of gilt moves, check that \(\text{Loss}(s)\) fits inside the buffer, and stop. A reverse stress test asks the question the other way round: what yield rise \(s^{*}\) uses up the buffer, and who else is forced to sell gilts at the same moment? The answer would have been a move outside history. That is exactly the kind of answer the last pitfall in Section 04 warns against dismissing as "too unlikely."

Where the textbook version breaks

The replay formula \(w^{\top} r^{\text{hist}}\) holds today's weights \(w\) fixed and treats the shock as something that happens to the book from outside. In September 2022 neither held. The funds' forced gilt sales were part of the yield move itself, so the shock fed on the positions being stressed, and \(\text{Loss}(s)\) steepened as \(s\) grew instead of staying a straight line. The size of \(s\) had also been anchored to the largest moves in the record, and the record was beaten.

Desk test: nothing found analogue-weeks: history replays a loss; it does not forecast the next move. The tempting trading extension of historical replay is to find the past weeks that look most like this one and trade what followed them. Walk-forward over 400 weeks, the ten nearest weeks across 21 series were right 75% of the time on the S&P against an unconditional 76%; EUR/USD 53% vs 54%; gold 61% vs 69%. Size error was larger than the unconditional median on all three. Use replay to size a loss if a shock recurs, never as a lean on what comes next.
Section 04

How practitioners actually use this

  • Regulatory stress testing programs combine all three. Post-crisis supervisory frameworks (the Basel Committee's stress-testing principles, and national programs like the Federal Reserve's Comprehensive Capital Analysis and Review, CCAR) typically require both historical/ hypothetical scenario stress testing and, in some jurisdictions, an explicit reverse stress-testing exercise — the UK's Financial Services Authority made reverse stress testing a formal requirement for regulated firms starting in 2009, specifically because forward-only scenario testing had repeatedly missed the actual channel that broke firms during the 2008 crisis.
  • Reverse stress tests are run at the "business model survival" level, not just a trading book. A full reverse stress test for a firm often asks a broader question than "what shock breaks this specific portfolio" — it asks what combination of events would render the firm's entire business model unviable, which can surface risks (funding withdrawal, reputational contagion) that a single trading book's stress test would never capture.
  • Historical replay and Monte Carlo stress are triangulated against each other, not chosen exclusively. A risk team that only runs historical replays is implicitly betting the next crisis resembles a past one; a team that only runs Monte Carlo stress is implicitly betting its assumed stress distribution is realistic. Serious practitioners report both, and treat a large gap between them as itself informative about model uncertainty.
  • Reverse stress test results feed directly into risk-limit design. Once a reverse stress test identifies the specific factor combination that produces an unacceptable loss, the most direct action is often a position or concentration limit targeted at exactly that factor — a much more surgical response than an across-the-board size reduction.
⚠️
Common pitfalls

Ways stress testing gets over-trusted or misapplied

  • Treating historical replay as covering "the crash" in general. The honest caveat stated plainly: historical scenarios are anchored to what already happened, and the next crisis rarely repeats the last one's exact shape — a different set of assets, a different trigger, a different correlation pattern. A portfolio that would have survived 2008 comfortably is not thereby shown to survive whatever specific shape the next crisis actually takes.
  • Trusting a Monte Carlo stress result without interrogating its assumptions. A simulated stress distribution is only as good as \(\Sigma^{\text{stress}}\) and \(\mu^{\text{stress}}\) — if the assumed correlation regime understates how far correlations actually move in a real crisis, or the assumed shock size is too small, the simulated loss distribution will look reassuring for entirely the wrong reason: not because the portfolio is genuinely safe, but because the stress wasn't stressed enough.
  • Running a reverse stress test and stopping at the number, not the "how." The valuable output of a reverse stress test isn't the shock size \(s^{*}\) alone — it's the specific combination of factor moves that gets there, which is where hidden concentration risk actually shows up. A report that states "a 2.1x shock breaches our capital floor" without identifying which factor combination that shock corresponds to has skipped the useful half of the exercise.
  • Assuming a plausible-sounding scenario is automatically a probable one, or vice versa. Reverse stress testing deliberately asks for the smallest, most plausible route to a bad outcome — but "smallest and most plausible" is a judgment call, not something the math alone determines. A risk team that dismisses the identified scenario as "too unlikely to worry about" has, in effect, talked itself out of the exact finding the exercise was designed to surface.
Scenario drill · 26 September 2022

The gilt move is heading past what your buffer was built for

You oversee the hedge of a UK defined-benefit pension scheme. It runs through a leveraged LDI fund that holds gilts, gilt repo and swaps, with a collateral buffer sized to cover a yield rise of a chosen size, taken from the history of gilt moves. On Friday the government's mini-budget announced large unfunded tax cuts. Long gilt yields are jumping and collateral calls are arriving. At this pace the rise will soon pass anything your buffer was set for. Every other LDI fund holds a similar book. What do you do first?

  1. Wait. A move this extreme has to reverse.
    A move past history is exactly what a buffer sized to history cannot handle. Calls you cannot meet do not wait for a reversal; the fund gets closed out at the worst level.
  2. Meet each call as it comes by selling gilts.
    It keeps the hedge alive for now, but you and every similar fund are selling the same gilts into a market with few buyers. That pushes yields up again and triggers the next round of calls.
  3. Run the reverse test now: what further yield rise uses up what is left of the buffer, counting everyone else forced to sell at once? Then raise collateral from other assets and cut leverage to survive that answer, not the historical one.
    The forward test is about to fail. Solving backward for the shock that breaks you, including the forced-selling loop that steepens the loss, tells you how close the edge really is and how much cash and de-leveraging you need.
  4. Re-run the historical replay with the worst week on record and report that the book survives it.
    The record is being beaten as you watch. Replaying it again only measures a storm you already know is too small.

Section 05

Key Takeaways

✓Historical scenario replay is internally consistent. Because it uses a real jointly-observed shock, every correlation between factors is automatically correct — no separate modeling assumption needed.
✓Monte Carlo scenario stress explores what hasn't happened yet. A shocked covariance regime (e.g. "correlations go to 1") can cover scenarios no historical episode has actually produced.
✓Reverse stress testing solves backward from an unacceptable loss. It routinely surfaces hidden concentration — the worked example's two "different" strategies sharing one real risk factor is the textbook illustration.
⚠Historical scenarios are anchored to the past, honestly. The next crisis rarely repeats the last one's exact shape — surviving a replayed 2008 is not evidence of surviving whatever comes next.
⚠Monte Carlo stress is only as good as its assumptions. An understated stress correlation or shock size produces a reassuring number for the wrong reason.
✗A buffer sized to history fails on the move history hasn't produced, and forced selling makes the shock feed on itself. UK LDI, September 2022. Reading history forward does not help either: analogue weeks were right 75% of the time on the S&P against an unconditional 76% (analogue-weeks, NULL).
✗Nothing here is built or run in this repo. This lesson explains the three techniques conceptually; it is not a claim that any stress-testing pipeline exists or has been validated on this project's actual portfolios.
Section 06

Self-test

Q1. In a historical scenario replay, why do you apply today's portfolio weights to a past shock, rather than looking at how the portfolio actually performed back when the shock originally occurred?
The goal is to answer "if that exact historical shock happened again tomorrow, what would happen to the book I actually hold right now" — not "how did some past version of the book do." The portfolio you hold today may not have existed during the original episode, or may have held entirely different positions; applying the historical returns to today's weights isolates the question of interest (today's exposure to a real, jointly-observed shock pattern) from how the book happened to be positioned at the time.
Q2. Why does "correlations go to 1" show up so often as an assumption in hypothetical/Monte Carlo stress scenarios?
It reflects a widely observed pattern in real crises: assets that normally diversify each other (different asset classes, different geographies) have repeatedly been seen to move together far more strongly than their ordinary-times correlation would suggest, once a broad enough risk-off shock hits. Assuming correlations move toward 1 under stress is a way of building that empirically-motivated pattern into a hypothetical scenario, rather than relying on ordinary-times correlations that likely understate how a real crisis would actually behave.
Q3. What question does a reverse stress test ask that a forward scenario test does not?
A forward scenario test picks a scenario and asks "what's my loss under this." A reverse stress test starts from an unacceptable loss threshold and asks "what scenario, or combination of factor moves, would actually produce this loss" — solving backward from the outcome to the cause, rather than forward from an assumed cause to its outcome.
Q4. In the worked example, why did the two desks look diversified under forward scenario testing but not under the reverse stress test?
Forward testing evaluated each desk's own historical-style shock in isolation — equity volatility hitting Strategy A, credit spreads widening for Strategy B — and each desk survived its own shock comfortably, which looked like diversification. The reverse stress test instead solved for the smallest shock along the single underlying risk-off factor that both strategies actually share, and found that a moderate move in that one factor (not two separate large shocks) was enough to reach the unacceptable loss threshold on both desks simultaneously — revealing that the apparent diversification was actually concentration in one latent factor.
Q5. Why is it a mistake to stop a reverse stress test at the shock-size number \(s^{*}\) without reporting which factor combination produces it?
The number \(s^{*}\) alone only tells you "how big" a shock is required — the actionable insight is "what kind" of shock it is, i.e. which specific factor or combination of factors gets there. That's where hidden concentration risk is actually identified, and it's what a risk limit or hedge would need to be targeted at. Reporting only the magnitude and skipping the composition discards the part of the exercise that makes it useful.
Q6. State the honest caveat that applies to both historical replay and Monte Carlo scenario stress, in your own words.
Historical replay is anchored to what has already happened — it can't capture a future crisis that unfolds along a genuinely different path than any observed one, and the next crisis rarely repeats the last one's exact shape. Monte Carlo scenario stress can explore hypothetical regimes never directly observed, but its output is entirely dependent on the assumed correlation/distribution inputs — if those assumptions understate how extreme or how correlated a real future shock could be, the simulated result will look reassuring for the wrong reason.
