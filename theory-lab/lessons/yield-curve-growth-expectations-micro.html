What the Yield Curve Says About Growth

The yield curve is the bond market's best guess at where interest rates are going, plus a fee for waiting. That makes it a real reading of what the market expects — and a much shakier forecast of what will happen.

The whole idea
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

Every time the yield curve inverts, my inbox fills up with recession warnings. What is the curve actually telling me?

Start with what a long yield is. Lending to the government for ten years has to compete with lending for two years and then rolling over. So the ten-year yield ends up close to the average short rate people expect over the next ten years, plus a small fee for locking money away.

And that fee is the term premium.

Right. Nobody sees it directly. Models estimate it, and it can even be negative. But mostly, the shape of the curve is a picture of where the market thinks the central bank will take rates.

So what does it mean when the two-year yields more than the ten-year?

The market is saying short rates are high now and should be lower later. Rates are usually cut when the economy weakens. That is why an inversion reads as an expected slowdown. Notice the word expected.

Give me numbers.

Say the three-month bill pays five point three percent, the two-year four and a half, and the five-year four. Lend for five years and you lock in twenty percent in total. Two years at four and a half earns nine of that. So the three years in between must average eleven divided by three, about three point seven percent.

That is well below the bill rate.

More than a point and a half below. Allow for a small term premium and it is roughly two points of cuts priced. That is what the market expects. It is not a promise.

How do inversions usually start?

At the front end. The Fed hikes, and the two-year is dragged up above the ten-year. They usually end with a bull steepening, when the two-year falls faster as cuts get priced.

And has the expectation been right?

Sometimes, with a long fuse. Through 2006 the curve was flat or inverted, but stocks did not peak until October 2007. The recession was dated from December 2007, and that date was only announced a year later.

And when it was wrong?

The curve inverted in July 2022 and stayed that way until around September 2024, the longest run in the series. Recession forecasts were everywhere. As of this writing, no recession has been dated to follow it.

Who decides what counts as a recession, anyway?

A committee at the National Bureau of Economic Research, in hindsight, often many months later. Two negative quarters is newspaper shorthand. The first estimates for 2022 showed two, and no recession was declared.

So the one-line version?

The curve is a good reading of what the market expects, and a poor clock for when anything will happen. Read it. Don't time with it.

Four moves

Bull or bear, steeper or flatter

Bull = yields falling. Bear = yields rising. Steepening = 10y minus 2y widens.

Bull steepening

Front end falls faster — cuts being priced. How inversions usually end.

Bear steepening

Long end rises faster — growth/inflation, term premium, or bond supply worries.

Bear flattening

Front end rises faster — hikes priced. How most inversions are made.

Bull flattening

Long end falls faster — long-run growth fading, or a rush into duration.

Worked example

What an inverted front end is pricing

3m 5.30%, 2y 4.50%, 5y 4.00%, 10y 4.10% (illustrative).

Step by step
2s10s = 4.10 − 4.50 = −40bp
3m10y = 4.10 − 5.30 = −120bp
f(2→5) ≈ (5×4.00 − 2×4.50) / 3 = 11 / 3 ≈ 3.67%
minus a +0.30% term premium → expected ≈ 3.37%

Roughly two percentage points of cuts priced from the 5.30% bill rate. That is what the market expects. It says nothing about whether the expectation is right.

Why it matters

What the curve is said to predict is only labelled after the fact. Even if the curve were right, you could not know in time to trade it.

It actually happened

Three inversions

Right — with a fuse of more than a year, during which equities rose.

What the desk found

curve-inversion: null for the tradeable half

Desk test: nothing foundTen inversion episodes, 1976–2026 (12,581 daily 10y–2y readings; the unit is the episode). Recession within 24 months: 6 of 10 — 60%, 95% CI [31%, 83%]; within 12 months 3 of 10. Forward Nasdaq vs month-matched control: +12m +10.5% vs +13.0% (p=0.33). The mirror killed it: steepenings returned −1.1% at +12m (n=6) — worse than inversions.
Flipping the conditional

P(inversion | recession) is high. P(recession | inversion) is 60%, from 31% to 83%. Four of ten: no recession at all.

What survives

"The market expects a slowdown" — a measurement, reported descriptively, always with the denominator.

Using it

How to read it without over-reading it

1

Which end moved?

2-year up = the Fed's next meetings. 10-year down = long-run growth.

2

What is priced?

Back out the 2→5 forward — and remember QE-era term premia can squeeze it.

3

What is it worth?

As an equity timing signal, on this desk's test: nothing.

🏠

Rates you pay

Bills → savings; 2–10y → car and business loans; 10y → the mortgage benchmark.

Question 1

The 2-year falls 30bp and the 10-year falls 10bp. What is the move?

Yields fell (bull) and 10y minus 2y widened by 20bp (steepening), led by the front end.
Question 2

2-year 4.8%, 5-year 4.2%. Roughly what rate is implied for years 2–5?

(5×4.2 − 2×4.8)/3 = (21 − 9.6)/3 = 3.8%. Below today's 2-year, so cuts are priced (before any term premium).
Question 3

Who dates US recessions?

The NBER picks peak and trough months on depth, breadth and duration, announcing months later. Early 2022 had two negative GDP quarters and no recession.
Question 4

What killed the desk's curve-inversion test?

Leave-one-out passed (2008 is not carrying it), and 6 of 10 inversions were followed by a recession. But curve shape did not separate equity outcomes, and inversions never beat a matched control.
Your call
Scenario drill · July 2022

The curve has just inverted. Do you sell?

It is July 2022. The Fed began hiking in March and is raising rates faster than at any time in four decades. This month the 2s10s curve has inverted. A morning note says: "The curve just inverted. A recession is coming. Sell equities now." You hold an equity position. What do you do?

  1. Sell everything today. Inversion means recession, and recession means stocks fall.
    The curve measures expectations, not timing. In 2006 to 2007 the curve was flat or inverted for more than a year before the recession, and stocks rose in between. Even when the curve is right, the fuse can be long.
  2. Read it as expectations. The 2-year was dragged up by hikes, so the forwards are pricing cuts later. Note the expected slowdown, and judge the equity position on its own evidence.
    This uses the curve for what it measures: the market's expected path of rates. A recession is only dated in hindsight, so the inversion cannot tell you when, or even whether, to sell.
  3. Trim a little and wait for an official recession call before doing more.
    A small trim is defensible risk control. But the official call comes from the NBER many months after the turning point (the December 2007 peak was announced in December 2008), so you would be waiting for a label, not a signal.
  4. Ignore the curve. The bond market knows nothing about growth.
    The curve is a genuine reading of where the market expects the Fed to take rates, and why. Throwing it away loses real information about policy, even if it is a poor clock.

What happened. The 2s10s curve stayed inverted until around September 2024: the longest continuous inversion in the series, and at its deepest in 2023 around a full percentage point below zero. The first GDP estimates for the first half of 2022 showed two negative quarters, yet the NBER declared no recession and unemployment stayed low. As of this writing, no recession has been dated to follow that inversion. It ended with a bull steepening as the Fed began cutting.

Recap

What to carry forward

01

A yield ≈ expected policy path + term premium. The curve is a measurement of expectations.

02

Inversions are usually built at the front end: the Fed hikes, the 2-year climbs over the 10-year.

03

Bull/bear × steepening/flattening tells you which end moved and what is being repriced.

04

The NBER dates recessions in hindsight; "two negative quarters" is shorthand.

05

curve-inversion is NULL as a trade: 6 of 10 (60%, CI [31%, 83%]) recessions within 24 months, no equity separation from control, steepenings worse.
