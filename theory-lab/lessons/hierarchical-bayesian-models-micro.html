Hierarchical Bayesian Models, the Principled Middle Ground

Five trading strategies, five trade counts, five noisy Sharpe estimates — should the one with 6 lucky-looking trades really be trusted as much as the one with 400? Partial pooling is the answer between "trust every estimate fully" and "ignore the differences entirely," and a hierarchical model is what lets the data decide how much of each to use, instead of an analyst guessing.

Five strategies (call them A–E), each with a raw sample-average Sharpe ratio. Two have barely any trades behind them at all (amber). Two have real track records (green). One sits in between.

A strategy traded for two years has hundreds of trades behind its Sharpe estimate; one launched last month might have six. Treating a 6-trade estimate and a 400-trade estimate as equally trustworthy is the first mistake almost everyone makes with small samples — and it's the exact mistake this lesson is about avoiding, without throwing away real differences between groups either.

Not just Sharpe ratios

Swap "strategy" for currency pair, analyst, or trading desk, and "Sharpe ratio" for win rate, forecast skill, or expected return — the same problem shows up anywhere several related groups each produce their own noisy estimate from an unequal amount of data.

Section 2

Two ways to get this wrong

Both extremes are tempting. Both throw away real information.

No pooling
  • Estimate every group entirely on its own data
  • Strategy A's 6-trade 2.10 Sharpe is taken completely at face value
  • Ignores that the groups are probably related and roughly similar
  • A tiny sample gets exactly as much confidence as a huge one
Complete pooling
  • Treat every group as identical, pool all data into one number
  • Strategy A and Strategy B both get the same grand-mean Sharpe
  • Ignores that a 400-trade estimate has earned the right to differ
  • Real, informative differences between groups are discarded entirely
Neither extreme is "wrong" as arithmetic — both are honest answers to a question nobody actually wants asked. No pooling answers "what if the groups have nothing to do with each other?" Complete pooling answers "what if the groups are all secretly identical?" Real groups are usually somewhere in between — related, but not identical — and neither extreme has a dial for that.

Partial pooling isn't a compromise reached by averaging the other two answers — it's computed directly from a shrinkage formula that uses each group's own sample size. The next few slides build that formula.

Group j's own raw average

The "no pooling" number — group j's sample mean, using only its own data.

μ

The grand mean

The "complete pooling" number — the overall average across all groups combined.

Bⱼ

The shrinkage weight

Bⱼ = τ² / (τ² + σ²/nⱼ). More trades (bigger nⱼ) shrinks σ²/nⱼ toward zero, pushing Bⱼ toward 1 — trust the group's own data more.

τ²

Between-group variance

How much groups genuinely differ from each other, once sampling noise is accounted for — estimated from the data, not guessed.

Notice both extremes fall out of this one formula as limiting cases: as τ²→0 (groups don't really differ), Bⱼ→0 for everyone and θⱼ-hat→μ — complete pooling. As τ²→∞ (the groups tell you nothing about each other), Bⱼ→1 and θⱼ-hat→ȳⱼ — no pooling. Partial pooling is what happens for any finite, honestly-estimated τ² — the two "pure" strategies are just its endpoints.
Section 5

Where the hyperprior fits in the stack

"Hierarchical" means two layers of randomness, stacked — and the top layer is what lets the model learn Bⱼ instead of an analyst picking it.

Two levels, top to bottom
Hyperprior
μ, τ²
↓ generates
θⱼ ~ N(μ, τ²)
group j's true value
↓ generates
yᵢⱼ ~ N(θⱼ, σ²)
observation i in group j
Reading top to bottom: a shared population distribution (governed by μ and τ²) is where every group's true parameter θⱼ comes from — and each group's individual observations then scatter around its own θⱼ. Reading bottom to top is exactly the estimation problem: observed data → infer θⱼ for each group → infer μ and τ² for the population they came from.

Crucially, μ and τ² are not fixed in advance by the analyst — they're estimated from the full collection of groups. That's what makes this different from just picking a pooling amount by hand: the model looks at how much the five strategies' raw Sharpes actually scatter around each other and sets τ² — and therefore every Bⱼ — accordingly.

Where this needs qualifying

Estimating μ and τ² themselves adds its own layer of uncertainty, and five or ten groups is a genuinely small sample for pinning down a between-group variance. A fully Bayesian treatment puts a prior on μ and τ² too, rather than plugging in one point estimate as this lesson's numbers do for clarity.

Section 9

A surprising, provable result — and where practitioners use it

James & Stein (1961) proved something genuinely counterintuitive: shrinking three or more group estimates toward a common target achieves strictly lower total error than trusting every group's own raw average — even when the groups have nothing to do with each other in reality. It's not a claim the groups are secretly related; it's a mathematical fact about squared-error loss once three or more parameters are estimated at once, made famous by Efron & Morris using baseball batting averages.
⚾

Sports analytics

Shrinking early-season batting averages toward the league mean predicts end-of-season performance far better than trusting a hot start at face value.

🗺

Small-area estimation

Public-health and survey statisticians need a rate for every region, even sparsely-sampled ones — partial pooling across regions is the standard fix.

🧪

Many-variant A/B testing

Testing dozens of variants means most get modest traffic — hierarchical shrinkage keeps a lucky low-traffic variant from being crowned a winner too soon.

Section 10

Where partial pooling quietly goes wrong

Common pitfalls

Pooling groups that shouldn't be pooled. The benefit depends on groups genuinely sharing a common distribution — shrinking a carry-trade strategy toward a mean computed from unrelated momentum strategies can make the estimate worse, not better.

Treating μ and τ² as free of uncertainty. They're estimated from a finite — often small — number of groups. Plugging in one point estimate, as this lesson's worked numbers do, understates the true uncertainty versus a fully Bayesian treatment.

Confusing shrinkage with validation. A shrunk estimate is a more honest point estimate of what's already been observed — it is not evidence a strategy will keep performing that way. It still needs genuine out-of-sample proof before it earns real capital.

A systematic allocator sizing five sub-strategies by expected Sharpe would badly over-allocate to Strategy A using its raw 2.10 — precisely the small-sample noise the shrunk 1.478 corrects for. That's the logic of the approach, not a claim that this specific allocation rule is built, backtested, or running anywhere in this codebase today — see the note below.

Checked against this repo's actual code

The repo does contain a real n/(n+k) shrinkage estimator in forge/vol.py (event-day volatility multipliers shrunk toward 1.0 by sample size) — so "shrink a noisy estimate by how little data backs it" is a real, shipped technique here. But there is no code that shrinks a per-pair or per-strategy Sharpe, win rate, or return estimate toward a cross-pair or cross-strategy grand mean the way this lesson describes — the allocation scenario above remains conceptual, exactly as the full lesson already discloses.

Section 11

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

What does complete pooling assume, that no pooling does not?

Complete pooling treats θ₁=θ₂=...=θⱼ=μ and estimates one shared number. No pooling assumes the opposite — every group is entirely unrelated to the rest.
Question 2

In θⱼ ~ N(μ, τ²), what does τ² represent?

τ² is the between-group variance — how spread out the true group means are around the grand mean μ. σ² is the separate within-group observation noise.
Question 3

Why does Strategy A (n=5, raw Sharpe 2.10) shrink much harder than Strategy B (n=50, raw Sharpe 0.85)?

Bⱼ = τ²/(τ²+σ²/nⱼ). A small nⱼ inflates σ²/nⱼ, which dominates the denominator and drags Bⱼ toward 0 — most of the weight falls on μ, not on A's own noisy 2.10.
Question 4

As τ² → ∞, what does the hierarchical model collapse to?

If groups could differ arbitrarily (τ²→∞), the shared structure carries no correcting information, so Bⱼ→1 and every group is left with its own raw estimate — no pooling.
Question 5

Does a partially pooled Sharpe estimate prove a strategy will keep performing that way?

Shrinkage only corrects small-sample noise in data already collected. It adds no information about the future — the same out-of-sample discipline still applies before trusting the number going forward.
Section 12

What to carry forward

01

Complete pooling, no pooling, and partial pooling are one spectrum — the τ²→0, τ²→∞, and finite-τ² cases of the same hierarchical model, not three unrelated ideas.

02

The shrinkage formula θⱼ-hat = Bⱼ·ȳⱼ + (1−Bⱼ)·μ blends each group's own average with the grand mean, weighted by how much data that group actually has.

03

Bⱼ = τ²/(τ²+σ²/nⱼ) rises toward 1 as nⱼ grows — small samples get shrunk hardest, in either direction, flashy or ugly-looking.

04

The hyperprior θⱼ ~ N(μ,τ²) is what lets μ and τ² — and therefore every Bⱼ — be learned from the data, instead of an analyst guessing the pooling amount.

05

James-Stein's result: shrinking several estimates toward a common target lowers total error even when the groups are truly unrelated — a fact about squared-error loss, not an assumption about the world.

06

A shrunk estimate is a better point estimate, not a validated forecast — and pooling groups that don't actually share structure can make things worse, not better.

What this now lets you do

Compare several related groups' noisy estimates without either over-trusting a lucky-looking small sample or throwing away a large sample's hard-earned differences. Recognize complete pooling and no pooling as the two extremes of one formula, not separate techniques. Know exactly which lever — nⱼ or τ² — is doing the work whenever an estimate gets pulled toward a group average.
