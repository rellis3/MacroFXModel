Multivariate & Correlated SDEs

Every SDE so far in this curriculum — the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, geometric Brownian motion — followed one process driven by one source of randomness. Real books never look like that: an FX desk holds pairs that share a currency leg, Heston's own model needs a stock price and its variance to move together. This lesson builds the standard machinery for many correlated processes at once — a vector SDE, a diffusion matrix, and the Cholesky decomposition that turns a target correlation into simulatable shocks.

Section 1

One process was never going to be enough

Existence & Uniqueness and Numerical Schemes, the two lessons before this one, both worked with a single process — one OU or GBM path, one Brownian motion driving it. Nothing about a real trading book looks like that.

💱

Correlated FX pairs

EUR/USD, GBP/USD, and EUR/GBP all move on the same book, and they share currency legs — a EUR-strength move pushes two of them the same way and the third the opposite way. Three processes, not independently drawn.

📉

Heston's own two processes

The Heston model, covered earlier in this Theory Lab, is already a live example: a price process and a variance process, each its own SDE, correlated with each other by a single ρ. That ρ is this lesson's whole subject, just not named yet.

📈

A whole rate curve

Level, slope, and curvature of a yield curve don't move independently either — model the curve as several factors evolving together and the same correlated-shock problem shows up again.

The fix isn't a new kind of SDE — it's the same drift-plus-diffusion structure, just stacked into vectors and matrices. Everything built in the previous two lessons about existence, uniqueness, and simulating a single SDE carries over unchanged; the new piece is entirely about how several processes' randomness relates to each other.
Section 2

Stacking processes into a vector

Collect n processes into one vector Xₜ, and the same drift-plus-diffusion shape from every earlier lesson still applies — just with vectors and a matrix instead of numbers.

The vector SDE
dXt
=
μ(Xt,t) dt
+
Σ(Xt,t) dWt
Same shape as a single-process SDE — drift term plus diffusion term — except now every symbol is a vector or a matrix.
▤

Xₜ — an n-vector

n processes evolving together: n asset prices, n points on a curve, or a price and its variance (n = 2, Heston's case).

→

μ — an n-vector of drifts

One drift per process. Nothing new here versus a single-process SDE, just one per row.

▦

Σ — an n×m matrix

The diffusion loadings. This is the new object, and it's a matrix — see the Linear Algebra & Matrices lesson for the vocabulary "matrix acting on a vector" leans on.

🎲

Wₜ — an m-vector, independent

m separate Brownian motions, independent of each other by construction. That word "independent" is the whole hinge this lesson turns on.

Section 3

Independent dice, mixed into correlated outcomes

Here's the entire idea with no matrices yet. Two ordinary, completely independent dice — call the rolls z₁ and z₂.

The mixing trick
z₁, z₂
independent rolls
mix →
y₁ = z₁
y₂ = 0.6z₁ + 0.8z₂
y₁ and y₂ are now correlated — y₂ partly reuses z₁, so whenever z₁ comes up large, both y₁ and y₂ tend to be large. Change the weights 0.6 and 0.8, and the correlation changes, without ever touching z₁ or z₂ themselves.
This is the entire content of the Cholesky recipe two slides from now — continuous-time Brownian increments standing in for dice rolls, a matrix standing in for the two mixing weights. And those weights, 0.6 and 0.8, aren't arbitrary: they're exactly ρ = 0.6 and √(1−ρ²) = 0.8 for a target correlation of 0.6, the example this whole deck builds toward.
Where this needs qualifying

Keeping Wₜ's components independent and doing all the correlating through Σ is a modelling convention, not a mathematical necessity — but it's the universal one, because it cleanly separates "how much randomness does each process have" (Σ's diagonal magnitude) from "how does that randomness correlate across processes" (Σ's off-diagonal mixing).

Section 4

One identity carries the whole topic

Apply the same (dWₜ)(dWₜ)ᵀ → I dt rule that underlies Itô's Lemma, and the instantaneous covariance of the moves in Xₜ falls out directly.

Try it

=
Cov(dXt) / dt
Hand this direction a loading matrix Σ, and the covariance of every process pair is a free lookup — one matrix multiplication, done.
target ρ

Σ (or its factor L)
This is the direction that actually shows up in practice: you're handed a target correlation from market data or a calibration, and need to invert the identity to find a Σ that produces it. That inversion is exactly what Cholesky decomposition is for — next slide.

Every entry of ΣΣᵀ is a covariance (or, on the diagonal, a variance). The forward direction — Σ in, covariance out — needs nothing but matrix multiplication, the exact operation the Linear Algebra & Matrices lesson builds from scratch. The backward direction is where the real work of this lesson lives.

Section 5

The Cholesky recipe, step by step

A target correlation ρ = 0.6 between two processes. Walk the recipe through to a pair of correctly-correlated shocks.

Try it
ρ = [1, 0.6]
[0.6, 1]
Symmetric, 1's on the diagonal, the off-diagonal is the desired correlation. This is the target — what you got from historical data or a calibration.
L₂₁ = ρ = 0.600
L₂₂ = √(1−ρ²) = 0.800
L = [1, 0] / [0.6, 0.8] — lower-triangular, and it satisfies ρ = LLᵀ. This L always exists whenever ρ is a genuine, valid correlation matrix.
dW¹corr = dW¹ind
dW²corr = 0.6 dW¹ind + 0.8 dW²ind
Draw two independent standard shocks, dW¹ind and dW²ind, then mix them with L. What comes out the other side is what actually feeds each process's SDE.
Var(dW²corr) = 0.36dt + 0.64dt = dt ✓
Correct unit variance, and Corr(dW¹corr, dW²corr) works out to exactly 0.6 — the target, reproduced by construction, not by trial and error.

Section 9

From two processes to N — same idea, bigger matrix

Nothing about the recipe changes once a third, fourth, or Nth correlated process joins. Only the size of ρ and L grows.

Two processes
  • A single scalar ρ is all two processes ever need
  • 2×2 correlation matrix, closed-form L = [1,0]/[ρ,√(1−ρ²)]
  • This is exactly Heston's price-variance pair, from the earlier lesson in this Theory Lab
N processes
  • A full N×N correlation matrix — one entry per pair of processes
  • No closed form in general — L is solved column by column, but always exists for a valid ρ
  • Same mixing formula: dWcorrelated = L · dWindependent, just with bigger vectors
Heston's model was built — and is usually taught — without ever saying the word "Cholesky." But its single ρ is a Cholesky factor of a 2×2 correlation matrix, just never named as one. Recognising that is what makes the jump to three, five, or fifty correlated processes feel like the same problem rather than a new one.
Section 10

Where this shows up on a desk

🧺

Basket and spread option pricing

A basket call, a spread option, an FX cross built from two majors — any payoff depending on more than one underlying needs every underlying simulated together, via exactly this Cholesky recipe.

📉

Portfolio VaR and stress testing

Portfolio-level risk depends on the joint, not marginal, distribution of every position's returns. An empirical correlation matrix, Cholesky-decomposed, is the standard bridge from single-asset risk to portfolio-level risk.

📈

Multi-factor curve models

A yield curve modelled as level, slope, and curvature — the same factors nelson-siegel-svensson.html fits cross-sectionally — needs correlated shocks when projected forward in time.

Where this gets misapplied

An invalid correlation matrix. Cholesky decomposition requires ρ to be positive semi-definite — asynchronous or mismatched historical data can quietly violate this, and the decomposition then fails outright or produces nonsense. Treating a small-sample realization as proof of a bug. A handful of simulated paths can land visibly off-target from pure sampling noise, not a broken recipe. Assuming ρ is fixed. Real correlations shift over time and often spike toward one in a crisis — a static Cholesky-based simulation can't capture that on its own.

Section 11

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

In dXₜ = μdt + ΣdWₜ, are the components of Wₜ correlated with each other?

Wₜ's components are independent standard Brownian motions by construction. Every bit of correlation between the processes in Xₜ is introduced entirely by the matrix Σ mixing those independent shocks together.
Question 2

What equation defines the Cholesky factor L of a target correlation matrix ρ?

L is the lower-triangular matrix satisfying ρ = LLᵀ. It always exists, with positive diagonal entries, whenever ρ is a genuine, valid correlation matrix.
Question 3

Target ρ = 0.8, independent shocks z₁ = 1, z₂ = 1. What is dW²corr?

L₂₁ = ρ = 0.8, L₂₂ = √(1−ρ²) = √0.36 = 0.6. dW²corr = ρz₁ + L₂₂z₂ = 0.8(1) + 0.6(1) = 1.4.
Question 4

Heston's model correlates its price and variance SDEs with a single scalar ρ. How does this relate to Cholesky decomposition?

The Cholesky factor of the 2×2 matrix [[1,ρ],[ρ,1]] is L=[[1,0],[ρ,√(1−ρ²)]] — precisely Heston's construction, mixing two independent shocks into a correlated pair.
Section 12

What to carry forward

01

The vector SDE dXₜ = μ(Xₜ,t)dt + Σ(Xₜ,t)dWₜ generalizes every single-process SDE so far to n processes at once, with the same drift-plus-diffusion shape.

02

Wₜ's components are independent by convention — every bit of correlation between the processes in Xₜ is introduced entirely through the matrix Σ, never through Wₜ itself.

03

ΣΣᵀ = Cov(dXₜ)/dt is the one identity the whole topic reduces to — given Σ, the covariance is a free lookup.

04

Cholesky decomposition (ρ = LLᵀ) inverts that identity — given a target correlation, it hands back a mixing matrix L that turns independent shocks into correctly-correlated ones.

05

Heston's single scalar ρ is already this recipe's simplest case: the 2×2 Cholesky factor L=[[1,0],[ρ,√(1−ρ²)]], just never named as one.

06

The same idea scales cleanly from 2 to N correlated processes — a bigger correlation matrix, a bigger Cholesky factor, the identical mixing formula.

What this now lets you do

Take any target correlation structure — a handful of FX pairs sharing legs, Heston's price-variance pair, a whole curve of rate factors — and turn it into shocks you can actually feed into each process's SDE, with confidence the simulated correlation will converge to the one you targeted. That's the entire practical payoff of this lesson.
