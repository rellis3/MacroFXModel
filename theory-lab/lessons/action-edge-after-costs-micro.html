Does the Edge Survive Costs?

A backtest can prove an effect is real and still describe a strategy that loses money. Turn a gross edge into a net one — and get a break-even cost and a yes/no you can act on.

The whole idea
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

I've found an intraday fade on EUR/USD. Three thousand trades out of sample, and the edge is highly significant.

How big is it per trade?

Nine tenths of a pip, on average.

And what does one round trip cost you?

The spread is only four tenths of a pip.

You cross the whole spread, but that's not all. Slippage of two tenths on the way in and two tenths on the way out. Commission of five dollars a lot, which is half a pip. Add it up: one point three pips a trade.

So I make nine tenths and pay one point three.

You lose four tenths of a pip on every trade, about four hundred pips a year at a thousand trades. With great statistical confidence.

But the edge is real. The test said so.

It is real. That's the trap. Significance grows with the square root of the number of trades. The edge per trade doesn't grow at all. A huge sample can make a tiny edge look certain. Only its size, against the cost, tells you whether it pays.

So what number should I look at first?

The gross edge is your break-even cost. Here that's nine tenths of a pip. Can you trade this for less than that, every time, bad fills included? Then ask for a margin: edge at least twice the cost, and still positive if costs double.

What if I trade less often?

Often that's the fix. Keep only the strongest fifth of setups and the edge per trade can rise to three pips against the same one point three of cost. A slow USD/JPY swing makes forty pips a trade and pays three. Same cost per trip, much bigger moves.

Has this tripped up real money?

Value Line's stock rankings did well on paper for decades. Its real fund, trading the same rankings, earned far less. André Perold used that gap in nineteen eighty-eight to define implementation shortfall: the rankings weren't wrong, but the paper portfolio never paid to trade.

The one-line version?

Real is not the same as tradeable: compare the edge per trade with the full round-trip cost, and demand room to spare.

Section 1

Paper returns versus money returns

Perold (1988) for implementation shortfall; Almgren & Chriss (2000) for execution; Novy-Marx & Velikov (2016) for anomalies after costs.

How it reached trading

Backtests and model portfolios were judged on paper: buy and sell at the screen price. Real money never matched, and nobody measured the gap.

Perold named it the implementation shortfall — spread, impact, delay and unfilled trades. Almgren and Chriss turned execution into an optimisation. Novy-Marx and Velikov showed that whether a published effect survives depends on how often it must trade.

Section 2

What one round trip costs

c = spread + 2 × slippage + commission + financing
↔️

The full spread

Buy at the ask, sell at the bid: one whole spread per round trip — at the hour you trade, not the quiet quote.

🧲

Slippage, twice

On the way in and on the way out. Stops fill worse than their level.

🧾

Commission

$5 a standard lot on EUR/USD is 0.5 pips at $10 a pip.

🌙

Financing

Overnight swap. On slow trades it can be the biggest cost of all.

Your gross edge per trade is your break-even cost. Pay more than that per round trip and the strategy loses.

Section 4

Significance is not size

t = μ ÷ (σ/√n)
effect size d = μ ÷ σ

Quadruple the trades and t doubles. The edge per trade does not move. With enough trades, a microscopic edge becomes "significant at the 0.1% level".

Is it there?

3,000 trades, 0.9 pips gross, σ 15 → t = 3.29. Very probably real.

Is it worth having?

Cost 1.3 pips → net −0.4 a trade, net t = −1.46. Very probably a loser.

Section 6

The spread-to-range gate

g = spread ÷ ATR of one bar
line at 0.15

The share of a typical move the spread eats before you start. Shorter bars have smaller ATRs, so g rises as you speed up — that is why short-horizon strategies die first.

Necessary, not sufficient

Fast fade: spread 0.4 on a 4-pip ATR gives g = 0.10, inside the line — and it still loses, because commission and slippage are two-thirds of its cost.

Section 9 · It actually happened

Paper versus reality

1988 · Value Line

Value Line's top-ranked stocks did well on paper; the real fund trading the same rankings earned far less. André Perold used that gap to define implementation shortfall: spread, impact, delay and missed trades.

2016 · the anomaly zoo

Novy-Marx and Velikov put realistic costs on dozens of published stock anomalies. Slow-rebalancing ones mostly kept part of their premium; many fast ones lost most or all of it.

In both cases the effect was real in the data. What it cost to collect decided whether it was worth anything.

Section 12

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Spread 0.6, slippage 0.3 a side, commission 0.7. Cost per round trip?

0.6 + 2 × 0.3 + 0.7 = 1.9. Slippage is paid on the way in and on the way out.
Question 2

10,000 trades, 0.3 pips gross, σ = 10. Cost 1 pip. Which is true?

SE = 10/√10,000 = 0.1. Gross t = 3.0; net edge −0.7, net t = −7. Real and tradeable are different questions.
Question 3

Gross edge 2.5 pips. Most you should pay for a 2× margin?

Break-even is 2.5. A 2× margin means μ/c ≥ 2, so c ≤ 1.25.
Question 4

A strategy passes spread/ATR ≤ 0.15. What follows?

The gate ignores commission, slippage and financing, and the size of the edge. The worked example passes it and loses.
Your call
Scenario drill · an illustrative research review

A "highly significant" fade lands on your desk

A colleague brings you an intraday EUR/USD fade: 3,000 trades over three years out of sample, about 1,000 a year, average gross profit 0.9 pips, standard deviation 15 pips. The t-statistic is 3.29. All-in cost per round trip, measured honestly, is 1.3 pips. They want to start trading it on Monday. What do you say?

  1. Go ahead. A t of 3.29 means the edge is real.
    Real, yes, and smaller than its cost. At 0.9 pips gross and 1.3 pips a round trip, every trade loses 0.4 pips on average, about 400 pips a year. More trades make the loss more certain, not smaller.
  2. Not as it is. Its break-even cost is 0.9 pips, under the 1.3 it pays. Rework it to trade less on bigger moves, fix any filter in advance, and test it again on fresh data.
    This is the cost-check card: compare the edge per trade with the full round-trip cost, then look for a version with room to spare. A filter chosen after seeing the results has to be confirmed on data it was not chosen on.
  3. Trade it, but with a cheaper broker to cut commission.
    Cutting cost is the right instinct, but even free commission leaves 0.1 pips a trade, a net t near 0.37, and a loss if costs double. It is marginal at best, not a strategy.
  4. Trade it at double the size to make the small edge worth the effort.
    Size multiplies the net edge, and the net edge is negative. Bigger size also tends to mean worse fills, which raises the cost further.

The point. This drill is illustrative: it uses the lesson's worked example, not a real fund. The same pattern is documented in the real world. Perold's 1988 paper showed Value Line's real fund earning far less than its paper portfolio, and Novy-Marx and Velikov (2016) found that many high-turnover stock anomalies lost most or all of their premium to realistic trading costs.

Section 13

What to carry forward

01

Cost per round trip = full spread + slippage ×2 + commission + financing, at the hour you trade.

02

Gross edge = break-even cost. Ask for μ/c ≥ 2, net t ≥ 2, positive at double cost.

03

Significance grows with √n; the edge per trade does not. Compare edge with cost, not p with 0.05.

04

Cost drag on Sharpe is (c/σ)√N. Slower trades capture bigger moves for the same cost.

05

Desk tests: the 0.15 spread/ATR gate held up; a VWAP fade and a daily band fade found nothing tradeable — verdicts on those tests, not on mean reversion.
