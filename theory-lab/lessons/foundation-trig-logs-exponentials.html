Trigonometry, Logarithms & Exponentials

Sine, cosine, \(e^x\) and \(\ln x\) — the three functions behind cycles, continuous growth, and why log-returns are the honest way to add up performance over time.

CONCEPT — not built or tested in this repo ~16 min read Prereqs: Algebra & Functions Refresher
TL;DR
  • Sine and cosine measure the vertical and horizontal position of a point moving around the unit circle (radius 1) as its angle \(\theta\) increases — they're periodic, repeating identically every \(2\pi\) radians (360°), which is exactly why anything cyclical (seasons, intraday patterns) gets modeled with them.
  • Radians measure angle by arc length instead of degrees — \(2\pi\) radians is a full circle, and radians are what calculus-based formulas use natively, so degrees need converting first.
  • \(e^x\) (the natural exponential) and \(\ln x\) (its inverse, the natural logarithm) undo each other exactly: \(\ln(e^x)=x\) and \(e^{\ln x}=x\). The log rules — \(\ln(ab)=\ln a+\ln b\), \(\ln(a/b)=\ln a - \ln b\), \(\ln(a^n)=n\ln a\) — turn multiplication into addition.
  • Continuous compounding, \(e^{rt}\), is the limit of compounding more and more frequently (yearly → monthly → daily → every instant) — derived directly from the definition of \(e\) as that limit.
  • Log-returns are additive across time — sum daily log-returns and you get the total log-return over the whole period, exactly, with no compounding correction needed. Simple (percentage) returns don't have this property; they compound multiplicatively instead.
  • The worked example converts a 5% annual simple rate to its continuously-compounded equivalent (≈4.879%), and sums four daily log-returns to confirm they equal the 4-day total log-return exactly.
Section 01

Why this matters

Two unrelated-looking topics live in this lesson because they show up everywhere else in this curriculum for the same underlying reason: both describe things that grow or repeat in a structured, mathematically precise way. Sine and cosine describe anything that cycles — and cyclical behavior (seasonality, intraday rhythms, mean-reverting oscillation) is modeled with exactly these functions later in this curriculum's signal processing material. \(e^x\) and \(\ln x\) describe anything that compounds continuously — and almost every pricing model, growth-rate calculation, and returns series in finance is built on the natural exponential and its inverse. Get comfortable with both here, in their plainest form, and the notation stops being a barrier everywhere else.

Plain-English intuition

A point going around a circle, and money growing every instant instead of once a year

Imagine a point starting at the 3 o'clock position on a circle of radius 1, then walking counterclockwise around the rim. At any moment, that point has a height above the center and a horizontal distance from the center. Sine is that height; cosine is that horizontal distance. As the point keeps walking around and around, both values rise and fall in a smooth, repeating wave — and because the circle is the same shape every time around, the wave repeats exactly, forever. That's periodicity: whatever the sine and cosine values were one full lap ago, they're identical again now.

\(e^x\) and \(\ln x\) come from a completely different question: what happens to compound interest if, instead of compounding once a year, you compound twice a year, then monthly, then daily, then every single instant, with no gaps at all? Each time you compound more often, you earn a little more (interest on interest, more often) — but the gains from compounding more frequently get smaller and smaller each time, and the whole thing settles down to a specific, finite number as the compounding frequency goes to infinity. That settled-down number is \(e\) (approximately 2.71828), and \(e^{rt}\) is the formula for "growing at rate \(r\) continuously, with no gaps, for time \(t\)." The natural logarithm \(\ln x\) is simply the question run backwards: "what continuous growth rate, over what time, would it take to reach \(x\) starting from 1?"

Section 02

The math, step by step

The unit circle, sine, and cosine

Draw a circle of radius 1 centered at the origin — the unit circle. Pick an angle \(\theta\), measured counterclockwise from the positive horizontal axis, and walk that far around the circle's edge. The point you land on has coordinates \((\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\) — that's the definition, full stop. Cosine is the horizontal (\(x\)) coordinate; sine is the vertical (\(y\)) coordinate.

\[ (\text{point on unit circle at angle } \theta) = (\cos\theta,\ \sin\theta) \]
θ(theta) the angle swept counterclockwise from the 3 o'clock position
cos θthe horizontal coordinate of the point at that angle
sin θthe vertical coordinate of the point at that angle

Tangent is defined as the ratio \(\tan\theta = \sin\theta/\cos\theta\) — geometrically, the slope of the line from the origin through that point. Because it's a ratio, tangent is undefined wherever \(\cos\theta = 0\) (dividing by zero) — exactly the points where the line through the origin is perfectly vertical, at \(\theta = 90°\) and \(270°\).

\[ \tan\theta = \frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta} \]
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine walking around the rim of a merry-go-round that's exactly 1 step across the middle. At every spot you stand, sine tells you how high up you are, and cosine tells you how far sideways you are from the center. Walk all the way around and you land back where you started — and it works exactly the same way every single lap. Tangent is just sine divided by cosine — it tells you how tilted the line from the middle out to you would be.

Radians vs. degrees

Degrees split a full circle into 360 arbitrary pieces (a convention inherited from ancient Babylonian astronomy). Radians instead measure angle by the actual arc length traveled around a unit-radius circle — an angle of 1 radian sweeps out an arc exactly 1 unit long. Since the full circumference of a unit circle is \(2\pi\) (from \(C = 2\pi r\) with \(r=1\)), a full circle is exactly \(2\pi\) radians.

\[ 2\pi \text{ radians} = 360° \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \text{radians} = \text{degrees} \times \frac{\pi}{180} \]
π(pi) ≈ 3.14159, the ratio of a circle's circumference to its diameter
2π radone full trip around the circle — 360°

Radians aren't just an alternate convention chosen for style — every calculus-based trig formula elsewhere in this curriculum (derivatives of \(\sin\) and \(\cos\), the Taylor series behind Euler's formula in the next lesson) is only clean and correct when the angle is in radians. Plugging degrees into those formulas silently gives a wrong answer, so the habit of converting first is worth building now.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Degrees chop a circle into 360 make-believe slices, like cutting a pizza into a lot of skinny pieces for no special reason. Radians measure the circle a more honest way — by how far you actually walked along its edge. Walking all the way around a circle is always exactly \(2\pi\) radians, no matter how big or small the circle is.

Periodicity — why cycles use sine and cosine

Because walking one full \(2\pi\) radians around the circle returns you to exactly the same point, sine and cosine repeat their values identically every \(2\pi\) radians:

\[ \sin(\theta + 2\pi) = \sin\theta \qquad \cos(\theta + 2\pi) = \cos\theta \]
Foreshadowing: this exact repeating property — a function that returns to the same value after a fixed interval — is why cycle and seasonality analysis later in this curriculum is built out of sine and cosine waves of different frequencies added together. A repeating pattern in data (a weekly rhythm, a seasonal effect) is modeled as a wave with a matching period; decomposing a messy series into a sum of such waves is the whole idea behind spectral/Fourier-style analysis, covered properly in a later Signal Processing lesson.

The natural exponential, \(e^x\)

The number \(e \approx 2.71828\) is defined as the limit of compounding 100% growth over more and more sub-periods within one unit of time:

\[ e = \lim_{n\to\infty}\left(1+\frac{1}{n}\right)^n \approx 2.71828\ldots \]
nthe number of compounding sub-periods — the limit lets this grow without bound
ethe settled-down value the expression approaches — irrational, never terminates or repeats

\(e^x\) is the function "raise \(e\) to the power \(x\)" — the natural exponential. Its defining property, and the whole reason it's "natural" rather than an arbitrary choice of base, is that its own rate of change at any point equals its own value at that point (a calculus fact, not needed to use the function here, but the reason it appears constantly in growth models). For this lesson, treat \(e^x\) simply as continuous, unbounded growth: it's always positive, always increasing, and grows faster and faster as \(x\) increases.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine a piggy bank that grows a tiny bit bigger every single second, nonstop, instead of getting a lump of allowance once a week. \(e^x\) is the number you'd end up with after growing that way for a while — always getting bigger, never stopping, and never once dropping below zero.

The natural logarithm, \(\ln x\), and the log rules

The natural logarithm \(\ln x\) is defined as the inverse of \(e^x\) — exactly the "swap and rearrange" construction from the previous lesson, applied to this specific function:

\[ \ln(e^x) = x \qquad\qquad e^{\ln x} = x \quad (x>0) \]
ln x"what power must \(e\) be raised to, to get \(x\)?"
x > 0\(\ln x\) is only defined for positive \(x\) — \(e^{\text{anything}}\) is never zero or negative

Three log rules follow directly from the corresponding rules for exponents, and they matter enough to memorize — they're what turns multiplicative problems into additive ones throughout this curriculum:

ln(ab) = ln a + ln b
Product rule
Log of a product is the sum of the logs
ln(a/b) = ln a − ln b
Quotient rule
Log of a ratio is the difference of the logs
ln(aⁿ) = n·ln a
Power rule
An exponent inside the log becomes a multiplier outside it

These follow because \(\ln\) undoes \(e^x\), and exponents themselves obey \(e^a \cdot e^b = e^{a+b}\) — multiplying two exponentials adds their exponents. Taking \(\ln\) of both sides of that fact is exactly the product rule above.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

If \(e^x\) means "how big something gets after growing for x amount of time," then \(\ln x\) asks the same question backwards: "how long did this take to grow this big?" The log rules are just a shortcut — instead of multiplying two growing things together, you can add up how long each one grew.

Continuous compounding — deriving \(e^{rt}\)

Compounding a rate \(r\) per year, \(m\) times per year, for \(t\) years, gives the standard compound-growth formula:

\[ \left(1+\frac{r}{m}\right)^{mt} \]

As the compounding frequency \(m\) increases without bound — compounding monthly, then daily, then every second, then continuously — this expression approaches a limit. Substitute \(n = m/r\) (so \(m = nr\)) and the exponent becomes \(mt = nrt\):

\[ \left(1+\frac{r}{m}\right)^{mt} = \left[\left(1+\frac{1}{n}\right)^{n}\right]^{rt} \xrightarrow{\ m\to\infty\ (n\to\infty)\ } e^{rt} \]
mcompounding frequency per year — the thing being pushed to infinity
[(1+1/n)ⁿ]exactly the limit-definition of \(e\) from above, raised to the power \(rt\)
e^{rt}the continuously-compounded growth factor — the limit of ever-more-frequent compounding

This is not a coincidence or a separate formula to memorize — it's literally the definition of \(e\) from a few paragraphs above, appearing inside the ordinary compound-interest formula once the compounding frequency is pushed to infinity. \(e^{rt}\) is what "growing continuously at rate \(r\) for time \(t\), with compounding happening every infinitesimal instant rather than at discrete intervals" actually means, spelled out.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine a piggy bank that adds a tiny bit of extra money every single instant, with absolutely no breaks — not once a year, not once a day, but constantly. \(e^{rt}\) is exactly how much money you'd end up with if that piggy bank never, ever stopped adding.

Log-returns vs. simple returns, and why log-returns add

A simple return over one period is the familiar percentage change: \(R = (P_1 - P_0)/P_0\), equivalently \(P_1/P_0 = 1+R\). A log-return is defined instead as the natural log of the price ratio:

\[ r = \ln\left(\frac{P_1}{P_0}\right) \]
rthe log-return over one period
P₀, P₁the price at the start and end of the period

Chain two periods together — price moves from \(P_0\) to \(P_1\) to \(P_2\). The total log-return over both periods is \(\ln(P_2/P_0)\). Rewrite the ratio inside the log as a product of the two one-period ratios, then apply the product rule from above:

\[ \ln\!\left(\frac{P_2}{P_0}\right) = \ln\!\left(\frac{P_2}{P_1}\cdot\frac{P_1}{P_0}\right) = \ln\!\left(\frac{P_2}{P_1}\right) + \ln\!\left(\frac{P_1}{P_0}\right) = r_2 + r_1 \]
P₂/P₀ = (P₂/P₁)(P₁/P₀)the total price ratio is the product of the two step ratios — plain algebra
r₁ + r₂the log product rule turns that product straight into a sum of log-returns

That's the whole reason log-returns are additive: the log rules convert multiplication of price ratios (how compounding actually works) directly into addition of returns. Simple returns don't have this property — compounding two simple returns is \((1+R_1)(1+R_2)-1\), which is not \(R_1+R_2\) except as a rough approximation for very small returns. The returns primer elsewhere in this curriculum builds an entire argument about the gap between arithmetic and geometric compounding on exactly this distinction — this lesson is the algebraic reason that distinction exists in the first place.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Say you grow a plant for two weeks. If you use regular percentages, you can't just add week 1's growth and week 2's growth to get the right two-week total — it doesn't work that way once things are compounding. But if you use log-returns instead, you really can just add them up, like stacking two Lego bricks, and you get exactly the right total every time.

Worked example

Converting an annual rate to continuous compounding, and checking log-return additivity

Part 1 — 5% simple annual rate to its continuously-compounded equivalent. Find the continuous rate \(r_c\) that grows $1 by the same factor in one year as a 5% simple annual rate does. Simple annual growth factor: \(1+0.05=1.05\). Set the continuous formula equal to it and solve for \(r_c\):

\[ e^{r_c \cdot 1} = 1.05 \quad\Rightarrow\quad r_c = \ln(1.05) \]

Computing \(\ln(1.05)\) (e.g. via a calculator, or the series \(\ln(1+x)\approx x - x^2/2 + x^3/3-\dots\) for small \(x\)):

\[ r_c = \ln(1.05) \approx 0.04879 = 4.879\% \]

Check: \(e^{0.04879} \approx 1.05000\) — matches. A 4.879% continuously compounded rate grows money exactly as fast over one year as a 5% simple annual rate — continuous compounding always needs a slightly smaller quoted rate to match a given simple rate, because it's compounding infinitely often rather than once.

Part 2 — log-returns over a 4-day price series sum to the total log-return. Suppose a price series over 4 days is: \(P_0=100\), \(P_1=102\), \(P_2=101\), \(P_3=104\), \(P_4=103\). Compute each day's log-return:

\[ r_1=\ln\!\frac{102}{100}=0.019803,\quad r_2=\ln\!\frac{101}{102}=-0.009852,\quad r_3=\ln\!\frac{104}{101}=0.029266,\quad r_4=\ln\!\frac{103}{104}=-0.009662 \]

Sum the four daily log-returns:

\[ r_1+r_2+r_3+r_4 = 0.019803 - 0.009852 + 0.029266 - 0.009662 = 0.029555 \]

Now compute the total log-return directly from the start and end prices:

\[ \ln\!\left(\frac{P_4}{P_0}\right) = \ln\!\left(\frac{103}{100}\right) = \ln(1.03) \approx 0.029559 \]

The two match (0.029555 vs. 0.029559 — the tiny gap is only rounding in the intermediate steps). Summing four daily log-returns gives exactly the whole period's log-return, with no separate compounding calculation needed — precisely the additivity property derived above.

Real-world trading scenario

Aggregating a month of daily performance without a compounding error

A trader wants to report a month's performance from 21 daily returns. Two colleagues do it two different ways. Colleague A sums the 21 simple daily percentage returns and reports that total as "the month's return." Colleague B converts each day to a log-return, sums those 21 numbers, then converts the sum back with \(e^{(\cdot)}-1\) to get a simple return for the month.

Colleague A's number is wrong, and increasingly wrong the more volatile the month was — summing simple returns silently ignores that gains and losses compound multiplicatively, not additively (this is exactly the "+50% then −50% is a real loss, not a wash" trap covered in the returns primer elsewhere in this curriculum). Colleague B's number is exact, because log-returns really do sum across time, as derived above — the only extra step is converting the summed log-return back into a simple percentage at the very end, using \(e^{(\cdot)}-1\), the inverse of how log-returns were defined in the first place.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

Quant desks default to log-returns for almost every statistical calculation on a return series — computing volatility, correlating two instruments, running a regression — specifically because of the additivity property derived above: it lets multi-period statements (a week's return, a month's return) be built by simple addition instead of a compounding correction, and it also makes returns symmetric in a way simple returns aren't (a log-return of \(+x\) and a log-return of \(-x\) are exact opposites in a way \(+x\%\) and \(-x\%\) simple returns are not, once you check what price they actually imply). Continuous compounding, \(e^{rt}\), is likewise the default convention inside most pricing models — a bond's discount factor, an option's risk-free growth term, any "grows at rate \(r\)" assumption in a continuous-time model — because continuous-time mathematics (calculus, differential equations) is far cleaner to work with than a formula that has to specify a compounding frequency.

Sine and cosine waves are the standard building block for modeling any known-period cycle in a time series — a seasonal effect, an intraday volatility pattern, a repeating cross-asset relationship — by fitting a wave of the matching period (or a sum of several waves of different periods, letting each capture a different cycle length) and checking how much of the data's variation that wave actually explains. That decomposition, applied systematically across many frequencies at once, is exactly what the Fourier Transform does — the tool a later Signal Processing lesson in this curriculum covers properly, building directly on the sine/cosine vocabulary from this page.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where this goes wrong in practice

Feeding degrees into a formula that expects radians. Most calculus-based trig formulas, and most programming languages' built-in \(\sin\)/\(\cos\) functions, assume the input is in radians. Passing in a raw degree value silently produces a completely wrong number with no error raised — always convert first.

Summing simple returns instead of log-returns. As shown in the scenario above, adding raw percentage returns across multiple periods systematically misstates the true compounded outcome, and the error grows with volatility — a calm series barely notices, a volatile one is noticeably wrong.

Forgetting that \(\ln x\) is undefined for \(x\le0\). A log-return requires a positive price ratio; if a series ever contains a zero or negative "price" (common with spreads, or with data quality issues), \(\ln\) of that value is undefined or produces \(-\infty\), and silently propagating a resulting \(\text{NaN}\) or infinity through a downstream calculation is a real, common bug.

Treating a converted continuous rate as "the same number" as the simple rate. A continuously-compounded rate is always slightly smaller than the equivalent simple annual rate (as the worked example showed, 4.879% vs. 5%) — quoting one convention's number as if it were the other, without converting, understates or overstates the true growth.

Section 04

Key Takeaways

✓Sine and cosine are coordinates on the unit circle. \((\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\) is the point at angle \(\theta\) — periodic every \(2\pi\) radians, which is exactly why cyclical data gets modeled with them.
✓Radians, not degrees, are the native unit for calculus-based formulas. Convert before plugging an angle into anything beyond basic geometry.
✓\(e^x\) and \(\ln x\) are exact inverses. The log rules (product → sum, quotient → difference, power → multiply) are the reason logs turn multiplicative problems into additive ones.
✓\(e^{rt}\) is the limit of compounding infinitely often. It falls straight out of the definition of \(e\) itself — not a separate formula to memorize.
✓Log-returns are additive across time; simple returns are not. Sum log-returns for an exact multi-period total; summing simple returns is only a rough approximation, worse the more volatile the series.
⚠A continuously-compounded rate is always a bit smaller than the equivalent simple rate. 5% simple ≈ 4.879% continuous over one year — the two conventions are not interchangeable numbers.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. Convert 90° to radians, and state what point on the unit circle that angle corresponds to.
\(90° \times \pi/180 = \pi/2\) radians. At \(\theta = \pi/2\), the point on the unit circle is \((\cos(\pi/2), \sin(\pi/2)) = (0, 1)\) — straight up from the center, a quarter of the way around the circle from the starting 3 o'clock position.
Q2. Why does \(\ln(ab) = \ln a + \ln b\)? Derive it from the fact that \(e^a \cdot e^b = e^{a+b}\).
Let \(a = \ln x\) and \(b = \ln y\), so \(x = e^a\) and \(y=e^b\). Then \(xy = e^a \cdot e^b = e^{a+b}\) (exponent rule). Taking \(\ln\) of both sides: \(\ln(xy) = a+b = \ln x + \ln y\). The log product rule is just the exponent-addition rule, viewed through the inverse relationship between \(e^x\) and \(\ln x\).
Q3. A rate of 6% is compounded continuously for 2 years on $500. What's the ending value?
\(FV = 500 \times e^{0.06\times2} = 500\times e^{0.12} \approx 500 \times 1.1275 \approx \$563.75\). This uses \(e^{rt}\) directly, with \(r=0.06\) and \(t=2\).
Q4. A price moves from 50 to 55 in one period, then from 55 to 55 in the next (no change). What is the total log-return over both periods, computed two ways?
Way 1 (directly): \(\ln(55/50) = \ln(1.10) \approx 0.09531\). Way 2 (summing daily log-returns): period 1 is \(\ln(55/50)\approx0.09531\); period 2 is \(\ln(55/55)=\ln(1)=0\). Sum: \(0.09531+0=0.09531\) — matches exactly, confirming additivity even when one period has zero return.
Q5. Why is a degree-based angle dangerous to plug directly into a formula like \(\sin\theta\) inside a programming language or calculus formula?
Most calculus identities and most programming languages' built-in trigonometric functions assume the input angle is already in radians, not degrees. Plugging in "90" meaning 90 degrees, when the function expects radians, silently computes \(\sin(90\text{ radians})\) instead of \(\sin(90°)\) — a completely different, essentially arbitrary number (90 radians is many full trips around the circle past \(90°\)), with no error raised to flag the mistake. Always convert degrees to radians first: multiply by \(\pi/180\).
