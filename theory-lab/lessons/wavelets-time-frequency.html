Wavelets & Time-Frequency Analysis

The Fourier transform can tell you which cycles a series contains — but not when they happened. Wavelets trade a single global spectrum for a picture of frequency content changing over time, which is exactly what a non-stationary series needs.

CONCEPT — a classical signal-processing tool, explained here as background knowledge, not built or tested anywhere in this repo ~15 min read Prereqs: Fourier Analysis & the Frequency Domain, Stationarity, ACF & PACF
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • A plain Fourier transform answers "which frequencies are present across the whole series" but throws away all information about when — two series with an identical set of frequencies but in a different order over time can produce the same periodogram.
  • The continuous wavelet transform \(W(a,b) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{a}}\int x(t)\,\psi^*\!\left(\frac{t-b}{a}\right)dt\) fixes this by using a localized, scalable "mother wavelet" \(\psi(t)\) instead of an infinite sine wave — \(a\) (scale, roughly inverse frequency) and \(b\) (time-shift) together produce a time-frequency map called a scalogram.
  • This matters most exactly where stationarity (see the prerequisite lesson) breaks down: a regime where the dominant cycle length itself changes over time is precisely what a scalogram shows and a single periodogram cannot, because a periodogram implicitly averages frequency content over the entire sample.
  • A worked example below hand-computes a one-level Haar wavelet transform on an 8-point series with a single embedded shock, showing the transform pinpoint exactly when the shock occurred — something no Fourier-domain view of the same 8 points would localize in time.
  • Common named wavelet families — Morlet (smooth, good frequency resolution) and Haar (simple, good at localizing sharp jumps) — trade off time resolution against frequency resolution differently; neither is "the" correct choice, and picking one is itself a modeling decision.
  • Explaining wavelets is not a claim that a scalogram produces trading edge — it is background for reading a time-frequency plot correctly, and for knowing when it is the right tool over a plain periodogram.
  • How it reached trading: economists took wavelets from 1980s signal processing in the 1990s (Ramsey & Lampart, 1998) and finance followed (Gençay, Selçuk & Whitcher, 2002) to measure how correlations and betas differ by horizon and shift over time.
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

The Fourier lesson said a periodogram tells me which cycles are in a series. What's missing?

When. A periodogram is one picture for the whole sample. It's like a list of every note in a song, with how loud each one is. Useful, but you can't tell the verse from the chorus.

And wavelets are the sheet music.

Closer to it. Instead of a sine wave that hums forever, you use a short ripple that switches itself off. You slide it along the series to ask, is a pattern like this here? And you stretch or squeeze it to ask about slow patterns and fast ones.

So two knobs.

Yes. Where you are in time, and how stretched the ripple is, which plays the role of frequency. Do every combination and you get a map, time along one side and scale along the other. That map is called a scalogram.

Show me it catching something.

Eight daily returns, all tiny, except one jump of three percent in the middle. Pair the days up. For each pair, take half the difference. Three of the pairs give a tenth or less. The pair with the jump gives one and a half. More than ten times the others, and it sits exactly where the shock was.

And a periodogram of the same eight days?

A sudden jump contains a little of every frequency, so its energy gets smeared across the whole spectrum. It can tell you something happened. It can't tell you when.

Which wavelet should I use?

There's a trade-off you can't escape. Sharp timing costs you frequency precision, and the reverse. The Haar wavelet, a simple up-then-down step, is great at pinning down jumps. The Morlet, a smooth wiggle, is better at telling nearby cycles apart, so it's the usual choice for smoother financial cycles.

Where has this been used in markets?

Economists picked it up in the nineteen-nineties. Ramsey and Lampart in 1998 split money and income relationships by time horizon and found they differ a lot between horizons. Finance followed, measuring how correlations and betas change with the horizon you look at.

Any traps?

Two big ones. Near the start and end of the sample the ripple hangs off the edge of the data, so readings there are unreliable. And a scalogram has so many cells that some will light up by chance. The one-line version: wavelets tell you which cycles and roughly when, but a bright patch is a hint, not a signal.

Section 01

Why this matters

The previous lesson built the Fourier transform and its practical estimator, the periodogram — and closed with an honest limitation that goes beyond the statistical caveat covered there: a periodogram is computed once, over the entire sample, and reports one number per frequency describing the whole series. It cannot say "this cycle was present in the first half of the sample and gone by the second half," because by construction it mixes every time point together before ever looking at frequency content. That is a serious blind spot for financial time series, which are rarely stationary in the way Stationarity, ACF & PACF defines it — volatility regimes shift, a dominant cycle length can lengthen or shorten, a market structure change (a new venue, a new tick size, a central bank regime shift) can alter a series' periodic behavior partway through the sample. Wavelets exist specifically to answer "which frequencies, and roughly when" instead of just "which frequencies, averaged over everything." That is the single sentence this entire lesson exists to unpack.

💡
Plain-English intuition

A sheet-music score vs. a list of every note in the song

Imagine being handed two descriptions of the same three-minute song. The first is a Fourier-style description: a list of every note that appears anywhere in the song, and how loud each one is on average — "this song contains a C, an E, a G, mostly loud, and an F#, mostly quiet." That's genuinely useful information, but it tells you nothing about the song's actual structure — you can't tell the verse from the chorus, or which note opens the bridge. The second description is sheet music: the same notes, but now placed in time, showing exactly when each one plays and for how long. A wavelet-based, time-frequency analysis of a signal is much closer to that second description — instead of one global list of "which frequencies exist," it produces a picture with time along one axis and frequency (or its inverse, scale) along the other, showing where in the series each frequency component actually shows up. The tool trades away some of the Fourier transform's very precise frequency resolution to get that timing information back — there is no free lunch here, only a different, often more useful, trade-off.

How it reached trading

The same two markets, related differently at each horizon

Wavelets · Ramsey & Lampart, 1998; Gençay, Selçuk & Whitcher, 2002

A day trader and a pension allocator can look at the same two series and see different relationships, yet a single correlation or beta, or a Fourier spectrum over the whole sample, averages every horizon and every period together. Developed in 1980s signal processing, wavelets reached economics in the 1990s as a way to split a relationship by timescale.

James Ramsey and Camille Lampart (1998) decomposed the money–income and consumption–income relationships by timescale and found they differ markedly across horizons. Gençay, Selçuk & Whitcher's 2002 book brought the toolkit to finance, and their later work estimated betas scale by scale, finding systematic risk that depends on the horizon. Wavelet coherence became a common way to show co-movement between markets switching on and off across both time and frequency.

Section 02

The math, step by step

Watch the formula build
Where the continuous wavelet transform comes from, one term at a time
\[ x(t)\,\color{#fbbf24}{\psi^*(t)} \]

Start like Fourier: multiply the series by a test shape. But instead of an endless sine wave, use a mother wavelet \(\psi\), a short ripple that dies away to zero.

\[ x(t)\,\psi^*\!\left(t \color{#fbbf24}{- b}\right) \]

Shift it. \(b\) slides the ripple along the series, so the answer now depends on where you look.

\[ x(t)\,\psi^*\!\left(\frac{t-b}{\color{#fbbf24}{a}}\right) \]

Stretch it. A large scale \(a\) makes a slow ripple that matches long cycles; a small \(a\) makes a fast one that matches short cycles. Scale plays the role of inverse frequency.

\[ \color{#fbbf24}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}} x(t)\,\psi^*\!\left(\frac{t-b}{a}\right) \color{#fbbf24}{dt} \]

Add up the products, as the Fourier transform does. A big total means the series looks like that ripple, at that size, at that place.

\[ \color{#10b981}{W(a,b) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{a}} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t)\,\psi^*\!\left(\frac{t-b}{a}\right) dt} \]

Scale by \(1/\sqrt{a}\) so every size is compared fairly. Plotting \(|W(a,b)|^2\) over time and scale gives the scalogram. In the Haar worked example the same idea gives \(d_3 = (3.00 - (-0.05))/2 = 1.525\), right where the shock is.

Three pieces: (1) the core idea of a localized, scalable wavelet in place of an infinite sine wave; (2) the continuous wavelet transform itself and what its two parameters mean; (3) the resolution trade-off this all rests on, and the two named wavelet families worth knowing conceptually.

1. From an infinite sine wave to a localized "mother wavelet"

The complex sinusoid \(e^{-i2\pi ft}\) at the heart of the Fourier transform (previous lesson) is defined for all time — it never turns on or off, never localizes to a particular stretch of the series. A wavelet \(\psi(t)\) is deliberately built the opposite way: a small, oscillating waveform that is concentrated in a short span of time and decays to (near) zero outside it — hence "wave" (it oscillates) plus "-let" (it's a small, localized piece, not an infinite sine wave). Call \(\psi(t)\) the mother wavelet. The trick that makes it a genuine time-frequency tool, not just a time-localized filter, is that the mother wavelet gets both shifted in time and stretched or compressed in scale to produce an entire family of related wavelets, each one a scaled, shifted copy of the same basic shape.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

A plain sine wave never stops — it goes on forever in both directions, humming the same note eternally. A wavelet is more like a single short "boing" — a small ripple that rises and falls and then dies back down to nothing. Because it's short and localized, you can slide it along a signal and stretch or squeeze it, checking "does a ripple like this, at this size, show up right around here?" — a question a never-ending sine wave simply can't ask.

2. The continuous wavelet transform

\[ W(a,b) \;=\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{a}} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t)\, \psi^*\!\left(\frac{t-b}{a}\right) dt \]

ψ*((t−b)/a)the mother wavelet, shifted by b and stretched by scale a (complex conjugated)
1/√anormalizes energy so wavelets at every scale are compared fairly
W(a,b)how strongly that particular scale/location pattern matches the series

Reading the two parameters is the whole point: \(b\) is a time shift — it slides the wavelet along the series, the same way \(f\) indexed frequency for the Fourier transform, except here it indexes where in time the wavelet is centered. \(a\) is a scale — stretching the mother wavelet (large \(a\)) makes it oscillate slowly and match long, low-frequency patterns; compressing it (small \(a\)) makes it oscillate quickly and match short, high-frequency patterns. Scale is the wavelet world's stand-in for frequency, and the two are inversely related: large scale roughly corresponds to low frequency, small scale to high frequency. \(W(a,b)\) itself is computed exactly the way \(X(f)\) was in the previous lesson — slide the (now scaled) wavelet across the series, multiply pointwise, and integrate — except now the answer depends on two things, scale and location, rather than frequency alone. Squaring the result, \(|W(a,b)|^2\), and plotting it as a 2-D image with time (\(b\)) on one axis and scale (\(a\), often flipped so frequency reads top-to-bottom) on the other produces the scalogram — the direct visual analogue of the periodogram, except now it shows how the frequency content itself evolves over the sample instead of collapsing it into one static plot.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Think of scale as "how zoomed out you are" — a big, stretched-out wavelet catches slow, sweeping patterns, while a small, squeezed one catches fast, jittery ones. Time-shift is just "where along the series you're currently looking." Sliding a wavelet of a chosen size across the whole series and recording how well it matches at every point produces a map — the scalogram — showing which sizes of pattern showed up, and exactly when.

3. The resolution trade-off, and two named wavelet families

Nothing here escapes a basic trade-off closely related to the Heisenberg uncertainty principle in physics: a wavelet that is very narrow in time (good at pinpointing exactly when something happened) is necessarily spread out across many frequencies (poor at saying exactly which frequency), and vice versa — a wavelet stretched out to resolve a very precise, narrow frequency band is necessarily spread out over a long stretch of time. A pure sine wave (the Fourier basis) sits at one extreme of this trade-off: perfect frequency resolution, zero time resolution. Wavelets sit somewhere in between, and different mother wavelet choices sit at different points along that trade-off:

  • The Haar wavelet — the simplest and historically the first (Alfréd Haar, 1909) — is built from flat, rectangular steps: +1 over the first half of its span, −1 over the second half, zero everywhere else. It is extremely good at localizing sharp discontinuities and jumps precisely in time (its abrupt, step-like shape is a natural match for an abrupt, step-like feature in the data), at the cost of poor frequency resolution — it doesn't distinguish nearby frequencies well.
  • The Morlet wavelet — a complex sinusoid multiplied by a smooth Gaussian envelope — looks much more like a short burst of a genuine oscillation, tapering smoothly to zero at both ends rather than cutting off sharply. It gives much better frequency resolution than Haar (it can distinguish nearby frequencies more cleanly) at some cost to how sharply it can pin down an abrupt, instantaneous event in time. This is the far more common choice in economic and financial applications of wavelet analysis, precisely because financial cycles (a business cycle, a volatility regime) are smoother, more oscillatory phenomena than sharp, instantaneous jumps.

Neither family is "the correct one" in the abstract — choosing a mother wavelet is itself a modeling decision that trades time resolution against frequency resolution in a way suited (or not) to the specific feature being hunted for, exactly the kind of honest, stated trade-off this lesson's pitfalls section returns to below.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

You can't have a wavelet that's both perfectly good at saying "exactly this instant" and perfectly good at saying "exactly this frequency" — pinning down one blurs the other, the same trade-off behind the Heisenberg uncertainty principle in physics. A wavelet shaped like a sharp step (Haar) is great at catching the precise moment something jumps; a wavelet shaped like a smooth, tapering wiggle (Morlet) is great at telling nearby frequencies apart. Picking one is picking which kind of blurriness you're willing to accept.

The one-sentence version: Fourier asks "which frequencies, averaged over the whole series" and answers with perfect frequency precision and zero timing information; wavelets ask "which frequencies, and roughly when" and trade away some of that frequency precision to get timing back — there is no version of this tool that gives you both for free.

Worked example

Localizing a single shock with a one-level Haar transform, by hand

Take 8 toy daily returns (in percent), mostly small, with one large shock in the middle representing a surprise news event:

x = [0.10, −0.10, 0.05, −0.05, 3.00, −0.05, 0.05, −0.10]

A one-level discrete Haar transform pairs up consecutive points and, for each pair, computes an average (the smooth, "approximation" part) and a half-difference (the "detail" part — how much the pair disagrees with its own average). Using the simple average/difference form for clarity (a fully orthonormal Haar transform scales by \(1/\sqrt2\) instead of \(1/2\); the localization pattern below is identical either way):

\[ a_i = \frac{x_{2i} + x_{2i+1}}{2}, \qquad d_i = \frac{x_{2i} - x_{2i+1}}{2} \]

Applying this to the four pairs:

pair 1 (0.10, −0.10): a₁ = 0.000, d₁ = 0.100
pair 2 (0.05, −0.05): a₂ = 0.000, d₂ = 0.050
pair 3 (3.00, −0.05): a₃ = 1.475, d₃ = 1.525
pair 4 (0.05, −0.10): a₄ = −0.025, d₄ = 0.075

The detail coefficients are \([0.100,\ 0.050,\ 1.525,\ 0.075]\) — one of them, \(d_3\), is more than ten times larger than any of the others, and it corresponds exactly to pair 3, i.e. time steps 4 and 5, exactly where the shock sits in the original series. That's the payoff this lesson is built around: the transform doesn't just say "there is unusually large energy somewhere in this series" the way a periodogram computed on the same 8 points would (which would spread that energy across many frequency bins, since a sudden jump is broadband — it contains a little of every frequency at once, per the previous lesson's pitfalls) — it says where, down to which pair of time steps. That time-localization is exactly what a plain Fourier transform of the same 8 numbers cannot deliver.

🎯
Real-world trading scenario

Watching EURUSD 5-minute volatility around an ECB press conference

A researcher wants to characterize how EURUSD's intraday volatility cycle behaves around a scheduled ECB press conference — does the market's dominant oscillation length shorten (faster, choppier back-and-forth) in the minutes immediately following the announcement, and if so, for how long before it reverts to its pre-event character? A single periodogram computed over the whole session would report whichever cycle lengths were present somewhere that day, with no way to say whether the shorter cycle was active for the whole session or concentrated in a 20-minute window right after the release. A scalogram of the same 5-minute return series makes that distinction directly visible, showing scale (cycle length) against time-of-day, and would show the shortening (if it exists) as a visible narrow band right at the announcement time, fading back to the pre-event pattern afterward. That is a genuinely useful descriptive picture of market behavior around a known event — turning "the scalogram shows a pattern around news events" into an actual tradeable rule would still require the same honest out-of-sample validation bar as any other signal in this curriculum; the scalogram itself only describes what happened, it doesn't license a trade.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

Wavelet analysis has real, long-standing use outside finance — geophysics (its original driving application, analyzing seismic signals, is where the Morlet wavelet itself was first developed by Jean Morlet in the early 1980s working on oil-exploration seismograms), medical signal processing (EEG, ECG), and image compression (the Haar and related wavelets underpin parts of the JPEG2000 standard). In economics and finance, wavelet methods have been applied to business-cycle analysis (decomposing a macro series into short-run and long-run cyclical components without assuming, the way the next lesson's filters do, that the cycle length is fixed), to studying how the relationship between two markets' return co-movement changes across different time horizons simultaneously, and to volatility research looking for time-varying periodicity of the kind the ECB scenario above describes. As with the previous lesson, none of this amounts to a trading signal by itself — it is a descriptive, exploratory lens for understanding how a series' cyclical behavior evolves, which is a genuinely different (and often prior) question to whether that behavior can be turned into an edge.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where a scalogram misleads

  • Edge effects — the "cone of influence." Near the very start and end of a finite sample, a wavelet centered there is partly hanging off the edge of the available data, so the transform's estimate at those points is contaminated by whatever padding or edge-handling assumption was used, not a genuine feature of the series. Any scalogram reading inside this "cone of influence" near the two edges should be trusted far less than a reading safely in the middle of the sample.
  • Mother wavelet choice is a real, consequential decision, not a neutral default. Per the Haar-vs-Morlet trade-off above, a wavelet good at localizing sharp jumps is not the same one that's good at resolving nearby frequencies cleanly — the choice should match the feature actually being hunted for, and a different choice can produce a visibly different-looking scalogram from identical data.
  • Over-interpreting scalogram "hot spots" as confirmed cycles. A scalogram has many time-scale cells, and scanning across all of them for a bright patch is structurally the same multiple-comparisons problem this Theory Lab's Multiple Testing & the Look-Elsewhere Effect lesson covers for scanning many lags or many strategies — some cells will look "significant" by chance alone, and a real significance framework (not eyeballing brightness) is needed before trusting an isolated hot spot.
  • The time-frequency resolution trade-off is fundamental, not a limitation of a particular implementation. No choice of wavelet, scale range, or software package escapes the basic trade-off described above — sharper timing always costs some frequency precision, and vice versa. Reading a scalogram as if it had unlimited precision in both dimensions simultaneously is a category error, not a fixable implementation detail.
  • A descriptive time-frequency picture is not a validated trading signal. Same discipline as every other tool in this curriculum: a scalogram showing an interesting pattern around some historical event is an observation, not evidence of a repeatable, tradeable edge until it clears an honest out-of-sample test.
Scenario drill · an illustrative ECB afternoon

A bright band after the press conference

You run a Morlet scalogram of EUR/USD 5-minute returns over one session that included an ECB press conference. It shows a bright band at short scales in the 20 minutes after the press conference starts. It also shows bright patches at long scales at the very start and the very end of the session. What do you conclude?

  1. Both are real. Build a rule that trades the short cycle after ECB press conferences and the long cycle at the open and close.
    The edge patches sit inside the cone of influence, where the wavelet hangs off the end of the data, so they mostly reflect how the edges were handled. And one day's bright band is an observation, not a tested rule.
  2. Distrust the edge patches, then check whether the post-conference band shows up across many ECB days, with a proper significance test rather than brightness by eye.
    This handles both traps in the lesson. Edge readings get far less weight, and scanning many time-scale cells for a bright spot is a multiple-comparisons problem. A band that recurs across many events, and survives a real test, is worth taking further.
  3. Write up the band as a description of what happened that day, with no trading rule attached.
    Honest, and the scalogram is good at exactly this kind of description. But a single day says little about whether the pattern repeats, and the edge patches still need flagging in the write-up.
  4. Swap the scalogram for one periodogram of the whole session, since it is the more established tool.
    That throws away the one thing you wanted to know: when. A periodogram would report a short cycle somewhere in the day, with no way to say whether it was concentrated right after the press conference.

What the lesson says. This is an illustrative case built on the lesson's own ECB scenario, not a reported result. A scalogram can show a shortening cycle as a narrow band right at the announcement, which a whole-session periodogram cannot. But readings near the sample edges are contaminated, isolated hot spots need a real significance framework, and turning a pattern into a rule still needs an honest out-of-sample test.

Key Takeaways

✓Fourier shows which frequencies, wavelets show which frequencies and roughly when. A plain periodogram averages frequency content over the whole sample; a scalogram maps it against time as well.
✓The continuous wavelet transform has two parameters, not one. Scale \(a\) (inverse frequency) and time-shift \(b\) together produce \(W(a,b)\), a 2-D time-frequency map instead of a 1-D spectrum.
✓The worked example localizes a shock exactly. A one-level Haar transform on an 8-point series pinpoints the exact pair of time steps a large shock sits in — something a Fourier view of the same points cannot do.
⚠Time resolution and frequency resolution trade off against each other, always. No wavelet choice escapes this — Haar favors sharp timing, Morlet favors clean frequency separation.
⚠Edge effects (the cone of influence) and multiple-comparisons risk are real. Scalogram readings near the sample's edges, or an isolated "hot spot" scanned out of many cells, both need extra scrutiny before being trusted.
ℹThis lesson explains a technique, not a claim of edge. A scalogram is a descriptive lens for how cyclical behavior evolves over time — turning that into a trading rule still needs the same honest OOS bar as anything else.
ℹIn markets, wavelets answer "at which horizon?" A relationship can be strong at monthly scales, absent at daily ones, and switch on and off over time; wavelets measure it scale by scale instead of averaging everything into one number.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. Why can two series with identical periodogram spectra look completely different when viewed through a scalogram?
A periodogram is computed once over the entire sample and reports total energy at each frequency, collapsing away any information about timing. Two series can contain exactly the same set of frequencies at exactly the same overall strengths — one with a short cycle active the whole time and a long cycle active the whole time (running simultaneously), the other with the short cycle active only in the first half and the long cycle only in the second half — and produce nearly identical periodograms, because the periodogram cannot distinguish "always present" from "present somewhere." A scalogram, by mapping energy against both scale and time, makes that distinction directly visible as a difference in where the bright regions sit along the time axis.
Q2. In the continuous wavelet transform formula, what do the parameters a and b each control, and how do they map onto the Fourier transform's single parameter f?
\(a\) is scale — how stretched or compressed the mother wavelet is, playing the same conceptual role as frequency \(f\) did in the Fourier transform (inversely: large \(a\) means slow oscillation, roughly low frequency; small \(a\) means fast oscillation, roughly high frequency). \(b\) is a genuinely new parameter with no Fourier-transform analogue: it's the time-location the wavelet is centered at. The Fourier transform only ever asked "how much of frequency \(f\)," a one-dimensional question; the wavelet transform asks "how much of scale \(a\), centered at time \(b\)," a two-dimensional question, which is exactly why its output is a 2-D map (the scalogram) rather than a 1-D spectrum.
Q3. Why does the worked example's Haar transform produce one detail coefficient far larger than the rest, and what would happen to that same shock in a plain Fourier transform of the same 8 points?
The Haar detail coefficient for a pair is a half-difference between the two values in that pair; every pair except the one containing the shock has two similarly-sized small values, so their difference (and hence detail coefficient) is small. The shock pair (3.00, −0.05) has an enormous difference between its two values, producing a detail coefficient roughly 15-30 times larger than any other pair's — and that large coefficient's position in the list directly identifies which pair of time steps the shock occurred in. A plain Fourier transform of the same 8 points would still register the shock (a sudden jump is broadband — it contributes energy across essentially every frequency, not one clean spike), but that energy would be spread across many frequency bins with no indication of which of the 8 time steps it came from — exactly the kind of timing information a wavelet transform preserves and a Fourier transform discards.
Q4. What is the "cone of influence," and why does it specifically threaten a scalogram's readings near the start and end of a sample rather than in the middle?
A wavelet centered near the very start or end of a finite sample partly extends past the edge of the data that actually exists — there is no real data there to multiply against, so the transform has to rely on some edge-handling assumption (padding with zeros, reflecting the series, wrapping around) to compute an answer at all. That assumption contaminates the result near the edges specifically, because a wavelet centered safely in the middle of a long sample never runs into this problem — its full extent is covered by real data on both sides. The "cone of influence" is the region (typically widening toward the very edges, and wider for larger scales, since a large-scale wavelet is physically wider) where scalogram readings should be discounted for exactly this reason.
Q5. Why is choosing between a Haar and a Morlet mother wavelet described as "a real, consequential decision" rather than a neutral default setting?
The two wavelets sit at different points on the fundamental time-resolution/frequency-resolution trade-off: Haar's sharp, step-like shape is well matched to pinpointing abrupt jumps precisely in time but poorly distinguishes nearby frequencies, while Morlet's smooth, Gaussian-tapered oscillation distinguishes nearby frequencies much more cleanly but blurs exactly when an abrupt event happened. Applying Haar to a series whose interesting structure is a smoothly evolving cycle length (rather than sharp jumps) would under-resolve the frequency detail that actually matters; applying Morlet to a series where the interesting structure is a single sharp discontinuity would smear its timing. The right choice depends on what feature is being hunted for, which is exactly why it's a modeling decision and not a one-size-fits-all default.
