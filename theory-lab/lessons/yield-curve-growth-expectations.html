What the Yield Curve Says About Growth

The yield curve is the bond market's best guess at where interest rates are going, plus a fee for waiting. That makes it a genuine reading of what the market expects — and a much shakier forecast of what will happen. This lesson teaches you to read the shape, and then shows exactly where the famous "inversion means recession" chain breaks in the data.

CONCEPT — explained here; desk verdicts quoted where tested ~17 min read Prereqs: Bond Pricing & YTM, Central Bank Policy

TL;DR
  • A long yield ≈ the average policy rate the market expects over its life + a term premium. So the curve's shape is, mostly, a picture of the expected path of rates.
  • Upward-sloping = rates expected to rise or stay put, with growth and inflation holding up. Flat or inverted = cuts expected — usually because the market thinks the economy will slow.
  • Inversions are usually made at the front end: the central bank hikes, the 2-year rises above the 10-year. Bull/bear steepening/flattening tells you which end moved and in which direction.
  • Recessions are dated by the NBER, in hindsight — not by "two negative quarters".
  • The desk's verdict on the trade: NULL. Ten inversion episodes since 1976; a recession followed within 24 months in 6 of 10 (60%, CI [31%, 83%]); forward Nasdaq did not separate from a matched control; and steepenings were followed by worse returns. The curve measures expectations. It is not a timing signal.
Section 01

Why this matters

"The yield curve has inverted" is one of the most quoted lines in markets, and usually arrives attached to "...so a recession is coming". This sequence has been about how markets talk to each other: the domino chain across assets, investor mood and forced selling, and the VIX as uncertainty, not fear. The Treasury curve is the market that speaks most directly about the economy: it prices where the Fed will take rates, and the Fed takes rates where it thinks growth and inflation are going.

The difference between those two sentences — "the curve shows what is expected" and "the curve predicts what will happen" — is the whole lesson. The first is true almost by construction. The second is a claim this desk has tested, and the result is in Section 05.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Lend for two years, or lend for ten?

If you lend to the US government for ten years, you could instead have lent for two years and then re-lent at whatever rate exists in two years' time. Investors compare the two, so the 10-year yield ends up close to the average of the short rates people expect over the next ten years — plus a little extra for locking money away and bearing the risk that rates jump.

So when the 10-year yields less than the 2-year, the market is saying: "short rates are high now, and I expect them to be lower later." Rates are usually cut when the economy weakens. That is why an inverted curve is read as an expected slowdown. Notice the word: expected.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

The yield curve is a line of prices for borrowing money for a short time, a medium time and a long time. Normally borrowing for longer costs more, so the line slopes up, like a slide. When the long end costs less than the short end, the line tips over. That means people think rates will come down later — usually because they think the economy will get weaker. It's a guess by lots of people at once, not a crystal ball.

Reading the curve, step by step

1. A long yield is an expected path plus a premium

\[ y_n \;\approx\; \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1} \mathbb{E}_t\!\left[r_{t+i}\right] \;+\; TP_n \]
yn
The n-year yield
A point on the curve — the 2-year, the 10-year
(1/n)ΣE[r]
Expected path
The average policy rate the market expects over n years
TPn
Term premium
Extra yield for duration risk. Not observed; model-estimated; can be negative

The 2-year yield is dominated by the next few Fed meetings, which is why it is often called the market's vote on policy. The 10-year mixes a long expected path with a term premium that QE, inflation uncertainty and government bond supply all push around (see QE and QT). That second piece is the main reason the curve can mislead: a flat curve can mean "cuts expected" or simply "term premium squeezed".

2. Pulling the expected path out: forward rates

Two yields give you the rate the market is implicitly locking in between their maturities. Using simple annual compounding as an approximation:

\[ f_{m\to n} \;\approx\; \frac{n\,y_n - m\,y_m}{n-m} \]
f(m→n)the average rate implied for the years between m and n
n·yₙtotal return locked in by lending for n years
m·yₘthe part already earned over the first m years

If the 5-year yields less than the 2-year, the forward rate for years 2–5 is below today's 2-year: the market is pricing cuts (before allowing for any term premium inside the forward).

4. Four ways the curve can change

"Bull" means yields falling (bond prices rising); "bear" means yields rising. "Steepening" means the 10-year minus the 2-year gets bigger; "flattening" means it shrinks. Which end does most of the moving tells you what is being repriced:

Bull steepening

Front end falls faster than the long end. The market is pricing rate cuts — often as the Fed starts easing, or as a slowdown is feared. Typical of the end of an inversion.

Bear steepening

Long end rises faster. Stronger growth or inflation expectations, a rising term premium, or worries about bond supply and credibility — the front end is anchored by the Fed.

Bear flattening

Front end rises faster. Hikes being priced. This is how most inversions are made: the Fed lifts the 2-year above the 10-year.

Bull flattening

Long end falls faster. Long-run growth or inflation expectations fading, or a rush into duration for safety, while the front end stays pinned.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Think of the curve as a see-saw. If the short end goes up (the Fed raising rates), the see-saw tips flatter — and can tip all the way over. If the short end drops (the Fed cutting), it tips back steep. Watching which end moved tells you whether people are thinking about the Fed's next steps or about the far future.

5. Who actually calls a recession

In the US, recessions are dated by the National Bureau of Economic Research's Business Cycle Dating Committee. It picks the month of the peak and the month of the trough, judging the fall in activity on its depth, how widely it spreads across the economy, and how long it lasts — and it announces those dates in hindsight, typically many months after the turning point. "Two consecutive quarters of falling GDP" is a newspaper shorthand, not the definition. In 2022, the first estimates of GDP showed two negative quarters in a row, and no recession was declared.

This matters for anyone trying to trade the curve: the thing it is said to predict is only labelled after the fact. You cannot know, at the time, whether you are in one.

🧮
Worked example

What an inverted front end is pricing

1

The curve

3-month 5.30%, 2-year 4.50%, 5-year 4.00%, 10-year 4.10% (illustrative).

2

Slopes

2s10s = 4.10 − 4.50 = −40bp. 3m10y = 4.10 − 5.30 = −120bp. Both inverted.

3

The forward for years 2–5

\[ f_{2\to5} \approx \frac{5(4.00) - 2(4.50)}{3} = \frac{20 - 9}{3} \approx 3.67\% \]

The market is implicitly locking in an average of about 3.67% for years 2 to 5, well under today's 5.30% bill rate.

4

Take out a premium

If the term premium in that forward is, say, +0.30%, the expected rate is nearer 3.37% — roughly two percentage points of cuts priced from the bill rate. If the premium is negative, as some model estimates were after years of QE, fewer cuts are priced than the raw forward suggests. Either way the curve is telling you what is expected; it says nothing about whether that expectation is right.

Section 03

It actually happened

It actually happened

2006–07: the textbook case, with a long fuse

The Fed raised its target rate to 5.25% in June 2006 and held it there for over a year. Through much of 2006 and into 2007 the 2s10s curve sat flat or inverted. The curve had turned positive again by the middle of 2007, the Fed began cutting in September 2007, the stock market peaked in October 2007, and the NBER later dated the start of the recession to December 2007 — an announcement it made in December 2008, a full year after the fact. The curve was "right". But anyone who sold equities on the inversion gave up more than a year of rising prices first.

It actually happened

2022–24: the longest inversion — and no recession (as of writing)

The Fed began hiking in March 2022 and raised rates faster than at any time in four decades. The 2s10s curve inverted in July 2022 and stayed inverted until around September 2024, when the Fed began cutting — the longest continuous 2s10s inversion in the series, and at its deepest, in 2023, around a full percentage point below zero, the deepest since the early 1980s. Recession forecasts were everywhere. The first GDP estimates for the first half of 2022 even showed two negative quarters. Yet the NBER declared no recession, unemployment stayed low, and, as of this writing, none has been dated to follow that inversion. It un-inverted through a bull steepening: the 2-year fell faster than the 10-year as cuts were priced.

It actually happened

August 2019: right for the wrong reason

The curve inverted briefly in August 2019. A recession did follow — beginning in February 2020, caused by a pandemic no bond market priced in 2019. On the desk's own count, the Nasdaq-100 rose 48.5% over the year after that inversion. "The curve called it" and "you could have traded it" are different sentences.

🎯
Real-world trading scenario

A morning note says "the curve just inverted — de-risk"

Read it in three steps. Which end moved? If the 2-year rose on a hawkish surprise (bear flattening), the curve is telling you about the Fed's next meetings; if the 10-year fell on weak data (bull flattening), it is telling you about long-run growth. What is priced? Back out the 2→5 forward: how many cuts does it imply, and how much of that could be a squeezed term premium? What is it worth? As an equity timing signal, nothing on this desk's own test — ten episodes, no separation from a control.

The honest note: "The curve is pricing cuts — the market expects a slowdown. Historically that expectation has been wrong about four times in ten, and the equity follow-through has not been distinguishable from ordinary months." That is a description of expectations, not a trade.

Section 04

How practitioners actually use this

🏠

Rates people actually pay

Very roughly: bill rates feed savings accounts and money funds; the 2- to 10-year area feeds car loans and business borrowing; the 10-year is the benchmark US mortgage rates trade at a spread to. The curve's shape is the shape of the economy's borrowing costs.

🗳️

The 2-year as a policy vote

Traders read the 2-year (and futures on the policy rate) as the market's view of the next several meetings. A move in the 2-year after data is a repricing of the Fed.

📐

Level, slope, curvature

Most yield-curve moves decompose into three factors — everything up or down together, the slope changing, the middle bending. Curve trades (steepeners, flatteners, butterflies) bet on slope or curvature, not on direction.

🏦

Bank margins

Banks borrow short and lend long, so a steeper curve tends to help their net interest margin and an inverted one squeezes it — one of the more mechanical routes from the curve to an equity sector.

Section 05

What the desk found

The central folk claim — "an inverted curve means a recession is coming and risk assets will fall" — has been pre-registered and tested here.

Desk test: nothing found curve-inversion — NULL for the tradeable half. 12,581 daily 10y–2y observations, 1976-06 to 2026-10, collapse to ten inversion episodes (the unit is the episode — one inversion contributes hundreds of days). Recession within 24 months followed 6 of 10 — 60%, 95% CI [31%, 83%], an interval that contains a coin flip; within 12 months only 3 of 10. Forward Nasdaq against a month-matched control: +3m −1.6% vs +2.2%, +6m −1.7% vs +5.7% (p=0.057), +12m +10.5% vs +13.0% (p=0.33). Leave-one-out passed (2008 is not carrying it). The mirror killed it: steepening episodes returned −1.1% at +12m on n=6 — worse than inversions, so curve shape is not what separates the periods.

Three things to keep from that entry. First, the conditional is easy to flip: nearly every recession was preceded by an inversion, but only 60% of inversions were followed by a recession within two years, and four of ten by none at all. Second, even the half that is real cannot be traded, because the NBER dates recessions in arrears. Third, the one nominally significant horizon moved from p=0.029 to p=0.057 on a cosmetic change to how an episode end was labelled — fragility, not signal.

Two neighbouring results point the same way: a 2-year shock of ±14bp in a week was not followed by a more volatile FX week (front-end-shock, null), and conditioning on what yields did into a data release made a predictor worse than a shuffled placebo (priced-in-direction, null).

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where the curve gets misread

Flipping the conditional. "Every recession was preceded by an inversion" (high P(inversion | recession)) is not "every inversion leads to a recession" — on this desk's count, P(recession within 24m | inversion) is 60%, with an interval from 31% to 83%.

Ignoring the lag. In 2006–07, inversion came more than a year before the recession, and equities rose in between. In 2001, 2007 and 2020 the curve had already re-steepened by the time the recession began.

Forgetting the term premium. QE compressed long-end term premia. A flat curve built on a squeezed premium says less about expected cuts than the same shape would have in the 1990s.

Treating "two negative quarters" as the definition. It is not; the NBER is. 2022 is the counter-example.

Key Takeaways

✓A yield ≈ expected policy path + term premium. The curve's shape is mostly the market's expected path of rates — a measurement of expectations.
✓Inversions are usually built at the front end. The Fed hikes, the 2-year rises above the 10-year, and forwards price cuts later.
✓Bull/bear × steepening/flattening tells you which end moved and why. The end of an inversion is usually a bull steepening as cuts arrive.
⚠Recessions are NBER-dated in hindsight. "Two negative quarters" is shorthand, and what the curve "predicts" is labelled only after the fact.
✗curve-inversion is NULL as a trade. 10 episodes; recession within 24 months 6 of 10 (60%, CI [31%, 83%]); forward Nasdaq does not separate from a matched control; steepenings were followed by WORSE returns (−1.1% at +12m, n=6). Never write that an inversion means equities fall.
Section 07

Self-test

Q1. The 2-year is at 4.8% and the 5-year at 4.2%. What average rate is priced for years 2–5, and what does it suggest?
\(f_{2\to5}\approx(5\times4.2-2\times4.8)/3=(21-9.6)/3=3.8\%\). Lower than today's 2-year, so the market is pricing rate cuts over that window — before allowing for any term premium inside the forward.
Q2. The 2-year falls 30bp and the 10-year falls 10bp. Name the move and what it usually signals.
2s10s rises by 20bp with yields falling: a bull steepening. The front end is pricing cuts faster than the long end moves — typically the Fed starting, or being expected to start, easing.
Q3. Why is "the curve tells you a slowdown is expected" a safer statement than "the curve predicts a recession"?
The first is close to true by construction: an inverted forward curve is the market pricing lower rates later, which usually means it expects weaker growth. The second is a forecast claim, and the expectation has been wrong often — on the desk's count, four of ten inversions since 1976 were followed by no recession within two years.
Q4. Who dates US recessions, and why does that matter for trading the curve?
The NBER's Business Cycle Dating Committee, judging depth, breadth and duration and announcing peaks and troughs in hindsight — often many months later (the December 2007 peak was announced in December 2008). So the outcome the curve is said to predict cannot be known in time to trade on it.
Q5. What killed the curve-inversion test on this desk?
The mirror. If inversions specifically warn of weaker equities, steepenings should look different. Instead, steepening episodes returned −1.1% at +12 months on n=6 — worse than inversions — and the inversion returns never separated from a month-matched control. Curve shape is not what separates the periods.
