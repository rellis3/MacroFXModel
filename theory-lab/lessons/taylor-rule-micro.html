Lesson 17 · Macro & FX Theory

The Taylor Rule, or How a Central Bank "Should" Set Rates

Every carry trade and every yield-spread thesis quietly assumes something: that a central bank sets its policy rate in response to inflation and the state of the economy. That's not just a vague instinct traders pick up over time — economists have an almost embarrassingly simple formula for it. This lesson opens the formula up: what each piece means, how to compute it, and why the gap between what it says and what a central bank is actually doing is one of the most closely watched signals in FX.

Section 1

A thermostat for the economy

Picture a central bank running a thermostat with two sensors instead of one. The first reads "room temperature" — current inflation compared with the target the bank has publicly committed to. The second reads "how hard the system is already working" — the output gap, how far the economy is running above or below what it can sustainably handle. One lever, the policy rate, responds to both.

🌡

Sensor 1 — inflation vs. target

Room too hot (inflation above target) calls for more cooling: raise the rate. Too cold calls for less cooling, or active heating: cut it.

⚙

Sensor 2 — output vs. potential

Even before the room feels hot, a system already straining above capacity is a warning sign worth leaning against pre-emptively.

🎚

One lever — the policy rate

Both sensors feed the same single dial. The Taylor Rule is just that thermostat's control law, written down as an equation.

This project's own prior research notes on the Transatlantic Yield Spread treat a central bank's rate path as an observed input — something you read off a screen and trade the lead-lag relationship of. They never ask what actually generates that rate path in the first place. The Taylor Rule is the textbook answer to that prior question — the mechanism underneath the mechanism.
Section 2

One equation, four pieces

i = r* + π + a(π − π*) + b(y − y*). Step through what each piece is doing.

Try it
r* + π
The neutral real rate, expressed in today's inflation terms. It's what the policy rate would sit at if inflation were exactly on target and the economy exactly at potential.
a(π − π*)
Positive when inflation runs above target — adds extra tightening on top of the baseline. Negative when inflation runs below target — subtracts, calling for easier policy.
b(y − y*)
Positive when the economy runs above potential — adds tightening pre-emptively, before an overheating economy fully shows up in the inflation term itself.
i
The rule's single output — the baseline, plus whichever way the two dials are currently pushing.
r*

Neutral real rate

Not directly observable — a slow-moving estimate of the real rate consistent with full employment and stable inflation. Taylor's own paper simply set it at 2%.

π

Inflation, current vs. target

π is realized inflation (headline or core CPI/PCE); π* is the bank's explicit target — 2% for the Fed, ECB, and most developed-market inflation-targeters.

y−y*

The output gap

How far actual GDP sits from an estimate of potential GDP. Positive means overheating; negative means slack.

a,b

Response coefficients

How aggressively the rule reacts to each gap. Taylor's original paper set a = b = 0.5 — each percentage point of gap moves the rate by half a point.

Section 3

What the rule collapses to when nothing is out of line

Set inflation exactly on target and output exactly at potential. Both correction terms vanish.

π = π*
y = y*
→
i = r* + π*

This is the neutral nominal rate — the rate the rule says to hold at when the economy needs neither tightening nor easing. It's a genuinely useful anchor: any live policy rate can be read as "the neutral nominal rate, plus whatever the two gaps are currently adding or subtracting."

The neutral rate is the "everything's fine" setting on the thermostat — not too hot, not too cold, no correction needed. It's a useful reference point precisely because it's the baseline every other reading gets compared against.

The neutral nominal rate here would be only r* + π* = 2 + 2 = 4%. The rule prescribes 6% — a full two points higher — because both dials are active in the same direction: the 1pp inflation overshoot adds 0.5pp, the 1pp positive output gap adds another 0.5pp, and the baseline itself already embeds today's 3% inflation rather than the 2% target.

Either dial alone can push the rate up. Both can reinforce each other, as here — or they can partially offset, which the calculator on the next-but-one slide will let you find for yourself.

Section 7

The number that actually moves markets isn't i — it's the gap

No desk trades the Taylor Rule's level in isolation — they trade the gap between what the rule prescribes and what the central bank is actually doing. That gap is a measure of unresolved pressure on the policy rate, not a forecast of the next meeting.
Behind the curve

Actual policy sits below the Taylor-implied rate. Policy is looser than the data justify. Markets read this as more tightening (or a slower pace of cuts) still likely needed — hawkish-repricing risk skewed higher than what's currently priced.

Ahead of the curve

Actual policy sits above the Taylor-implied rate. Policy is already tighter than the gaps justify, common late in a hiking cycle. Markets read this as disinflationary/cut risk skewed higher than what's priced.

Section 8

Multiple rules, one trading habit

There is no single "correct" Taylor Rule — the Fed itself publishes several side by side.

📐

Classic rule

Taylor's original 1993 specification — a = b = 0.5, the version used throughout this deck.

🐢

Inertial rule

Partially anchors on the previous policy rate to avoid abrupt jumps meeting to meeting.

⚖

Balanced-approach rule

Doubles the output-gap coefficient (b = 1), leaning more heavily on labor-market slack.

On a desk, the rule's real use is comparative: computing a Taylor-implied rate for two economies at once — say the US and the euro area — turns directly into an expected relative rate-path story, the theoretical engine behind rate-differential and yield-spread trades. A widening gap between one country's implied-minus-actual rate and another's is a candidate leading explanation for why a yield spread might be about to move, before the spread itself has visibly shifted.

A hot CPI print, recomputed in seconds

Starting from the worked example (implied = 6%, actual = 4.5%, market-priced terminal = 4.75%): inflation surprises higher, from 3% to 4%, output gap unchanged at +1%. New implied rate = 2 + 4 + 0.5(2) + 0.5(1) = 7.5%. The gap versus what's priced jumps from 1.25pp to 2.75pp — more than doubling — purely from recomputing the same formula with the new print. That's a signal the bank looks more behind the curve than the market currently reflects, not a certainty about the next meeting.

Section 9

Where this needs qualifying

r* and the output gap are estimates, not facts

Neither is directly measurable — both are model-based estimates. Different proxies (Laubach-Williams r*, CBO potential GDP vs. an unemployment-gap proxy) can move the implied rate by a full percentage point or more from the exact same CPI print. Never treat one desk's Taylor number as the number without checking which proxies it used.

A benchmark, not a binding rule

Real committees weigh dual mandates, financial stability, forward-guidance credibility, and the effective lower bound — none of which the formula knows about. Actual policy rates routinely sit away from whatever a plugged-in Taylor Rule spits out for years at a time before correcting. A wide gap says pressure is building; it isn't a dated forecast of the next decision.

A checked claim, for this repo specifically: as of this lesson, MacroFXModel has no standalone Taylor Rule calculator wired into any trading system, bot, or backtest — a search of the codebase outside Theory Lab turns up nothing. This visual guide's own calculator, like the full lesson's, is a teaching tool, not a shipped signal.

Section 10

Quick check

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

What does a positive output gap (y − y*) mean?

A positive output gap means actual GDP is running above potential — the economy is "overheating," which tends to build inflationary pressure before it fully shows up in the inflation print itself.
Question 2

Using r* = 2%, π* = 2%, a = b = 0.5, output gap = +1%: what does the rule imply if inflation is 1.5%?

i = 2 + 1.5 + 0.5(1.5−2) + 0.5(1) = 2 + 1.5 − 0.25 + 0.5 = 3.75%. Cooling inflation below target pulls the rate down through a negative inflation-gap term, even as the positive output gap keeps pulling it back up.
Question 3

A central bank's actual rate sits well below its own Taylor-implied rate. What is this called?

Behind the curve: policy is looser than the data justify. Markets read this as more tightening (or slower cuts) still likely needed — hawkish-repricing risk skewed higher.
Question 4

Why do traders watch the gap between the implied and actual rate more than the implied rate's raw level?

The gap measures unresolved pressure — whether the bank is caught up with the data or not. The rule's level in isolation tells you comparatively little.
Question 5

Are r* and the output gap directly observable, like a market price?

Different proxies for r* and potential GDP can shift the implied rate by a full percentage point or more from the exact same inflation print — a key reason to treat the rule as a benchmark, not gospel.
Section 11

What to carry forward

01

The rule is a baseline plus two dials: i = r* + π + a(π−π*) + b(y−y*) — a neutral rate, corrected for how far inflation and output sit from target and potential.

02

When both gaps are zero, the rule collapses to the neutral nominal rate, r* + π* — the "everything's fine" anchor every other reading gets compared against.

03

The two dials can reinforce each other (both push the rate the same way) or partially offset — check both, never assume.

04

What moves markets is the gap — i_implied − i_actual — not the rule's raw level. Behind the curve signals unfinished tightening; ahead of the curve signals cut risk.

05

r*, the output gap, and even the coefficients a and b are estimates and choices, not universal constants — state them before quoting a number.

06

A wide Taylor gap is a measure of unresolved pressure, not a dated forecast of the next policy meeting — real deviations can persist for years.

What this now lets you do

Compute a rough Taylor-implied rate from a CPI print and an output-gap estimate in seconds, the way a desk does the moment data lands. Recognise "behind the curve" and "ahead of the curve" for what they actually mean, and connect a widening Taylor gap to the rate-differential logic behind yield-spread and carry trades — before the spread itself has visibly moved.
