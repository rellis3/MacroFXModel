Fama-French & Carhart: Beyond the Single Factor

CAPM's single market beta leaves two stubborn, replicated anomalies unexplained: small stocks outperform what their beta predicts, and so do cheap ("value") stocks. Fama and French's fix wasn't a better beta — it was two more zero-cost long-short portfolios added as regressors, and Carhart later bolted on a third for momentum.

CONCEPT — no literal SMB/HML/UMD factor exists anywhere in this repo; the closest real analog, forge/xsect.py's cross-sectional FX momentum/reversion basket, is genuinely built and walk-forward tested, and its honest result is a null ~19 min read Prereqs: CAPM: The Single-Factor Baseline, Interest Rate Parity — Covered vs. Uncovered

TL;DR
  • CAPM leaves two robust, decades-replicated anomalies on the table: small-cap stocks earn more than their market beta predicts (the size effect), and high book-to-market "value" stocks earn more than theirs (the value effect). Fama & French (1993) respond not by fixing beta, but by adding two more explanatory regressors — SMB and HML — each one itself a zero-cost, long-short "factor-mimicking portfolio."
  • The three-factor model: \(R_i - R_f = \alpha + \beta_{MKT}(R_m-R_f) + \beta_{SMB}\,SMB + \beta_{HML}\,HML + \varepsilon\). SMB and HML aren't abstractions — they're built by a specific, mechanical double sort into six value-weighted portfolios, rebalanced annually with a deliberate lag against look-ahead bias.
  • Carhart (1997) adds a fourth factor, UMD/MOM — past-12-month winners minus losers, skipping the most recent month — because momentum is a genuinely different, persistent continuation anomaly that value and size don't capture, and is empirically negatively correlated with HML, making it a diversifying addition rather than a redundant one.
  • Fama & French (2015) extend to five factors, adding RMW (profitability) and CMA (investment), motivated by a dividend-discount-model argument tying expected profitability and investment directly to expected returns. It's the modern academic standard, though 3- and 4-factor versions still get quoted constantly in practice.
  • The FX carry trade, covered in this curriculum's own Interest Rate Parity lesson, is this same story wearing a currency-market costume: a systematic long-short bet (high-yield currencies vs. low-yield funding currencies) that has paid a persistent average premium, is compensated for a well-documented crash risk, and has direct, published academic analogs to HML, UMD, and even MKT itself in currency markets.
  • This repo has no literal Fama-French implementation — but forge/xsect.py builds and walk-forward tests a genuine cross-sectional long-short FX momentum/reversion basket. Its honest result: a null, OOS t = 0.44, that doesn't even clear its own shuffled-score noise floor.
Section 01

Why this matters

CAPM: The Single-Factor Baseline makes one claim about what drives expected return: exposure to the market, and nothing else. That claim is elegant, and it is empirically incomplete in a specific, well-documented way. Sort stocks by market capitalization and, decade after decade, in market after market, the smallest stocks earn more than CAPM's own beta says they should — a market-cap effect a single beta simply doesn't have room to describe. Sort stocks instead by book value relative to market value and the same thing happens again: stocks priced cheaply relative to their book value ("value" stocks) outperform their CAPM-predicted return, while richly priced "growth" stocks underperform theirs. Both patterns survive controlling for beta. Both are far too large and far too persistent to wave away as noise.

Fama and French's 1993 answer is worth sitting with because of what it is not. It is not a claim that CAPM's logic is wrong, and it is not an attempt to build a better estimate of market beta. It is an admission that one factor is not enough to describe the cross-section of expected returns, followed by the most direct fix imaginable: measure the size effect and the value effect directly, as their own zero-cost long-short portfolios, and add them to the regression as two more explanatory variables sitting right next to the market factor. Carhart (1997) later found a third pattern CAPM and the three-factor model both still missed — price momentum — and bolted on a fourth factor the same way. This lesson builds all of it: what SMB, HML, and UMD actually are, exactly how they get constructed from real portfolio sorts (not just symbolically defined), why momentum needed its own factor rather than folding into value, and — the most distinctive piece of this particular lesson — the explicit, well-documented mapping between this entire equity-factor story and the FX carry trade this curriculum's own Interest Rate Parity lesson already walked through.

💡
Plain-English intuition

A factor is just a strategy, measured, then handed to a regression

CAPM's market factor, \(R_m - R_f\), is itself a strategy: buy the whole market, fund it at the risk-free rate, and measure what that round trip returns each period. SMB and HML are built exactly the same way — they are strategies first, factors second. SMB is literally the return of "go long a diversified basket of small stocks, short a diversified basket of large stocks, every period." HML is literally "go long cheap (high book-to-market) stocks, short expensive (low book-to-market) stocks." Neither is an abstract statistical construct dreamed up to make a regression fit better — each is a real, tradeable, self-financing portfolio whose return history is published monthly and has been since 1993.

Once you have that return history, using it as a regressor is no different from using the market factor as one: does this particular stock's return move with the small-minus-big spread? With the cheap-minus-expensive spread? A stock with a large positive loading on SMB behaves, in part, like a small-cap stock — regardless of whether it technically is one — and a large positive loading on HML means it behaves, in part, like a value stock. The whole apparatus is "build a portfolio that isolates one specific pattern, then ask every other asset how much of that pattern it secretly contains."

Section 02

The three-factor model, precisely

The Fama-French three-factor regression extends CAPM's single-factor regression by adding exactly two more terms on the right-hand side:

\[ R_i - R_f = \alpha + \beta_{MKT}(R_m - R_f) + \beta_{SMB}\,SMB + \beta_{HML}\,HML + \varepsilon \]
R_i − R_fasset i's excess return over the risk-free rate — the thing being explained
β_MKT(R_m−R_f)the CAPM term, carried over unchanged — exposure to the broad market's excess return
β_SMB·SMBexposure to the size factor's own return, times how much of that pattern this asset carries
β_HML·HMLexposure to the value factor's own return, likewise scaled by this asset's own loading
α
Alpha
Return this asset earns that none of the three factors explain — the object of an active manager's entire pitch
SMB
Small Minus Big
Return of a small-cap portfolio minus a large-cap portfolio — the size factor's own realized return each period
HML
High Minus Low
Return of a high book-to-market ("value") portfolio minus a low book-to-market ("growth") portfolio

Precisely: SMB (Small Minus Big) is the realized return, each period, of a portfolio long small-market-capitalization stocks and short large-market-capitalization stocks, sized so the position is self-financing (a genuine zero-cost long-short spread, not a directional long-only tilt). HML (High Minus Low) is the realized return of a portfolio long stocks with a high book-to-market ratio — book value of equity divided by market value of equity, so a high ratio means the market is pricing the company cheaply relative to its accounting book value, the classic "value" signature — and short stocks with a low book-to-market ratio, the "growth" stocks the market prices richly relative to book. Both are month-by-month return series in their own right, published and updated continuously, exactly like a market index's own return series.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine two extra thermometers next to the one CAPM already gives you. The first thermometer measures "how much better are small companies doing than big companies right now" — that's SMB. The second measures "how much better are cheap companies doing than expensive companies right now" — that's HML. Instead of guessing whether a specific stock is secretly small-company-flavored or cheap-company-flavored, you just check how much its own returns wiggle in sync with each thermometer. A stock that wiggles a lot with the "small" thermometer gets a big β_SMB, whether or not it's technically a small company.

Section 03

How the factor portfolios are actually built

It is easy to treat SMB and HML as if they were simply "the size effect" and "the value effect" in the abstract. They are not abstractions — they are the output of a specific, mechanical construction procedure, and the details of that procedure matter enough that skipping them is skipping the actual content of the model. Fama and French's original recipe, still the standard:

1

Independently sort on two dimensions

Every stock in the universe gets sorted twice, independently: once into 2 size buckets by a simple median split (Small / Big), and once into 3 book-to-market buckets using a 30/40/30 split (Low / Neutral / High). "Independently" matters — a stock's size bucket has no influence on which B/M bucket it lands in, or vice versa.

2

Intersect into six portfolios

The two independent sorts intersect into 6 portfolios: Small/Low, Small/Neutral, Small/High, Big/Low, Big/Neutral, Big/High. Each is value-weighted internally (larger holdings within the bucket count for more), exactly like a standard market-cap-weighted index would weight its own constituents.

3

SMB — average the three small, minus the average of the three big

\(SMB = \tfrac{1}{3}(\text{Small/Low}+\text{Small/Neutral}+\text{Small/High}) - \tfrac{1}{3}(\text{Big/Low}+\text{Big/Neutral}+\text{Big/High})\) — averaging across all three B/M buckets on each side is what makes this a clean size spread, purged of any value/growth tilt riding along with it.

4

HML — average the two high, minus the average of the two low

\(HML = \tfrac{1}{2}(\text{Small/High}+\text{Big/High}) - \tfrac{1}{2}(\text{Small/Low}+\text{Big/Low})\) — averaging across both size buckets on each side likewise purges any size tilt from what's meant to be a clean value spread. The middle "Neutral" B/M bucket is deliberately left out of HML entirely.

5

Rebalance annually, with a deliberate lag

Portfolios are rebalanced once a year (each June, in the original construction), using accounting book-value data with a built-in lag — book equity is taken from the prior fiscal year-end, not the most recent one — specifically so that no portfolio is ever formed using accounting information that would not genuinely have been public and knowable at the time.

That lag is not a minor implementation footnote; it is the exact same discipline this curriculum's own Feature Engineering, Leakage & Reproducibility lesson calls point-in-time computability — a feature (here, "which B/M bucket does this stock belong in") must be built only from information that had genuinely already been published at the moment the portfolio is formed. A B/M sort built using book value the market couldn't have known yet is, structurally, the identical look-ahead-bias failure that lesson names directly: a signal quietly reaching forward into information from its own future.

The Fama-French double sort: six portfolios from two independent splits
Two independent sorts (size, then book-to-market) intersect into six value-weighted portfolios. SMB averages across book-to-market (rows), so it isolates a clean size spread; HML averages across size (columns, excluding Neutral), so it isolates a clean value spread — each factor purged of the other dimension's tilt by construction, not by regression.
🎯
Real-world trading scenario

A quant desk evaluates a "small-cap value" manager's alpha claim

A fund manager pitches a track record of beating the S&P 500 by 3 percentage points a year, framed as stock-picking skill. Running the manager's monthly returns through a single-factor CAPM regression against the market alone might well show a large, statistically significant alpha — because CAPM has no other variable available to absorb the fact that the fund happens to hold small, cheap stocks as a structural, disclosed tilt, not a security-by-security bet. Running the identical return series through the three-factor regression instead often tells a very different story: large, significant loadings on SMB and HML, and an alpha that shrinks toward zero, or even turns negative, once those two factors are given somewhere to go. The manager wasn't necessarily picking better stocks — the fund was simply, structurally, long the same size and value tilts SMB and HML already measure, and any diversified investor could buy that same tilt directly and far more cheaply than paying an active manager's fee for it.

Section 04

Carhart's fourth factor — momentum earns its own line

Carhart (1997), studying mutual fund performance persistence, found that a fourth pattern survived even after controlling for market, size, and value exposure: stocks that had performed well over the trailing 12 months kept performing well for a while longer, and stocks that had performed poorly kept performing poorly — a continuation effect, momentum, entirely distinct from the mean-reverting logic value investing runs on. His fix follows the identical recipe as Fama-French: build a fourth zero-cost long-short portfolio, UMD (Up Minus Down, also written MOM), and add it as a fourth regressor.

\[ R_i - R_f = \alpha + \beta_{MKT}(R_m-R_f) + \beta_{SMB}\,SMB + \beta_{HML}\,HML + \beta_{UMD}\,UMD + \varepsilon \]
β_UMD·UMDthe new term — exposure to a portfolio long past winners, short past losers

UMD's own construction, precisely. Each period, rank stocks by their return over the trailing 12 months — but skipping the most recent month, i.e. using months \(t{-}12\) through \(t{-}2\), not \(t{-}12\) through \(t{-}1\). That one-month skip is deliberate, not an arbitrary implementation detail: it exists specifically to avoid contaminating the momentum signal with short-term reversal — a separate, well-documented pattern in which a stock's return in the immediately preceding month tends to partially reverse over the following month, for reasons unrelated to the multi-month continuation momentum is trying to isolate. Go long the winners (typically the top decile), short the losers (the bottom decile), and UMD's realized return each period is exactly that long-short spread.

Why this needed its own factor, and couldn't just fold into HML. Momentum is not a slower or noisier version of the value effect — it is a genuinely separate anomaly with its own distinct behavioral or risk-based explanation (continuation from underreaction to news, herding, or a slow diffusion of information, depending on which literature is consulted), and empirically it moves largely independently of, and at times directly against, value. In some periods and some samples, value and momentum have shown a negative correlation with each other — a stock screening as attractively cheap (high HML exposure) has, in some stretches, tended to be a recent loser (negative UMD exposure), and vice versa. That negative correlation is not a flaw in either factor; it is precisely what makes holding both together valuable from a diversification standpoint — two factors that don't move in lockstep combine into a smoother combined exposure than either alone, the same logic that makes a two-asset portfolio's variance benefit from low or negative covariance between its holdings.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Value investing is "buy things that got cheap and wait for them to bounce back." Momentum is almost the opposite instinct: "buy things that are already going up, because they tend to keep going up for a while." Those are genuinely different bets, and sometimes they even disagree — a stock can look cheap (a value buy signal) while it's still falling (a momentum sell signal). Carhart's point was: don't force momentum to squeeze into the value factor's box. Give it its own line item, because it's telling you something value and size genuinely can't.

Section 05

The five-factor extension, briefly

Fama & French (2015) extended their own model again, adding two more factors motivated by a dividend-discount-model argument rather than a purely empirical anomaly hunt: if a stock's price is the present value of its expected future cash flows, then — holding everything else fixed — a firm expected to be more profitable should command a higher expected return, and a firm expected to invest more aggressively (all else equal) should command a lower one, since aggressive investment today competes with cash that would otherwise be paid out or compounded at the discount rate. That logic motivates two more zero-cost long-short portfolios, built by the same double-sort recipe as SMB and HML:

\[ R_i - R_f = \alpha + \beta_{MKT}(R_m-R_f) + \beta_{SMB}\,SMB + \beta_{HML}\,HML + \beta_{RMW}\,RMW + \beta_{CMA}\,CMA + \varepsilon \]
RMW
Robust Minus Weak
Return of a portfolio long high-operating-profitability firms, short low-profitability firms
CMA
Conservative Minus Aggressive
Return of a portfolio long low-investment ("conservative") firms, short high-investment ("aggressive") firms

The five-factor model is the modern academic standard, and momentum (UMD) is notably not one of its five — Fama and French have argued momentum sits somewhat apart methodologically from the other four, which is part of why Carhart's four-factor model and Fama-French's own three-factor model both remain in routine practical use alongside the newer five-factor version, chosen based on context rather than one being a strict, universally-agreed replacement for the others. This lesson does not derive the five-factor model's own empirical motivation in the same depth as the three- and four-factor cases above — the mechanical recipe is the same double-sort logic already covered in full, applied to two new characteristics.

Section 06

The FX analogy: this curriculum already taught you a factor model

This is the piece of this lesson worth reading twice. This curriculum's own Interest Rate Parity — Covered vs. Uncovered lesson spent an entire lesson on the forward premium puzzle: uncovered interest rate parity predicts that a higher-yielding currency should depreciate, on average, by roughly its rate advantage — and Fama (1984) found the opposite sign, repeatedly, across decades and currency pairs. The FX carry trade — long high-yielding currencies, short low-yielding ("funding") currencies — exists because that prediction fails. That is not a vaguely similar story to Fama-French. It is the identical structure: a real, published, longstanding academic finding that a specific systematic long-short portfolio earns a persistent average return that a simpler baseline model doesn't explain, formalized as its own tradeable, zero-cost factor.

Equity factors → currency factors, mapped directly

MKT → the "dollar factor." The average return across a broad basket of currency pairs against the US dollar plays a role structurally similar to the equity market factor — a broad, common exposure every individual currency pair carries some loading on. HML → the FX carry factor. Long high-interest-rate currencies, short low-interest-rate currencies — this is precisely HML's risk-premium logic transplanted: compensation for bearing some form of risk (debated in the literature between crash risk and consumption risk — see Lustig & Verdelhan (2007) on currency risk premia and consumption growth risk), not a mispricing to be arbitraged away. UMD → FX momentum, directly, not just by analogy: Menkhoff, Sarno, Schmeling & Schrimpf (2012) document a genuine, published currency momentum factor built the same way UMD is — rank currencies by trailing return, go long the winners, short the losers. A separate, real-exchange-rate-based FX "value" factor is documented too, completing the mapping.

Where the analogy is exact, and where it isn't

The construction logic is identical: rank a universe on some characteristic, go long one end and short the other, measure the spread's own return, and treat that return series as a factor other assets can be regressed against. What differs is the underlying economic story for why each spread earns a premium — HML's value premium is debated as distress risk or behavioral mispricing of growth expectations; the FX carry factor's premium is debated as compensation for crash risk (the Interest Rate Parity lesson's own AUD/JPY unwind scenario is exactly that crash risk realizing) or global consumption risk. Different debates, same underlying methodological move: a systematic long-short spread, formalized as a factor, is not the same claim as "a free lunch."

A UIP "failure" and a Fama-French anomaly are the same kind of finding, not a coincidence of vocabulary. Both are cases where a simple baseline model (CAPM; UIP) makes a clean prediction, a specific systematic long-short portfolio earns a return that prediction doesn't explain, and the honest response in both literatures has been to formalize that portfolio as its own priced factor rather than either dismiss the anomaly or claim it's risk-free. The carry trade being short a fat left tail (this curriculum's own Interest Rate Parity lesson's 31 July 2024 AUD/JPY scenario) is the FX-market version of exactly the same warning value investors have heard for decades: a persistent factor premium is compensation for something, and "the model doesn't explain it yet" is not the same claim as "it's free."
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Stock-market researchers noticed that "buy cheap stocks, short expensive ones" made steady extra money that a simple model couldn't explain, so they named that strategy HML and started tracking it like its own asset. Currency researchers noticed the exact same thing happening with interest rates: "buy high-interest currencies, short low-interest currencies" made steady extra money a simple model (UIP) said shouldn't exist. Different market, same discovery, same fix: give the strategy a name, track its own return, and admit it's a real, separate ingredient in what drives returns — one that gets paid, in both cases, for a real risk that occasionally bites hard.

⚠️
What this repo's own factor construction actually shows

A real, walk-forward-tested FX cross-sectional factor attempt — and its honest null result

This is not a hypothetical extension of the analogy above — this repo genuinely contains the same methodological move, applied and tested. forge/xsect.py builds a cross-sectional long-short basket across a 26-instrument FX (and gold) universe: rank every instrument by its own signed distance from that week's opening price (in ATR units), go long one end of the ranking and short the other, and — critically — the module offers both a "momentum" sign (follow the leaders) and a "reversion" sign (fade the extremes) as pre-registered, walk-forward-selected alternatives, exactly the same non-overlapping, cost-net, shuffled-null-tested discipline this curriculum's research process demands. Separately, forge/yield_basket.py builds a genuine interest-rate-differential cross-sectional basket on top of the live YieldSpreadBot engine's own rate-differential z-scores — a real, if not-yet-run (it is blocked on FRED API access), rate-differential-based FX cross-sectional signal.

The honest result, walk-forward tested across 6 folds over 10 years and the full 26-instrument universe: OOS n = 987, mean +0.0081, t = 0.44, hit rate 51.0% — and a shuffled-score null test (permuting which instrument's score gets used to rank, separately on each date) produced t-statistics ranging from −2.05 to +1.68, a wider range than the real result. The real strategy's t-stat does not clear its own null's noise floor. This is exactly the discipline the FX analogy above is not license to skip: a documented, published academic factor (FX momentum, FX carry) existing in the broader literature is a completely different claim from a specific signal, built and tested on this repo's own data, clearing an honest out-of-sample bar. Here, it didn't.

Section 07

Worked example — the extra factors move the prediction

🧮
Worked example

The same stock, predicted by CAPM alone vs. by the four-factor model

Consider a small-cap value stock, Stock Q, over one period in which the market's excess return \((R_m - R_f)\) was 2.0%, SMB was 1.5%, HML was 1.0%, and UMD was 0.5% — all illustrative, made-up figures for one toy period, not fitted to real data.

What CAPM alone would predict. Suppose a single-factor CAPM regression (the previous lesson's own method) on Stock Q's return history estimates its market beta at \(\beta_{CAPM} = 1.30\) — plausibly higher than its "true" multi-factor market loading, since a single-factor regression has nowhere else to put the part of Stock Q's return that actually comes from its size and value tilts, and that omitted-variable effect tends to load onto the only beta available. CAPM's predicted excess return is just:

\[ \hat{R}_{CAPM} = \beta_{CAPM}\,(R_m-R_f) = 1.30 \times 2.0\% = 2.60\% \]

What the four-factor model predicts. Now suppose the four-factor regression, with SMB and HML given somewhere to absorb Stock Q's size and value tilt, estimates a smaller market loading \(\beta_{MKT}=1.10\), alongside \(\beta_{SMB}=0.80\) (a meaningful small-cap tilt), \(\beta_{HML}=0.60\) (a meaningful value tilt), \(\beta_{UMD}=-0.10\) (a small negative momentum loading — consistent with the value/momentum diversification discussed above), and a small residual alpha \(\alpha = 0.30\%\):

\[ \hat{R}_{4F} = 0.30\% + 1.10(2.0\%) + 0.80(1.5\%) + 0.60(1.0\%) + (-0.10)(0.5\%) \]
\[ = 0.30\% + 2.20\% + 1.20\% + 0.60\% - 0.05\% = 4.25\% \]

Compare both to what actually happened. Suppose Stock Q's actual realized excess return that period was 4.10%. CAPM alone predicted 2.60% — an error of 1.50 percentage points, under-predicting badly. The four-factor model predicted 4.25% — an error of just 0.15 percentage points. Nothing here is a live fitted model; it is a constructed illustration of the mechanism the whole lesson describes: the extra factors don't change the underlying return that occurred, they change how much of that return a model can actually explain rather than leave sitting unexplained in a single, overloaded beta.

Section 08

How practitioners actually use this

Multi-factor regressions are the standard tool for performance attribution: given a manager's or a strategy's monthly return series, regress it on MKT, SMB, HML (and UMD, and possibly RMW/CMA), and read the resulting betas as a style profile — how much size tilt, value tilt, and momentum tilt the strategy structurally carries — with whatever alpha survives being the only part of the return actually attributable to something the factors don't already explain. This is exactly the mechanism the quant-desk scenario above walks through, and it's routine due-diligence practice at allocators and fund-of-funds: separating "this manager has genuine skill" from "this manager is quietly just long a well-known, cheaply-replicable factor tilt" is one of the most common uses of this entire model family in institutional practice.

Factor investing as a product category — "smart beta" ETFs targeting value, size, quality, or momentum tilts directly and cheaply — is a direct commercial descendant of this research: if SMB, HML, and UMD are genuinely tradeable, persistent premia, a fund can be built to harvest them systematically without claiming any stock-picking skill at all, at a fraction of an active manager's fee. The FX-market equivalent is exactly the systematic carry and momentum overlay strategies described in the Interest Rate Parity lesson's own "How practitioners actually use this" section — long-short baskets across many currency pairs, explicitly built to harvest a documented factor premium rather than to bet on any single pair's direction.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where multi-factor models get misread

Treating a factor premium as a free lunch. SMB, HML, UMD, and the FX carry and momentum factors alike are all risk-based or behaviorally-based explanations for a persistent average return, not riskless arbitrage. Value stocks can underperform for years at a stretch (as they conspicuously did through much of the 2010s); the FX carry trade can give back years of accumulated gain in two weeks, as the Interest Rate Parity lesson's own AUD/JPY scenario shows in detail. A factor's long-run average premium and its single-period realized return are not the same promise.

Confusing "explained by known factors" with "no real skill." A large alpha shrinking toward zero once SMB/HML/UMD are added doesn't necessarily mean the manager did nothing useful — a manager who correctly, deliberately, and persistently tilted toward small value stocks over decades made a real, differentiated, structural bet. What it does mean is that bet is now cheaply replicable, which changes what fee it's reasonable to pay for accessing it — not whether the historical return was real.

Forgetting the look-ahead-bias discipline in the construction itself. SMB and HML's annual rebalance with a deliberate accounting lag exists for exactly the reason this curriculum's own Feature Engineering, Leakage & Reproducibility lesson insists on point-in-time computability: a factor built from book-value data the market couldn't actually have known yet at formation time isn't measuring a tradeable strategy at all — it's measuring an unachievable, leakage-inflated one. The same discipline applies identically to any FX cross-sectional signal, this repo's own forge/xsect.py included.

Treating a documented academic factor as validated on this repo's own data. The FX carry and momentum factors are real, published, extensively replicated findings in the academic literature (Lustig & Verdelhan, 2007; Menkhoff, Sarno, Schmeling & Schrimpf, 2012). That is a categorically different claim from "a specific factor construction, tested on this repo's own data with its own costs and its own walk-forward split, clears an honest out-of-sample bar" — forge/xsect.py's own null result above is the concrete, honest illustration of exactly that gap.

Section 09

Key Takeaways

✓CAPM's single beta leaves the size and value effects unexplained. Fama & French (1993) respond by adding SMB and HML — real, tradeable, zero-cost long-short portfolios — as two more regressors, not by re-estimating beta.
✓SMB and HML have a precise, mechanical construction: independent 2×3 sorts on size (median) and book-to-market (30/40/30) into six value-weighted portfolios, rebalanced annually with a deliberate accounting lag against look-ahead bias.
✓Carhart (1997) adds UMD/MOM as a genuinely distinct fourth factor — a persistent continuation effect, empirically diversifying against (sometimes negatively correlated with) HML, not a redundant restatement of value or size.
⚠Fama-French (2015) adds RMW and CMA via a dividend-discount-model argument; it's the modern academic standard, but 3- and 4-factor versions remain in routine practical use.
✓The FX carry trade is this same methodological move, in a currency-market costume — a systematic long-short spread compensated for real, debated risk, with direct published analogs for MKT (the dollar factor), HML (carry), UMD (FX momentum — Menkhoff et al. 2012), and a documented FX value factor too.

Section 10

Self-test

Q1. What two empirical anomalies does CAPM's single market beta leave unexplained, and what was Fama & French's (1993) core methodological response?
The size effect (small-cap stocks earn more than their CAPM beta predicts) and the value effect (high book-to-market stocks earn more than theirs). Rather than trying to fix or re-estimate beta itself, Fama & French measured each anomaly directly as its own zero-cost, long-short "factor-mimicking portfolio" — SMB for size, HML for value — and added both as additional explanatory regressors alongside the market factor.
Q2. Precisely how are the six portfolios that produce SMB and HML actually formed, and how is SMB itself computed from them?
Two independent sorts: stocks are split into 2 size buckets by a median split (Small/Big) and, separately, into 3 book-to-market buckets using a 30/40/30 split (Low/Neutral/High). The two sorts intersect into 6 value-weighted portfolios. SMB is the average return of the 3 small portfolios (across all three B/M buckets) minus the average return of the 3 big portfolios — averaging across B/M on each side purges any value/growth tilt, isolating a clean size spread.
Q3. Why did Carhart (1997) give momentum its own fourth factor (UMD) instead of treating it as a variant of HML?
Momentum — the tendency of past 12-month winners (skipping the most recent month, to avoid short-term reversal contamination) to keep outperforming past losers — is a genuinely distinct, persistent continuation anomaly, not a slower version of the value effect's mean-reversion logic. Empirically, value and momentum have shown periods of negative correlation with each other, making UMD a diversifying addition to the model rather than a redundant one; a stock can screen as cheap (high HML exposure) while still being a recent loser (negative UMD exposure) at the same time.
Q4. What do RMW and CMA stand for in the Fama-French five-factor model, and what economic argument motivates adding them?
RMW is Robust Minus Weak (a profitability factor: high- minus low-operating-profitability firms); CMA is Conservative Minus Aggressive (an investment factor: low- minus high-investment firms). Both are motivated by a dividend-discount-model argument: holding price fixed, a firm expected to be more profitable should command a higher expected return, and a firm investing more aggressively (competing today's cash against future payouts) should command a lower one. The five-factor model is the modern academic standard, though 3- and 4-factor versions remain widely quoted in practice.
Q5. In what precise sense is the FX carry trade "the same story" as a Fama-French factor, rather than merely a loose analogy?
Both are cases where a specific, real, systematic long-short portfolio — high-yield currencies minus low-yield funding currencies for carry; small minus big, or high minus low book-to-market for SMB/HML — earns a persistent average return that a simpler baseline model (UIP; CAPM) predicts should not exist, and the honest response in both literatures has been to formalize that spread as its own tradeable, published factor rather than dismiss it or claim it's risk-free. The construction method is identical (rank a universe, go long one end and short the other, track the spread's own return); currency markets even have published, direct analogs to MKT (the dollar factor), HML (the carry factor), and UMD (FX momentum, Menkhoff et al. 2012) specifically.
Q6. Does this repo contain a literal Fama-French or Carhart factor implementation? What is the closest real analog, and what did it honestly find?
No — a repo-wide search finds no SMB, HML, UMD, or Fama/Carhart factor construction anywhere in this codebase; this repo trades FX and gold, not equities, so that absence is expected rather than a gap. The closest real analog is forge/xsect.py, which builds a genuine cross-sectional long-short FX/gold basket (26 instruments) with both a "momentum" and a "reversion" sign as pre-registered, walk-forward-tested alternatives — the same methodological move as UMD's construction, applied to a distance-from-weekly-open score rather than trailing return. Its honest result, walk-forward tested over 10 years and 6 folds: OOS t=0.44, mean +0.0081, which does not clear its own shuffled-score null's noise floor (−2.05 to +1.68) — a clean null, not a validated edge. A second module, forge/yield_basket.py, builds a genuine rate-differential-based FX cross-sectional basket but is written and untested, currently blocked on FRED API access.

Further reading
