Thinking in Expected Value

A hit rate on its own tells you almost nothing. Here is the one number that decides whether a trade is worth taking: what it pays on average, after costs. You'll also see how to get a probability out of an analysis that never gives you one.

The whole idea
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

I'm right seventy percent of the time. That has to be a good system.

Maybe. What does a win pay, compared with a loss?

I take profits quickly. A win is about four tenths of what a loss costs.

Then do the sum. Seven wins of four tenths is two point eight. Three full losses is three. Over ten trades you're down a little, before any costs.

So being right isn't enough.

Being right is half the sum. The other half is what each win pays. Expected value multiplies them through: how often you win, times what a win pays, minus how often you lose, minus the cost of trading.

And there's a line I have to clear?

Every payoff has a break-even win rate. With wins at four tenths of a loss, you need just over seventy-one percent. You're at seventy, so you're under the line. Now take someone right only forty percent of the time, but whose wins are twice their losses. They need just a third. They're well above it, and they make about a fifth of their risk on every trade.

So I'll just widen my target. Bigger wins.

That's the trap. In a market with no edge, a wider target gets hit less often, by exactly the amount that cancels the bigger payoff. Move from one times your risk to three times, and your hit rate drops from a half to a quarter. Expected value stays at zero, and costs make it negative.

Then where does an edge come from?

From beating that no-edge hit rate. Start from it as your base rate. Count how often your setup actually worked in the past. And if the sample is small, pull the count back toward the base rate, because twelve wins in thirty cases could easily be luck.

How long before my results prove it?

Longer than you'd like. For the forty percent trader with a fifth of a unit of edge, it takes around two hundred trades before the record can tell the edge from luck.

Has a high win rate ever really blown up?

Short volatility in early twenty eighteen. Through the calm of twenty seventeen it made a little money almost every month. On the fifth of February the VIX more than doubled in a day, and the best known of those products lost more than ninety percent. The win rate was real. The size of the rare loss was the number nobody had measured.

The one-line version?

Your edge is how far your win rate sits above the break-even line for your payoff, after costs. Not how often you're right.

Section 1 · How it reached trading

From the card table to the desk

How it reached trading

Edward Thorp's Beat the Dealer (1962) showed that blackjack's expected value changes from hand to hand, and that a player should bet more exactly when it turns positive. He took the same idea to markets: Beat the Market (1967) and then a hedge fund.

Discretionary traders learned it as "expectancy" in R-multiples (profit ÷ initial risk), a vocabulary popularised in the 1990s.

Section 2

40% can beat 70%

E = p·b − (1 − p) − c

Measured in R, where 1R is what you lose at the stop. b = average win ÷ average loss. c = cost per trade.

Trader A · right 70%

Wins pay 0.4R. E = 0.7×0.4 − 0.3 = −0.02R. After a 0.05R cost, −0.07R. Break-even win rate 71.4%.

Trader B · right 40%

Wins pay 2R. E = 0.4×2 − 0.6 = +0.20R. After a 0.05R cost, +0.15R. Break-even win rate 33.3%.

What matters is how far your win rate sits above the break-even rate for your payoff, not how high it is.

Section 4

Moving the target doesn't create an edge

In a market with no edge (a driftless random walk), the chance of hitting target T before stop S is

P(target first) = S / (S + T) = 1 / (1 + b)

That is exactly the break-even win rate. Every stop and target combination has EV = 0 before costs, and −c after. A 1R target hits 50% of the time, a 3R target 25%, and both average zero.

Use it

S/(S+T) is the base rate for any stop-and-target trade. Your analysis has to beat that number. Picking a different target can't do it for you.

Section 5

Your analysis didn't give a probability. Build one.

1

Base rate

No analysis at all: S/(S+T), or the long-run share of up-weeks.

2

Count

Past cases with the same reading, same exits: k wins of n. Use only what was knowable then.

3

Shrink

p̂ = (k + m·p₀) / (n + m). With small n you stay near the base rate.

4

Range → odds

Vol forecast σ and target d: P(close beyond) = 1 − Φ(d/σ); P(touch) ≈ twice that.

Section 7 · Worked example

Long EUR/USD 1.0850, stop 1.0810, target 1.0950

1 · S = 40 pips = 1R, T = 100 pips → b = 2.5; cost 1.5 pips → c = 0.0375R
2 · Base rate S/(S+T) = 40/140 = 28.6%
3 · Back-check: target first in 12 of 30 similar cases (40%)
4 · Shrink, m = 30: (12 + 8.57)/60 = 34.3% (raw SE ±8.9 pts)
5 · E = 0.343×2.5 − 0.657 − 0.0375 = +0.16R ≈ 6.5 pips a trade
6 · Break-even (1.0375)/3.5 = 29.6% → margin 4.6 pts, half an SE
7 · Decision: take it, reduced size, log it

With the raw 40% you'd have believed +0.36R, more than double. Most of the work is in getting the probability right.

Section 9 · It actually happened

February 2018: short volatility

Through the calm of 2017, being short volatility, often through notes that rose when the VIX fell, made a little money most days. The hit rate was high and the curve was smooth.

On 5 February 2018 the VIX more than doubled in one session. Credit Suisse's XIV note lost more than 90% of its value in a day and was terminated.

The EV reading

Many small wins tell you the win rate. The sign of EV depended on the rare loss: how big it was and how often it came, which was the number nobody had measured well.

Section 11 · The desk's verdict

What was tested here

Desk test: held upThe audited 2-year yield-spread FX sleeve, as EV: ~109 out-of-sample trades, 63% win, profit factor 2.2. So b ≈ 2.2×0.37/0.63 ≈ 1.29 and E = 0.37×(2.2−1) ≈ 0.44 average losses per trade. That is about 19 points above its ≈44% break-even. Backtest evidence; not forward-proven.
Desk data: base rateHow far a backtest win rate can fall: one Fibonacci-level system, 85.7% in backtest, 39.6% live. EV can't be better than its p.

Section 12

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

45% win rate, average win 1.5R, average loss 1R, no cost. EV?

0.45×1.5 − 0.55 = 0.675 − 0.55 = +0.125R. Break-even is 1/2.5 = 40%.
Question 2

No-edge market. You move the target from 1R to 3R, same stop. EV before costs?

The hit rate drops from 50% to 25% (S/(S+T)). 0.5×1 − 0.5 = 0 and 0.25×3 − 0.75 = 0.
Question 3

A setup "worked 9 of 12 times". Base rate 50%, prior weight 12. What p goes into EV?

(9 + 12×0.5)/(12 + 12) = 15/24 = 62.5%. Twelve cases can't pin down 75%.
Question 4

Weekly σ 1.5%, target 3% away, no drift. Chance of closing the week beyond it?

d/σ = 2, and 1 − Φ(2) ≈ 2.3%. Touching it during the week is about twice that, ≈ 4.6%.
Your call
Scenario drill · 31 January 2018

A short-volatility note that almost never loses

Through the very calm markets of 2017 you have held an exchange-traded note that rises when the VIX falls. Most days, and most months, it has made a little money. The equity curve is smooth and the hit rate is high. A friend says you should double the position, since it "basically never loses". What do you do?

  1. Double it. A hit rate that high is proof of an edge.
    A hit rate on its own says nothing. EV also needs the size of the losing side, and with a short-volatility note the rare loss can be a large multiple of a typical win. A long run of small wins is exactly what this shape looks like before the loss arrives.
  2. Write out the EV with the loss side made explicit: how big the rare loss could be and how often it might come. Stress both, then size so that loss is survivable.
    This is the lesson's method. With a high win rate and a huge, rare loss, the sign of EV depends almost entirely on the loss you have not seen yet. If you cannot pin that number down, size as if it is bad.
  3. Keep the position as it is. It has worked, so there is no reason to change.
    Better than doubling, but it still judges the trade by its recent results. The question is whether the position survives the rare loss, and you have not asked it.
  4. Judge it by its smooth equity curve: low volatility means low risk.
    A smooth curve is what many small wins look like. It tells you the win rate, not the tail. The risk sits in the one outcome the curve has not shown you.

What happened. On 5 February 2018 U.S. stocks fell sharply and the VIX more than doubled in a single session. The best-known short-volatility note, Credit Suisse's XIV, lost more than 90% of its value in a day, and Credit Suisse announced it would terminate it. Several other short-volatility funds took heavy losses in the same days. The win rate had been real; the payoff on the losing side was the number nobody had measured well.

Sources: Cboe VIX daily close via FRED (17.31 on 2 February, 37.32 on 5 February 2018); Credit Suisse media release, "Credit Suisse AG Announces Event Acceleration of its XIV ETNs", 6 February 2018.

Section 13

What to carry forward

01

E = p·b − (1−p) − c, in R. A 40% win rate at 2R beats a 70% win rate at 0.4R.

02

Edge is the gap to break-even, (1+c)/(1+b), not the win rate.

03

With no edge, hit rate = S/(S+T) = break-even. Moving the target changes the hit rate, not EV.

04

Build p: base rate → count → shrink → range to odds.

05

EV needs hundreds of trades to show. Backtest win rates shrink live, as the desk's own records show.
