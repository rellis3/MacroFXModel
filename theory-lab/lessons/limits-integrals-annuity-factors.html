Limits, Integrals & Annuity Factors

The limit hiding underneath every derivative and integral, formalized properly and then aimed squarely at fixed income: duration as a derivative of bond price, continuous discounting as an integral, and the annuity factor derived from a finite geometric series through to its perpetuity and continuous-compounding limits.

CONCEPT — not built or tested in this repo ~18 min read Prereqs: Calculus for Finance (Deeper Foundations 2)
TL;DR
  • A limit formalizes "getting arbitrarily close without needing to actually arrive": \(\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = L\) — the machinery underneath every derivative and integral used anywhere in this curriculum.
  • The derivative (already built in Calculus for Finance as \(f'(x)=\lim_{h\to0}[f(x+h)-f(x)]/h\)) gets only a brief recap here — this lesson's real contribution is pushing it further, straight into duration: \(D_{mod} = -\frac{1}{P}\frac{dP}{dy}\), literally the derivative of bond price with respect to yield.
  • The integral as a limit of a Riemann sum turns the familiar discrete present-value sum into its continuous-time twin, \(PV=\int_0^T CF(t)e^{-rt}\,dt\) — the same accumulation idea, applied to a continuum of cash flows instead of discrete coupon dates.
  • The annuity factor is derived properly from scratch: starting at the finite geometric series \(A(n,y)=\sum_{t=1}^n(1+y)^{-t}\), multiplying by \((1+y)\), subtracting, and solving gives the closed form \(A(n,y)=\frac{1-(1+y)^{-n}}{y}\) — algebra, not an assertion.
  • Two limits sit inside that one formula: letting \(n\to\infty\) gives the perpetuity value \(A\to1/y\); letting the compounding frequency \(m\to\infty\) within a period recovers continuous compounding and connects the annuity factor directly to \(e^{-rt}\).
  • Duration, DV01, and the instantaneous forward rate \(f(t)=\frac{d}{dt}[t\cdot r(t)]\) are all, without exception, a derivative or a limit wearing a trading-desk name — not a separate piece of fixed-income folklore.
Section 01

Why this matters

Every fixed-income formula in this curriculum — duration, convexity, DV01, the annuity factor itself, and forward-rate extraction from a yield curve — is a limit, a derivative, or an integral wearing a trading-desk name. Bond Pricing & Yield to Maturity builds the pricing formula this lesson differentiates. Swap Curves & OIS Discounting already uses the term "annuity factor" for a swap's fixed-leg PV01 basis without deriving where it comes from — this lesson is that derivation, worked from first principles instead of taken on faith. Curve Fitting: Nelson-Siegel & Svensson and Short-Rate Models: Vasicek, CIR & Hull-White both quietly lean on the forward-rate-as-derivative relationship this lesson makes explicit near the end.

A practitioner who can only plug numbers into these formulas, without seeing the calculus underneath, will misapply them exactly when it matters most: a large rate move where a linear (first-derivative-only) duration estimate quietly breaks down, an unusual compounding convention that silently changes an annuity factor's exact value, or a curve-fitting edge case where the underlying limit stops being a good approximation of the discrete reality it's standing in for.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Getting arbitrarily close, without needing to arrive

A limit is a genuinely honest, non-hand-wavy idea, even in plain English: it describes what a quantity approaches as some input is pushed arbitrarily close to a target — without that input ever needing to actually reach the target itself. (The fully rigorous version — for any tolerance you name, however small, inputs sufficiently close to the target produce outputs within that tolerance — is the formal "epsilon-delta" definition; it's mentioned here for completeness, not derived, because this is a finance curriculum, not a real-analysis course. Everything below only needs the informal reading.)

Two concrete fixed-income pictures use exactly that idea, and both recur constantly for the rest of this lesson. Duration is "how fast the bond's price wiggles for a tiny wiggle in yield" — take the average wiggle-per-wiggle over a shrinking gap in yield, and the value that gets approached as the gap shrinks to nothing is the derivative \(dP/dy\). The annuity factor is "what a stream of $1-per-period payments is worth today" — built by adding up discount factors one payment at a time, a sum that for an ordinary bond stops after a fixed number of years, but which the perpetuity case pushes to literally infinitely many terms, another limit entirely.

Section 02

The math, step by step

What a limit formally captures

A limit answers a precise question: as an input \(x\) is pushed arbitrarily close to a target value \(a\) — without necessarily ever equaling \(a\) — what value does \(f(x)\) get arbitrarily close to? Written formally:

\[ \lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L \]
x → athe input approaching, but not necessarily reaching, the target
Lthe single value the output settles arbitrarily close to

The most important limit already in this curriculum is the one Calculus for Finance built the derivative from — the difference quotient, the average rate of change of \(f\) over a shrinking gap \(h\):

\[ f'(x) = \lim_{h\to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h} \]

That is a limit in exactly the formal sense above — the input being pushed toward a target is \(h\), the target is \(0\), and \(L\) is the derivative's value at \(x\). This lesson doesn't re-derive that limit (Calculus for Finance already works a full first-principles example, \(d/dx(x^2)=2x\)); the job here is to take the derivative and the integral it recaps and push both further than that earlier lesson had room to.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine walking toward a door that's always exactly half the remaining distance away, then half of what's left, then half of that. You never technically touch the door, but you can get closer to it than any distance anyone names, no matter how tiny. A limit is a name for the spot you're closing in on — not a spot you necessarily ever stand on, just the one place you can get arbitrarily near.

From derivative to duration: differentiating the bond-pricing formula

Recall the bond-pricing formula from Bond Pricing & Yield to Maturity: a bond's price is the sum of every cash flow \(CF_t\) (a coupon, or the final coupon plus face value at maturity) discounted at yield \(y\):

\[ P(y) = \sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{CF_t}{(1+y)^t} \]

Differentiate term by term with respect to \(y\), using the power rule \(\frac{d}{dy}(1+y)^{-t} = -t(1+y)^{-t-1}\) on each term:

\[ \frac{dP}{dy} = -\sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{t \cdot CF_t}{(1+y)^{t+1}} \]
t·CFₜeach cash flow, weighted by how many periods away it lands
(1+y)^{t+1}one extra power of (1+y) than the price formula itself has

Every term in that sum is negative (all the pieces are positive numbers with a minus sign out front), which is exactly the algebraic reason price and yield move in strictly opposite directions — the fact Bond Pricing & Yield to Maturity established without yet showing the derivative behind it. Modified duration is defined as the negative of that derivative, scaled by price, so that the sign convention practitioners actually quote comes out positive:

\[ D_{mod} = -\frac{1}{P}\frac{dP}{dy} \]

A small, fully worked example makes this concrete rather than abstract. Take a 2-year, $1,000 face bond with a 5% annual coupon (\(C=\$50\)), priced at a 5% yield — a par bond, so \(P=\$1{,}000\) exactly:

\[ \frac{dP}{dy} = -\left[\frac{1\times 50}{(1.05)^2} + \frac{2\times 1{,}050}{(1.05)^3}\right] = -\left[45.35 + 1{,}814.06\right] = -1{,}859.41 \]
\[ D_{mod} = -\frac{1}{1{,}000}\times(-1{,}859.41) = 1.8594 \text{ years} \]

Every input to that calculation is a number already sitting in the pricing formula — no new machinery, just the same formula differentiated. The trading-desk quantity built directly on top of \(D_{mod}\) is DV01 (dollar value of a one-basis-point move): multiply modified duration by price and by 0.0001 (one basis point as a decimal) to get the dollar price change for a 1bp yield move, \(DV01 = P \times D_{mod} \times 0.0001\) — the exact quantity the hedging scenario below is built around.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Duration answers one question: if the interest rate wiggled by a tiny amount, how much would the bond's price wiggle in response? That's a speedometer reading, exactly like the Calculus for Finance lesson's car analogy — except the "road" is the yield and the "distance traveled" is the bond's price. Differentiating the pricing formula is just reading that speedometer directly off the math instead of guessing.

From integral to continuous-time discounting

An integral is the sibling limit: instead of a single ratio shrinking toward an instant, it's a sum of many thin slices shrinking toward a continuum. Chop the interval \([a,b]\) into slices of width \(\Delta t\), evaluate \(f\) at each slice, add them up, then let the slices become infinitesimally thin:

\[ \int_a^b f(t)\,dt = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \sum_{i} f(t_i)\,\Delta t \]

Applied to discounting, the ordinary discrete present-value sum \(P=\sum_t CF_t/(1+y)^t\) is itself already a sum of discrete slices — one cash flow per coupon date. Imagine instead a continuum of infinitesimal cash flows \(CF(t)\,dt\) arriving at every instant rather than on discrete coupon dates, each discounted continuously at rate \(r\) (the \(e^{-rt}\) discount factor from Time Value of Money). Letting the gap between payment dates shrink to zero turns the discrete sum into exactly this integral:

\[ PV = \int_0^T CF(t)\,e^{-rt}\,dt \]
CF(t)the cash-flow rate at instant t — dollars per unit time, not a single lump payment
e^{-rt}the continuous-compounding discount factor for time t

Nothing about this integral is exotic once the discrete sum is already familiar — it's the identical accumulation idea, just applied to a continuum of cash flows instead of a handful of discrete ones. This is exactly the parallel structure the annuity factor's own continuous limit reuses two sections below.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Picture a bond's coupons arriving less and less far apart — annually, then monthly, then every second — while each individual payment shrinks proportionally so the total paid per year stays the same. In the limit, it's not a handful of lump payments anymore, it's a smooth trickle of cash. Adding up a smooth trickle, instead of a countable list of payments, is exactly what an integral does.

The annuity factor, derived from a finite geometric series

An annuity factor is the present value of a stream of $1-per-period payments — the coupon-only building block sitting inside every coupon-bearing bond's price, and (per Swap Curves & OIS Discounting) exactly the sum a swap's fixed leg needs to be valued. Define it as a finite geometric series with \(n\) terms and common ratio \(x=(1+y)^{-1}\):

\[ A(n,y) = \sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{1}{(1+y)^t} = x + x^2 + \cdots + x^n \]

The closed form isn't asserted — it falls out of a short, standard trick: multiply the whole series by \((1+y)\) (equivalently, by \(1/x\)), then subtract the original series from that.

1

Multiply by (1+y)

Multiplying every term of \(A\) by \((1+y)\) shifts each power of \(x\) down by one — \((1+y)\cdot x^t = x^{t-1}\) — so the series becomes:

\[ (1+y)A = 1 + x + x^2 + \cdots + x^{n-1} \]
2

Subtract the original series

Line the two series up. Every middle term (\(x\) through \(x^{n-1}\)) appears in both and cancels exactly, leaving only the leading 1 from the shifted series and the trailing \(x^n\) from the original:

\[ (1+y)A - A = \big(1 + x + \cdots + x^{n-1}\big) - \big(x + x^2 + \cdots + x^n\big) = 1 - x^n \]
3

Solve for A

The left side simplifies to \(yA\), since \((1+y)A - A = A[(1+y)-1] = yA\). Substituting \(x=(1+y)^{-1}\) back in:

\[ yA = 1-(1+y)^{-n} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad A(n,y) = \frac{1-(1+y)^{-n}}{y} \]

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

This is the exact same "multiply, subtract, solve" trick used for any repeating pattern that shrinks by the same factor each step — like figuring out how much a pile of ever-smaller chocolate bars weighs in total without stacking every single one on a scale one at a time. You compare the pile to a slightly shifted copy of itself, notice that almost everything overlaps and cancels, and only the two mismatched ends are left to deal with.

Two limits inside the annuity factor

The closed form \(A(n,y)=\frac{1-(1+y)^{-n}}{y}\) has exactly two limits worth taking, corresponding to two different things that can be pushed toward infinity.

n → ∞ — the perpetuity limit

For \(y>0\), \((1+y)^{-n}\to 0\) as \(n\to\infty\), since raising a number greater than 1 to an ever-larger power sends its reciprocal to zero. So \(A(n,y)\to \frac{1-0}{y}=\frac{1}{y}\) — a perpetuity, a stream of $1 payments running forever, is worth exactly \(1/y\) today. The chart below shows this convergence directly.

m → ∞ within a period — the continuous-compounding limit

Compounding \(m\) times within a period (per Time Value of Money's \(\lim_{m\to\infty}(1+r/m)^m=e^r\)) sends each discrete discount factor \((1+y)^{-t}\) to \(e^{-yt}\). The annuity sum becomes the continuous-time integral \(\int_0^n e^{-yt}\,dt = \frac{1-e^{-yn}}{y}\) — the same shape, with \((1+y)^{-n}\) simply replaced by \(e^{-yn}\).

Notice the structural echo: the continuous annuity integral above is just the general continuous-time PV integral, \(PV=\int_0^T CF(t)e^{-rt}dt\), with the constant cash-flow rate \(CF(t)=1\) plugged in and evaluated. The two limits this lesson builds — the derivative behind duration and the integral behind continuous discounting — aren't separate ideas colliding by coincidence here; the annuity factor's continuous limit is a special case of the integral already derived above.

Forward rates as a derivative of the yield curve

Curve Fitting: Nelson-Siegel & Svensson fits a smooth function \(r(t)\) — the zero-coupon (spot) yield to maturity \(t\) — through observed market prices. A zero-coupon bond maturing at \(t\) prices at \(P(t)=e^{-r(t)\,t}\) under continuous compounding, so its cumulative yield exposure is \(t\cdot r(t)\). The instantaneous forward rate — the rate the market implicitly expects to prevail for an infinitesimally short period starting at \(t\), exactly what short-rate models like Vasicek, CIR, and Hull-White attempt to describe dynamically — is defined as the derivative of that cumulative yield:

\[ f(t) = \frac{d}{dt}\big[t \cdot r(t)\big] \]

Applying the product rule (differentiating a product of two functions of \(t\), \(t\) itself and \(r(t)\)) expands this to:

\[ f(t) = r(t) + t\cdot r'(t) \]
r(t)today's spot yield to maturity t
t·r'(t)a correction for how steeply the spot curve is sloping right at t

Where the yield curve is flat (\(r'(t)=0\)), the forward rate equals the spot yield exactly. Where the curve slopes upward (\(r'(t)\) positive), the forward rate sits above the spot yield at that maturity — a derivative of a fitted curve, wearing yet another trading-desk name.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

The spot yield is like your average speed for an entire road trip so far. The forward rate is your speed right now, at this exact point on the trip. If your average speed has been climbing the whole way (an upward-sloping curve), your current speed has to be running a bit above that average just to keep pulling it up — that's exactly what the extra \(t\cdot r'(t)\) term captures.

Worked example

A 5-year, $1-per-year annuity at 4%: from the geometric series to duration

Take the simplest possible case of the closed form just derived: a 5-year stream of $1 payments (\(n=5\)) at a 4% yield (\(y=0.04\)) — the coupon-only building block that sits inside any coupon-bearing bond's price.

1

Sum the five discount factors directly, as a check

\[ 0.96154+0.92456+0.88900+0.85480+0.82193 = 4.45182 \]
2

Confirm it against the closed form

\[ A(5,0.04) = \frac{1-(1.04)^{-5}}{0.04} = \frac{1-0.82193}{0.04} = \frac{0.17807}{0.04} \approx 4.4518 \]

The two methods agree to four decimal places, exactly as they must — the closed form is nothing more than the same five-term sum, algebraically compressed.

3

Differentiate the annuity factor itself, for its duration

Applying the same differentiation move used on the bond-pricing formula above, but to \(A(n,y)\) directly (via the quotient rule on \(\frac{1-(1+y)^{-n}}{y}\)) and simplifying gives a clean closed form for the modified duration of a level annuity — verified algebraically, not merely asserted:

\[ D_{mod} = \frac{1}{y} - \frac{n}{(1+y)\big[(1+y)^n-1\big]} \]
\[ D_{mod} = \frac{1}{0.04} - \frac{5}{1.04\times\big[(1.04)^5-1\big]} = 25 - \frac{5}{1.04\times 0.21665} = 25 - 22.191 \approx 2.809 \text{ years} \]

And since \(D_{mod}=D_{Mac}/(1+y)\), the Macaulay duration is \(D_{Mac} = 2.809\times 1.04 \approx 2.922\) years — a little under 3 years, which makes sense: the payments run out to year 5, but the earlier, larger-weighted discount factors on the closer payments pull the cash-flow-weighted average time back well below the final maturity.

4

Cross-check the closed form with a numerical finite difference

Evaluate the annuity factor at \(y=3.9\%\) and \(y=4.1\%\) (a symmetric ±10bp bump around 4%) and take the central difference, exactly the finite-difference practice covered in the practitioner section below:

\[ A(0.039) \approx 4.4644 \qquad A(0.041) \approx 4.4393 \]
\[ \frac{A(0.041)-A(0.039)}{0.002} \approx -12.51 \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad D_{mod} \approx \frac{12.51}{4.4518} \approx 2.810 \]

The finite-difference estimate (2.810) and the closed-form derivative (2.809) agree to within about 0.04% — the small residual is exactly the kind of discretization error the Calculus for Finance lesson's Taylor-series section already quantified, not a sign anything here is wrong. Two independent routes to the same number is exactly the point: duration isn't a separately memorized formula, it's this annuity factor, differentiated.

Real-world trading scenario

A rates desk hedges a short bond position with DV01, not intuition

A rates desk that market-makes 10-year Treasury notes has just sold (gone short) \(\$10{,}000{,}000\) face value of a 10-year note, priced near par at 100, with a modified duration of 8.50 years. Before the trader can walk away flat, the desk needs to know exactly how many 10-year Treasury futures contracts to buy to neutralize that yield exposure — not a rough guess, a specific number derived from the exact derivative built above.

\[ DV01_{\text{position}} = \frac{100}{100}\times 10{,}000{,}000 \times 8.50 \times 0.0001 = \$8{,}500 \text{ per basis point} \]

Being short the bond, a 1bp fall in yield (bond price rises) costs the desk $8,500; a 1bp rise in yield (bond price falls) gains the desk $8,500. To offset that, the desk needs a position that moves the opposite way per basis point — and a long futures position, like being long the underlying bond, gains when yields fall and loses when yields rise, exactly the mirror image of the short cash position. If the 10-year Treasury future's DV01 is approximately $85 per contract (an illustrative, CTD-dependent figure — real desks compute this from the future's own cheapest-to-deliver bond, not a round number), the hedge ratio is a plain division:

\[ N_{\text{contracts}} = \frac{DV01_{\text{position}}}{DV01_{\text{per contract}}} = \frac{8{,}500}{85} = 100 \]

The desk buys 100 futures contracts. Check the hedge: if yields rise 1bp, the short bond gains $8,500 while the 100 long futures lose \(100\times\$85=\$8{,}500\) — net roughly flat. If yields fall 1bp, the short bond loses $8,500 while the long futures gain $8,500 — again roughly flat. Every piece of that hedge ratio is the derivative \(dP/dy\) from this lesson's math section, computed once for the cash bond and once (implicitly, inside the quoted futures DV01) for the future, then divided.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine you're holding a seesaw that's about to tip one way, and you have a second, smaller seesaw you can load up to balance it. DV01 tells you exactly how heavy each side tips for a tiny nudge. Once you know that number for both seesaws, balancing them isn't a guess — it's just dividing one number by the other to find out how many small seesaws it takes to cancel the big one.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

Duration and DV01 are almost never hand-differentiated in production. The closed forms this lesson derives are exactly right for a plain-vanilla bond or annuity, but most real fixed-income instruments — callable bonds, mortgage-backed securities, structured notes with embedded optionality — have no clean closed-form price formula to differentiate at all. Practitioners instead compute effective duration numerically: bump the entire yield curve up by a small amount, reprice the instrument through the full pricing model, bump it down by the same amount, reprice again, and take the finite difference — literally the same central-difference technique used to cross-check the closed-form annuity duration in the worked example above, just applied to instruments where no closed form exists to check it against.

\[ D_{\text{eff}} \approx -\frac{1}{P}\cdot\frac{P(y+\epsilon)-P(y-\epsilon)}{2\epsilon} \]

The annuity factor shows up directly, by name, in swap valuation. Swap Curves & OIS Discounting defines the par swap rate as \(K_{\text{par}}=\frac{1-DF(T_n)}{\sum_{i=1}^n DF(t_i)}\) and calls that denominator the swap's "annuity factor" without deriving it — it's exactly \(A(n,y)\) from this lesson, generalized to a possibly non-flat discount curve. A swap's fixed leg is, structurally, nothing more than a level annuity; the same closed form and the same duration derived above apply directly, which is precisely why interest-rate swap risk is often quoted as "PV01" (present value of one basis point) — the same DV01 concept, applied to the fixed leg's annuity.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where this goes wrong in practice

Treating duration as valid for large yield moves. Duration is a first-derivative, local linear approximation — exactly the first-order Taylor term the Calculus for Finance lesson warned about. For a small yield move it's accurate; for a large one, the price-yield curve's genuine curvature (its second derivative, the subject of the follow-on Duration & Convexity lesson) pulls the true price away from the duration-only estimate, and ignoring that correction is the single most common misuse of duration in practice.

Assuming "the annuity factor" means one universal number for a given bond. Day-count conventions (actual/360, 30/360, actual/365) and compounding frequency (annual, semiannual, continuous) all change the exact value of \(A(n,y)\) for what colloquially gets called "the same bond" — exactly the kind of convention mismatch Swap Curves & OIS Discounting flags as a common real-world error. An annuity factor is only meaningful once its compounding and day-count convention are pinned down.

Confusing Macaulay duration with modified duration. They are related but not interchangeable: Macaulay duration \(D_{Mac}\) is a cash-flow-weighted average time (in years); modified duration \(D_{mod}\) is a price sensitivity (percent price change per unit yield change). The precise relationship, for a bond compounding \(m\) times per year, is:

\[ D_{mod} = \frac{D_{Mac}}{1+y/m} \]

Using one where the other is meant — quoting a Macaulay duration as if it directly gave a percentage price sensitivity, or vice versa — silently produces a wrong number by a factor of \((1+y/m)\), usually small but never exactly zero.

Key Takeaways

✓A limit is "arbitrarily close, without needing to arrive." Every derivative and every integral in this curriculum is built from exactly that one idea, applied to a ratio or to a sum.
✓Duration is a derivative. \(D_{mod}=-\frac{1}{P}\frac{dP}{dy}\) — differentiate the bond-pricing formula with respect to yield, flip the sign, normalize by price. Nothing more exotic.
✓Continuous-time discounting is an integral. \(PV=\int_0^T CF(t)e^{-rt}dt\) is the discrete PV sum's limit as payment dates get infinitesimally close together.
✓The annuity factor is a finite geometric series, derived not asserted. \(A(n,y)=\frac{1-(1+y)^{-n}}{y}\) comes from the standard "multiply, subtract, solve" trick — the same one that sums any geometric series.
✓Two limits sit inside that one formula. \(n\to\infty\) gives the perpetuity value \(1/y\); compounding frequency \(m\to\infty\) recovers continuous compounding and connects directly to \(e^{-rt}\).
⚠Duration is a local, first-order approximation. It degrades for large yield moves without a convexity correction, and gets silently distorted by day-count/compounding convention mismatches or by confusing Macaulay with modified duration.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. In plain language, what does \(\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = L\) actually assert, and why does the "epsilon-delta" formalization matter even though this lesson doesn't derive it in full?
It asserts that as \(x\) is pushed arbitrarily close to \(a\) (without necessarily ever equaling \(a\)), \(f(x)\) gets arbitrarily close to a single value \(L\). The epsilon-delta formalization exists to make "arbitrarily close" precise and testable — for any tolerance (epsilon) around \(L\) you name, however small, there's a corresponding closeness (delta) to \(a\) that guarantees landing inside that tolerance. It's mentioned rather than derived because the informal reading is sufficient for every calculation this lesson actually performs — the point is honesty about what the informal version is standing in for, not full rigor.
Q2. Starting from the bond-pricing formula \(P(y)=\sum_t CF_t/(1+y)^t\), derive \(dP/dy\), and explain why every term in that derivative comes out negative.
Differentiating term by term with the power rule, \(\frac{d}{dy}(1+y)^{-t} = -t(1+y)^{-t-1}\), gives \(dP/dy = -\sum_t \frac{t\cdot CF_t}{(1+y)^{t+1}}\). Every term is negative because \(t\), \(CF_t\), and \((1+y)^{t+1}\) are all positive for a plain bond, and the minus sign from the power rule is applied uniformly to every term — there is no configuration where raising yield increases any individual discounted cash flow. That's the algebraic proof, not just an empirical tendency, behind "price and yield move in opposite directions" from Bond Pricing & Yield to Maturity.
Q3. Derive the annuity factor's closed form from the finite geometric series \(A=x+x^2+\cdots+x^n\) (with \(x=(1+y)^{-1}\)), showing the multiply-and-subtract step explicitly.
Multiply the series by \((1+y)=1/x\): every term shifts down one power, giving \((1+y)A = 1+x+\cdots+x^{n-1}\). Subtract the original series from this: every middle term from \(x\) through \(x^{n-1}\) appears in both and cancels exactly, leaving \((1+y)A-A = 1-x^n\). The left side simplifies to \(yA\), since \((1+y)A-A=A[(1+y)-1]=yA\). Substituting \(x=(1+y)^{-1}\) and dividing both sides by \(y\) gives \(A(n,y)=\frac{1-(1+y)^{-n}}{y}\) — the closed form, obtained purely by algebra rather than asserted.
Q4. Explain both limits that can be taken on \(A(n,y)=\frac{1-(1+y)^{-n}}{y}\), what each one represents financially, and why they are genuinely different limits (different variables going to infinity).
Letting \(n\to\infty\) with \(y\) fixed sends \((1+y)^{-n}\to 0\) (since \(y>0\) makes \((1+y)>1\), and raising a number greater than 1 to an ever-larger power sends its reciprocal to zero), so \(A\to 1/y\) — the value of a perpetuity, a $1-per-period stream running forever. Letting the compounding frequency \(m\to\infty\) within a fixed period instead uses a completely different limit, \(\lim_{m\to\infty}(1+r/m)^m=e^r\), which sends each discrete discount factor \((1+y)^{-t}\) to \(e^{-yt}\) and turns the discrete sum into the continuous integral \(\int_0^n e^{-yt}dt=\frac{1-e^{-yn}}{y}\). They're different limits because they push different quantities to infinity — the number of periods in one case, the number of compounding sub-divisions within each period in the other — and they answer different financial questions (how much is an infinitely long cash-flow stream worth, versus what happens as discrete compounding becomes continuous).
Q5. In the worked example, the closed-form modified duration (2.809 years) and the central finite-difference estimate (2.810 years) don't match exactly. Is that a mistake, and why or why not?
No — it's the expected, correct behavior of a finite difference. The closed-form derivative is the exact limit as the bump size \(h\to 0\); the central finite difference uses a small but genuinely non-zero \(h=0.001\) (10bp each side), so it's approximating that limit, not computing it exactly. The residual gap (about 0.04%) is precisely the discretization error the Calculus for Finance lesson's Taylor-series section already quantified for finite step sizes — a real, expected artifact of using a finite \(h\) rather than evidence anything in the derivation is wrong. A smaller \(h\) would shrink that residual further, converging toward the closed-form answer.
Q6. What is the precise relationship between Macaulay duration and modified duration, and what specifically goes wrong if the two are treated as interchangeable?
\(D_{mod} = D_{Mac}/(1+y/m)\), where \(m\) is the compounding frequency per year. Macaulay duration is a cash-flow-weighted average time to receive a bond's payments, measured in years; modified duration is a percentage price sensitivity to a unit change in yield. Treating them as interchangeable — for instance, using a quoted Macaulay duration directly as if it were the percentage price move per unit yield change — silently misstates the sensitivity by a factor of \((1+y/m)\). That factor is usually modest (a few percent for typical yields and annual or semiannual compounding) but is never exactly 1, so the substitution is never exactly correct, only approximately so.
