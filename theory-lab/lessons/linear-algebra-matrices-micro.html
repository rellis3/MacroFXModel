Deeper Foundations 1

Vectors & Matrices, the Machines Behind PCA and Kalman

Three lessons later in this Theory Lab — PCA, the Kalman Filter, Hidden Markov Models — all quietly assume you already know what an "eigenvector" is and why a matrix gets "multiplied through" a state. None of them stop to explain it. This deck does: what a vector and a matrix actually are, what an eigenvector and eigenvalue mean geometrically, and why every one of those three lessons turns out to be this same small idea, wearing a different name.

Section 1

A vector is an arrow — or a list of numbers

Two ways to picture the exact same thing: v = (3, 1).

Picture it
As a point: walk 3 right, 1 up, put a dot. As an arrow: same destination, but now it carries a direction and a length too. Same numbers, two pictures — you'll want both.
▲

Just an ordered list

v = (v₁, v₂) in 2-D. Nothing more exotic than two numbers written in a fixed order — order matters, (3,1) and (1,3) are different vectors.

↗

Direction and length

Read as an arrow, a vector carries both — which way it points, and how far. Two different arrows can point the same way but have different lengths.

💹

What it means here

In this repo, a vector might be two FX pairs' daily returns on one day, a 2-D hidden-state estimate in a Kalman filter, or a row of factor loadings — different meanings, identical arithmetic.

Section 2

Adding and scaling, geometrically

The two operations everything else in this lesson is built from.

Try it
Walk vector a from the origin, then walk vector b starting exactly where a ended (tip-to-tail). Where you land is a+b — the green arrow, drawn straight from the origin, ends at the same spot.
Multiplying a vector by a plain number ("scaling") stretches it (2v — twice as long, same direction) or flips it end to end (−v — same line, opposite way). It never rotates the vector off its own line. Hold onto that fact — it's the entire definition of an eigenvector, two sections from now.

Combining two trades' P&L, or two return series, is vector addition. Sizing a position up or down without changing its direction — long is still long, just bigger or smaller — is scaling. Both are the whole toolkit a matrix will use next.

Section 3

A matrix is a machine that moves every vector

Not "a table of numbers" — a fixed rule that takes any vector in and hands a new one back.

↻

Rotate

Swing every vector around the origin by some angle, lengths unchanged.

↕

Stretch or shrink

Lengthen or shorten vectors along one or more directions.

▱

Shear

Tilt the plane so squares become parallelograms, one axis dragged sideways.

•

The origin never moves

Whatever the mix of the above, straight lines stay straight and (0,0) always maps to (0,0) — that's what "linear" means here.

Worked by hand
v = (5, 1)
A
Av = (13, 9)
With A = [[2, 3], [1, 4]]: first output entry = 2(5) + 3(1) = 13; second entry = 1(5) + 4(1) = 9. Each row of A gets "dotted" with v — multiply matching entries, add the results.
Two multiplications and one addition per output row, always. That's the entire operation, however large the matrix.
Section 4

Chaining two machines — and order matters

MN means "apply N first, then apply M to the result." Compare it with NM.

MN — N first, then M

M = [[1,2],[0,1]], N = [[1,0],[1,1]].
MN = [[1(1)+2(1), 1(0)+2(1)], [0(1)+1(1), 0(0)+1(1)]] = [[3, 2], [1, 1]]

NM — M first, then N

Same two matrices, order flipped.
NM = [[1(1)+0(0), 1(2)+0(1)], [1(1)+1(0), 1(2)+1(1)]] = [[1, 2], [1, 3]]

MN ≠ NM — unlike ordinary numbers, where 3×5 always equals 5×3. "Squish then twist" is a genuinely different motion than "twist then squish," even with the exact same two machines. This is why, in the Kalman Filter lesson, the order a state vector gets multiplied through a transition matrix and an observation matrix is written with care, not treated as interchangeable arithmetic — swapping it is a real bug, not a harmless simplification.
Section 5

One direction never rotates. Watch it.

A = [[2, k], [0, 1]]. Drag k and watch the square, and the grey spokes, swing around — while the red arrow never does.

Try it
Shear amountk = 0.00
Grey spokes are eight ordinary vectors — every one of them changes direction as k moves. The red arrow, along the x-axis, is stretched exactly 2× and never rotates, whatever k is: that's an eigenvector, λ=2. The blue line is also an eigenvector (λ=1, length unchanged) — but which direction it sits in depends on k. Eigenvalues here are fixed at 2 and 1 for every k; only one eigenvector's direction is guaranteed not to move.
Section 6

An eigenvector is a direction the matrix respects

➡

Same line, different length

Feed the matrix a vector lying exactly along an eigenvector's direction, and the output is a scaled copy of the same vector — longer, shorter, or flipped, but never off that line through the origin.

Av=λv

The defining equation

The matrix's full action (rotate-and-scale, in general) and plain multiplication by a number produce an identical result — that coincidence is what makes v an eigenvector.

▢

Only square matrices qualify

Av and v must live in the same space to be compared at all — a non-square matrix maps between spaces of different size and simply has no eigenvectors in this sense.

A generic 2×2 matrix has at most two such directions. Most matrices that actually arise from real data — in particular, the symmetric matrices that covariance and correlation matrices always are — are guaranteed to have them, with a bonus: those eigenvectors come out mutually perpendicular.
Section 7

The eigenvalue is the stretch factor — and there's a formula for it

Av = λv rearranges into a plain quadratic in λ.

The characteristic equation
λ² − trace(A)·λ + det(A)
=
0
trace(A) = a + d (sum of the diagonal). det(A) = ad − bc. Solve this ordinary quadratic for λ and you have every eigenvalue A has — up to two of them, for a 2×2 matrix.
Once a specific λ is known, plug it back into (A − λI)v = 0 and solve that ordinary linear system for v, the matching eigenvector.

Larger λ means that direction is stretched more — and, in a covariance matrix, accounts for more of the total variance. Section 5's demo had trace = 2+1 = 3 and det = 2(1) − k(0) = 2, giving λ² − 3λ + 2 = 0, which factors to (λ−2)(λ−1) = 0 — exactly the 2 and 1 the slider never moved.

Section 9

Why PCA leans on exactly this

📊

Principal components ARE eigenvectors

Of a covariance matrix, ranked by their eigenvalues. Largest eigenvalue first means "the direction that explains the most variance, listed first."

✓

Covariance matrices are always symmetric

A = Aᵀ, guaranteed by construction — which guarantees real eigenvalues and mutually perpendicular eigenvectors, no extra assumption required.

The same matrix, as two FX pairs' covariance

A = [[3,1],[1,3]] as EUR/USD & GBP/USD daily return covariance: diagonal = each pair's own variance (3 and 3), off-diagonal = their covariance (1) — a correlation of 1/√(3×3) ≈ 0.33. Total variance = trace(A) = 6. The eigen-split: λ₁=4 along (1,1) — both pairs moving together — is 4/6 ≈ 66.7% of variance; λ₂=2 along (1,−1) — the pairs diverging — is the remaining 33.3%.

Effective independent bets = (Σλ)² / Σλ² = 6² / (4²+2²) = 36/20 = 1.8. Two nominal positions, but only about 1.8 truly independent ones — not "really just one bet," but measurably less diversified than two uncorrelated trades.
Where this needs qualifying

A correlation matrix estimated from a rolling window is a sample statistic, not a permanent structural fact — it shifts as the window rolls, and when two eigenvalues sit close together, small data changes can noticeably rotate the corresponding eigenvectors even while the eigenvalues themselves barely move.

Section 10

The same machinery, two more names

🧭

Kalman Filter

A state vector is repeatedly multiplied through a transition matrix and an observation matrix, and a covariance matrix is propagated through matrix multiplication at every update step — nothing but Av and AB, chained, in a load-bearing order.

🔀

Hidden Markov Models

Every row of a transition matrix is a probability distribution that sums to 1 — which guarantees the matrix always has an eigenvalue of exactly 1. Its eigenvector, normalized, is the "stationary distribution": Av = 1·v, nothing more exotic.

Nobody on a desk solves a characteristic equation by hand day to day — software does the arithmetic. The intuition that survives is: an eigenvector of a covariance matrix is a portfolio, and its eigenvalue is how much of the book's total variance that portfolio accounts for. Rank them by eigenvalue, and you've answered "in the fewest independent moves, what is this basket's risk actually made of?"
Section 11

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

A = [[2,3],[1,4]], v = (5,1). What is Av?

First entry: 2(5)+3(1)=13. Second entry: 1(5)+4(1)=9 — each row of A dotted with v.
Question 2

Is matrix multiplication commutative — does AB always equal BA?

MN = [[3,2],[1,1]] but NM = [[1,2],[1,3]] for the same two matrices — "apply N then M" is a genuinely different motion from "apply M then N."
Question 3

What makes a vector v an eigenvector of matrix A?

Av = λv means the matrix's full action and plain multiplication by the number λ produce an identical result for that specific v.
Question 4

Why are a covariance matrix's eigenvectors always guaranteed to be perpendicular?

Any symmetric matrix has mutually perpendicular eigenvectors and real eigenvalues, as a general theorem — and A = Aᵀ is exactly what a covariance or correlation matrix is by construction.
Question 5

A Hidden Markov Model's "stationary distribution" is the eigenvector of the transition matrix with eigenvalue equal to what?

Every row of a transition matrix sums to 1, which guarantees an eigenvalue of exactly 1 — its eigenvector, normalized, is the long-run stationary distribution.
Section 12

What to carry forward

01

A vector is an arrow (or an ordered list of numbers) — a point in space, or a direction and a length, same thing viewed two ways.

02

A matrix is a machine, not a table — Av rotates, stretches, shrinks, or shears every vector by one fixed rule, and the origin never moves.

03

Matrix multiplication composes two machines, and order matters — AB and BA are generally different composite motions.

04

An eigenvector is a direction the matrix only scales, never rotates off — Av = λv — and λ, the eigenvalue, is the amount of stretch. Solve λ² − trace(A)λ + det(A) = 0 to find every eigenvalue a matrix has.

05

Symmetric matrices — which covariance and correlation matrices always are — are guaranteed real eigenvalues and perpendicular eigenvectors, no extra assumption required.

06

This vocabulary is the direct prerequisite for PCA (eigenvectors of a correlation matrix), the Kalman Filter (chained matrix multiplication), and Hidden Markov Models (the stationary distribution is the eigenvector for eigenvalue 1).

What this now lets you do

Read "eigenvector of the correlation matrix," "propagate the covariance matrix," and "stationary distribution of the transition matrix" as three instances of the same small idea, not three separate walls of jargon. Compute a 2×2 matrix's eigenvalues and eigenvectors by hand, and recognise when a discriminant going negative means the matrix simply rotates, with no real eigenvector to find.
