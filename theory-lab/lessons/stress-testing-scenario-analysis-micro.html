Stress Testing, Scenario Analysis & Reverse Stress Tests

Coherent Risk Measures (Risk Deep Dive 1) built VaR and CVaR from a return series' ordinary, everyday range of outcomes. Tail Risk Hedging & Portfolio Convexity (Risk Deep Dive 2) showed why a payoff's shape, not just its expected return, decides whether a book bleeds slowly or blows up. This deck picks up exactly where both left off: what happens when the shock isn't ordinary at all — when correlations spike, tails cluster, and the statistics those two lessons relied on stop describing the world. Three techniques address it, and the third — asking "what would have to happen to lose an unacceptable amount" instead of "how bad could losses get" — is the one most likely to surface a risk nobody thought to look for.

Section 1

The statistics break exactly when it matters most

VaR and CVaR are built from a distribution assumption. Genuine crises are precisely the moments that assumption is least trustworthy.

📊

Built from ordinary days

VaR and CVaR summarize the range of outcomes a return series has actually produced — mostly calm, occasionally rough, rarely catastrophic.

🔗

Correlations aren't fixed

Assets that diversify each other in calm markets have repeatedly been observed moving together far more strongly once a real risk-off shock hits.

🌪

Tails cluster

A genuine crisis doesn't look like an ordinary bad day scaled up — it looks like a different regime, where several "unlikely" events happen together.

❓

History may not have shown it yet

A statistical summary can only describe the range of outcomes that has actually occurred in the sample it was built from.

This isn't a flaw unique to VaR and CVaR — it's a limit of any risk measure built purely from a historical or assumed statistical distribution. Stress testing exists to answer the question those measures were never built to answer: not "how risky does this look on an ordinary day," but "what happens to this exact book under conditions the ordinary-days statistics don't fully capture."
Section 2

Three ways to ask "what if"

A risk team typically layers all three, in this order.

1

Historical scenario replay

Take a real past shock — an actual severe quarter — and apply it to today's current portfolio. "If that exact storm hit again tomorrow, what happens to the house I live in right now?"

2

Monte Carlo scenario stress

Simulate thousands of hypothetical extreme scenarios from an assumed stress distribution, rather than relying only on what has literally happened before.

3

Reverse stress testing

Start from a specific unacceptable loss and solve backward for what combination of moves would actually produce it — the question nobody thought to ask first.

The direction each one runs

The first two start from a scenario and compute a loss — forward. The third starts from a loss and solves for the scenario — backward. Same underlying loss function, used in opposite directions.

The key design choice: today's weights, a past shock.

The point isn't "how did this portfolio do in 2008" — it may not have existed then, or held completely different positions. It's "if that exact historical shock repeated tomorrow, what happens to the book I actually hold right now."

Because the historical returns were jointly observed, every correlation and co-movement between factors is automatically correct — equities, credit spreads, and currencies really did move together that way, once, so nothing needs to be separately modeled. The honest limitation: the next crisis rarely repeats the last one's exact shape. Surviving a replayed 2008 comfortably is not evidence of surviving whatever comes next.

Section 4

Monte Carlo stress: imagine storms that haven't happened yet

Draw many hypothetical scenarios from an assumed stress distribution.

What it does

Assumes a stress-regime covariance matrix — commonly, correlations across risk factors moving toward 1, since diversifying assets have repeatedly been seen to move together far more than usual once a real crisis hits. Draws thousands of simulated return vectors from it, applies today's weights to each, and reads off a full simulated loss distribution rather than a single number.

What it buys you

It can explore scenarios that have literally never occurred in the historical record — not limited to replaying the past, the way historical replay is. That flexibility is also its weak point: the result is only as trustworthy as the assumed correlation regime and shock size fed into it.

Where this needs qualifying

An understated stress correlation or shock size produces a reassuring loss number for entirely the wrong reason — not because the book is genuinely safe, but because the simulated stress wasn't stressed enough. More Monte Carlo draws make the estimate smoother, never more accurate about a bad assumption.

Section 5

Reverse stress testing: start from the loss, solve backward

The question flipped — and the one most likely to surface what forward tests miss.

Unacceptable loss, Y*
→ solve backward →
Scenario, s*, that causes it

Forward tests pick a scenario and compute a loss. Reverse stress testing starts from the loss the risk committee has already decided is intolerable — insolvency, a breached capital floor — and solves for the shock, or combination of factor moves, that would actually produce it. With several risk factors, the useful output isn't the size of the shock alone — it's which combination of moves gets there.

A portfolio whose only route to an unacceptable loss requires several large, independent, implausible shocks together is structurally safer than one where a single, modest, entirely plausible move on one factor gets there alone. Finding out which kind of book you actually hold is the entire point — and it's a question a forward-looking scenario list never has to ask, because nobody had to think to construct that exact scenario first.

Section 10

Diversified on paper, concentrated underneath

The same $10M book — $6M short-vol equity carry (β=30%), $4M high-yield credit carry (β=15%) — read two ways.

Forward-only verdict
  • Strategy A alone loses 30% of its $6M under an equity-vol spike — a survivable, well-understood drawdown for that desk
  • Strategy B alone loses 15% of its $4M under a credit-spread widening — also survivable, on its own
  • Each desk, tested against its own scenario, looks fine in isolation
  • Conclusion drawn: the book looks diversified across asset classes and desks
Reverse-stress verdict
  • Both strategies are actually driven by one latent "risk-off" factor s, just with different sensitivities
  • Solving backward for a $5M (50%) loss gives s* ≈ 2.08 — only about twice an ordinary bad quarter
  • That one moderate shock hits both desks at once, because it's the same factor underneath both
  • Conclusion drawn: what looked like diversification was concentration in a single factor
It's two commuters who look totally independent — one drives, one bikes — until you notice they cross the same one bridge every day. Test each separately and everything looks fine. Ask "what's the smallest thing that stops both at once," and the answer is one ordinary bridge closure, not two separate disasters.
Section 11

How this actually gets used — and a grounding check

🏛

Regulators require all three

Post-2008 frameworks (Basel's stress-testing principles, the US CCAR program) require historical and hypothetical scenario testing; the UK's FSA made reverse stress testing a formal requirement in 2009, specifically because forward-only testing had repeatedly missed the channel that actually broke firms.

⚖️

Neither forward method is trusted alone

Only running historical replays bets the next crisis resembles a past one; only running Monte Carlo bets the assumed stress distribution is realistic. Serious teams report both and treat a large gap between them as itself informative.

🎯

Reverse results feed risk limits directly

Once a reverse stress test names the specific factor combination that produces an unacceptable loss, the natural response is a concentration limit targeted at exactly that factor — more surgical than an across-the-board size cut.

🏢

Run at the whole-business level too

A firm-wide reverse stress test asks what combination of events would make the entire business model unviable — surfacing risks like funding withdrawal that a single trading book's stress test would never capture.

Checked against this repo's actual code

The full lesson states plainly that no stress-testing pipeline is built in this repo — it's explained as a concept, not a claim that historical replay, Monte Carlo stress, or reverse stress testing has been implemented here. That check was repeated for this deck: the repo does have real backtesting infrastructure (e.g. backtestSystem/) and an actual "Credit-Stress Index" risk overlay (js/creditStressCore.js) that gates exposure using rolling z-scores of credit spreads and VIX — but that overlay is a continuous, ongoing risk gate, not a historical-crisis replay, a Monte Carlo stress simulator, or a reverse-stress solver in the sense this lesson defines those terms. The lesson's "concept only, nothing like this is built here" framing still holds.

Section 12

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

In historical scenario replay, why apply today's weights to a past shock instead of looking at how the book actually performed at the time?

The book may not have existed then, or held entirely different positions. Applying the real historical shock to today's actual exposure isolates the question that matters.
Question 2

Why does "correlations move toward 1" show up so often as a Monte Carlo stress assumption?

It builds an empirically-motivated crisis pattern into the stress distribution, rather than relying on ordinary-times correlations that likely understate a real crisis.
Question 3

What question does a reverse stress test ask that a forward scenario test doesn't?

Forward tests pick a scenario and compute a loss. Reverse stress testing starts from the loss and solves backward for the scenario.
Question 4

In the worked example, why did the two desks look diversified under forward testing but concentrated under the reverse stress test?

Each desk survived its own scenario comfortably in isolation. Solving backward found the one shared factor that gets both at once, with a shock roughly twice an ordinary bad quarter.
Question 5

Why is it a mistake to report only the reverse-solved shock size s*, without saying which factor combination produces it?

A report stating "a 2.1x shock breaches our capital floor" without naming the factor combination has skipped the half of the exercise that's actually useful for setting a risk limit.
Section 13

What to carry forward

01

VaR and CVaR (Risk Deep Dive 1) are built from ordinary-days statistics — genuine crises spike correlations and cluster tails in ways that history may not yet have captured.

02

Historical scenario replay applies a REAL past shock to TODAY'S current book — every correlation is automatically right, because it actually happened jointly, once.

03

Monte Carlo scenario stress simulates many hypothetical extremes from an assumed stress distribution — it can explore what hasn't happened yet, but is only as good as that assumption.

04

Reverse stress testing solves backward from an unacceptable loss to the scenario that causes it — routinely surfacing hidden concentration forward tests never think to check for.

05

The worked example: two "diversified" strategies shared one latent risk-off factor — a moderate shock, not an exotic one, took out half the book.

06

What matters from a reverse stress test is the WHICH — the specific factor combination — not just the HOW BIG of the shock size alone.

What this now lets you do

Recognize when a VaR or CVaR figure is being asked to answer a question it wasn't built for. Layer historical replay and Monte Carlo stress to triangulate a book's real tail exposure instead of trusting either alone. And — the sharpest tool here — run a reverse stress test to hunt for the hidden single factor that makes a "diversified-looking" book actually concentrated, before a real shock finds it for you.
