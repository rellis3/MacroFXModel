Graph Theory & Networks 1 · Graph Theory & Networks

Graphs, and Three Different Answers to "Who Matters Most"

A graph is nothing more than dots and lines — nodes connected by edges — but that plain idea can redraw a correlation matrix, a counterparty exposure ledger, or a network of hyperlinks as the same kind of picture. Once it's a picture, one question keeps coming up: which node matters most? This lesson builds the vocabulary to ask that question precisely, then shows the three classic ways to answer it — degree, betweenness, and eigenvector centrality — which can, and often do, crown three different winners on the exact same network.

Section 1

Dots and lines describing almost anything

A graph G = (V, E) is a set of nodes V and a set of edges E connecting pairs of them. That's the whole definition — everything else is what you choose to call a node and an edge.

🔗

A correlation matrix, redrawn

Every entry in a correlation matrix relates two instruments with a number. Call each instrument a node and draw an edge for every strong relationship, and the matrix becomes a picture instead of a grid.

🏦

A counterparty exposure network

Who owes whom, and how much, across a set of banks or funds. Nodes are institutions; a directed, weighted edge is one exposure running from one to another.

🌐

The web itself

Every page is a node, every hyperlink a directed edge. This is the exact graph PageRank — covered later in this deck — was built to rank.

👥

Any relationship at all

Friendships, citations, shared board members, supply chains — if it connects pairs of things, it's a graph, and the vocabulary below doesn't care which one it is.

The reason this belongs in a finance curriculum: a correlation matrix, an exposure ledger, and a supply-chain map are three completely different-looking spreadsheets. Redraw all three as nodes and edges, and the exact same handful of questions — and the exact same three centrality measures below — apply to every one of them, unchanged.
Section 2

Four words that do all the work

Degree, path, connected component, weighted — nearly everything downstream is built from these four.

•

Degree

How many edges touch a node directly. A node with edges to two others has degree 2 — nothing about which two others, just the count.

→

Path

A sequence of edges hopping from one node to another. The shortest path is the one with the fewest hops (or, on a weighted graph, the least total edge weight).

◯

Connected component

A cluster where every node can reach every other node by some path. Two components with no edge between them are, structurally, two separate networks sharing one page.

⚖

Weighted vs. unweighted

Unweighted: an edge simply exists or doesn't. Weighted: every edge carries a number — a correlation strength, a dollar exposure — and "degree" becomes "strength," a sum of weights instead of a count.

Section 3

One small network, used for everything below

Seven currency-bloc nodes: a European trio (EUR, GBP, CHF) and a commodity-bloc trio (AUD, NZD, CAD), each trio fully connected internally, joined by a single bridge node, JPY, which links to exactly one node in each trio.

Every one of those numbers under a node — its degree — is also a full row of the adjacency matrix, the grid form of the exact same information: EUR's row has a 1 in the GBP, CHF, and JPY columns and 0s everywhere else. A matrix and this picture are two views of one object, and everything below is computed straight off it.

Degree is cheap, direct, and often a genuinely useful first cut — but it's entirely local. It can't tell a node connected to three nobodies from one connected to three of the network's most important members, and it's completely blind to a node's role as a bridge between parts of the network it doesn't directly touch at all. Both of those blind spots get fixed below.

Section 5

Betweenness centrality: the unavoidable route

For every other pair of nodes s and t, ask what fraction of their shortest paths passes through v — and add that up across every pair.

JPY has only two edges — one of the lowest degrees in the network — but every one of the 9 shortest paths from a European-cluster node to a commodity-cluster node has to cross that single bridge. Betweenness gives JPY credit for all 9; the other four non-hub nodes (GBP, CHF, NZD, CAD) sit on zero shortest paths between other pairs, since every path involving them is either a direct edge or stays inside their own cluster.

A node with high betweenness isn't necessarily well-connected itself — it just happens to sit on the route between two otherwise-separate parts of the network. That's a genuinely different kind of "important" than degree measures, and it's exactly the property a risk desk cares about when asking "which single position, if it broke, would cut off information flow between two halves of the book?"
Section 6

Eigenvector centrality: important friends make you important

A node's score should be proportional to the sum of its neighbors' scores. For the whole network at once: Ax = λx.

↻

A recursive definition

x is unknown on both sides of its own equation — a node's importance depends on its neighbors' importance, which depends on their neighbors', and so on around the whole network.

λ

Solved as an eigenvector problem

The unique, all-positive solution (guaranteed by the Perron-Frobenius theorem on a connected graph) is the leading eigenvector of the adjacency matrix — the one paired with the largest eigenvalue.

🔎

PageRank is its directed, damped cousin

Google's original ranking algorithm is this exact idea applied to web pages instead of people, with a "damping factor" added so a page with no outgoing links can't trap importance forever.

This is the same eigenvector equation as the leading eigenvector in the PCA lesson — solved the same mechanical way, by repeatedly multiplying by a matrix and rescaling — pointed at a completely different question: not "which direction does the data vary most," but "who is important around here." The next slide watches that repeated-multiply-and-rescale process happen, one iteration at a time.

Section 9

Disagreement is the point, not a bug

"Which position carries the most direct exposure?"

Degree centrality answers this: hedge EUR or AUD, the two nodes with the most direct connections.

"Which position, if lost, would blind the desk to an entire linkage?"

Betweenness answers this one differently: hedge JPY — losing it doesn't cost one position's risk, it severs the only route between two clusters.

Suppose a risk desk models its book as exactly this network and can only keep hedging capacity on one node. There is no single correct answer — there's a correct answer per question, which is precisely why real network analysis reports more than one centrality measure rather than picking a favorite.

Where this needs qualifying

Every score in this deck is computed on one fixed snapshot of a 7-node toy network. Real correlation or exposure networks are estimated over a finite window and shift across regimes; and on a network this small, centrality rankings can shift noticeably if even one edge is added, removed, or reweighted — treat the exact numbers as illustrative of the mechanism, not as stable measurements.

Section 10

Where practitioners actually use this

🕸️

Social & web networks

PageRank's original application — ranking web pages by the structure of who links to whom, not keyword counting alone.

🏦

Interbank & payment networks

Central banks and the BIS use betweenness- and eigenvector-style measures to find "too interconnected to fail" institutions.

🧬

Biology & epidemiology

Betweenness finds "super-spreader" bridge individuals in a contact network; eigenvector-style measures find hub proteins in an interaction network.

📈

Cross-asset structure

Treating a correlation matrix as a graph, rather than only decomposing it (PCA), opens up exactly the node-level questions this lesson introduces.

Section 11

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

In the toy network, why does JPY score highest on betweenness despite one of the lowest degrees?

Betweenness counts how many of the network's shortest paths run through a node, regardless of that node's own degree. JPY is the only bridge, so all 9 cross-cluster shortest paths run through it.
Question 2

What's the formula for normalized degree centrality of node v, in a network of n nodes?

n − 1 is the maximum possible degree in a network of n nodes (every other node, connected directly) — dividing by it rescales degree to a [0,1] "fraction of everyone else" score.
Question 3

Eigenvector centrality's scores are found by solving for which quantity?

In practice this is found by power iteration — repeatedly multiplying a guess vector by the adjacency matrix and rescaling — exactly the slider from a couple of slides back.
Question 4

What problem does PageRank's damping factor fix that plain eigenvector centrality doesn't handle on a directed graph?

With probability 1 − d, PageRank's "random surfer" jumps to a uniformly random page instead of following a link — guaranteeing the process never gets permanently stuck at a page with no outbound links.
Question 5

A report says "Node X has the highest centrality" without naming which measure. What's the problem?

Degree, betweenness, and eigenvector centrality named different winners on the exact same toy network. "Highest centrality" is underspecified unless it names which measure — the claim can be true for one definition and false for another.
Section 12

What to carry forward

01

A graph is nodes plus edges — the same abstraction fits a correlation matrix, an exposure ledger, or a friendship map without changing the math at all.

02

Degree, path, connected component, and weighted vs. unweighted are the four building blocks nearly everything else in graph theory is built from.

03

Degree centrality counts direct connections only — cheap and simple, but blind to what those connections themselves connect to.

04

Betweenness centrality finds bridges — a low-degree node can hold the network's highest betweenness if it's the only route between two clusters.

05

Eigenvector centrality is recursive importance, solved by power iteration until the scores stop moving; PageRank is its directed, damped cousin.

06

The three measures can, and often do, disagree about "who matters most" on the exact same network — reporting only one is a choice, and it should be a stated one.

What this now lets you do

Redraw any network-shaped problem — a correlation matrix, a counterparty exposure ledger, a supply chain — as nodes and edges, and ask which node matters most with the right question in mind: raw connectivity (degree), bridging role (betweenness), or recursive importance (eigenvector centrality, or its directed cousin PageRank).
