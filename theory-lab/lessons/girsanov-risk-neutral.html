Girsanov's Theorem & the Risk-Neutral Measure

The Black-Scholes lesson called \(N(d_2)\) "the risk-neutral probability the option finishes in the money" and moved on. This lesson is about that phrase specifically — what a "risk-neutral probability" formally is, why it's legitimate to price with it instead of your actual view of the market, and the 1960 theorem that makes the substitution rigorous rather than a hand-wave.

CONCEPT — the theorem behind Black-Scholes' "risk-neutral probability" claim ~17 min read Prereqs: Itô's Lemma, Black-Scholes-Merton Option Pricing
TL;DR
  • An option's no-arbitrage price is a discounted expectation under the risk-neutral measure \(\mathbb{Q}\), not the real-world measure \(\mathbb{P}\) — so the underlying's true expected return \(\mu\) never enters the formula, only the risk-free rate \(r\).
  • Girsanov's theorem (1960) is what makes that substitution rigorous: it says you can shift a Brownian motion's drift by \(\theta = (\mu-r)/\sigma\) and still have a valid Brownian motion, just under a different, equivalent probability measure \(\mathbb{Q}\) that assigns the same possible paths different likelihoods.
  • \(\theta=(\mu-r)/\sigma\) is the unique shift that makes the discounted price \(\tilde S_t=e^{-rt}S_t\) a martingale under \(\mathbb{Q}\) — that martingale property, not convenience, is why pricing "ignores" \(\mu\).
  • That discounted-price martingale under \(\mathbb{Q}\) is the exact same object as the EMH lesson's "fair game" martingale under \(\mathbb{P}\) — here it holds by construction rather than as an empirical, testable claim.
  • \(N(d_2)\) is a real, well-defined probability (\(\mathbb{Q}(S_T>K)\)) — it's the measure it's computed under that's a deliberate pricing device, not a real-world forecast, which is why a bullish real-world view is a separate bet, not a reason to reprice the option.
Section 01

Why this matters

The Black-Scholes lesson derived the no-arbitrage PDE and, in passing, made a fairly remarkable claim: the underlying's true expected return \(\mu\) — the number every market forecaster argues about — cancels out of the option-pricing formula entirely, replaced by the risk-free rate \(r\). That lesson called this "pricing under the risk-neutral measure" and explained the mechanical reason it happens (the replication argument only needs the hedge to cancel randomness, not to bet on direction), but it deliberately left one thing unexplained: what is a "measure," formally, and what licenses you to swap one out for another and still call the result a legitimate price rather than a convenient fiction?

That question has a precise answer, and it is not a trick. Girsanov's theorem (1960) is the mathematical result that makes "price as if everything grew at the risk-free rate" rigorous rather than a plausible-sounding shortcut. It says you can take the exact same underlying random process — same volatility, same source of randomness, same set of possible future paths — and legally re-describe it under a different probability weighting in which its drift is whatever you need it to be, provided you reweight consistently. Applied to option pricing, the theorem is what turns "assume everything drifts at \(r\)" from a suspicious sleight of hand into a specific, correct answer to a specific, well-posed mathematical question. This lesson derives that answer and connects it to a concept already built in this Theory Lab: the martingale property from the Efficient Market Hypothesis lesson, which turns out to be exactly what the risk-neutral measure is engineered to produce.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Same coin, different odds you're allowed to use

A probability measure, informally, is nothing more than a specific weighting of how likely you consider each possible future outcome to be. For a stock's price path over the next year, the real-world measure (mathematicians write it \(\mathbb{P}\)) is whatever weighting reflects what investors actually, truly expect — a genuinely risky stock has some expected return above the risk-free rate, because investors demand compensation (a risk premium) for bearing uncertainty they can't diversify away. That expected-return-above-\(r\) is the \(\mu\) from the Black-Scholes SDE, and it is a fact (or at least a belief) about the real economy.

Girsanov's theorem says something that sounds almost too convenient at first: you can take that exact same stock — same volatility, same randomness, same possible paths from here to expiry — and describe it under a different weighting, one under which its expected return is exactly \(r\) instead of \(\mu\). Critically, this new weighting isn't a different stock, or a fantasy stock, or an approximation. It assigns different probabilities to the same paths (paths that end up far above where \(\mu\)-drift would put them become less likely; paths that end up lower become more likely), but it never invents a new path that couldn't happen under the real-world measure, and never rules out a path that could. That's the technical meaning of "equivalent" measures: \(\mathbb{P}\) and \(\mathbb{Q}\) agree on which outcomes are possible at all — probability zero stays probability zero both ways — they only disagree on how likely the possible ones are. Think of it as the same set of racehorses and the same race, but a bookmaker's odds sheet redrawn so that, mathematically, "average return" comes out to exactly the risk-free rate. Nobody is claiming that's what will actually happen. It's a deliberately constructed accounting device — and the next section shows precisely why that particular device, and no other, is the one a no-arbitrage price needs.

Section 02

The math, step by step

Three pieces, in order: (1) the same SDE written under two different drifts, (2) Girsanov's theorem, which says the shift between them is a legitimate change of measure, and (3) the payoff — why the specific shift to drift \(r\) makes the discounted price process a martingale, which is the entire reason risk-neutral pricing works.

1. Same process, two measures

Recall the underlying's real-world dynamics from the Black-Scholes lesson, written here with an explicit \(\mathbb{P}\) superscript on the Brownian motion to flag which measure it lives under:

\[ dS_t = \mu S_t\,dt + \sigma S_t\,dW_t^{\mathbb{P}} \]

Girsanov's theorem lets you construct an equivalent measure \(\mathbb{Q}\) under which the same underlying — same \(\sigma\), same source of randomness — instead satisfies:

\[ dS_t = r S_t\,dt + \sigma S_t\,dW_t^{\mathbb{Q}} \]

The relationship between the two driving Brownian motions is a simple shift:

\[ W_t^{\mathbb{Q}} = W_t^{\mathbb{P}} + \theta t, \qquad \theta = \frac{\mu - r}{\sigma} \]

The content of Girsanov's theorem, precisely, is that \(W_t^{\mathbb{Q}}\) — defined as \(W_t^{\mathbb{P}}\) plus a deterministic shift — is itself a valid, standard Brownian motion, just under the new measure \(\mathbb{Q}\) rather than \(\mathbb{P}\). That is the non-obvious part: shifting a Brownian motion by a constant rate doesn't merely look like a new random process, it provably still has all of Brownian motion's defining properties (mean-zero increments, variance \(t\), independent increments) once you're willing to reweight the underlying probabilities to match.
\(\theta\)The market price of risk — how much extra expected return, per unit of volatility, the real world demands for holding this asset (it's the asset's Sharpe ratio in these units: excess return \(\mu - r\) divided by \(\sigma\)). \(\theta\) is exactly the size of the drift shift needed to move from \(\mathbb{P}\) to \(\mathbb{Q}\), and the next section shows why that specific size is the one that matters for pricing.
\(\sigma\)Volatility — identical in both SDEs. This is the concrete content of "same randomness": the diffusion term \(\sigma S_t\,dW_t\) is untouched by the change of measure. Only the drift term changes; the model's uncertainty about how much the price moves around is not up for renegotiation, only its center.

Notice what did not change: \(\sigma\) is identical in both equations, and the set of possible future paths for \(S_t\) is identical (a Brownian motion shifted by a deterministic drift traces out the same family of continuous paths either way — only which paths get more or less probability weight differs). Only the drift term moved, from \(\mu\) to \(r\). That is the precise sense in which risk-neutral pricing "ignores the real-world expected return" — it isn't ignoring information by accident or by approximation, it is deliberately re-describing the same randomness under a measure where drift has been relabeled to \(r\), and the next section explains exactly why that relabeling is the economically correct one for pricing.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine reweighting a bet — not changing what could happen, just changing how likely you're pretending each outcome is — so that the math for "what's a fair price" becomes simple. That's all a change of measure is: the same coin, the same set of possible flips, just different odds written on the betting sheet. Nothing about the coin changed; only which odds you're allowed to use for pricing purposes did.

2. Girsanov's theorem — why the shift is a legitimate change of measure

Girsanov's theorem (Igor Girsanov, 1960) is the general result that licenses the move above. Stated informally but precisely enough to use: given a Brownian motion \(W_t^{\mathbb{P}}\) under a measure \(\mathbb{P}\), and a reasonably well-behaved process \(\theta_t\) (in the Black-Scholes setting, a constant, but the theorem allows it to vary), there exists an equivalent probability measure \(\mathbb{Q}\) under which

\[ W_t^{\mathbb{Q}} \;=\; W_t^{\mathbb{P}} + \int_0^t \theta_s\,ds \]

is itself a standard Brownian motion. "Equivalent" is doing precise work here: \(\mathbb{P}\) and \(\mathbb{Q}\) assign different probabilities to most events, but they agree exactly on which events have probability zero — nothing \(\mathbb{P}\) rules out becomes possible under \(\mathbb{Q}\), and nothing \(\mathbb{Q}\) rules out was possible under \(\mathbb{P}\). The object that formally carries out the reweighting between the two measures — the precise multiplicative factor you'd apply to a \(\mathbb{P}\)-probability of any given path to get its \(\mathbb{Q}\)-probability — is called the Radon-Nikodym derivative, written \(d\mathbb{Q}/d\mathbb{P}\). In this setting it takes the explicit form

\[ \left.\frac{d\mathbb{Q}}{d\mathbb{P}}\right|_{\mathcal{F}_T} = \exp\!\left(-\theta W_T^{\mathbb{P}} - \tfrac{1}{2}\theta^2 T\right) \]
dℚ/dℙRadon-Nikodym derivative — the reweighting factor itself
ℱ_Teverything observable up to time T

Deriving that this exponential is exactly the right reweighting factor — and proving it's a well-defined probability change at all — is a genuine stochastic-calculus result (it relies on the Radon-Nikodym derivative itself being a \(\mathbb{P}\)-martingale, plus a technical integrability condition called Novikov's condition, ensuring the reweighting doesn't "leak" probability); that full derivation is outside this lesson's scope. The operational fact worth taking away is narrower and more useful: this object exists, is unique for a given \(\theta\), and its existence is exactly what makes "shift the drift, keep everything else" a legitimate mathematical operation rather than a plausible-looking substitution. \(\mathcal{F}_T\) here denotes the information available up to time \(T\) — the same filtration notation the EMH lesson uses for "everything observable so far."

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

The Radon-Nikodym derivative is basically an exchange rate between two ways of weighting the same set of outcomes — the exact number you'd multiply an old-measure probability by to get the new-measure probability, for any outcome you care to ask about. Girsanov's theorem is the guarantee that this exchange rate exists and behaves itself (never inventing possibilities out of nowhere, never destroying real ones) whenever you shift a Brownian motion's drift by a constant amount.

3. Why this particular shift — the discounted price becomes a martingale

Girsanov's theorem says a drift shift by any \(\theta\) produces some equivalent measure. It does not, by itself, say which \(\theta\) you should use for pricing. That choice — \(\theta = (\mu-r)/\sigma\), landing the underlying's drift exactly on \(r\) — is pinned down by a single requirement: it is the unique shift that makes the underlying's discounted price process a martingale under the new measure. Define the discounted price \(\tilde{S}_t = e^{-rt}S_t\) — today's dollars, stripping out the pure time-value-of-money growth every asset earns regardless of risk. Applying the product rule (no stochastic correction needed here, since \(e^{-rt}\) is deterministic) to \(\tilde{S}_t\) under the \(\mathbb{Q}\)-dynamics from step 1:

\[ d\tilde{S}_t = -re^{-rt}S_t\,dt + e^{-rt}\,dS_t = -r\tilde{S}_t\,dt + e^{-rt}\big(rS_t\,dt + \sigma S_t\,dW_t^{\mathbb{Q}}\big) = \sigma\tilde{S}_t\,dW_t^{\mathbb{Q}} \]

The two \(r\tilde{S}_t\,dt\) drift terms — one from differentiating the discount factor, one from the underlying's own \(\mathbb{Q}\)-drift — cancel exactly. What's left has no drift term at all: the discounted price moves purely on the random shock \(\sigma\tilde{S}_t\,dW_t^{\mathbb{Q}}\), with expected instantaneous change of zero. A process with zero drift is, by definition, a martingale:

\[ \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\big[\tilde{S}_T \mid \mathcal{F}_t\big] = \tilde{S}_t \]

This is the direct callback promised at the top of the lesson: it is exactly the martingale property from the Efficient Market Hypothesis lesson (\(\mathbb{E}[P_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}] = P_{t-1}\)), applied to the discounted price under \(\mathbb{Q}\). The EMH lesson treated "prices are a martingale" as an empirical, testable claim about real markets under \(\mathbb{P}\) (and one that mostly, if imperfectly, holds up — see that lesson's autocorrelation tests). Here, the martingale property under \(\mathbb{Q}\) is not an empirical claim at all — it is true by construction, the entire reason \(\mathbb{Q}\) was built the way it was. Girsanov's theorem supplies the "you're allowed to do this" license; choosing \(\theta = (\mu-r)/\sigma\) specifically is what cashes that license in for a discounted price process that is a fair game, under \(\mathbb{Q}\), for every tradeable asset simultaneously (the same construction applies to any asset in the same economy, since they all share the same risk-free discounting).

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

A martingale is just a fair game — knowing everything that's happened so far gives you no edge in predicting whether the next move is up or down. Once you strip a price down to "today's dollars" (discounting away the guaranteed time-value-of-money growth every asset gets for free), what's left, under the risk-neutral measure, is exactly that kind of fair game: no built-in lean in either direction, by design rather than by luck.

The payoff is now direct. If a derivative's own discounted price must also be a martingale under \(\mathbb{Q}\) — which the fundamental theorem of asset pricing (Harrison & Kreps, 1979; not derived here) shows is forced by no-arbitrage, since the derivative and its hedge portfolio must move together — then a derivative paying \(V_T\) at time \(T\) has today's price given by a discounted conditional expectation, computed under \(\mathbb{Q}\):

\[ V_0 = e^{-rT}\,\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\big[V_T\big] \]
V_0the derivative's no-arbitrage price today
e⁻ʳᵀdiscounting back to today's dollars at the risk-free rate
𝔼^ℚ[V_T]expected payoff at T, computed under ℚ — never 𝔼^ℙ

Nowhere in that formula does \(\mu\) — the real-world expected return — appear. It has been replaced entirely by \(r\), not as an approximation or a simplifying assumption of convenience, but because \(r\) is precisely the drift that makes the discounted price process a martingale, which is precisely the property no-arbitrage pricing requires. This is the rigorous version of what the Black-Scholes lesson stated as a fact about the PDE: pricing an option needs a volatility estimate, never a directional forecast, because the entire pricing framework is built on a measure engineered so that direction, by construction, doesn't matter.

It also completes the earlier lesson's unfinished sentence about \(N(d_2)\). Under \(\mathbb{Q}\), \(\ln S_T\) is normally distributed with mean \(\ln S_0 + (r - \sigma^2/2)T\) — the same lognormal shape as under \(\mathbb{P}\), but centered on \(r\) instead of \(\mu\). \(N(d_2)\) is literally \(\mathbb{Q}(S_T > K)\): the actual, well-defined probability, under the actual, well-defined measure \(\mathbb{Q}\), that the option finishes in the money. There is nothing fictitious about the number \(N(d_2)\) — what's fictitious (deliberately, usefully so) is the measure it's computed under.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

\(N(d_2)\) is a genuinely real number, computed by genuinely real math — it's not a made-up placeholder. What's "fake" is only the odds sheet it was computed from: like reading off a probability from a rigged casino game deliberately set so every bet is fair, then being careful to remember that number describes the rigged game, not what will actually happen on the street outside.

🎯
Real-world trading scenario

A bullish EUR/USD view is a separate bet, not a pricing input

Suppose a trader has a genuinely bullish real-world view on EUR/USD — they believe, based on their own macro read, that the pair will appreciate more over the next six months than the two currencies' interest-rate differential implies. That view is a statement about \(\mu\) under \(\mathbb{P}\): a real, arguable, potentially valuable opinion about the real world.

It is not, however, a reason to price or hedge a EUR/USD option any differently. The option's no-arbitrage price and its hedge ratios (delta, gamma, from the Black-Scholes lesson) are computed under \(\mathbb{Q}\), where by construction EUR/USD drifts at the interest-rate differential (the FX risk-free rate, as that lesson noted) regardless of the trader's bullish view. If the trader instead prices the option using their own higher \(\mu\) — effectively substituting their view for \(r\) in the pricing formula — they will compute a call price that is too high relative to what the market (and any correctly-hedged counterparty) will actually pay, and a hedge ratio that is systematically wrong, since \(N(d_1)\) computed under the "wrong" measure no longer matches the delta a replicating portfolio actually needs. Their bullish view doesn't disappear — it's a real, separate directional bet, correctly expressed by simply buying EUR/USD, or buying a call and not delta-hedging it (an intentionally directional options position). What the theorem rules out is doing both at once inside a single mispriced number: using a real-world view to justify a different price for the derivative itself, rather than layering the view on top of a correctly no-arbitrage-priced instrument. Conflating "my view on the market" with "the no-arbitrage price of this derivative" is exactly the mistake Girsanov's theorem, and the whole risk-neutral pricing apparatus built on it, exists to make precise and avoidable — and it is a genuinely costly mistake in practice: a desk that lets a directional house view leak into its pricing models will misprice consistently in the direction of that view, and get picked off by counterparties who hedge correctly.

Same underlying, two measures — same shape, shifted center
Real-world measure ℙ — drift μ (includes risk premium) Risk-neutral measure ℚ — drift r (risk-free rate only)
Same underlying, same volatility \(\sigma\) (both curves have identical width and shape), same set of possible future prices. The only difference is where the probability mass is centered: the real-world curve sits further right because \(\mu > r\) for a genuinely risky asset (investors demand a risk premium), while the risk-neutral curve is centered exactly at the risk-free forward price. Girsanov's theorem is the statement that you're allowed to relabel one curve's probabilities into the other's without changing the underlying randomness at all — nothing about \(\sigma\) or the shape moves, only the center.
Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

No trading desk sits down and derives the Radon-Nikodym derivative before pricing a swap. Girsanov's theorem is infrastructure, not a daily calculation — but the practices it justifies are used constantly and industry-wide:

💰

Risk-neutral valuation is the default

Every derivatives pricing model, from plain Black-Scholes through local-volatility and stochastic-volatility models, computes a discounted expectation under some \(\mathbb{Q}\)-measure, never under \(\mathbb{P}\).

🎲

Monte Carlo engines simulate under ℚ

Pricing a path-dependent exotic by averaging a discounted payoff over simulated paths uses drift \(r\) (or the FX rate differential) in every path — never \(\mathbb{P}\)'s \(\mu\).

🔄

Calibration works "backwards" through ℚ

Backing out implied volatility from a traded price infers a parameter of \(\mathbb{Q}\), not \(\mathbb{P}\) — why implied and realized volatility are related but distinct objects.

📉

Risk management switches back to ℙ

VaR, stress tests, and real-world scenario probabilities are computed under the actual historical/estimated measure — "how bad could this get" is a \(\mathbb{P}\)-question.

A single derivatives book routinely uses both measures for different jobs in the same day: \(\mathbb{Q}\) to price and hedge, \(\mathbb{P}\) to ask "how bad could this actually get."

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where the risk-neutral measure is misunderstood or misused

The trap: confusing "risk-neutral" (a mathematical property of a measure) with "investors don't care about risk" (a false claim about psychology) is the single most common misreading in this lesson — see below.

Treating \(\mathbb{Q}\)-probabilities as forecasts. \(N(d_2)\), or any risk-neutral probability computed from a pricing model, is not a statement about what will actually happen. It's the probability under a measure specifically constructed so that discounted prices are martingales — a pricing device, not a forecast. Reading a rising risk-neutral probability of some event as "the market thinks this is getting more likely" conflates \(\mathbb{Q}\) with \(\mathbb{P}\); it can be directionally suggestive in practice (the two aren't wholly unrelated — a genuine shift in real-world odds often does move option-implied probabilities too) but it is not the same claim, and the gap between them is exactly the risk premium \(\theta\sigma = \mu - r\).

Believing "risk-neutral" means investors are risk-neutral. The name is a description of the measure's mathematical property (every asset drifts at \(r\) under it), not a claim about actual investor psychology. Real investors are risk-averse — that's precisely why \(\mu > r\) under \(\mathbb{P}\) in the first place, and precisely why a nontrivial shift \(\theta\) is needed to get from \(\mathbb{P}\) to \(\mathbb{Q}\) at all. If investors really were risk-neutral, \(\mu\) would already equal \(r\) and the two measures would coincide.

Assuming any drift can be shifted away for free. Girsanov's theorem requires a technical condition (Novikov's condition, or a weaker sufficient variant) on the process \(\theta_t\) for the change-of-measure exponential to be a genuine, "no probability leakage" martingale. It isn't automatic for arbitrary, badly-behaved drift processes. In the constant-\(\theta\) Black-Scholes setting this is a non-issue, but the condition becomes a real constraint in more exotic models (certain stochastic-volatility or interest-rate models) where it can fail — flagged here as a reason "just apply Girsanov" isn't always as free as the constant-drift case makes it look.

Layering a directional view into the pricing model instead of on top of it. As the trading scenario above shows, the failure mode isn't abstract — it's a specific, repeatable trading mistake: substituting a personal or house view of \(\mu\) into a pricing or hedging calculation that is supposed to run under \(\mathbb{Q}\). The correct place for a directional view is a separate, explicit position (spot, forwards, an undelta-hedged option), never a silent adjustment to the assumed drift inside a pricing model.

Section 05

Self-test

Q1. Why doesn't an option's real-world expected-return view belong in its no-arbitrage price?
Because the no-arbitrage price is defined as a discounted expectation under the risk-neutral measure \(\mathbb{Q}\), and \(\mathbb{Q}\) is constructed — via Girsanov's theorem, choosing \(\theta = (\mu-r)/\sigma\) — specifically so that every tradeable asset's discounted price is a martingale under it. That construction replaces \(\mu\) with \(r\) by design; it isn't that the real-world view is irrelevant to the world, it's that the no-arbitrage price is answering a different question ("what price makes replication/hedging arguments consistent") than "what do I expect this asset to do." The real-world \(\mu\) still matters enormously for risk management and for directional trading — just not for this specific price.
Q2. What does it mean, precisely, for the discounted price process to be a martingale under the risk-neutral measure?
Defining \(\tilde{S}_t = e^{-rt}S_t\) (the underlying's price expressed in today's dollars, stripping out risk-free growth), the martingale property is \(\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\tilde{S}_T \mid \mathcal{F}_t] = \tilde{S}_t\): given everything observable up to time \(t\), the best \(\mathbb{Q}\)-expected value of the discounted price at any future time \(T\) is simply today's discounted price — no predictable drift left after discounting. This is the exact same mathematical property the EMH lesson defines for real-world prices (a "fair game"), except here it holds for the discounted process under \(\mathbb{Q}\) by mathematical construction, not as an empirical claim about real markets that needs testing.
Q3. What is the market price of risk \(\theta\), and what specific role does it play in moving from \(\mathbb{P}\) to \(\mathbb{Q}\)?
\(\theta = (\mu - r)/\sigma\) is the asset's excess expected return (over the risk-free rate) per unit of volatility — its Sharpe ratio in these units. It is exactly the size of the deterministic shift applied to the real-world Brownian motion (\(W_t^{\mathbb{Q}} = W_t^{\mathbb{P}} + \theta t\)) to construct the risk-neutral measure. Girsanov's theorem guarantees that this shifted process is itself a valid Brownian motion under the new measure \(\mathbb{Q}\); the specific choice of \(\theta\) (rather than some other shift) is what makes the underlying's drift under \(\mathbb{Q}\) land exactly on \(r\), which in turn is what makes the discounted price a martingale.
Q4. What does Girsanov's theorem actually assert, and what role does the Radon-Nikodym derivative play?
Girsanov's theorem asserts that given a Brownian motion under a measure \(\mathbb{P}\) and a suitably well-behaved drift-shift process \(\theta_t\), there exists an equivalent measure \(\mathbb{Q}\) — one agreeing with \(\mathbb{P}\) on which outcomes are possible at all — under which the shifted process \(W_t^{\mathbb{P}} + \int_0^t\theta_s\,ds\) is itself a standard Brownian motion. The Radon-Nikodym derivative \(d\mathbb{Q}/d\mathbb{P}\) is the specific object that formally reweights probabilities between the two measures — the exact multiplicative factor converting a \(\mathbb{P}\)-probability into the corresponding \(\mathbb{Q}\)-probability for any event. Its existence (subject to a technical integrability condition) is what makes the change of measure a rigorous mathematical operation rather than an informal relabeling.
Q5. Why is \(N(d_2)\) not the real-world probability that an option finishes in the money?
\(N(d_2)\) is \(\mathbb{Q}(S_T > K)\) — a probability computed from \(\ln S_T\)'s distribution under the risk-neutral measure, which has mean \(\ln S_0 + (r - \sigma^2/2)T\), using \(r\) rather than \(\mu\). The real-world probability of finishing in the money would instead use \(\ln S_T\)'s distribution under \(\mathbb{P}\), with mean \(\ln S_0 + (\mu - \sigma^2/2)T\). Since \(\mu \geq r\) for a typical risky asset (a positive risk premium), the real-world probability of finishing in the money is generally higher than \(N(d_2)\); the two coincide only in the special case \(\mu = r\), i.e. \(\theta = 0\), no measure change needed at all.
Q6. In the EUR/USD trading scenario, what specific mistake does the theorem prevent, and what should the trader do instead?
The mistake is substituting a personal bullish view (a higher \(\mu\)) into the pricing or hedging of the option itself — effectively pricing as if the real-world measure, not the risk-neutral measure, governed the no-arbitrage relationship. That produces a systematically mispriced option and a wrong hedge ratio, since delta/gamma computed off the "wrong" drift no longer match what a genuine replicating portfolio needs. The correct approach is to price and hedge the option under \(\mathbb{Q}\) exactly as the no-arbitrage argument requires, and to express the bullish view as a separate position — outright spot or forward exposure, or a deliberately un-hedged (or partially hedged) option position — rather than folding the view into the pricing model where it silently corrupts the "fair value" the desk is quoting.
