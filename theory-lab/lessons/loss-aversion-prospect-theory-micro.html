Why a Loss Hurts Twice as Much

Prospect theory in pictures: why you play safe when you are winning, gamble when you are losing, and how "I'll wait until it gets back to even" brought down a 233-year-old bank.

The whole idea
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

I know the rule: cut your losses and let your winners run. I still do the opposite. Why?

Because your brain keeps score from where you started, usually your entry price. And it counts losses about double.

Double? Where does that come from?

Tversky and Kahneman, 1992. Their typical lab subject weighed a loss about two and a quarter times as much as a gain of the same size. Offer most people a coin flip that costs them a hundred dollars on tails, and they want about two hundred on heads before they'll play.

So I'm cautious. That doesn't sound so bad.

The problem is the shape. How good a gain feels flattens out as it grows, so you grab a sure gain. But the loss side flattens too, so a second loss hurts less than the first. That makes you gamble to avoid a sure loss.

Give me numbers.

Offer someone a sure five hundred dollars, or a coin flip for a thousand. Most take the sure five hundred. Now make it a sure loss of five hundred, or a coin flip to lose a thousand or nothing. Most take the flip. Same sizes, same odds, same averages. Only the sign changed.

And on my screen?

You're down a thousand and price is sitting on your stop. Pull the stop, and tomorrow is a coin flip between getting back to even and losing twenty-two hundred. On average that loses eleven hundred, worse than the stop. But measured from your entry price, the gamble feels less bad. So you move the stop.

How do I stop doing that?

Move the reference point. Ask: would I open this trade, at this size, at today's price? From today, holding is a coin flip to win a thousand or lose twelve hundred. Plainly a bad bet.

Has this ever really blown anyone up?

Nick Leeson at Barings. He hid losses in an error account numbered eight eight eight eight eight, and kept betting bigger to get back to even. Then the Kobe earthquake hit in January 1995, the Nikkei fell, and his positions lost together.

How big did it get?

About eight hundred and twenty-seven million pounds, more than the bank's capital. Barings, founded in 1762, collapsed that February and was sold for one pound.

So I just need more willpower.

No. Knowing about the bias doesn't remove it. Set your exits and your maximum loss while you're flat, and let something other than you, in the moment, enforce them.

So the one-line version?

A loss hurts about twice as much as a gain, so decide your exits before the trade, while the curve has no vote.

Section 1

You feel changes, not levels

Kahneman and Tversky (1979): people code every outcome as a gain or loss from a reference point.

Classical theory says a $100,000 account is worth the same to you whether it was $90,000 or $110,000 yesterday. Nobody feels it that way. For a trader the reference point is almost always the entry price, and the P&L column shows the feeling directly, in red or green.

Why it matters

Move the reference point and the same position feels like a different bet. That is the trick professionals use, and you will see it work on the "Down $1,000, stop right there" slide.

Find your own λ

A coin flip: tails you lose $100. What's the smallest win on heads that makes you happy to play?

A robot that only cared about averages would play for anything over $100. Most people want about $200. That gap is loss aversion, and it is why "losses hurt twice as much" is the slogan.

Section 6

Down $1,000, stop right there

Let the stop hit (a sure −$1,000), or pull it and hold: 50% back to even, 50% out at −$2,200. Holding averages −$1,100: worse.

Measured from entry

Stop feels like −982. Hold feels like −892. The worse bet feels better, so the stop moves.

Measured from today

Hold is a fresh flip for +$1,000 or −$1,200: feels like −340 against 0 for walking away. Plainly bad.

Same trade, same odds. Only the reference point moved. "Would I open this trade, at this size, right now?" puts it back at today.
Section 7 · It actually happened

Barings, 1995: account 88888

It actually happened

Nick Leeson ran Barings' Singapore futures desk and its back office. From 1992 he hid losses in error account 88888 and traded bigger to win them back: short Nikkei straddles, then large long Nikkei futures. The Kobe earthquake (17 Jan 1995) sank the Nikkei; he bought more. London wired margin, believing the book was hedged. Losses of roughly £827m exceeded the bank's capital. Barings, founded in 1762, collapsed on 26 Feb 1995 and was sold to ING for £1.

Thaler and Johnson (1990) named the pull: the break-even effect. After a loss, any bet that offers a way back to even looks attractive.

Section 8 · It actually happened

Not just traders: Boston condos

It actually happened

Genesove and Mayer (2001) studied downtown Boston condominiums after the early-1990s price fall. Owners facing a loss against their purchase price asked more than comparable owners and took longer to sell. The price they had paid, irrelevant to what the flat was now worth, was the reference point.

Where the textbook version breaks

Holding a loser is not proof of bias: sometimes the thesis is intact and the stop was badly placed. The tell is changing the rule because you are losing. And Barings also needed a control failure: the bias was the motive, one person running trading and settlement was the opportunity.

Section 9

What this desk has, and hasn't, tested

The claim: loss aversion costs traders money, and fixed exits beat discretionary ones.

Not tested here No ledger entry measures trader behaviour. The desk tests market claims. Its guard is procedural: every study's rules, sample and kill criterion are fixed before results are seen, and nulls are logged as nulls. A fair test would compare the same signal's pre-set exits with the exits actually taken, trade by trade.
The professional fix

Decide while flat. Exits and maximum loss are set at entry and held by something other than the person in the trade: a hard stop at the broker, a daily loss limit, a risk manager. Benartzi and Thaler (1995), and experiments since: checking P&L less often makes loss aversion bite less.

Question 1

Same expected value, but you take the sure +$500 and gamble on the −$1,000. Which part of the curve does that?

This is the reflection effect. Any curvature below 1 produces it, even with λ = 1. λ is what makes you reject a 50/50 bet with a positive average.
Question 2

What does λ = 2.25 (Tversky and Kahneman, 1992) mean?

It is a weight on feelings, estimated as a median from lab choices, not a fact about markets and not a universal constant.
Question 3

Which question best stops you moving a stop to "get back to even"?

It moves the reference point to today, where holding is a fresh bet judged on its own odds instead of a way back to even.
Question 4

What has this desk found about loss aversion in trading results?

The desk applies the remedy (rules fixed before results are seen) but has not measured the effect. Saying so plainly is the point.

Scenario drill · 17 January 1995

The earthquake has hit, and the hidden losses just got bigger

You run a futures operation in Singapore, and you also control the back office that records your trades. For a while you have parked losing trades in an error account, and the hidden loss has grown. You are short Nikkei straddles and long Nikkei 225 futures. This morning the Kobe earthquake struck and the Nikkei is falling sharply. Both legs are losing together. What do you do?

  1. Buy more futures. Supporting the market is the fastest way back to even.
    This is the convex loss side of the curve choosing for you. A bigger bet offers a chance of getting back to even, and measured from "even" that chance feels better than a certain loss. It is a larger position placed at the moment your judgement is worst.
  2. Report the full loss now, and close or hedge the positions, taking the certain loss.
    Move the reference point to today. From here, the question is whether you would open these positions, at this size, right now. You would not. A certain, visible loss today is smaller than what a hidden, growing one becomes.
  3. Stop adding, keep the positions hidden, and wait for the Nikkei to recover.
    Not adding is better than adding. But hiding the loss keeps the reference point at "even", the margin calls keep coming, and you are still betting the bank on a rebound you cannot control.
  4. Sell more options to raise premium and cover the margin calls.
    It turns a hidden loss into a bigger hidden bet. Collecting premium today adds more risk to the same falling market, all to avoid booking a loss.

What happened. Nick Leeson bought more Nikkei futures, as if to hold the market up. The exchange's margin calls were met with funds wired from Barings in London, which still believed the business was hedged. In late February 1995 Leeson left Singapore. By 27 February the hidden losses had reached about £827 million, more than the bank's capital, and closing out the positions took the total to about £927 million. Barings, founded in 1762, was placed into administration on 26 February 1995 and sold to ING for £1. The Bank of England's Board of Banking Supervision reported that July and put the failure on the absence of basic controls, starting with one person running both trading and settlement.

Sources: Report of the Board of Banking Supervision inquiry into the circumstances of the collapse of Barings, 18 July 1995; Reserve Bank of Australia Bulletin, Implications of the Barings Collapse for Bank Supervisors, November 1995; ING, "ING's £1 deal", 5 March 2025.

Section 12

What to carry forward

01

You feel gains and losses from a reference point, usually your entry price, not your final wealth.

02

The value curve is concave for gains, convex for losses and steeper for losses: λ ≈ 2.25 (Tversky and Kahneman, 1992).

03

Small probabilities are overweighted: the pull of lottery-ticket trades.

04

Being behind makes you risk-seeking, at exactly the wrong moment. Move the reference point to today.

05

Barings, 1995: hidden losses plus doubling down to get back to even, ~£827m, a 233-year-old bank sold for £1.

06

Desk verdict: untested. The guard is deciding while flat: rules and kill criteria fixed in advance.
