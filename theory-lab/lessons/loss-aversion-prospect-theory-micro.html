Why a Loss Hurts Twice as Much

Prospect theory in pictures: why you play safe when you are winning, gamble when you are losing, and how "I'll wait until it gets back to even" brought down a 233-year-old bank.

Section 1

You feel changes, not levels

Kahneman and Tversky (1979): people code every outcome as a gain or loss from a reference point.

what you end up with
−
reference point
=
the gain or loss you feel

Classical theory says a $100,000 account is worth the same to you whether it was $90,000 or $110,000 yesterday. Nobody feels it that way. For a trader the reference point is almost always the entry price, and the P&L column shows the feeling directly, in red or green.

Why it matters

Move the reference point and the same position feels like a different bet. That is the trick professionals use, and you will see it work on the "Down $1,000, stop right there" slide.

Section 4

Find your own λ

A coin flip: tails you lose $100. What's the smallest win on heads that makes you happy to play?

Try it
Smallest win you'd accept$200
your implied λ ≈ 2.0
average profit you demand before playing: +$50 per flip

A robot that only cared about averages would play for anything over $100. Most people want about $200. That gap is loss aversion, and it is why "losses hurt twice as much" is the slogan.

Section 6

Down $1,000, stop right there

Let the stop hit (a sure −$1,000), or pull it and hold: 50% back to even, 50% out at −$2,200. Holding averages −$1,100: worse.

Measured from entry

Stop feels like −982. Hold feels like −892. The worse bet feels better, so the stop moves.

Measured from today

Hold is a fresh flip for +$1,000 or −$1,200: feels like −340 against 0 for walking away. Plainly bad.

Same trade, same odds. Only the reference point moved. "Would I open this trade, at this size, right now?" puts it back at today.
Section 7 · It actually happened

Barings, 1995: account 88888

It actually happened

Nick Leeson ran Barings' Singapore futures desk and its back office. From 1992 he hid losses in error account 88888 and traded bigger to win them back: short Nikkei straddles, then large long Nikkei futures. The Kobe earthquake (17 Jan 1995) sank the Nikkei; he bought more. London wired margin, believing the book was hedged. Losses of roughly £827m exceeded the bank's capital. Barings, founded in 1762, collapsed on 26 Feb 1995 and was sold to ING for £1.

Thaler and Johnson (1990) named the pull: the break-even effect. After a loss, any bet that offers a way back to even looks attractive.

Section 8 · It actually happened

Not just traders: Boston condos

It actually happened

Genesove and Mayer (2001) studied downtown Boston condominiums after the early-1990s price fall. Owners facing a loss against their purchase price asked more than comparable owners and took longer to sell. The price they had paid, irrelevant to what the flat was now worth, was the reference point.

Where the textbook version breaks

Holding a loser is not proof of bias: sometimes the thesis is intact and the stop was badly placed. The tell is changing the rule because you are losing. And Barings also needed a control failure: the bias was the motive, one person running trading and settlement was the opportunity.

Section 9

What this desk has, and hasn't, tested

The claim: loss aversion costs traders money, and fixed exits beat discretionary ones.

Not tested here No ledger entry measures trader behaviour. The desk tests market claims. Its guard is procedural: every study's rules, sample and kill criterion are fixed before results are seen, and nulls are logged as nulls. A fair test would compare the same signal's pre-set exits with the exits actually taken, trade by trade.
The professional fix

Decide while flat. Exits and maximum loss are set at entry and held by something other than the person in the trade: a hard stop at the broker, a daily loss limit, a risk manager. Benartzi and Thaler (1995), and experiments since: checking P&L less often makes loss aversion bite less.

Section 11

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Same expected value, but you take the sure +$500 and gamble on the −$1,000. Which part of the curve does that?

This is the reflection effect. Any curvature below 1 produces it, even with λ = 1. λ is what makes you reject a 50/50 bet with a positive average.
Question 2

What does λ = 2.25 (Tversky and Kahneman, 1992) mean?

It is a weight on feelings, estimated as a median from lab choices, not a fact about markets and not a universal constant.
Question 3

Which question best stops you moving a stop to "get back to even"?

It moves the reference point to today, where holding is a fresh bet judged on its own odds instead of a way back to even.
Question 4

What has this desk found about loss aversion in trading results?

The desk applies the remedy (rules fixed before results are seen) but has not measured the effect. Saying so plainly is the point.
Section 12

What to carry forward

01

You feel gains and losses from a reference point, usually your entry price, not your final wealth.

02

The value curve is concave for gains, convex for losses and steeper for losses: λ ≈ 2.25 (Tversky and Kahneman, 1992).

03

Small probabilities are overweighted: the pull of lottery-ticket trades.

04

Being behind makes you risk-seeking, at exactly the wrong moment. Move the reference point to today.

05

Barings, 1995: hidden losses plus doubling down to get back to even, ~£827m, a 233-year-old bank sold for £1.

06

Desk verdict: untested. The guard is deciding while flat: rules and kill criteria fixed in advance.
