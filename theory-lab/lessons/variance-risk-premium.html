The Variance Risk Premium: Why Implied Volatility Usually Beats Realised

Options have, on average, been priced for more movement than then arrived. That gap pays the people who sell insurance, and it is why selling options looks so good right up to the month it does not.

CONCEPT — a documented average and its price, not a strategy or a signal ~18 min read Prereqs: Black-Scholes, The Four Option Positions
Risk. This is education, not advice. Options can lose the whole premium you pay, and a short option can lose many times the premium you collect. The simulation below is a toy model, not a forecast of anything.

TL;DR
  • Implied volatility is the speed of movement an option is priced for. Realised volatility is the speed the market then actually moved. For S&P 500 options the first has, on average, been higher than the second. That average gap is the variance risk premium.
  • In the one-month windows we measured for this lesson (January 1990 to late 2025), the VIX averaged 19.3 and the index then realised 15.4, a gap of about 3.9 volatility points. The seller of that insurance came out ahead in 364 of 431 windows (84.5%).
  • The average hides a left tail. A short option gains at most the premium and can lose many times it. The five worst windows made up 57% of all the losses, and every one of them was a crisis.
  • The premium exists because investors pay for protection and dealers cannot hedge jumps perfectly. It is compensation for carrying variance that spikes exactly when stocks fall. It is not a mispricing you can lock in.
  • It is also not free money: it is smaller after costs, it is not constant, it is weak or absent in some markets, and it is no timing signal. Size it for the worst month, not the average one.
  • Real-world episode: on 19 February 2020 the VIX closed at 14.38. By 16 March it closed at 82.69, the highest close in Cboe's history from 1990, and the S&P 500 had fallen 33.9% by 23 March. The market was charging for 15% volatility and delivered more than 80%.

I keep hearing that selling options is free money, because implied volatility is almost always higher than what the market then does. Is that true?

Half true, which is the dangerous kind. Start with the true half. Implied volatility is an option's price expressed as a speed: how much movement the market is charging for. Realised volatility is how much it then moved. Over thirty-five years of one-month windows on the S&P 500, the market charged for about nineteen, and the index then moved at about fifteen.

So it charged for nineteen and delivered fifteen.

On average, yes. Call it four points of volatility. And in about eighty-four months out of every hundred, the seller of that insurance came out ahead.

Eighty-four percent. That does sound like free money.

Look at the other sixteen. They are not the mirror image of the good months. In the window that began in March twenty-twenty, the market charged for about fifty-four and then moved at about eighty-nine.

So the average hides a tail.

A short option can only ever earn the premium, but it can pay out many times that. In variance terms, the five worst windows out of four hundred and thirty-one made up more than half of all the losses.

Why would the market overcharge at all? Surely somebody arbitrages it away.

It is not a mispricing you can lock in. It is insurance pricing. Investors buy puts to protect portfolios. The dealers who sell them cannot hedge perfectly, because prices jump, so they charge for that. And volatility spikes exactly when stocks fall, so the seller loses when everyone else is already hurting. Being paid for that is like being paid to sell hurricane cover.

Does the research back it up?

Yes. Carr and Wu, in two thousand and nine, measured it using variance swaps. Coval and Shumway, in two thousand and one, found that buyers of at-the-money straddles lost about three percent a week on average. Bondarenko found that S&P five hundred puts looked too expensive to fit standard models.

Give me one number I can hold on to.

Sell a thirty-day straddle priced at twenty percent volatility, and say the market then moves at fifteen. You collect about four point six percent of the price. On average you pay back about three point four. You keep about one point one percent.

One percent, with the risk of losing fifteen in a bad month.

Exactly. In a simulation with a small chance of a crash each month, the average month is positive, and even so the typical worst drawdown over ten years is about seventeen percent. Triple the size, and more than four paths in ten fall by half at some point.

And the one-line version?

The premium is real on average, and it is the price of a tail you will eventually meet. Size for the worst month, not the average one.

Section 01

Why this matters

Almost everything people say about trading options for income comes back to one fact: option prices have, on average, been higher than the moves they insure. If you sell something for more than it costs you on average, that sounds like a business. Whether it is a business, or a slow way of collecting small amounts before a large one, depends on understanding exactly what the gap is, how big it is, why it exists and what it does to the shape of your results.

This lesson is the first one in the series that is about why a style of options trading might make money at all, which makes it the right place to be most careful. The evidence for the premium is strong. The evidence that you can harvest it safely is much thinner, and the way it goes wrong is specific: long runs of small gains, then a loss that is a large multiple of them.

💡
Plain-English intuition

An option seller is an insurer

A home insurer charges you more each year than it expects to pay out in claims, on average. That gap is how it stays in business. It is not a trick: you are paying for the right to be made whole after a bad year, and the insurer is paid for standing ready to do it.

Now picture an insurer that has had thirty good years in a row. Its accounts look wonderful. Then a hurricane arrives and takes back a decade of profit in a week. Nothing about the earlier years was fake, and the premiums were not "too high" in any sense that lets you collect them without bearing the storm. The average was real and it was the price of the storm.

Selling a stock-index option is the same business. Buyers pay for protection against a sharp fall, or simply for the right to benefit from a big move. Sellers collect the premium. The variance risk premium is the average amount by which those premiums exceeded the movement that then arrived.

Section 02

The math, step by step

Watch the formula build
Where the seller's average profit comes from, one term at a time
\[ \text{premium} \approx \color{#fbbf24}{\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{\pi}}\;\sigma_{imp}\sqrt{T}}\;S \]

An at-the-money straddle (a call plus a put at the same strike) costs roughly 0.8 times the implied volatility times the square root of time, as a fraction of the price \(S\). This is what the seller collects.

\[ \text{premium} \approx \sqrt{\tfrac{2}{\pi}}\,\sigma_{imp}\sqrt{T}\,S \;-\; \color{#fbbf24}{\mathbb{E}\,|S_T - S|} \]

At expiry the seller pays out the distance the price has moved, in either direction. Subtract the average payout.

\[ \mathbb{E}\,|S_T - S| \approx \color{#fbbf24}{\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{\pi}}\;\sigma_{real}\sqrt{T}}\;S \]

If the market then moves at a realised volatility of \(\sigma_{real}\), the average distance moved has the same form as the premium, with realised in place of implied.

\[ \text{average P\&L} \approx \sqrt{\tfrac{2}{\pi}}\,\sqrt{T}\,S\;\color{#10b981}{(\sigma_{imp} - \sigma_{real})} \]

The seller's average profit is proportional to the gap between implied and realised volatility. That gap, in volatility points, is the premium. On average it is positive. In any one month it can be large and negative.

What exactly is being priced. An option's price depends on the volatility you expect between now and expiry. The market's version of that expectation is the implied volatility, \(\sigma_{imp}\): the volatility that, put into the Black-Scholes formula, reproduces the traded price. What then happens is the realised volatility, \(\sigma_{real}\): the annualised standard deviation of the daily returns that actually occurred over the same period. The difference is not a forecast error to be argued about. If you could trade the two directly, you would be paying one and receiving the other.

You can. A variance swap is a contract that does exactly this. At the end of its life, one side pays the other the difference between realised variance and a fixed price agreed at the start, the variance strike:

\[ \text{payoff to the variance buyer} = N_{var}\,\bigl(\sigma_{real}^2 - K_{var}\bigr) \]
N_varvariance notional: money per one point of variance
σ²_realrealised variance over the life of the contract
K_varthe fixed strike, set so the swap costs nothing at the start

Because the swap costs nothing to enter, its strike is the market's price for future variance: \(K_{var}\) is (the risk-neutral) expected realised variance. Carr and Wu (2009) showed it can be replicated by a portfolio of options across many strikes. The Cboe's VIX is built on the same idea. It is the 30-day variance strike implied by S&P 500 options, quoted as a volatility. So the variance risk premium has a precise definition:

\[ \text{VRP} \;=\; K_{var} \;-\; \mathbb{E}\bigl[\sigma_{real}^2\bigr] \;\approx\; \sigma_{imp}^2 - \sigma_{real}^2 \]
VRPthe premium: what the variance seller is paid, on average
K_varthe price of variance in the option market (what the VIX squares to)
E[σ²_real]the variance that then arrives, on average

A note on signs. Carr and Wu quote the premium as realised minus the swap rate, which is negative when sellers are paid. Many practitioners flip it. We use implied minus realised, so a positive number means the seller was paid.

Volatility points and variance points. Volatility is quoted in points (a 20% implied volatility is 20 points). Variance is the square, so the same gap looks bigger: 20 implied against 15 realised is 5 volatility points but \(20^2 - 15^2 = 175\) variance points. The variance form is the one that matters for a variance swap and for the way losses scale, because variance punishes large moves quadratically. A realised volatility of 30 against 20 implied is only 10 points the wrong way, but it is \(900 - 400 = 500\) variance points.

\(\sigma_{imp}\)Implied volatility: the annualised volatility the option's price implies, a fraction (0.20 means 20%).
\(\sigma_{real}\)Realised volatility: the annualised standard deviation of daily returns that actually occurred.
\(T\)Time to expiry in years (30 days is 30/365 = 0.082).
\(S, S_T\)The underlying price now, and at expiry.
\(\sqrt{2/\pi}\)About 0.798: the average size of a normal random variable, \(\mathbb{E}|Z|\). It converts a volatility into an average move.
\(K_{var}\)The variance strike of a variance swap, the market price of future variance.
\(N_{var}\)Variance notional: the money paid per one variance point.
VRPThe variance risk premium: implied variance minus the variance that then arrives, on average.
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine you sell umbrella cover. You charge people as if it will rain a lot. Most weeks it rains less than you charged for, so you keep the difference. The gap between "how much rain I charged for" and "how much rain came" is the premium. It is a real thing, but it is the pay for the week the sky falls in.

Why the average hides a left tail. A short straddle's profit in one period is the premium minus the distance moved, \(P - |R|\). The distance \(|R|\) is never negative, so the profit can never be more than the premium \(P\). But \(|R|\) has no ceiling. Most months it is smaller than \(P\), which is why most months pay. Occasionally it is several times \(P\). The distribution of profit is therefore capped on the right and has a long tail on the left. Its average is positive and its typical bad outcome is far from its average.

The trap. "It makes money in 85% of months" and "it makes money on average" are both true, and neither tells you what the strategy does to your account in the other 15%. The size of the left tail, not the hit rate, decides whether you survive to collect the average.

Worked example

Sell a 30-day straddle priced at 20% volatility; the market moves at 15%

Spot is 100, 30 days to expiry (\(T = 30/365\)), rates zero. Use the exact Black-Scholes at-the-money straddle price, \(2\,(2N(\sigma\sqrt{T}/2) - 1)\) as a fraction of spot.

\[ \text{collected} = 2\bigl(2N(0.20\times0.2867/2)-1\bigr) = 4.57\% \]
σ√T0.20 × √(30/365) = 0.0573
N(·)the normal cumulative distribution
4.57%the premium, as a share of spot

If the price then really moves at 15% volatility, the average payout is the same formula at 15%, so \(2(2N(0.15\times0.2867/2)-1) = 3.43\%\). Because the distance moved averages the straddle value, the seller's average profit is the difference:

\[ 4.57\% - 3.43\% = 1.14\% \text{ of spot} \]

The quick formula agrees: \(0.798 \times 0.2867 \times (0.20 - 0.15) = 1.14\%\). In variance terms the gap is \(20^2-15^2 = 175\) points. The break-even range at expiry is 4.57% either side of spot, from 95.43 to 104.57.

Now the tail. If the index instead falls 10% in the month, the seller pays 10%, so the result is \(4.57\% - 10\% = -5.43\%\). A fall of 15% gives \(-10.43\%\). One such month undoes about nine average months of 1.14%, and the average month already assumed such months were rare.

Section 03

The evidence: how big is the gap, and how often does it pay?

The research on this is old and consistent in direction. These are the studies this lesson relies on. Each is a real paper; the findings are paraphrased from their abstracts, so check the paper for sample periods and exact figures.

Carr and Wu (2009)Variance Risk Premiums, Review of Financial Studies. Builds the variance swap rate from a portfolio of options and compares it with realised variance across five stock indices and 35 individual stocks. The estimated premium is negative (realised below the swap rate, so in the seller's favour) for the S&P 500, S&P 100 and Dow Jones indices, and smaller for the Nasdaq 100 and most individual stocks.
Coval and Shumway (2001)Expected Option Returns, Journal of Finance. Zero-beta at-the-money straddles on the S&P 500 lose, on average, about 3% a week for the buyer. Their reading: a risk such as stochastic volatility is priced in option returns.
Bondarenko (2014)Why Are Put Options So Expensive?, Quarterly Journal of Finance. Historical S&P 500 put prices look too high for standard asset-pricing models. He argues no model in the class he studies can explain it.
Bakshi and Kapadia (2003)Delta-Hedged Gains and the Negative Market Volatility Risk Premium, Review of Financial Studies. Delta-hedged index option positions lose on average for buyers, consistent with a priced volatility risk.
Bollerslev, Tauchen and Zhou (2009)Expected Stock Returns and Variance Risk Premia, Review of Financial Studies. The gap between implied and realised variance helps predict future equity returns, most clearly over a few months.
Christensen and Prabhala (1998)The Relation between Implied and Realized Volatility, Journal of Financial Economics. Implied volatility forecasts realised volatility better than past volatility does, though it is a biased forecast.

Our own count. To see the shape yourself, we took Cboe's daily VIX closes and the S&P 500's daily closes and cut January 1990 to late 2025 into 431 non-overlapping windows of 21 trading days. For each window we compared the VIX at the start with the annualised close-to-close volatility of the next 21 trading days. This is a rough check, not a research-grade estimate: the VIX is not an at-the-money volatility (it includes the price of out-of-the-money puts), and close-to-close realised volatility ignores what happened inside the day.

⚠️
Read this evidence carefully

One history, a few events

This is a single market, one 35-year history, and the red dots are dominated by two crises. An average like this depends heavily on whether disasters of that size happen to be in the sample. If a larger one arrives, the average moves. If none had arrived, the premium would look larger and safer than it is. Overlapping windows, which many studies use, would make the sample look bigger without adding independent events.

Section 04

Why the premium exists

A premium that big needs an explanation, because otherwise someone would sell it until it vanished. The explanations are not exclusive, and they point the same way: sellers are being paid for carrying something unpleasant.

💡
Four reasons, in plain English

Insurance demand, dealer risk, bad-time risk, jumps

1. Demand for protection. Portfolio managers and pension funds buy index puts to cap their downside, and they are price-insensitive because the alternative is explaining a large loss. Garleanu, Pedersen and Poteshman (2009) model how that one-sided demand pushes option prices up when dealers cannot fully hedge what they sell.

2. Dealer risk. The dealer who sells you the option hedges by trading the underlying (see Gamma Exposure & Dealer Hedging Flows). That hedge is perfect only if prices move smoothly and costlessly. When prices jump, or the market gaps overnight, the hedge slips. A dealer charges for that slippage, and for the capital it ties up.

3. It pays out in bad times. Volatility tends to rise when stocks fall (see The VIX Is an Uncertainty Index, Not a Fear Gauge). A buyer of variance therefore holds something that gains in exactly the states when the rest of their wealth is falling. That is what insurance is, and insurance costs more than its average payout. The seller earns the average difference as a reward for taking those states on.

4. Jumps. Black-Scholes assumes smooth paths. Real prices sometimes gap, and no hedge works against a gap. The premium includes a price for that risk.

Where it is smaller or missing. The premium is not a law of nature. Carr and Wu report smaller premia for most of the individual stocks in their sample than for the major indices, and Driessen, Maenhout and Vilkov (2009) trace the index premium largely to the price of correlation risk, not to the variance of single stocks. FX variance premia are studied separately (for example by Londono and Zhou, 2017) and are generally smaller and less consistent than the equity-index one. It also varies through time: the gap is bigger after volatility spikes, but so is the risk, as the next section shows.

Section 05

It actually happened: February to March 2020

It actually happened

The market was charging for 15% and delivered more than 80%

On 19 February 2020 the S&P 500 closed at 3,386.15, a record, and the VIX closed at 14.38. On 10 February, the VIX stood at 15.04: a 30-day at-the-money straddle priced at that volatility cost about 3.4% of the index level.

Then Covid-19 spread outside China. On 11 March the World Health Organization called it a pandemic. The S&P 500 fell 7.6% on 9 March, 9.5% on 12 March and 12.0% on 16 March, and by the close on 23 March it was 2,237.40, 33.9% below its February peak. The VIX closed at 82.69 on 16 March, the highest close in Cboe's daily history, which starts in 1990. Over the 23 trading days from 20 February to 23 March the index's realised volatility, from close-to-close returns, was about 84% a year, and six of those days moved the index by 5% or more, in one direction or the other.

In our window count, the one that started on 10 February (VIX 15.0) saw 51% realised in the following 21 trading days, and the one that started on 11 March, when the VIX was already 53.9, saw 89%. The index fell 18.2% from 10 February to 11 March. A seller of a 30-day straddle on 10 February at the VIX would have collected about 3.4% and paid out about 18.2%, a loss of roughly 15% of the index level, about 13 months of the 1.14% average gain from the worked example. That approximation treats the VIX as an at-the-money volatility, which it is not exactly.

⚠️
Where the textbook version breaks

The premium assumes you are still there to collect it

The average is the average of a very large number of months. Your account lives one path. Margin requirements rise when volatility rises, so the trader is asked for more money at exactly the wrong time. Brokers and risk managers can close a position well before the "long run" arrives. The average premium accrues only to a seller with enough capital and discipline to be there for all of it, and that is a statement about the seller, not about the market.

A weather forecaster who always says "big storm coming" is wrong most days, so people think the forecasts are silly. But the people who bought umbrellas on the one day a storm arrived were glad they paid. The seller of umbrellas looks clever for months and then loses on the one bad day. Both stories are true at the same time.

Section 06

How practitioners actually use this

Dealers and market makers sit on the other side of end-user demand and are structurally exposed to the premium; they earn it as part of the spread and manage the tail with hedges and limits, not with hope. Systematic volatility funds sell index options or variance on a schedule, size them against a loss budget, and often buy cheap far-out-of-the-money options as a floor. Index products such as the Cboe S&P 500 PutWrite and BuyWrite indices systematically sell options, and are used as benchmarks for the idea. Corporates and funds that need protection are on the paying side and treat the premium as a cost of insurance (see Tail Risk Hedging & Portfolio Convexity). Retail traders most often meet the premium as covered calls and cash-secured puts. Later in this series: how to hedge an option, how short-volatility books are sized, and what the spread and margin do to the numbers.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where "sell the premium" goes wrong

Confusing the hit rate with the edge. Winning in 85% of months is compatible with a small, or even negative, total, if the losing months are large enough. Look at the worst month, not the percentage of winners.

Sizing by the average. The simulation above shows the same edge at 1× and 3× size: the median path is nicer at 3×, and over four in ten paths halve at some point. Size comes from the worst case you can survive (see Drawdowns, Risk of Ruin, and Why Good Systems Get Abandoned).

Treating a high VIX as a safe moment to sell. The premium is bigger after spikes, and so is the chance of a second leg down. Our own count has windows that started above 50 and still lost badly.

Counting the gap in volatility points as the profit. The profit is the gap times \(\sqrt{T}\) times about 0.8, as a share of the underlying, before the bid-ask spread, commissions and the cost of margin. At one-percent-a-month edges those costs are not small.

Assuming it carries over. An equity-index premium measured over 35 years does not tell you the size of an FX, rate or single-stock premium. Measure it for the thing you trade, net of costs.

What this lesson does not say. It does not say selling options is a good idea for you. It does not say the premium is constant, large, or the same everywhere. It does not say high implied volatility is a signal to sell, or low is a signal to buy. It does not say the premium survives costs at any particular size. And it does not say the loss month will not come. It says, narrowly, that over a long history of S&P 500 options the average price of movement was above the average movement, and that the difference behaves like a payment for bearing crash risk.
Scenario drill · 3 November 2017

The quietest market on record, and a perfect year

You have sold a one-month at-the-money S&P 500 straddle every month for two years, sized to your comfort, and every month has made money. Today the VIX closes at 9.14, the lowest close in Cboe's daily history, and the index has realised only about 5% volatility over the last month. A 30-day straddle at 9.14% volatility pays about 2.1% of the index level. What do you do with your size?

  1. Add size. The gap is still positive and the strategy has not lost in two years.
    The premium is thin: about 2.1% of the index for a month, against a tail that is as large as ever. Two perfect years are a short sample for a strategy whose losses come from rare events. Adding size here buys the least pay per unit of tail risk you will ever be offered.
  2. Go the other way. Volatility cannot go lower, so buy straddles instead.
    Low can stay low, and the same research says buyers lose on average, month after month, to the premium they pay. "Cheap" is not the same as "about to rise". You would be paying to be early.
  3. Keep size set by the worst month you could survive, not by the calm of the last two years, and consider capping the tail with bought options.
    The premium on offer is at its lowest, so the case for taking more risk is at its weakest. Sizing from the worst case and paying for protection costs some of the thin premium and keeps you in the game for the month that matters.
  4. Carry on unchanged. The long-run average says the premium is positive.
    Defensible, and it is what a rule-based seller would do. But it ignores that sizes chosen in a calm market tend to be too large for a volatile one, and that a thin premium is thin pay for the same tail.

What happened. On 2 February 2018 the VIX closed at 17.31. On Monday 5 February it closed at 37.32, up 20.01 points or 115.6% in a day, and the S&P 500 fell 4.1%. Realised volatility over the 21 trading days from 5 February was about 25%, against about 7% across 2017. A single episode cannot prove a rule; it shows the shape. Calm months paid small amounts, then a few days returned many months of them. Later in this series we look at how short-volatility products and books failed that week.

Section 07

Key Takeaways

✓The premium is the gap between implied and realised variance. Positive on average for S&P 500 options: in our count the VIX averaged 19.3 and the next 21 days' realised volatility 15.4.
✓The seller's average profit is about 0.8 × √T × (σimp − σreal) of spot. At 20 implied and 15 realised over 30 days that is 1.14% a month, before costs.
✓The research is consistent. Carr and Wu, Coval and Shumway, Bondarenko, and Bakshi and Kapadia all find option sellers paid on average in index options.
✓It is insurance pricing, not a loophole. Investors demand protection, dealers cannot hedge jumps, and volatility rises when stocks fall.
⚠The average hides a left tail. Gains are capped at the premium, losses are not, and the five worst windows made up 57% of all losses.
⚠It is smaller after costs, varies through time, and is weaker in many other markets. Measure it for what you trade.
✗It is not free money, and a high VIX is not a safe-to-sell signal. Size for the worst month you can survive; February to March 2020 showed 15% priced and over 80% delivered.
Section 08

Self-test

Q1. Define the variance risk premium, and say what sign convention this lesson uses.
It is the market's price for future variance (the variance strike, which is what the VIX squares to) minus the variance that then arrives, on average: \(\text{VRP} \approx \sigma_{imp}^2 - \sigma_{real}^2\). This lesson uses implied minus realised, so a positive number means the seller was paid. Carr and Wu quote realised minus the swap rate, which is negative when sellers are paid.
Q2. A strategy wins in 85% of months and has a positive average. Why can it still ruin an account?
A short straddle's gain is capped at the premium and its loss is not. The few losing months can be many times the typical gain, so one or two of them can undo years of premium. If the position is large relative to capital, a loss month can also trigger margin calls that end the trade before the average can arrive. The hit rate says how often you win, not how much you lose when you do not.
Q3. Give two reasons the premium exists, and why neither makes it a free lunch.
Investors pay for protection and for the right to benefit from large moves, which pushes prices up; and dealers charge for the risk that they cannot hedge jumps and for the capital that hedging ties up. Also, volatility tends to rise when stocks fall, so the seller loses when wealth is already low. Each is a payment for bearing a specific risk, so the average is compensation for a tail and not a mispricing you can lock in.
Q4. Implied 24, realised 20, 30 days. What is the seller's approximate average P&L as a share of spot?
\(0.798 \times \sqrt{30/365} \times (0.24 - 0.20) = 0.798 \times 0.2867 \times 0.04 \approx 0.92\%\). In variance terms the gap is \(24^2 - 20^2 = 176\) points.
Q5. Why is "the VIX is high, so selling is safer" a poor rule?
Because the premium and the risk both rise after a spike. In our count the largest positive gap followed the 2008 spike (a VIX of 60.7 followed by 35.8% realised), but a window that started with a VIX of 53.9 on 11 March 2020 went on to realise 89%. The premium is larger on average, not guaranteed, and the tail you are exposed to is larger too.
Q6. What does the evidence in this lesson not show?
It does not show that selling options is profitable after costs for a particular trader, that the premium is the same in FX, rates or single stocks, that it is constant through time, or that it gives a market-timing signal. It is one market over one 35-year history, driven by a handful of crises, and it describes an average of many months, not what any single account experiences.
