Hidden Markov Models, Reading the Regime You Can't See

A market never announces whether it's trending or chopping — it only leaves behind a return, a candle, a volatility print. A Hidden Markov Model treats the regime itself as a variable you can't observe directly, infers it from what you actually see, and hands back a probability instead of a hard-coded flag. That last part is the whole point: it's a statistically principled replacement for a hand-set threshold rule, not just a fancier name for one.

Section 1

You can't see the regime — only what it produces

A friend in another city can't show you the weather. They can only send a photo of a stranger's coat. The coat is the clue; the weather stays hidden.

What a chart actually shows you
Only the line is real data. The shading — which stretches are "calm" and which are "volatile" — is exactly what has to be inferred, never something a real chart hands you directly.
Two things keep this problem tractable instead of hopeless. First, regimes are sticky — if the market was calm yesterday, it's probably still calm today; the state doesn't teleport independently each day. Second, what you observe is informative but noisy — a big return is much more likely on a volatile day than a calm one, but it isn't proof; a calm day can still throw off the occasional large move. A Hidden Markov Model formalizes exactly this: a hidden state that persists with some probability, producing an observable signal via its own distribution.
Section 2

Only today matters for guessing tomorrow

The defining assumption underneath all of this is called the Markov property.

The Markov property
P(state tomorrow | entire history)
=
P(state tomorrow | state today)
Everything before today is, formally, irrelevant once you know today. Not because the chain is literally forgetful, but because today's state is assumed to already summarize everything about the past that matters for predicting tomorrow.
1

One-step memory

The whole state sequence collapses down to "what happened yesterday" — that's the only input the model needs to predict today.

⚙

What makes the math work

This is the assumption that turns "track every possible path through history" into something computable at all. Without it, the algorithms in this lesson don't exist.

⚠

A real simplification

Whether you've been trending for 10 days or 1 day is assumed not to matter — only that you're trending right now. That's a strong claim to make about a market.

Where this needs qualifying

Real markets plausibly have longer memory than "just yesterday" — a slow-building macro trend or a multi-week liquidity drought doesn't reset its influence every 24 hours. The Markov property is a deliberate simplification, the same trade-off every tractable model in this curriculum makes somewhere, not a claim that markets are literally this forgetful.

Section 3

The transition matrix: a table of odds

With N possible states, one matrix A captures every one-step probability at once: Aij is the chance of moving from state i today to state j tomorrow.

Two states, four probabilities
A_i1 + A_i2 + … + A_iN
=
1
Every row is a full probability distribution over "where do I go next, given I'm in row-state i right now" — you have to go somewhere, possibly right back to the same state, so each row is forced to sum to exactly 1.

The diagonal entries (A_ii, A_jj) capture persistence — how sticky each regime is. The off-diagonal entries (A_ij, A_ji) capture how often the regime actually switches. A diagonal near 1 is a slow, persistent system; a diagonal near 0.5 for a 2-state chain is nearly a coin flip every single step.

Section 4

Same two states, three different personalities

Same 2×2 shape, wildly different behaviour — just by changing the diagonal.

Both regimes barely let go: expected duration is 1 / (1 − 0.90) = 10 periods each. Day-to-day, this market looks almost regime-blind — whatever state you're in today is a great predictor for next week too.
Barely stickier than a coin flip: expected duration is 1 / (1 − 0.55) ≈ 2.2 periods each. This market punishes any rule that assumes yesterday's regime is still today's.
Our worked example: choppy is the stickier, more common regime (~6.7 days, ~66.7% of the time); trending is rarer and breaks faster (~3.3 days, ~33.3% of the time) — a reasonable FX prior, where range-bound trade is more often the default state.
Section 5

The same hidden state looks a little different every time

The transition matrix says how the hidden state moves. The emission distribution says what it looks like once you can actually observe it.

📈

One distribution per state

The standard choice for returns is a Gaussian per regime — its own mean and variance. Calm might mean a small drift with low variance; volatile might mean near-zero drift with high variance.

🎲

Informative, not certain

A big move is much more likely on a volatile day than a calm one — but it isn't proof. A calm regime can still throw off a large return occasionally; that's exactly why inference gives a probability, not a certainty.

🔗

Where GARCH comes back

"Different regimes, different volatility" is the same clustering idea volatility-clustering models already lean on — an HMM just makes the regime itself an explicit, inferred variable instead of a rolling-window statistic.

Put the pieces together and the model is fully specified by three things: how the hidden state moves (the transition matrix), where it starts (an initial distribution), and what each state looks like once observed (its emission mean and variance). Everything the model can tell you flows from those three.
Section 6

One model, three different questions

Almost all practical use of an HMM reduces to one of these — none needs deriving line-by-line to use productively.

?

Evaluation — forward algorithm

"How likely is this entire data sequence, under my model?" Sums the probability over every possible hidden path efficiently. Used to compare a 2-state model against a 3-state one.

✓

Decoding — Viterbi algorithm

"What's the single most likely hidden path that produced this data?" Picks the one best explanation instead of summing over all of them. Used for a post-hoc regime label at each point in history.

↻

Learning — Baum-Welch (EM)

"What are the transition and emission parameters, estimated from data alone?" Guess the hidden states, refit the parameters as if the guess were truth, repeat until it stops improving.

Evaluation answers "how plausible is this data overall." Decoding answers "what specifically happened." Learning answers "I don't even know the rules yet — figure them out by watching." Three different questions, three different classical algorithms, one shared model underneath.
Section 7

A 2-state transition matrix, worked by hand

Trending (state 1) and choppy (state 2) — an illustrative, made-up-but-sensible starting point, not a fitted result.

The matrix
Trending → Trending: 0.70
/
Trending → Choppy: 0.30
Choppy → Trending: 0.15
/
Choppy → Choppy: 0.85
Both rows sum to 1, as required. Choppy is the stickier regime here (0.85 vs. 0.70) — a reasonable prior for FX, where range-bound, low-conviction trade is often the more common baseline state.

Expected regime duration. If a regime has self-transition probability p, the expected number of consecutive periods you stay in it is 1 / (1 − p) — each period you either "stay" with probability p or "leave" with probability 1 − p. Trending: 1 / (1 − 0.70) = 1 / 0.30 ≈ 3.33 days. Choppy: 1 / (1 − 0.85) = 1 / 0.15 ≈ 6.67 days.

Long-run share. Let the chain run forever and it settles into a stationary distribution: π*trending = 0.15 / (0.30 + 0.15) ≈ 33.3%, π*choppy = 0.30 / (0.30 + 0.15) ≈ 66.7% of all periods.

Worth noticing

Trending has the higher escape probability (it leaves 30% of the time) yet ends up with the lower long-run share. A state you leave more often, you spend less total time in — the matrix's own numbers already tell you something concrete and checkable, before a single emission distribution gets involved.

Section 9

A threshold is a wall; an HMM is a probability

This is the single most practical reason any of this is worth knowing.

Hand-set threshold rule
  • A fixed cutoff, guessed once: "if realized vol crosses 1.5× its 20-day average, call it turbulent"
  • Binary — a value at 1.49× and a value at 1.51× flip to opposite labels despite being nearly identical
  • No sense of confidence: the flag is on or off, with nothing in between
  • Doesn't know or use the fact that regimes persist — every bar is judged in isolation
HMM regime probability
  • Transition and emission structure estimated from data, not guessed by eye
  • Outputs P(state | data) — a graded 0–100% call, not a binary flag
  • 72% confident and 99% confident are visibly different, and both are more informative than a flag
  • Persistence is built in: the model already expects a regime to keep going, the way real regimes do
A threshold and an HMM can even watch the same input and disagree at the same boundary point — but only one of them tells you how sure it is. That distinction is exactly what a position-sizing rule can use, and exactly what a single binary flag throws away.
Where this needs qualifying

None of this is a free lunch. An HMM assumes the Markov property and (usually) Gaussian emissions, needs real data to fit, and Baum-Welch can converge to a mediocre local optimum. It's a more principled tool for the same job a threshold does — not a magic upgrade that requires no validation of its own.

Section 10

How this gets used — and misused — on a real desk

📊

Regime-conditional sizing

Fit an HMM, then size or switch strategy off the current smoothed probability of each regime — not a hard, all-or-nothing label.

🔍

Filtered, not smoothed, when live

Viterbi labeling uses the whole sequence, including the future relative to any given day. A live system needs the filtered probability — computed from data up to now, and only that.

⚖️

Model comparison via likelihood

More hidden states will always raise the fitted likelihood — that alone is never evidence a richer structure is real. AIC/BIC and genuine out-of-sample testing come first.

🧭

One voice among several

Practitioners rarely bet size purely on one model's regime call — it's blended with other signals, the same way a real desk treats any single indicator.

Common pitfalls

Using a Viterbi-labeled history to "trade" a backtest quietly leaks future information into the past. Treating a fitted 2-state model as a literal, permanently stable description of the market — rather than a useful simplification — is a common overreach. And collapsing a "72% trending" call to the same binary flag as a "99% trending" call throws away exactly the information an HMM was built to keep.

Section 11

Quick check

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

State the Markov property in one sentence.

P(state tomorrow | entire history) = P(state tomorrow | state today). Everything before today is assumed irrelevant once today is known — that's the whole simplification.
Question 2

Why must every row of the transition matrix sum to exactly 1?

A row lists "where do I go next, given I'm currently in this row's state" — those possibilities have to exhaust all outcomes. Columns carry no such requirement.
Question 3

In the worked example (A₁₁ = 0.70, A₂₂ = 0.85), which regime is more persistent, and roughly how long does it last?

1 / (1 − 0.85) ≈ 6.67 days for choppy, versus 1 / (1 − 0.70) ≈ 3.33 days for trending. Choppy is the stickier regime.
Question 4

What does an HMM's regime call give you that a hand-set threshold rule doesn't?

A threshold is a binary flag at a guessed cutoff. An HMM outputs P(state | data) — a graded, adaptive confidence, fit from data rather than eyeballed once.
Question 5

Which quantity is safe to use in a live trading signal: the smoothed/Viterbi state probability, or the filtered one?

Viterbi/smoothed labeling uses the entire sequence, including the future relative to any given point — fine for after-the-fact analysis, but a lookahead leak in a live signal. Filtered probability uses only data available up to now.
Section 12

What to carry forward

01

A hidden state can't be observed directly — it's inferred from the observable data it produces (the emission), not read off a rule.

02

The Markov property says only today's state matters for predicting tomorrow — a strong simplification that makes the whole machinery tractable.

03

The transition matrix's rows each sum to 1; the diagonal is persistence, and expected regime duration is 1 / (1 − A_ii).

04

Each hidden state has its own emission distribution — the same state can still look a little different every time you observe it, which is why inference gives a probability, not a certainty.

05

An HMM replaces a fixed, hand-set threshold with an adaptive, data-fit probability — a graded confidence instead of a binary flag, with persistence built in rather than judged bar by bar.

What this now lets you do

Read a transition matrix and translate its diagonal straight into an expected regime duration and a long-run share, by hand. Recognise why a live signal needs the filtered probability, never the smoothed or Viterbi one. And see a hand-set threshold rule for what it is — one brittle, binary special case of a much more general, more honest tool.
