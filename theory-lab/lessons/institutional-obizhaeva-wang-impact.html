Obizhaeva-Wang Transient Price Impact

Kyle's impact is permanent because it's information, and it never fades. Almgren-Chriss's temporary impact reverts the instant you stop trading, with no memory of what you did a moment ago. Obizhaeva-Wang sits in between: impact that decays, but not instantly — a limit-order-book "reservoir" that needs real time to refill — and that one assumption is enough to turn the optimal way to trade a large order from a smooth curve into something lumpier at both ends.

TL;DR
  • Every trade consumes resting liquidity from a finite limit-order-book reservoir near the mid-price, pushing the executed price away from mid. Unlike Kyle's impact, this displacement decays back toward mid over time as the book replenishes — that decay, called resilience, is the entire idea behind the word "transient."
  • The mechanics: between trades, impact \(D_t\) decays via \(dD_t = -\rho D_t\,dt\) (\(\rho\) = resilience rate); each new trade of size \(dx\) jumps \(D_t\) up by \(\kappa\,dx\) instantly (\(\kappa\) = impact-per-unit-traded). That decay-plus-jump process is the model's confidently-known mechanism; the exact closed-form optimal execution schedule it implies is a genuine dynamic-programming result from the original paper, not something safely reconstructed from scratch here.
  • This is a different kind of memory than Almgren-Chriss's temporary impact. AC's temporary impact is a pure function of the instantaneous trading rate — it vanishes the moment you stop trading at that rate, with no separate state variable and no decay speed to even name. OW's transient impact has a genuine half-life: a trade from a few minutes ago is still partly displacing the book when the next one arrives.
  • Because of that finite memory, the optimal execution schedule under this model is famously not a smooth curve. It typically features discrete block trades at the very start and end of the horizon, with lighter, smoother trading in between — because a block placed in the middle has to push through your own still-decaying earlier impact, while blocks at the two boundaries don't pay that penalty.
  • A toy numeric illustration below (\(\rho=2\), \(\kappa=0.01\), \(Q=100\) units over 5 evenly-spaced trades) puts a real number on it: trading it all in one block costs ≈50.0; spreading it evenly costs ≈26.45; a simple front-and-back-loaded split costs ≈25.92 — cheapest, but only by a modest ~2%, because bigger boundary blocks also cost more in instantaneous self-impact. That tension, not a free lunch, is the actual content of the result.
  • Same impact-vs-risk trade-off as Almgren-Chriss underneath it all — trade fast and pay more impact, trade slow and eat more timing risk. The resilience assumption doesn't change that trade-off; it changes the shape of the answer.
  • How it reached trading: Obizhaeva & Wang (2005 working paper, 2013) gave execution desks impact that decays at a finite rate, and with the propagator models that followed it shapes how algorithms space out child orders.
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

When I sell in slices, the second slice always seems to fill worse than the first. Why?

Because the book has not healed yet. Your first slice ate the resting bids near the mid. Those bids come back, but not instantly. Your second slice is pushing into your own footprint.

That's the Obizhaeva and Wang idea?

Yes. A working paper in 2005, published in 2013. They treat the book as a reservoir that refills at a steady rate. They call that rate resilience.

How is that different from the usual impact models?

Kyle's impact is permanent: the market learns something and never goes back. Almgren and Chriss's temporary impact vanishes the moment you slow down. This one sits in between. It has a real half-life.

So two rules?

Two rules. Each trade pushes the price away by an amount proportional to its size. Between trades, that push shrinks steadily back towards zero. Each new trade pays whatever push is left over, plus half of its own.

Put numbers on it.

The lesson sells a hundred units in five trades. Sell it all at once and the cost is fifty. Split it evenly, twenty each, and the cost falls to about twenty-six and a half. The book gets time to heal between slices.

Can I do better than even?

A little. Trade forty at the start, forty at the end, and a sliver in between, and it costs about twenty-five point nine. Roughly two percent cheaper.

Why are the ends special?

At the start the book is fully rested, so there is nothing of yours to fight. At the end, whatever you leave behind never comes back to hurt you, because there is no later trade. In the middle you pay both ways.

So push the ends as hard as I can?

No. Two blocks of fifty and nothing in between costs about twenty-eight point four, worse than the even split. Big blocks pay more for their own push. The real answer is a balance the paper solves exactly.

What's the catch in practice?

The resilience rate is hard to measure, and it probably moves. Books are likely thinner around the open, the close and data releases, and slower to heal in stressed markets. Get it wrong and the shape of your schedule is wrong.

One line?

Impact heals at a finite speed, so space your slices to let it heal, and trade bigger where your footprint cannot hurt you: the start and the end.

Section 01

Why this matters

This is the second of three lessons in this curriculum's Liquidity category, and it sits between two close relatives that are easy to blur together with this one — worth separating cleanly before any equations show up. The Kyle-Obizhaeva Market Microstructure Invariance lesson (lesson 1) is about something else entirely: how the overall scale of trading activity — bet sizes, trade counts, dollar volume — compares across different assets and regimes in a way tied to volatility and turnover. It happens to share a coauthor, Anna Obizhaeva, with this lesson's model, but it is a genuinely different paper answering a genuinely different question — nothing in it says anything about how one specific order's own impact decays over the course of its own execution. That is this lesson's entire subject, and the two should never be conflated just because the name "Obizhaeva" appears in both titles.

The Market Impact Models & Almgren-Chriss lesson is the closer relative. It already splits impact into temporary (reverts once trading stops) and permanent (an information-driven repricing that doesn't revert), and derives the optimal execution trajectory that trades off impact cost against timing risk. Read carefully, that lesson's own temporary-impact term is a function of the instantaneous trading rate alone — it costs \(\tfrac12\eta\dot x(t)^2\) at each instant and, critically, has no memory of anything that happened a moment earlier; the model contains no parameter for how fast a concession fades, because within that framework it fades essentially the instant you stop pushing at that rate. Obizhaeva & Wang (2013, building on an earlier widely-circulated working paper) ask what changes if that assumption is relaxed: what if the book doesn't snap back instantly, but refills gradually, at some finite resilience rate \(\rho\)? The mechanics of the answer are this lesson's subject, and the single most famous consequence — that the optimal schedule stops looking smooth — is the headline result worth carrying away.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Footprints in wet sand, not a memory-foam pillow

The Almgren-Chriss lesson uses a memory-foam pillow for temporary impact: press your thumb in, let go, and the dent springs back essentially immediately. That's a fine picture for impact with no decay speed of its own. Obizhaeva-Wang needs a different one. Picture instead a footprint pressed into wet sand at the tide line. The print doesn't disappear the instant your foot lifts — water has to slowly seep back in and level the sand out again, which takes real, measurable time. If you step in the exact same spot again before that time has passed, your foot sinks in deeper than it would have on fresh sand, because you're pressing into ground that hasn't finished recovering from the last step.

That is exactly the mechanism. The limit order book near the mid-price is a shallow reservoir of resting orders. Trading draws it down on your side, pushing the price away from where it would otherwise sit. Left alone, new limit orders queue back in — the book's own resilience — and the price drifts back toward the level it would have been at without your trade. But if you trade again before that refill finishes, you're drawing from a reservoir that's already partly drained, and you pay more for the same size than you would have if you'd waited for it to recover. Nothing here is about new information changing anyone's beliefs — it's purely a mechanical, liquidity-supply story, and that's exactly what makes it transient rather than permanent.

How it reached trading

Impact that fades, but not instantly

Transient impact · Obizhaeva & Wang, working paper 2005, published 2013 → execution scheduling

Almgren-Chriss (2000) gave execution desks their first standard schedule, but its temporary impact has no memory: stop trading and it vanishes at once. Traders slicing a large order know the book does not refill that fast, so a child order sent too soon after the last one pays for both.

Anna Obizhaeva and Jiang Wang modelled the order book as a reservoir that refills at a finite rate (resilience), first as a 2005 NBER working paper and then in the Journal of Financial Markets in 2013. Its result (blocks at the start and end, steady trading in between) changed how execution researchers thought about schedule shape. Alfonsi, Fruth & Schied (2010) extended it to general book shapes, and Gatheral (2010) linked decaying impact to no-arbitrage conditions, alongside the propagator models of Bouchaud and co-authors.

Today decaying-impact (propagator) models are a standard part of execution research and of how algorithm designers set the spacing between child orders.

Section 02

The math, step by step

Watch the formula build
Where a schedule's cost comes from, one term at a time
\[ dD_t = \color{#fbbf24}{-\rho\,D_t\,dt} \]

Between trades the impact state heals back towards zero at rate \(\rho\), with half-life \(\ln(2)/\rho\). At \(\rho = 2\) that is about 0.35.

\[ D_{t^+} = D_{t^-} + \color{#fbbf24}{\kappa\,dx} \]

A trade lands on top of whatever has not healed yet and pushes the state up by \(\kappa\) per unit traded.

\[ \text{cost}_i = q_i \color{#fbbf24}{D_{t_i^-}} + \tfrac12\kappa q_i^2 \]

Each trade pays two things: the leftover push from your own earlier trades, and half of its own instantaneous push.

\[ \text{Total} = \color{#fbbf24}{\sum_i} \left( q_i D_{t_i^-} + \tfrac12\kappa q_i^2 \right) \]

Add up across the schedule. Bigger trades raise the self-impact part; trades placed while the book is still displaced raise the leftover part.

\[ \color{#10b981}{\text{one block } 50.00 \quad\; \text{even } 10.00 + 16.45 = 26.45 \quad\; \text{ends } 16.67 + 9.25 = 25.92} \]

The worked example, \(Q = 100\), \(\rho = 2\), \(\kappa = 0.01\), five trades: loading the ends pays more self-impact but much less leftover, and wins by about 2%.

The confidently-known part of this model is the mechanism itself — a single state variable that decays continuously and jumps at each trade. What follows describes that mechanism precisely. It deliberately stops short of re-deriving the paper's exact closed-form optimal trajectory: that result comes from a genuine dynamic-programming / calculus-of-variations argument in the original paper, and presenting invented algebra as if it reproduced that derivation exactly would be worse than being honest about the limit of what's safely reconstructed here.

\(D_t\)The price-impact state — how far the price currently sits from where it would be with no trading at all. This is the model's one piece of "memory."
\(\rho\)(rho) The resilience rate — how fast \(D_t\) decays back toward zero between trades, in units of 1/time. Larger \(\rho\) means a faster-healing book.
\(\kappa\)(kappa) The instantaneous impact coefficient — how much \(D_t\) jumps per unit traded, the instant a trade happens.
\(dx\)An infinitesimal trade size at a given instant.
\(p_t^0\)The unaffected price process — what price would do with no trading from this order at all.
\(p_t\)The actual observed price, \(p_t = p_t^0 + D_t\), in the base version of this model.
1

Between trades: exponential decay

With no trading happening, the impact state decays continuously back toward zero:

\[ dD_t = -\rho\,D_t\,dt \]

This is the same first-order linear decay used for mean reversion in the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck lesson elsewhere in this series — on its own, with no jumps, \(D_t\) just relaxes back to zero at rate \(\rho\), with half-life \(\ln(2)/\rho\).

2

At a trade: an instantaneous jump

A trade of size \(dx\) at time \(t\) pushes the impact state up immediately, before any decay has a chance to act:

\[ D_{t^+} = D_{t^-} + \kappa\,dx \]

\(D_{t^-}\) is whatever the previous trade's impact has decayed down to by the time this new trade arrives — the "leftover displacement" from your own earlier activity. \(D_{t^+}\) is the state immediately after this trade lands on top of it.

3

The price you actually trade at

The base Obizhaeva-Wang setup treats \(D_t\) as the entire displacement from the unaffected price — there is no separate, non-decaying permanent component sitting alongside it in the original model (later extensions in the literature add one; that generalization isn't needed to get the core resilience story right). A trade executed while the book is displaced by \(D_{t^-}\) pays roughly that concession, plus roughly half of its own instantaneous push (the same triangular "walking the book" approximation used elsewhere in this curriculum for a linear-shaped book):

\[ \text{cost of trading } dx \text{ at } t \ \approx\ dx\left(D_{t^-} + \tfrac12\kappa\,dx\right) \]
D_tthe "leftover push" your own trading has left in the book right now
-ρD_t dtcontinuous decay back toward zero — the book healing itself
κ dxan instant jump up, the moment a new trade lands
D_{t⁻} + ½κdxwhat a marginal unit of the current trade actually pays
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Two rules, and that's the whole engine. Rule one: if nobody's trading, the leftover push from the last trade shrinks smoothly over time, like a ripple flattening out. Rule two: the instant a new trade happens, the push jumps back up, right on top of whatever ripple hadn't flattened out yet. Everything this lesson says about why block trades happen comes from just those two rules working together.

Three impact models, one axis to compare them on: does the impact you create have a memory, and if so, how long does it last?

📈

Kyle (1985) — permanent, no decay at all

\(\Delta p = \lambda Q\): a linear, information-driven repricing that never fades, because it isn't a liquidity-supply effect in the first place — it's the market rationally updating its belief about fundamental value because your trade revealed something. There's nothing to decay back from; the new belief simply persists.

⚡

Almgren-Chriss's temporary impact — memoryless, instant reversion

Cost \(\tfrac12\eta\dot x(t)^2\) at each instant, a pure function of the current trading rate with no separate state variable behind it. It reverts as soon as the rate drops, with no notion of "how fast" — the model has no resilience parameter because it doesn't need one; recovery isn't gradual, it's built in as immediate.

🌊

Obizhaeva-Wang's transient impact — a genuine half-life

\(D_t\) is a real state variable with its own dynamics: it jumps up at each trade and decays continuously at rate \(\rho\) in between. A trade a few minutes ago is still partially displacing the book right now, unless \(\rho\) is large enough (or enough time has passed) for it to have mostly washed out. This is the one genuinely new ingredient relative to both of the other two.

Section 03

Why the optimal schedule isn't smooth

Almgren-Chriss's optimal trajectory can be a straight line (risk-neutral) or a smoothly front-loaded curve (risk-averse), but it's always smooth — no sudden jumps in trading rate anywhere in the interior. That falls straight out of the memoryless assumption above: since impact cost only depends on the current rate, and rate enters the cost quadratically, there's never a reason to trade in an abrupt lump rather than spreading the same size out slightly, since a lump costs disproportionately more per the convex \(\dot x^2\) penalty with nothing to offset it. Once impact has real memory, that logic breaks, and it breaks specifically at the two ends of the trading horizon.

Trading at the boundaries — cheap, in a specific sense

At \(t=0\), \(D_0=0\) by construction — there's no prior impact to fight, so a large trade right at the start pushes into a completely rested book. At \(t=T\), the opposite argument applies: whatever impact this last trade leaves behind will never have to be paid for again, because no further trading from this order is coming to collide with it. Neither boundary trade has to "pay to restart" against leftover displacement from its own earlier activity — there either isn't any yet, or there's no future trade left to be hurt by it.

Trading in the middle — you fight your own footprint

A trade placed in the interior of the horizon has to push through whatever \(D_{t^-}\) is left over from the order's own earlier trading — the book hasn't fully healed yet. Worse, the impact that this trade adds will itself partially decay away before the order finishes, meaning part of the "cost" paid to move the price just evaporates unused rather than helping execute the next chunk any more cheaply. Money spent pushing the price mid-horizon is money partly wasted on a push that heals before it can be leveraged again.

That asymmetry — boundaries are structurally cheaper to push hard, the interior structurally isn't — is the entire intuitive content of Obizhaeva & Wang's famous result: the cost-minimizing schedule places discrete block trades at \(t=0\) and \(t=T\), with a lighter, smoother trading rate filling in the interior between them. It is not, however, an argument for pushing the boundary blocks to their logical extreme — the worked example below shows exactly why not.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Stepping in fresh sand at the very start of your walk is easy — nothing's been disturbed yet. Stepping exactly where you'll never walk again, right at the very end, is also fine — you'll never have to step there again while it's still soft. But stepping in the same soft patch in the middle of your walk, over and over, means every step after the first one sinks in deeper than it needs to, and some of that sinking heals back before you even get to use the flattened spot again. Front and back are the two places where a big step doesn't cost you anything extra later.

Worked example

Comparing three schedules under a toy resilience rate

Sell \(Q=100\) units over a normalized horizon \(T=1\), in 5 evenly-spaced trades at \(t=0,\,0.25,\,0.5,\,0.75,\,1.0\), with \(\rho=2\) and \(\kappa=0.01\). Cost of each trade uses the formula from Step 3 above: \(\text{cost}_i = q_i D_{t_i^-} + \tfrac12\kappa q_i^2\), with \(D\) decaying between trades and jumping by \(\kappa q_i\) right after each one. This is a simplified numerical illustration of the mechanism, not the paper's own optimized solution.

All-at-once (A) is worst by far — every unit pays the full quadratic self-impact cost of a 100-unit trade at once, with no chance for anything to decay first. Front-and-back-loading (C) beats even splitting (B), but only by about 2% (25.92 vs. 26.45) — not the dramatic win the "block trades" headline might suggest. The breakdown columns show why: C's boundary trades are bigger, so their own instantaneous self-impact cost is higher (16.67 vs. B's 10.00) — but that's more than made up for by a much lower residual cost from fighting leftover displacement (9.25 vs. B's 16.45). The two effects mostly offset; a small net win survives.

Pushing the idea to its extreme makes the trade-off obvious: trading the entire 100 units in exactly two 50-unit blocks at \(t=0\) and \(t=1\), with nothing at all in between, costs ≈28.38 — worse than either B or C above, because a block that large pays so much extra self-impact cost that it swamps the decay savings. "Front-and-back-load" does not mean "as extreme as possible" — finding the genuinely cost-minimizing block size and interior rate is exactly the optimization the original paper solves, and this toy comparison only samples a few illustrative candidates around it.

Section 04

How practitioners actually use this

Very few desks solve a full Obizhaeva-Wang-style optimization by hand for a live order — but the resilience framing shapes real execution-algorithm design in a specific, recognizable way: it's the formal justification behind why adaptive execution algorithms sometimes deliberately "wait out" a period of unusually thin depth rather than trading straight through it at a constant rate, and why some scheduling logic explicitly avoids clustering large child orders too close together in time even when the target participation rate would otherwise allow it — both are informal, practitioner-level echoes of "don't trade into your own still-decaying impact."

Real-world trading scenario

Two desks, one large FX order, two very different assumptions about how the book heals

A desk needs to work a large EUR/USD order over the next hour. Desk A models temporary impact the Almgren-Chriss way: a rate-dependent cost with no notion of "how fast the book heals," so its optimal schedule — whatever shape risk aversion gives it — is smooth throughout, with no structural reason to ever place a sudden large child order mid-window rather than a slightly smaller one spread over a few extra seconds. Desk B assumes something closer to Obizhaeva-Wang: liquidity providers rebuild depth at some finite, estimated resilience rate, so Desk B's algorithm is willing to front-load a meaningfully larger opening child order (the book is fully rested at the start), trade lighter through the middle of the hour specifically to avoid pushing into its own still-recovering depth, and place a similarly larger closing child order right before the deadline, since nothing will trade against its leftover displacement afterward.

Both desks are internally consistent with their own stated model — the disagreement is entirely about which assumption (memoryless instant recovery, or gradual resilience at some rate \(\rho\)) better describes how FX liquidity in this specific pair, at this specific time of day, actually behaves. That is an empirical question about the book's real resilience rate, not a question either model can settle on its own — and it's exactly the kind of parameter the next pitfall below flags as genuinely hard to pin down.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where this model is a stylized approximation, not a photograph of the book

Linear impact and pure exponential resilience are both simplifications. The model assumes each unit traded adds exactly \(\kappa\) to \(D_t\) regardless of how much has already been traded (no square-root-law-style concavity, unlike the empirical evidence the Almgren-Chriss lesson's own square-root section documents), and assumes the book heals along a single clean exponential curve with one resilience rate. Real order books plausibly recover along a mix of timescales — a fast initial partial bounce followed by a slower drift — that a single \(\rho\) cannot capture.

The resilience rate \(\rho\) is genuinely hard to estimate, and it moves around. Unlike a volatility estimate, which has decades of standard estimation machinery behind it, \(\rho\) has to be backed out from how quickly observed post-trade price reversals actually happen — a noisier, less-standardized exercise. It also plausibly varies intraday (thinner around the open and close, or around scheduled data releases) and by volatility regime (a stressed, fast market likely heals slower than a calm one), meaning a single calibrated \(\rho\) is, at best, a time-varying quantity being treated as fixed for tractability.

This is not the invariance framework, and doesn't inherit its insight. Nothing here says anything about how the resilience rate \(\rho\), the impact coefficient \(\kappa\), or the optimal block sizes should scale from one asset to another, or across volatility regimes — that cross-asset comparability question is exactly what the sibling Kyle-Obizhaeva Market Microstructure Invariance lesson addresses, from an entirely different paper. A calibrated \(\rho\) for one FX pair says nothing, on its own, about the right \(\rho\) for a different pair or a different regime — that would require exactly the kind of scaling argument this lesson does not make.

Scenario drill · an illustrative execution hour

Laying out a large sell order over the next hour

You must sell a large EUR/USD order within the hour, in five child orders at evenly spaced times. Your estimate is that the book heals at a finite, moderate rate: as in the lesson's toy example, about 40% of one child order's push has healed by the time the next one goes. Using the lesson's toy costs as a guide (one block 50.00, even split 26.45), how do you lay out the five child orders?

  1. Sell it all in the first child order and be done.
    One block pays the full self-impact of the whole size at once, with no time for the book to heal: 50.00 in the toy example, almost twice the even split.
  2. Make the first and last child orders larger and trade lighter in between.
    At the start the book is fully rested, and whatever the last order leaves behind never hurts a later trade. The middle orders push into your own leftover impact, so they should be smaller. In the toy example, 40 / 6.67 / 6.67 / 6.67 / 40 costs 25.92, the cheapest of the schedules shown.
  3. Two half-size blocks, at the start and the end, with nothing in between.
    Pushing the idea to its extreme backfires. Each block pays so much self-impact that it swamps the healing saved: 28.38 in the toy example, worse than an even split.
  4. Five equal child orders.
    Close to the best: 26.45 against 25.92 in the toy example, about 2% more. A sensible default when you do not trust your estimate of how fast the book heals.

The point. This is an illustrative situation using the lesson's toy numbers, not a real order or a real calibration. With finite resilience the cheap places to trade bigger are the two ends. The saving over an even split is modest, and it depends on a healing rate that is hard to estimate and moves through the day, so the even split remains a respectable fallback.

Section 05

Key Takeaways

✓Impact here has a genuine half-life. Between trades, \(dD_t=-\rho D_t\,dt\); at each trade, \(D_t\) jumps up by \(\kappa\,dx\). That decay-plus-jump process is the entire mechanism — the exact optimal-trajectory algebra it implies belongs to the original paper.
✓Three impact models, one axis: does the impact decay, and how? Kyle — permanent, no decay. Almgren-Chriss's temporary impact — memoryless, instant reversion, no resilience parameter at all. Obizhaeva-Wang — genuine finite-speed resilience.
⚠The optimal schedule is boundary-loaded, not smooth — because trades at t=0 and t=T don't have to fight leftover impact the way an interior trade does — but the worked example's ~2% edge over even splitting shows this is a real, modest effect, not a dramatic free lunch.
✗More front/back-loading isn't automatically better. Pushing the boundary blocks to their extreme (two blocks, nothing in between) cost more in the worked example than a moderate split — bigger blocks pay more instantaneous self-impact, which can outweigh the decay savings.
⚠Linear impact, pure exponential resilience, and a hard-to-estimate, regime-varying \(\rho\) are the model's real limitations — a stylized approximation of book dynamics, not a literal description of one.
✗This is not the Kyle-Obizhaeva Invariance framework. Same coauthor, different paper, different question — nothing here says how \(\rho\) or \(\kappa\) should scale across assets or regimes.
ℹOn a desk it sets the spacing of child orders. Resilience, how fast the book refills, decides how long to wait between slices; decaying-impact (propagator) models in this family are standard in execution research.
Section 06

Self-test

Q1. Write the two rules that define the Obizhaeva-Wang impact state D_t.
Between trades, \(D_t\) decays continuously toward zero: \(dD_t = -\rho D_t\,dt\), where \(\rho\) is the resilience rate. At the instant of a trade of size \(dx\), \(D_t\) jumps up immediately: \(D_{t^+} = D_{t^-} + \kappa\,dx\), where \(\kappa\) is the instantaneous impact coefficient. Together, continuous decay between trades and an instant jump at each trade are the entire mechanism.
Q2. How does Obizhaeva-Wang's transient impact differ from Kyle's impact, specifically in terms of decay?
Kyle's impact is permanent — it never decays at all, because it isn't a liquidity-supply effect; it's an information-driven repricing that the market has no reason to walk back once it's happened. Obizhaeva-Wang's transient impact is a mechanical, liquidity-consumption effect with no informational content of its own, and it decays continuously back toward zero at rate \(\rho\) whenever no new trade is adding to it. "Transient" versus "permanent" is precisely the decays-eventually versus never-decays-at-all distinction.
Q3. How does Obizhaeva-Wang's transient impact differ from Almgren-Chriss's temporary impact, given that both are said to "revert"?
Almgren-Chriss's temporary impact is memoryless: it is a pure function of the current instantaneous trading rate, \(\tfrac12\eta\dot x(t)^2\), with no separate state variable behind it, and it reverts essentially the instant the trading rate drops — the model has no parameter for how fast it fades, because it doesn't model gradual fading at all. Obizhaeva-Wang's transient impact is a genuine state variable, \(D_t\), with its own dynamics and a real half-life set by \(\rho\); a trade from a few minutes ago can still be partially displacing the book when a new trade arrives, which is impossible by construction in the memoryless Almgren-Chriss framework.
Q4. Why, intuitively, are the two boundaries of the trading horizon (t=0 and t=T) structurally cheaper places to trade a large block than the interior?
At \(t=0\), \(D_0=0\) by construction, so a large trade there pushes into a fully rested book with no leftover impact to fight. At \(t=T\), whatever impact the final trade leaves behind never has to be paid for again, because no further trading from this order will collide with it. An interior trade has neither advantage: it has to push through leftover displacement from the order's own earlier trading, and part of the price concession it pays will itself decay away, unused, before the order finishes — money spent moving the price that doesn't help execute the rest of the order any more cheaply.
Q5. In the worked example, why did trading the entire 100 units in exactly two 50-unit blocks (nothing in between) cost more than the moderate front-and-back split (40/6.67/6.67/6.67/40)?
Both schedules avoid the interior-trading penalty in a similar boundary-loaded way, but the two-block version's boundary trades are much larger — 50 units each versus 40. Since the instantaneous self-impact cost of a trade grows with the square of its own size (\(\tfrac12\kappa q^2\)), the two-block schedule pays substantially more in self-impact cost than it saves in reduced residual/decay-fighting cost, and ends up more expensive overall (≈28.38) than the moderate split (≈25.92). Bigger boundary blocks are not automatically better — the genuinely cost-minimizing block size balances both effects, which is exactly what the real optimization (not this toy comparison) solves for.
Q6. True or false: the Kyle-Obizhaeva Market Microstructure Invariance hypothesis and the Obizhaeva-Wang transient impact model in this lesson are the same idea from two papers with the same title.
False. They share one coauthor (Anna Obizhaeva) but are two distinct papers answering two distinct questions. The invariance hypothesis (with Kyle) is about how the scale of trading activity — bet sizes, trade counts, volume — compares across different assets and regimes. The transient-impact model in this lesson (with Wang) is about how a single order's own price impact decays over the course of its own execution. Neither one says anything about the other's specific question, and this lesson does not inherit the invariance framework's cross-asset scaling insight.
