Granger Causality, Does X's Past Actually Help?

The VECM lesson answered one narrow question for one narrow situation: for an already-cointegrated pair, which side does the correcting? This lesson asks a broader, more precisely defined question that applies to any two series at all — does knowing X's history genuinely sharpen a forecast of Y, beyond what Y's own history already gives you — and answers it with an exact F-test, not a glance at a chart.

The whole idea
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

On my charts one currency always seems to move a day before the other. Is that a signal?

Maybe. But your eye is a terrible judge of that. Granger's test replaces the impression with a number.

How does it work?

You build two forecasts of the follower. The first uses only the follower's own past. The second also gets to see the leader's past. Then you ask whether the second forecast is better by more than luck would give you.

Why include the follower's own past at all?

Because a price that trends or bounces can look like it is following anything. If its own history already explains the move, the other series has added nothing. That is the difference between this test and just eyeballing correlations at different lags.

Walk me through the numbers.

The lesson has twelve observations. Using the follower's own past, the squared forecast errors add up to about zero point four three. Add the leader's past and that falls to about zero point zero eight. Most of the error is gone.

So it's real.

You still have to weigh it. Any extra regressor helps a little by chance. The F-test divides the improvement by the noise per spare data point. Here that gives about thirty-nine. The one percent cut-off is about ten and a half. So yes, in this toy sample, the leader's past genuinely helps.

Then it causes the other move.

No, and that is the famous trap in the name. It means the past helps forecast, nothing more. A shared driver that reaches one price feed a few seconds earlier gives the same result. So does a market that closes hours before the other, so its price is simply older.

What else can go wrong?

Run it on price levels instead of returns, and two trending series look linked when they are not. Choose the number of lags after peeking at the result, and you are data mining. Pick the lags first, with an information criterion.

And direction?

Test both ways. Each direction is its own regression. Both can be significant at once. That is a feedback loop, not a broken test.

Has this found anything real?

The classic is Kawaller, Koch and Koch in 1987. They found S and P 500 futures leading the cash index by several minutes. It became a standard tool for price discovery.

One line?

A lead is only real if the leader's past still helps after the follower's own past has had its say.

Section 1 · How it reached trading

Does one market move first?

Granger causality · Granger, 1969 → futures–cash lead-lag studies, 1980s

How it reached trading

Granger (1969) defined "X leads Y": X's past improves forecasts of Y beyond Y's own past. Lead-lag studies such as Kawaller, Koch & Koch (1987) found S&P 500 futures leading the cash index by minutes.

It became the standard first screen for lead-lag across futures and cash, rates and FX, credit and equity. Granger shared the 2003 Nobel with Engle.

Section 2

A narrower, more honest way to ask "who moves first"

Eyeballing a chart is an impression. This is a specific, testable procedure.

👀

The chart-reading trap

"The spread always seems to dip before FX sells off" is a real observation about one dataset — and exactly the kind of pattern easy to convince yourself of after the fact.

📐

Granger's actual proposal (1969)

Build the best forecast of Y from Y's own past alone. Build a second forecast that also sees X's past. Test — formally — whether the second one is significantly better.

🐓

The name is misleading

"Granger-causes" ≠ "causes." Roosters crowing before sunrise would pass this test — the Earth's rotation drives both, and the test can't see that.

🔭

Already previewed, not yet built

The ARIMA & VAR lesson introduces this test in one paragraph and flags "no such fit has been run yet." This deck is that paragraph, built all the way out.

Treat every result below as "X's history has genuine forecasting content for Y" — never as "X causes Y." That distinction isn't pedantic. It's the entire content of what this test can and can't tell you.
Section 3

Two nested regressions, one question

The unrestricted model reduces exactly to the restricted one when every X-lag coefficient is zero.

The two models
Restricted
:
Y_t = c + Σ a_i Y_{t-i}
→
RSS_r
Unrestricted
:
Y_t = c + Σ a_i Y_{t-i} + Σ b_j X_{t-j}
→
RSS_u
H₀: b₁ = b₂ = ... = b_p = 0 — X's lags add nothing. The F-test asks whether dropping RSS from RSS_r to RSS_u is a real improvement, or just what q extra useless regressors would buy by chance.

Both models are fit by OLS on the same sample, with the same fixed lag order p, chosen ahead of time by AIC/BIC — never tuned until the causality test itself looks significant.

Section 4

The F-statistic, in full

The formula
F
=
(RSS_r − RSS_u) / q
÷
RSS_u / (n − k)
Numerator: RSS improvement per restriction removed. Denominator: the unrestricted model's own residual-variance yardstick. Under H₀, F follows an F(q, n−k) distribution.

q = number of restrictions (the dropped X-lag coefficients). k = total parameters in the unrestricted model (intercept + all Y-lags + all X-lags). n = observations actually used, after losing p to lagging. A large F means the improvement is bigger than q meaningless extra regressors would plausibly buy — reject H₀.

Section 5

Two mandatory pre-checks — both silently break the test if skipped

Try it
Pick p with AIC/BIC first. Fit the Y-only autoregression at several candidate lags, take whichever minimizes AIC or BIC, then use that same p in both Granger regressions. Searching lags until the causality test itself looks best is a multiple-testing shortcut that inflates false positives.
Test returns, not price levels. Fitting directly on trending, non-stationary levels risks the classic Granger & Newbold (1974) spurious-regression result — an impressive-looking F-stat from two series that simply both trend over time, with no genuine link.
Cointegrated pairs need a VECM, not a differenced VAR. Naively differencing throws away the long-run relationship. Test the error-correction term (Z_{t-1}, from the VECM lesson) alongside the short-run lags instead of silently dropping it.
Section 6

Test both directions — feedback is a real outcome, not a broken test

One-directional
  • X Granger-causes Y (test 1 significant)
  • Y does not Granger-cause X (test 2 not significant)
  • X behaves like a genuine leading indicator for Y
Bidirectional — feedback loop
  • Both tests significant
  • Each series' past helps forecast the other
  • Neither is a pure leader — common for closely related instruments

X-causes-Y and Y-causes-X are two separate hypotheses on two separate regressions. Running only one direction and assuming the reverse is automatically ruled out is a common, avoidable mistake.

F(1,9) critical value ≈ 5.12 at 5%, ≈ 10.56 at 1%. 38.65 clears both by a wide margin — reject H₀: X Granger-causes Y in this toy sample, well past the 1% level.

Section 11

Where this quietly goes wrong

📉

Non-stationarity

Fitting on trending price levels instead of returns risks the classic Granger & Newbold spurious-regression result — a confident-looking F-stat from two series that simply both trend.

🎭

A common confounder

A shared risk-sentiment shock hitting two FX pairs' feeds at slightly different times can produce a clean, significant, but entirely spurious one-directional result.

🕐

Synchronicity effects

Different market close times or update timestamps can make one series mechanically look like it "leads" another — a data-construction artifact, not a real predictive relationship.

📏

Low power, short samples

Every added lag shrinks n−k. Too many lags or too short a sample can hide a real relationship entirely — a false negative, the opposite risk from the pitfalls above.

Section 12

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

What does "X Granger-causes Y" precisely mean?

It's a statistical, incremental-predictive-power claim within a specific regression comparison — never a claim about true causation.
Question 2

RSS_r = 0.60, RSS_u = 0.20, q = 2, n = 30, k = 5. What's F?

F = (0.60−0.20)/2 ÷ 0.20/(30−5) = 0.20 ÷ 0.008 = 25.
Question 3

Why must the lag order p be chosen by AIC/BIC before testing, not searched until the test looks best?

The restricted and unrestricted models must share one fixed, principled lag order for the F-test's reference distribution to be valid.
Question 4

Two genuinely cointegrated series are differenced and tested with a plain Granger F-test. What goes wrong?

A plain differenced VAR silently drops the error-correction term (Z_{t-1}) that carries the pair's long-run linkage — exactly the ARIMA & VAR lesson's own warning about cointegrated pairs.
Question 5

A shared risk-sentiment shock hits both currency pairs A and B, reaching A's feed slightly earlier. A bivariate Granger test on A and B alone comes back significant. What does this illustrate?

The test cannot distinguish "A's history genuinely helps forecast B" from "an unobserved third factor drives both, hitting A's feed first." The shared shock does all the work here.
Question 6

Does this repo have a real Granger causality F-test running anywhere?

js/nasdaqResearch.js, js/creditLeadLagEngine.js, and js/yieldCouplingCore.js all run real, live cross-correlation-at-various-lags studies — the simpler, non-regression cousin of this lesson's test, not the F-test itself.
Your call
Scenario drill · an illustrative research morning

A cross-correlation peak at lag two

You trade two commodity currencies against the dollar. A colleague shows you daily returns where one pair's move correlates with the other pair's move two days later, a clear peak at lag +2. He wants to trade it from tomorrow: buy the follower when the leader rises. What do you do first?

  1. Trade it. A clear peak away from lag zero is a lead.
    A raw cross-correlation does not control for the follower's own momentum. If the follower is simply autocorrelated and the two pairs are correlated in general, you get the same peak with no extra information in the leader at all.
  2. Run the Granger test on the two price levels, trying lag orders until one is significant.
    Two mistakes at once. Trending levels produce spurious significance, and searching lag orders for the smallest p-value is data mining. Either one can manufacture a result from nothing.
  3. Pick the lag order with AIC or BIC on returns, run the Granger F-test in both directions, and check the timestamps and closing times behind each series.
    This is the test the lesson builds. It credits the leader only if its past helps after the follower's own past is in the model, it allows for feedback either way, and the timestamp check rules out a lead that is just one close being older than the other.
  4. Run the Granger test one way, leader to follower, on returns at a lag picked in advance.
    The core test is right. But it skips the reverse direction and the timing check, so a feedback loop or a stale-close artefact could still pass as a clean one-way lead.

The point. This is an illustrative situation, not a real trading day or a real result. A cross-correlation peak is a reason to run the Granger test, not a reason to trade. Even a clean significant result only says the leader's past helps forecast; a shared risk driver or mismatched timestamps can produce it with no real link between the two pairs.

Section 13

What to carry forward

01

Granger causality has one precise, statistical definition: X's lags significantly improve forecasting Y, beyond Y's own lags — nothing more.

02

The test is a nested F-test: F = (RSS_r−RSS_u)/q ÷ RSS_u/(n−k), comparing a restricted and unrestricted regression against an F(q, n−k) reference distribution.

03

Two pre-checks are mandatory: pick the lag order with AIC/BIC first, and confirm stationarity — or route a cointegrated pair through the VECM's error-correction framework instead.

04

Test both directions. Feedback (both significant) is a coherent, common outcome — not a broken test.

05

"Granger-causes" is never "causes" — confounding, non-stationarity, and measurement-timing artifacts can all produce a significant result with no real link.

06

On a desk: from Granger (1969) and the 1980s futures-cash studies, it is the first screen on any lead-lag signal.

What this now lets you do
