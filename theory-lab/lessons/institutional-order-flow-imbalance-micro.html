Order Flow Imbalance & the Microprice

Every other lesson in this category reconstructs something about a market from historical trade data, after the fact. This one closes the category by building the two signals a live electronic desk actually watches move, update by update, tick by tick, right now — order flow imbalance and the microprice, both built directly from the order book's own bid/ask price and size changes.

Section 1

Watching the lines grow, not just who got served

Trade classification watches a security-camera replay of who already traded. OFI watches the two lines themselves, live.

Trade-based signals

Look at trades that already executed and infer, after the fact, who was probably the aggressor. Inherently a lagging signal — it only exists once a trade has crossed the spread.

Order Flow Imbalance

Built from every book update — including the far more numerous updates where nothing traded at all. Pressure can build up visibly before it ever shows up as a run of same-signed trades.

The Limit Order Books lesson already gave this category its bid/ask price and size notation, Pb, Qb, Pa, Qa. Nothing here reintroduces that structure — this deck takes it as given and asks a narrower question: given how the book just changed, which way is pressure leaning, right now?
Section 2

Building OFI, one update at a time

Each side of the book contributes a signed value at every update. Step through the three cases.

Try it
Pbn-1 = 1.1004
→
Pbn = 1.1005
Bid price improved up. The whole new size counts: ebn = Qbn — a fresh, better bid is unambiguous positive pressure.
Qbn-1 = 400
→
Qbn = 600
Bid price unchanged, size grew. ebn = Qbn − Qbn-1 = 200. More resting buy interest at the same price is still positive pressure — just a smaller dose than a price improvement.
Qan-1 = 400
→
Qan = 300
Ask size shrinks. ean = 300−400 = −100. Since OFIn = ebn − ean, subtracting −100 adds 100 to OFI — a thinning ask is buy-side pressure too, even though nothing traded.

OFIn = ebn − ean, summed over a short window. The subtraction is what makes the mirror logic work: whatever makes the ask side less of an obstacle to buyers reads as positive, exactly like the bid side building up directly.

Section 3

The microprice: weight each price by the OTHER side's size

⚖

The naive midpoint ignores size entirely

(P_b+P_a)/2 treats a book with 10 units on one side and 10,000 on the other exactly the same as a perfectly balanced book.

🔀

Cross-weight instead

P_micro = (P_b·Q_a + P_a·Q_b) / (Q_b+Q_a) — the bid price is weighted by the ASK's size, and the ask price by the BID's size.

🧱

Heavy side = more resistance

A thick wall of resting size on one side means more has to be consumed before price reaches through it — that side is less likely to be where price moves next.

➡️

The formula leans toward the light side

Because the heavy side's size becomes the OTHER price's weight, the heavy side's own price gets down-weighted — the average leans toward whichever side is thinner and easier to move through.

If ten people hold one end of a tug-of-war rope and two hold the other, you don't bet the rope stays in the middle — you bet it drifts toward the two-person end, where there's less resistance. The microprice is that bet, written as a formula.

Section 5

Queue imbalance: the number underneath the microprice

QI = (Q_b − Q_a) / (Q_b + Q_a). Ranges from −1 (all size on the ask) to +1 (all size on the bid).

The algebra actually works out clean

Write P_b = M − h and P_a = M + h (midpoint M, half-spread h). Substitute into the microprice formula and everything collapses to P_micro = M + h·QI — the naive midpoint, nudged by half the spread in the exact direction the book is lopsided. This isn't an approximation; it's an exact rearrangement of the same formula.

QI = 0 collapses the microprice straight back to the plain midpoint — no lopsidedness, no adjustment. QI = +1 pushes the microprice all the way to the ask price; QI = −1 pushes it all the way to the bid price. The microprice can never sit outside the [P_b, P_a] range, no matter how extreme the imbalance gets.

Section 10

Skewing quotes before a trade ever tells anyone anything

Real-world trading scenario

A market-making desk quoting both sides of a pair continuously can't wait for a run of trades to confirm which way pressure is leaning — by then it's already been picked off. Watching OFI turn strongly positive and QI climb toward 0.5, as in the worked example, lets a desk lean its own bid and ask up before the price moves — re-centering quotes on the microprice rather than a stale, size-blind midpoint.

This is the single most common practical pairing of the two signals: OFI as the early-warning pressure gauge, the microprice as the actual reference price a desk re-centers its quoting around, tick by tick.

Section 11

Where both signals quietly mislead

Displayed size can vanish
  • Resting size can be cancelled an instant after being observed
  • Spoofing manufactures a false QI reading on purpose, then pulls the order
Short horizon only
  • Both signals are built for the next few updates or ticks, not minutes or hours
  • A queue can refill or reverse within the same second a reading was taken
A strongly positive OFI is not proof of informed trading

OFI says pressure is building at the top of the book right now — it says nothing about whether that pressure is genuine new information (PIN's question) or a large, uninformed order simply working through the book. The two lessons answer different questions and shouldn't be substituted for each other.

Section 13

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

What is OFI actually built from?

OFI is event-driven off the book itself, including updates where nothing traded — a genuinely different, often earlier signal than trade-sign methods.
Question 2

Why does a shrinking ask size (price unchanged) add a POSITIVE amount to OFI?

e^a_n = Q^a_n − Q^a_{n-1} < 0 when ask size shrinks; OFI_n = e^b_n − e^a_n, so subtracting a negative number adds to OFI — correctly reading a thinning ask as buy-side pressure.
Question 3

Why does the microprice weight the bid price by the ASK's size (not the bid's own size)?

Cross-weighting means a heavy side's own size becomes the OTHER price's weight — pulling the average toward the lighter, more easily-moved side, exactly matching the intuition that more resistance makes a side less likely to be reached.
Question 4

Midpoint = 1.5000, spread = 0.0010, QI = −0.4. What's the microprice?

h = 0.0010/2 = 0.0005. P_micro = 1.5000 + 0.0005×(−0.4) = 1.5000 − 0.0002 = 1.49980 — pulled down, toward the bid, since QI is negative (ask-heavy).
Question 5

What does this lesson add on top of PIN (lesson 4 of this category), rather than replace?

PIN is a historical, after-the-fact estimate of how much trading is information-driven. OFI and the microprice are what a desk actually watches move in real time — different timescales, different questions, neither substituting for the other.
Section 14

What to carry forward

01

OFI is built from bid/ask price and size changes between consecutive book updates, not from trade signs — it can flag pressure building before any trade happens.

02

Each side contributes a signed value (price improved, size changed, or price worsened), summed with mirrored signs — a thinning ask reads as buy pressure exactly like a thickening bid does.

03

The microprice weights each side's price by the OPPOSITE side's size, pulling the fair-value estimate toward whichever side has less resting size — less resistance — standing in its way.

04

Queue imbalance and the microprice are the same idea, algebraically: P_micro = M + (S/2)·QI — the midpoint, nudged by half the spread toward whichever side is lopsided.

05

Both signals are short-horizon and built on displayed size that can vanish an instant later — genuinely useful, but not a multi-minute forecast and not immune to spoofing.

06

This closes the category's arc: trade classification, Roll, Glosten-Milgrom and PIN explain and estimate spreads and informed trading from history; OFI and the microprice are what a live desk actually watches, tick by tick.
