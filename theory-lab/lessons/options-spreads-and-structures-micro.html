Lesson 3 · Options in Practice

Option Spreads & Structures, or Four Bricks, Many Shapes

A bull call spread, a straddle, a butterfly, an iron condor, a collar: they sound like different products. They are not. Each is the same four bricks combined to make a view cheaper, cap the loss, or get paid only for the exact move you expect. Educational, not advice: a long option can lose its whole premium, and a short option can lose far more.

The whole idea
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

I know what a call and a put are. But then people say iron condor and butterfly and I switch off. Is that a different product?

No, and that is the good news. There are only four bricks: buy a call, sell a call, buy a put, sell a put. A straddle is two of them. A butterfly is three or four. A condor is four. Learn the bricks and the rest is arithmetic.

Arithmetic how?

You add the payoffs. Say the stock is at one hundred, three months out, volatility twenty percent. The one hundred call costs about four point two three. That is your bought brick. Now you also sell the one hundred and five call, and you collect about two point two two. Your net cost is two point zero one.

So the call you sold pays for more than half of the one you bought.

Yes. And what did you give up? Everything above one hundred and five. The best you can make is the five points between the strikes, minus what you paid: two point nine nine. The worst is the two point zero one. You break even at about one hundred and two.

So it is a cheaper call with a ceiling.

Exactly. That is the first reason structures exist: you sell what you do not need in order to pay for what you want. The second is to cap the loss. The third is to shape the bet, for example to be paid only if the stock stays in a range.

The range one sounds safe. The iron condor?

Careful. In the lesson's example it collects about one point four nine and the worst case is about eight and a half. On the model's own numbers it makes money roughly three times in four. But the wins are small and the losses are big, and at fair prices that is simply a fair bet in a different shape. Defined risk is not low risk.

So where is the edge?

Nowhere, by construction. Every leg is fairly priced in the model, so no mix of fair legs is a bargain. A structure changes what you are paid for, not whether you have an edge. And every extra leg pays its own bid-offer spread.

What about before expiry?

That surprises people. The diagram at expiry is the end of the road. A butterfly's best case is about four point oh three, with the stock exactly at the middle strike. Halfway to expiry, with the stock sitting right there, it is worth about thirty-eight cents. Time pays the butterfly, and only at the end.

And the Greeks?

They add up, leg by leg. A straddle is long gamma, long vega, and pays time decay. A condor or a butterfly is the mirror image: it collects time decay and is short gamma and short vega. That is why one wants a big move and the other wants quiet.

Has this actually hurt anyone?

Barings, January 1995. A trader sold straddles on the Nikkei: collect the premium, no wings. The Kobe earthquake hit on the seventeenth and the market moved. Losses eventually reached eight hundred and twenty-seven million pounds. A bought option on each side would have put a ceiling on the loss from that one structure.

One line to take away?

A structure is four bricks added up. Before you trade it, ask what you pay, what you can lose, where you break even, and what you sold to make it cheap.

Section 1

Four bricks, and everything else is a sum

Buy or sell, call or put. Stack them at different strikes and expiries and you get every named structure.

↗

Buy a call

Pay 4.23 for the 100 strike. Loses at most the premium; gains without limit above the strike.

↘

Sell a call

Collect the premium. Gains at most that; the loss grows without limit above the strike.

↙

Buy a put

Pay 3.73 for the 100 strike. Loses at most the premium; gains as price falls, down to zero.

↖

Sell a put

Collect the premium. Gains at most that; the loss grows as price falls, down to zero.

Stacking bricks adds their profit and loss at every price, and adds their Greeks too. The payoff, the cost, the worst case and the Greek signs of any structure are just sums.

A spread is two bricks: buy one, sell another

Where the two ramps point in opposite directions, they cancel into a flat shelf. That shelf is the whole trick.

Section 3

Why structures exist, and what each one costs you

💷

Cheaper

Sell the part of the move you do not need. The 100/105 spread costs 2.01 against 4.23 for the call alone. You pay with the upside above 105.

🧱

Capped loss

A bought wing turns a short option's open-ended loss into a known worst case. You pay with part of the premium you would have kept.

🎯

A precise bet

Straddles pay for movement, butterflies for a pin, condors for quiet. You pay with every outcome the shape ignores.

At fair prices every leg is fair, so no combination of fair legs is a bargain. Structures change where you win, not whether you have an edge. Always ask: what did I sell to make this look attractive?
Section 4

Verticals: pay to be right, or be paid for not being wrong

Debit: bull call 100/105
  • Buy the 100 call, sell the 105 call
  • Pay 2.01; best 2.99; worst 2.01
  • Break-even 102.01
  • Reward to risk 1.49 to 1
Credit: bull put 95/90
  • Sell the 95 put, buy the 90 put
  • Receive 1.10; best 1.10; worst 3.90
  • Break-even 93.90
  • Reward to risk 0.28 to 1: loses 3.56 times what it makes

Same strikes, call or put: a debit call spread (2.01) and a credit put spread (2.97) add up to the discounted width, 4.98. They are the same bet in different clothes.

The expected-move rule

At the money, call plus put is about 0.8 × spot × σ√T: 7.98 against the model's 7.97. The straddle price is the market's priced move.

Where this needs qualifying

Sell the straddle and you collect the premium and have no ceiling on the loss in either direction. That is Barings, below.

Section 6

Butterfly and condor: a tent and a plateau

Long butterfly 95/100/105
  • Buy 95 call, sell two 100 calls, buy 105 call
  • Pay 0.97; best 4.03 only at exactly 100, at expiry
  • Break-evens 95.97 and 104.03
  • Halfway to expiry, spot at 100: worth only 0.38
Short iron condor 80/90/110/120
  • Sell 90 put and 110 call; buy 80 put and 120 call
  • Receive 1.49; worst 8.51
  • Break-evens 88.51 and 111.49
  • Model chance of profit 75%, but reward to risk only 0.17 to 1

The butterfly and the iron butterfly are the same payoff built from calls or from puts: 0.97 plus 4.00 is 4.98, the discounted width. The condor is a bull put spread plus a bear call spread.

Section 7

Calendar, collar, risk reversal

📅

Calendar: sell near, buy far

Sell the 1-month 100 call (2.39), buy the 3-month (4.23): debit 1.85. Long theta and vega, short gamma. Its best case, about 1.57, falls to 0.76 if vol drops 5 points.

🛡

Collar: shares, a put, a short call

Buy the 95 put (1.74), sell the 105 call (2.22): a credit of 0.48 at flat vol. Loss capped at 4.52, gain at 5.48. With downside skew the credit vanishes: a debit of 0.04 at −3.

⇄

Risk reversal: buy a call, sell a put

Buy the 105 call, sell the 95 put: a debit of 0.48 at flat vol, gain without limit, and a short put that can lose up to the strike. In FX it is quoted as a vol difference that reads the skew.

Each one finances a bought leg with a sold leg. "Zero cost" means the premium nets out; it does not mean nothing was given up.

Long gamma, long vega, pays theta
  • Straddle: Γ +0.0794, Θ −0.044, ν +0.397
  • Strangle: Γ +0.0701, Θ −0.039, ν +0.350
  • Wants a big move, or vol to rise

Greeks add leg by leg. A calendar is the odd one: long theta and vega (+0.016, +0.084) but short gamma (−0.0293).

Section 10

Defined risk is not low risk

Where this needs qualifying

The condor makes money about 75% of the time in the model. When it wins it averages 1.42; when it loses it averages 4.31. The expected value is −0.01: a fair price, not an edge. Four legs also pay four bid-offer spreads: at 0.05 a leg the credit falls from 1.49 to 1.29.

Where the textbook version breaks

The expiry diagram is the last day. The butterfly's best case is 4.03, but halfway to expiry with spot at 100 it is worth 0.38. Short American-style legs can be assigned early, and flat vol hides the skew that makes a "free" collar cost money.

Section 11 · It actually happened

Barings, 1995: a short straddle with no wings

It actually happened

As widely reported, on 16 January 1995 a Barings trader sold a straddle on the Nikkei 225: a bet the market would not move much, with a premium collected and no wings. The Kobe earthquake struck on 17 January and the market fell. Losses reached about £827 million (US$1.4 billion), about twice Barings' available trading capital, and Barings was declared insolvent on 26 February 1995. Hidden positions and weak controls made it far worse; the option structure is only one part of the story.

Section 12

Quick check

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

A 100/105 bull call spread costs 2.01. What is its maximum gain?

The two ramps cancel above 105, so the most the spread can pay is the width, 5. The profit is that less what you paid: 5 − 2.01 = 2.99. The debit is the worst case.
Question 2

A long 100 straddle costs 7.97. Where does it break even?

Above 100 the call pays one for one and the put is worthless; below 100 the put does the same. You recover the premium at 100 plus or minus 7.97.
Question 3

The iron condor profits about 75% of the time in the model. Is that an edge?

Wins average 1.42, losses average 4.31, so 0.75 × 1.42 − 0.25 × 4.31 is about zero. Defined risk limits the loss; it does not remove it.
Question 4

Why do a debit call spread and a credit put spread on the same two strikes behave like the same bet?

2.01 plus 2.97 is 4.98, which is 5e^(−rT). Both are bullish with the same capped shape.
Question 5

The butterfly's best case at expiry is 4.03. Halfway to expiry, with spot at 100, what is it worth?

Before expiry, the sold middle options hold nearly as much time value as the bought wings, so the net value is small. The expiry diagram is the last day only.
Your call
Scenario drill · 16 January 1995

You want to be paid for the Nikkei sitting still

You trade Nikkei 225 index options and believe the market will not move much over the next few weeks. You want to collect premium for that view. Rather than a plain bought option, you are choosing a structure. Which do you use?

  1. Sell an at-the-money straddle: the most premium for the view.
    It collects the most, and nothing stops the loss in either direction. If the market is hit by a shock, the loss is a multiple of the premium with no ceiling. This is the structure that turned an ordinary range view into a catastrophe.
  2. Sell a wider strangle and rely on a stop-loss order.
    A wider strangle is safer on a normal day, but the loss is still uncapped. A stop order does not protect you from a gap: it is a trigger to trade, and if the market jumps through your level, you are filled at the next available price, far beyond it.
  3. Sell an iron butterfly or iron condor: the same short premium, with a bought option on each side as a wing.
    You collect less, but the worst case is fixed in advance at the wing width less the credit. A shock costs you that amount and no more. You pay for the protection out of the premium; that is the price of surviving the surprise.
  4. Skip the trade: your view of "quiet" is not strong enough to be paid for.
    Defensible and cheap. If you cannot say what you sold to collect the premium, and what the worst case is in points and in money, standing aside is a position. It earns nothing, but it loses nothing.

What happened. As widely reported, on 16 January 1995 a trader at Barings sold a straddle on the Nikkei 225. The Kobe earthquake struck early on 17 January and the market fell. The trader added to other positions hoping for a recovery. Losses reached about £827 million (US$1.4 billion) and Barings was declared insolvent on 26 February 1995. The losses were made far larger by hidden positions and failures of control, so this drill is about the option structure only. A wing on each side of that one option position would have capped its loss; it would not have fixed the controls.

Sources: Board of Banking Supervision, Report of the Board of Banking Supervision Inquiry into the Circumstances of the Collapse of Barings, HMSO, July 1995; Nick Leeson, Rogue Trader, Little, Brown, 1996. Figures (£827 million, US$1.4 billion, 26 February 1995) are as commonly reported; the exact position size is not stated here.

Section 13

What to carry forward

01

Every structure is four bricks (buy or sell, call or put) added up. Payoff, net premium and Greeks are all sums over the legs.

02

Structures buy you cheapness, a cap on the loss, or precision. Each is paid for by what you sold: the 100/105 spread costs 2.01 instead of 4.23 and gives up everything above 105.

03

Debit and credit versions on the same strikes are the same bet (their prices add to the discounted width); a collar is a bull call spread plus cash.

04

Read the signature: straddles and strangles are long gamma and vega and pay theta; condors, butterflies and credit spreads are the mirror image.

05

Defined risk is not low risk and a high win rate is not an edge: the condor wins about 75% of the time and has an expected value near zero. The expiry diagram is not today's value, and four legs pay four spreads.

06

It actually happened, Barings 1995: a short straddle with no wings. A bought option on each side caps a structure's loss.

What this now lets you do

Take any named structure apart into its legs, compute its cost, worst case, break-evens and Greek signs yourself, and ask the right question before you trade it: what did I sell to make this attractive?
