Lesson 5 · Time Series & Regimes

Reading a Price Series' Memory

Almost every trading decision reduces to one binary question: at this level, right now, should the system fade the move or follow it? This lesson is one way to answer that — not by predicting direction, but by measuring how "persistent" the recent path has been, independent of any price model. Two tools do this: the Hurst exponent, and the variance ratio test that a rigor-obsessed pair of economists built to do the same job properly.

The whole idea
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

Everyone tells me to check whether a market is trending or mean-reverting before I pick a strategy. How do you actually measure that?

Picture three people leaving a bar. One wanders at random. One keeps stumbling the same way. One is on a leash held by a friend at the door. The question is which one you are watching.

And you tell them apart by how far they get?

By how their distance grows with time. The random walker's distance grows with the square root of the number of steps. The stumbler gets further, faster. The one on the leash stays closer. The Hurst exponent puts one number on that. A half means random. Above a half means trending. Below a half means mean-reverting.

Where does the name come from?

Harold Hurst, a British hydrologist. He studied the Nile's flood records to size a reservoir, and found that wet years clustered with wet years. His number came out well above a half. Decades later Mandelbrot took the method into markets.

You said there's a second test, and that it's the better one.

The variance ratio, from Lo and MacKinlay in 1988. If daily moves are independent, the wobble of a five-day return should be exactly five times the wobble of one day. Independent wobbles just add up.

So you compare the real five-day wobble with five times the daily one.

Yes. Say the daily variance is about point one three. Five times that is about point six four. If the real five-day figure comes out at point four five, the ratio is about point seven. The days are cancelling each other out. That is mean reversion.

And if it came out at point nine five?

Then the ratio is about one and a half. The days are teaming up. That is trending. And unlike the Hurst slope, this test comes with a proper standard error, so you can ask whether the gap from one is real or just noise.

Does persistence actually show up in real markets?

It comes and goes. Trend followers bet on it. In 2008 the stock market lost about thirty-seven percent, and broad managed-futures indices finished up by double digits. Much of the 2010s was lean for the same rules. Then in 2022 inflation peaked at nine point one percent, the Fed hiked hard, yields rose and the dollar climbed for most of the year, and trend followers had a very strong year.

So if I get a Hurst reading of point five five on the last few weeks, I follow the trend?

Careful. On short samples the classic method is biased towards finding trends, even in pure noise. A reading from a few dozen bars can flip across a half on noise alone. And neither number tells you direction, only whether recent moves have reinforced or undone each other.

So what is it good for?

As a gate. It tells you which family, fade or follow, has a right to work. And near the middle, it tells you to stand aside.

Give me the one-line version.

Watch how the wobble grows with time: faster than the square root means trending, slower means mean-reverting, and a short window is a noisy hint, not a fact.

Section 1

Three drunks leaving the same bar

Same starting point, three different kinds of "randomness." Only one of them is a true coin flip.

🎲

The random walker

Each stumble is an independent coin flip — no memory of the last step. Distance from the door grows with the square root of time. That's the whole story. No edge to be found in the shape of the path.

↗

The self-reinforcing drunk

Stumble left, and the next stumble is more likely to also go left — momentum, overcorrection into overcorrection. Distance from the door grows faster than the square root of time. This is a trending series.

🔗

The one on a leash

Stumble left, and the leash tension makes the next stumble more likely to go right, back toward center. Distance grows slower than the square root of time. This is a mean-reverting series.

Nobody hands you a label saying which drunk you're watching. What you get is a price series and a question: does it have memory that reinforces past moves, memory that undoes them, or no memory at all? That property is exactly what decides whether a fade strategy or a follow strategy has any right to work right now.
Section 2

H — a single number that names the drunk

The Hurst exponent is extracted purely from how a series has wandered historically — no model of "why," just a measurement. Pick a regime.

Try it
H ≈ 0.3. Every push is met with a pull back the other way — small, frequent reversals that never let the path stray far from the dashed centerline. Range grows slower than √n.
H ≈ 0.5. No memory at all — pure coin-flip wandering, drifting up here only by chance. Range grows exactly as √n. This is the "no edge in the path shape" baseline every trending or mean-reverting claim gets measured against.
H ≈ 0.7. Up days beget more up days — momentum compounding rather than getting undone. Fewer, longer, smoother swings that persist before eventually reversing. Range grows faster than √n.

Formally: H < 0.5 is anti-persistent / mean-reverting, H = 0.5 is a random walk, H > 0.5 is persistent / trending. H near 0 is strongly mean-reverting; H near 1 is strongly trending, in the limit a near-straight line.

Section 3

How H actually gets measured: rescaled range

Take a chunk of n returns. Walk a dot up or down by each day's deviation from the chunk's own mean, starting at zero.

R(n)
=
max Yₜ − min Yₜ
S(n)
=
std. deviation of the chunk

R(n) is that walk's total swing — highest point minus lowest point. S(n) is the chunk's own volatility, used to put the swing on a scale-free footing: a chunk of 1% daily moves and a chunk of 0.1% daily moves should give comparable (R/S) values once you divide out their own volatility. Hurst's empirical finding — first made on Nile river flood records, not markets — is that (R/S)ₙ scales as a power law: (R/S)ₙ ≈ c · nH.

Why logs

Taking logs of both sides turns that power law into a straight line: log(R/S)ₙ = log c + H·log n. Split the series into windows of several lengths (say 8, 16, 32, 64 bars), plot log(R/S)ₙ against log n for each length, and fit a line. The slope of that line IS the Hurst exponent — the entire point of taking logs, and exactly the OLS step this project's own live Hurst estimator runs.

Section 4

A hydrologist, then a fractal geometer

1951 — Harold Edwin Hurst

A British hydrologist studying the Nile's flood records for the Aswan reservoir. He needed to know how large a reservoir had to be to smooth out runs of wet and dry years, and found the Nile's records had H well above 0.5 (estimates run from about 0.7 to 0.9) — strong long-run persistence, wet years clustering with wet years. Very much not a random walk, with real engineering consequences for how much storage a dam needed.

Decades later — Benoit Mandelbrot

Picked up Hurst's rescaled-range method and applied it to financial time series, arguing that market returns show long-range dependence and "wild" statistical behavior that a pure random-walk model of finance understates. A straight line from a Nile River engineering problem to a line of code in a forex entry-bias feature.

This is worth pausing on because H is the same fade/follow question Ornstein-Uhlenbeck mean reversion and trend-day classification ask from other angles — but a third, model-free lens on it. It assumes no particular generative process, no OU equation, no drift/diffusion decomposition. It just measures how the empirical range has scaled historically.

Section 5

The cleaner alternative: variance ratio

R/S has real statistical weaknesses (small-sample bias, ahead). Lo & MacKinlay (1988) formalized a sharper, testable version of the identical question.

A true random walk has one precisely falsifiable property: variance is additive across independent increments. If one-day returns have variance σ², then a k-day return — the sum of k independent one-day returns — must have variance exactly kσ². No assumption beyond "increments are uncorrelated" is required.

Var(rt(k))
÷
k × Var(rt(1))
=
VR(k)

where rt(k) is the k-period return (e.g. 5-day) and rt(1) is the 1-period (daily) return. Under the random-walk null, VR(k) = 1 for every k, exactly. Deviations are directly interpretable — no eyeballed regression slope required.

VR(k) > 1 — trending

k-day variance is larger than the random-walk prediction. Returns are positively autocorrelated — days are teaming up to wobble more than independence would predict. Same story as H > 0.5.

VR(k) < 1 — mean-reverting

k-day variance is smaller than the random-walk prediction. Returns are negatively autocorrelated — days are canceling each other out more than independence would predict. Same story as H < 0.5.

Lo & MacKinlay also derived the statistic's asymptotic distribution under the null — an actual standard error and z-statistic, not just "does this slope look off." That's what makes VR the more rigorous of the two tools.

Section 6

A tiny variance-ratio calculation by hand

10 daily log returns, in percent: 0.4, −0.2, 0.6, 0.1, −0.5, 0.3, −0.1, 0.5, −0.4, 0.2.

Worked example
Step 1 — 1-day variance. Variance (population formula) of the 10 raw values ≈ 0.1289 %².
Step 2 — build 5-day returns. Two non-overlapping blocks: block 1 = 0.4 − 0.2 + 0.6 + 0.1 − 0.5 = 0.4; block 2 = 0.3 − 0.1 + 0.5 − 0.4 + 0.2 = 0.5. (A real test needs dozens of blocks — this is illustrating the arithmetic, not a real test.)
Step 3 — random-walk-implied 5-day variance. 5 × 0.1289 = 0.6445 %².
Step 4 — suppose the actual 5-day sample variance, from a longer dataset, is 0.45. VR(5) = 0.45 ÷ 0.6445 ≈ 0.70.
0.70 is meaningfully below 1: 5-day variance is smaller than 5× the 1-day variance — the series damps itself over a week more than independence predicts. That's mean reversion. If the actual 5-day variance had instead come out to 0.95, VR(5) = 0.95 ÷ 0.6445 ≈ 1.47 — the opposite verdict, trending. Whether either hypothetical is statistically different from 1 rather than sampling noise is exactly what Lo & MacKinlay's standard error answers — ten raw observations is nowhere near enough data to make that call for real.

Section 8

Where this lives in this repo

This isn't just theory here. js/rangeBiasCore.js has a featureHurst function that runs live R/S analysis — lags of 2, 4, 8, and 16 bars on the last ~80 daily closes, the identical log-log regression from Section 3 — as one of the entry-bias features scored alongside ADX, swing structure, TWAP slope, and EMA/RSI. Its call is the same regime-gate logic from Section 2: H < 0.45 confirms a fade, H > 0.55 confirms a follow, and anything in between abstains.

A postscript worth knowing

The math checks out — but that feature was later dropped from the live conviction score, and the repo's own code comments say exactly why. Benchmarked out-of-sample across 10 instruments, its incumbent R/S read had no usable predictive relationship (well under the project's own edge bar) — and worse, it read H ≈ 0.88 (trending) on every single instrument in the trending bucket, with zero exceptions. That's not noise; it's an unconditional vote against whatever direction was being tested, silently dragging down the conviction score on setups that were otherwise well-confirmed. The function is kept — it still runs, and still backs a research benchmark — but it no longer votes on a live entry.

The lesson underneath the lesson: a diagnostic can be mathematically sound and still fail to earn its place in a live decision. Persistence measured correctly is not automatically an edge — that has to be shown with real out-of-sample evidence, not assumed from the formula alone.

Section 9

How this actually gets used on a desk

🚦

A regime gate, not a signal

Compute H or VR(k) on a rolling window and use the reading to decide which strategy family is even allowed to trade — mean-reversion switched on when anti-persistent, trend/breakout switched on when persistent, standing aside near H≈0.5 / VR≈1 where neither has a demonstrated edge.

🧩

The same selector pattern, different math

This is precisely the "score → choice" pattern this repo's own dayTypeScore → selectStrategy logic implements elsewhere — a different lens on the identical fade-or-follow decision.

⏳

A decaying prior, not a fact

Both statistics are estimated from a short, noisy, non-stationary sample — they describe the recent past, with no guarantee it holds going forward. A window reading H=0.35 last month can read H=0.55 this month with no structural change at all.

⚠

Neither predicts direction

A high H says moves have recently reinforced each other, not "the market is going up." It licenses a trend-following approach to whatever direction is already in motion — it does not forecast one.

The pitfall that matters most here

R/S is systematically biased toward finding H > 0.5 on true random-walk data when the sample is short — and a feature computed on lags of 2–16 bars over 20–80 daily bars sits squarely in that small-sample regime. A single H = 0.55 reading from a short window is much weaker evidence than the same number from thousands of observations.

Section 10 · It actually happened

Trend following's fat years and lean years

It actually happened

Mandelbrot called Hurst's persistence the Joseph effect: seven fat years, then seven lean ones. Trend following bets on it in markets. In 2008 the S&P 500 lost about 37% with dividends while broad managed-futures indices finished up by double digits. Much of the 2010s was lean for the same rules. In 2022, with US CPI peaking at 9.1% and the Fed hiking in 75bp steps, bond yields and the dollar moved one way for months, and trend followers had a very strong year. Same rules throughout; what changed was whether monthly moves persisted.

Section 11

What this desk found when it tested the claim

The tempting claim: when the path looks persistent, follow it.

Desk test: nothing found trend-following-fx: FX-only basket null, verified not-a-bug with an independent rebuild. Broad multi-asset: modest, ~0.3 out of sample, barely beating buy-and-hold.
Desk test: held up tape-speed-persistence: a slow 15 minutes is followed by a slow hour, a fast one by a fast hour, monotonic in 68 of 68 cells across 17 pairs. Pace persists; direction is not part of the claim.
Where the textbook breaks: H and VR treat persistence as one fixed property of a series. In markets it is episodic: it switches on when one macro force pushes the same way for months, and off for years. A full-sample H near 0.5 can hide both, and the persistence of move size is far more reliable than that of direction.
Section 12

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

What Hurst exponent corresponds to a pure random walk?

A random walk's range grows exactly as √n, matching the R/S power law (R/S)ₙ ≈ c·n^H at H = 0.5 — no memory, no edge in the path shape.
Question 2

A 5-day return series gives VR(5) ≈ 1.47. What does that indicate?

VR(k) > 1 means k-day variance exceeds the random-walk prediction of k×Var(1) — returns are positively autocorrelated, the same story as H > 0.5.
Question 3

Why does variance scale exactly as k·σ² under the random-walk null?

If k one-period returns are independent, the covariance cross-terms in Var(sum) vanish, leaving Var(r⁽ᵏ⁾) = Σ Var(r⁽¹⁾) = kσ². Any measured deviation of VR(k) from 1 is direct evidence against that independence assumption.
Question 4

Why is R/S analysis known to be biased on short windows?

The small-sample expected value of R/S (Anis & Lloyd 1976) and later simulation studies show classical R/S systematically overestimates H — finds spurious persistence — even on true random-walk data, when the window is short. A live feature on 2–16 bar lags sits right in that regime.
Your call
Scenario drill · 13 July 2022

Inflation at 9.1%, and your Hurst reading just dipped below a half

US CPI for June has just printed at 9.1% year on year. The Fed raised rates by 75bp a month ago. Bond yields have risen for most of the year and the dollar is strong. You run trend rules that trade on horizons of weeks to months, and they are short bonds and long the dollar. Suppose a Hurst reading on the last 30 daily bars has just slipped from 0.58 to 0.47 (a hypothetical reading for this drill). What do you do?

  1. Close the trend book. H is below 0.5, so the market has turned mean-reverting.
    One reading from 30 bars is squarely in the small-sample zone, where R/S is noisy and biased, and can flip across 0.5 on estimation noise alone. It is weak evidence of a regime change, and the rules trade a longer horizon than the window measures.
  2. Keep running the rules at their normal size, and treat the short reading as noise until longer-horizon evidence says otherwise.
    The gate question is about persistence at the horizon the rules trade. A short-window wobble around 0.5 does not answer it, and neither statistic forecasts direction anyway. Sticking to the process avoids trading on noise.
  3. Double the size. Inflation at 9.1% means the trend has to continue.
    Neither H nor the variance ratio says which way the next move goes, and a strong macro story is not a measurement. Doubling up on conviction is how a trend book gets caught by the reversal it cannot see coming.
  4. Trim a little, and wait for a variance ratio on a longer window, with its standard error, before deciding more.
    Asking for a proper test with a standard error is the right instinct. But trimming on one noisy reading still lets noise move your size, which costs you when the persistence is real.

What happened. The Fed raised rates by 75bp three more times, on 27 July, 21 September and 2 November. Yields kept rising for most of the year, and by late September the dollar had reached its strongest level in about two decades. Trend followers, short bonds and long the dollar, had a very strong year. The 30-bar Hurst reading in the setup was hypothetical; the real lesson is that one short-window reading crossing 0.5 is a noisy hint, not a regime change.

Section 13

What to carry forward

01

The Hurst exponent H is the slope of log(R/S)ₙ regressed on log n — H < 0.5 mean-reverting, H = 0.5 random walk, H > 0.5 trending.

02

The variance ratio test asks the identical question more rigorously: VR(k) = Var(r⁽ᵏ⁾) ÷ (k×Var(r⁽¹⁾)) equals exactly 1 under a random walk, with an actual standard error attached to any deviation.

03

Neither statistic predicts direction — both only license which strategy family, fade or follow, has a right to work in the current regime.

04

R/S is biased toward finding H > 0.5 in small samples — exactly the regime a feature computed on a few dozen daily bars sits in.

05

A single H or VR reading is a noisy, decaying prior about the current regime, not a fact about the market's permanent nature — and even a mathematically sound feature still has to earn its place with real out-of-sample evidence, as this repo's own history with featureHurst shows.

06

Persistence is episodic: trend followers thrived in 2008 and 2022 and struggled through much of the 2010s with the same rules. On this desk, FX-only trend following was null; what validated was persistence of tape speed (68 of 68 cells), not direction.

What this now lets you do

Read a rolling H or VR(k) reading and know immediately which strategy family — fade or follow — the current window is licensing, and which regime is too close to 0.5 / 1 to license either. Build a variance-ratio calculation by hand from raw returns. Recognize a regime-gate pattern (score → strategy choice) the next time it shows up under a different name.
