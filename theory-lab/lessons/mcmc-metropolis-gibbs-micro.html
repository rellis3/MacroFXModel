MCMC: Sampling a Posterior You Can't Write Down

The previous lesson's closed-form updating — Beta-Binomial, Normal-Normal — only works for a short list of matched prior/likelihood pairs. Almost every realistic Bayesian model has no such shortcut: the posterior's normalizing constant is an integral with no closed-form answer. Markov Chain Monte Carlo sidesteps the integral entirely, by building a random walk engineered so that, after enough steps, how often it visits each region matches how plausible that region actually is — without ever computing the constant that would have made the posterior a "proper" formula.

Section 1

Conjugate updating was the easy case, not the normal one

The moment a model has more than one interacting parameter, the algebra runs out.

Conjugate case — previous lesson
  • Beta prior + Binomial likelihood → Beta posterior, read off directly
  • Normal prior + Normal likelihood → Normal posterior, same story
  • The posterior is a known distribution family — no simulation needed
  • Only exists for a short, specific list of matched prior/likelihood pairs
Everything else — this lesson
  • Prior × likelihood is still valid math — the shape is real
  • But the normalizing integral has no closed-form answer
  • In high dimensions, you often can't even integrate it numerically
  • You can still cheaply evaluate the unnormalized density at any point
That last line is the whole trick MCMC is built on: a ratio of two unnormalized-density evaluations, f(θ′)/f(θ), is exactly as informative as the ratio of true posterior densities p(θ′)/p(θ) — because whatever constant normalizes p cancels out of any ratio of it against itself. You never need the constant. You only ever need to compare two points.
Section 2

A hiker who never refuses to go downhill

Picture the posterior as a landscape — hills where a parameter value is plausible, valleys where it isn't.

Current point θ
propose a random nearby step →
Candidate θ′
θ′ more plausible?
always move →
Accept
θ′ less plausible?
move anyway, sometimes →
Accept with probability α

Never refusing every downhill move is the entire idea. Refuse them all, and the walk marches straight to the nearest hilltop and sits there forever — never exploring the rest of the landscape, never discovering a second hill somewhere else. Take enough of these compare-two-points, sometimes-go-down-anyway steps, and a histogram of everywhere the walk has stood becomes an accurate picture of the whole landscape — even though it never once computed the landscape's exact height, only ever compared two heights to each other.

What this describes

This is the mechanics of the method, not a claim that this repo has built or validated a live MCMC-fitted model anywhere. It's explained here because the next lesson (hierarchical models) and things like Hidden Markov Models lean on exactly this trick.

Section 3

The acceptance rule, term by term

α(θ,θ′) = min( 1,  [ p(θ′)·q(θ∣θ′) ] / [ p(θ)·q(θ′∣θ) ] )
1

Propose — θ′ ~ q(θ′∣θ)

Draw a candidate next point from the proposal distribution — commonly a Normal centered on the current point, a "random walk" proposal.

2

Compute α = min(1, ratio)

p(θ′)/p(θ) says how much more (or less) plausible the candidate is. The q-ratio corrects for a proposal that isn't equally easy to run in reverse.

3

Draw u ~ Uniform(0,1)

Accept if u < α, otherwise reject. A rejected step is not discarded — the chain repeats its current value as the next sample.

4

The chain — θ₁, θ₂, …

After enough steps, its long-run visit frequencies approximate p(θ) — the target posterior — to arbitrary accuracy.

Where this simplifies

When the proposal is symmetric — a Normal random walk centered at the current point, so q(θ′∣θ) = q(θ∣θ′) — the q-terms cancel and the rule collapses to the original 1953 Metropolis algorithm: α = min(1, p(θ′)/p(θ)). Hastings' 1970 generalization keeps the q-correction, allowing asymmetric proposals. Every example in this deck uses the simpler, symmetric form.

Section 4

Detailed balance: the ledger that has to zero out

Why does this particular accept/reject rule guarantee the chain settles on the posterior, and not something else?

p(θ)·T(θ→θ′)
=
p(θ′)·T(θ′→θ)

A Markov chain has p(θ) as its long-run stationary distribution if probability flows between any two states at exactly matched rates in both directions — that equation, called detailed balance, where T is the chain's transition probability (proposal × acceptance). The Metropolis-Hastings α formula is deliberately built so this holds automatically: plug it into both sides and the min() terms and proposal densities cancel out to leave an identity.

Think of two neighbouring towns connected by one road, with people crossing every day. If the traffic in each direction is exactly balanced, both towns' populations stay steady over time — even though individual people keep moving. Detailed balance is that same two-way balance, enforced between every pair of points in parameter space, and it's why the chain's visit frequencies settle into a stable pattern matching the target distribution.
The fine print

Detailed balance is a sufficient condition, not the only possible one — but it's the one MCMC is built around. It also isn't enough alone: the chain additionally needs to be able to eventually reach any region of positive probability (irreducibility). Together, the two guarantee convergence to p(θ) regardless of the chain's starting point.

Section 9

Gibbs sampling: the special case that never rejects

When you can sample a variable's full conditional distribution exactly — often because it's conjugate, one parameter at a time.

Metropolis-Hastings — general case
  • Proposes an arbitrary candidate θ′ from some proposal q
  • Needs an accept/reject coin flip on every single step
  • Acceptance probability is usually < 1 — the chain often repeats
  • Works even when the true conditional can't be sampled directly — only the unnormalized density needs to be evaluable
Gibbs sampling — special case
  • Proposes θⱼ directly from its own true full conditional p(θⱼ ∣ θ₋ⱼ)
  • Cycles through every parameter, one at a time, given the rest
  • Acceptance probability collapses algebraically to exactly 1 — no coin flip, ever
  • Requires every conditional to be sampleable in closed form — a real constraint, not a free lunch

Substitute q(θⱼ′∣θ) = p(θⱼ′∣θ₋ⱼ) — proposing exactly from the true conditional — into the general Metropolis-Hastings ratio, and the forward and reverse move probabilities cancel the target density's own conditional structure exactly, leaving the ratio identically 1. This is why Gibbs sampling is usually taught separately even though it's mathematically just Metropolis-Hastings with one particular proposal choice — there is no tuning knob left, only the choice of which conditional to derive and sample next.

Regular Metropolis-Hastings guesses where to step next and checks whether the guess was any good. Gibbs sampling skips the guessing — for each parameter in turn it draws directly from the exact right answer for "given everything else fixed, what should this one be." Nothing to double-check, so nothing ever gets rejected.
Section 10

A chain that ran is not automatically a chain that converged

Four standard diagnostics separate a genuine posterior sample from a chain that only looks like one.

📉

Trace plots

Sampled value against iteration number. A healthy chain looks like noisy static around a stable band; drifting, long flat stretches, or an obvious trend mean poor mixing or non-convergence.

✂️

Burn-in

The first chunk of iterations, taken before the chain has settled into the target's typical region, is discarded — it reflects the arbitrary starting point more than the posterior.

🔗

Effective sample size (ESS)

MCMC draws are autocorrelated — each resembles the one before it. ESS estimates how many truly independent draws the chain's correlated samples are actually "worth."

📐

Gelman-Rubin R̂

Run several chains from deliberately different starting points. If they all converged to the same target, between-chain variance should be no larger than within-chain variance.

W — within-chain variance
vs.
B — between-chain variance
→
R̂ = √(Var⁺ / W)

If every chain has converged to the same target, B is small relative to W, and R̂ → 1 (commonly, trusted below about 1.01). If chains are stuck exploring different regions — say one trapped near θ=+1 and another near θ=−1, neither having crossed — B is large, and R̂ sits noticeably above 1: a direct, quantitative flag that the chains disagree about where the posterior actually is.

Section 11

Where MCMC quietly goes wrong

🏃

Mistaking "it ran" for "it converged"

A badly-tuned step size, a multimodal target, or too few iterations happily produces ten thousand numbers that look like a posterior sample and are actually biased toward whichever region the chain wandered into first.

🎚️

Step size wrong in either direction

Too small: the chain crawls, high autocorrelation, low ESS. Too large: almost every proposal rejects, also high autocorrelation, for the opposite reason. Both look like "it's running" without producing a useful sample.

1️⃣

Running only one chain

A single chain can look perfectly well-behaved in its own trace plot while having converged to the wrong mode entirely in a multimodal posterior — nothing inside that one chain's history reveals the mistake.

✅

Treating R̂ ≈ 1 as proof, not evidence

R̂ close to 1 means the chains agree with each other — it doesn't prove they found the true posterior if every chain shares the same blind spot. Diagnostics reduce risk; they don't eliminate it.

Section 12

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Why doesn't Metropolis-Hastings ever need the posterior's normalizing constant?

Because p only ever appears as a ratio, the algorithm only needs the unnormalized f(θ′)/f(θ) — never the true p(θ) itself.
Question 2

In the worked example, why was θ=0 → θ′=0.8 accepted with no coin flip needed?

α = 1 means the move is always taken — there's nothing for a uniform draw u ≤ 1 to compare against.
Question 3

Why is Gibbs sampling's acceptance probability always exactly 1?

Substituting q = the true full conditional into the general ratio cancels every term, leaving 1 identically — no accept/reject step is needed.
Question 4

A Gelman-Rubin R̂ of 1.30 across several chains tells you what?

R̂ well above 1 means independently-started chains haven't agreed on the same target — e.g. each stuck near a different mode. The fix is longer runs, a better proposal, or reconsidering the model — not trusting the current samples.
Question 5

Large steps sound like they'd explore more. Why can a too-large step size still produce poor mixing?

The chain is technically "capable" of big jumps but accepts so few of them that it barely moves — high autocorrelation and low ESS, the same symptom as a step size that's too small, for the opposite reason.
Section 13

What to carry forward

01

Most real posteriors aren't conjugate — MCMC replaces the intractable normalizing integral with a random walk whose visit frequencies approximate the posterior.

02

Metropolis-Hastings: propose a random jump, always accept uphill moves, accept downhill moves with probability α = min(1, ratio) — never refusing every downhill move is what prevents getting stuck on the first hill found.

03

Detailed balance is why it works — the accept/reject rule makes probability flow between any two states balance exactly, making the target posterior the chain's stationary distribution.

04

Gibbs sampling is Metropolis-Hastings with the proposal set equal to the true full conditional — the ratio collapses to exactly 1, so every draw is accepted, with no tuning, but every conditional has to be sampleable.

05

Step size is a real tuning problem: too small crawls (high autocorrelation), too large gets rejected almost every time (also high autocorrelation) — both look like "it's running" without producing a useful sample.

06

A chain that ran isn't automatically a chain that converged — trace plots, burn-in, effective sample size, and multi-chain R̂ are the standard, non-optional checks before trusting any MCMC output.

What this now lets you do

Recognise when a posterior needs simulation instead of algebra, read a Metropolis-Hastings acceptance rule term by term, and tell the difference between "Metropolis-Hastings" and "Gibbs sampling" by asking one question: can every conditional be sampled directly? Just as importantly, know which diagnostics — trace plots, ESS, R̂ across multiple chains — separate a genuine posterior sample from a chain that only looks like one.
