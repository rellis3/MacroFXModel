Roll's Implicit Spread Estimator, Measuring a Spread You Never See

No quotes. No order book. Just a plain series of trade prices. Roll's 1984 result is that the bid-ask spread still leaves a fingerprint in that series — it forces consecutive price changes to bounce against each other — and that bounce alone is enough to recover the spread's size.

Section 1

All you're handed is a list of trade prices

No bid. No ask. No depth. Just price, timestamp, price, timestamp.

📄

Thinner than it sounds

For decades this was the entire state of a lot of market history — no consolidated quote feed, just prints. Plenty of data today is still this thin.

❓

Roll's exact question

Given nothing but a series of transaction prices, can the effective bid-ask spread still be recovered?

✅

The answer is yes

Not by seeing the spread directly — by noticing a specific statistical fingerprint it leaves in how consecutive prices move.

Section 2

The true price never bounces. What you actually see does.

Same underlying security, two ways of looking at it.

Try it
The unobservable "true" price \(m_t\) — a random walk with no serial correlation in its own changes. It can drift, but it never mechanically reverses itself.
Every printed trade price \(P_t\) — bouncing between a bid and an ask that straddle the true price above. The zig-zag is real in the data even though the dashed true price beneath it never actually reverses.

Nobody trading against this security sees the dashed line — only the solid, bouncing one. Roll's whole method is about reading that bounce backwards to recover how far apart the bid and ask must have been.

Section 3

Roll's model, in one line

Every observed trade price is the true price, plus or minus a half-spread.

The model
P_t
=
m_t
+
(s/2) · q_t
q_t flips between +1 (this trade hit the ask) and −1 (this trade hit the bid), coin-flip-like and independent from one trade to the next. The bracketed term is the entire bounce — everything spurious in the printed price.

Section 4

Why negative, and why divided by 4

The derivation lands on Cov(ΔP_t, ΔP_t−1) = −s²/4. Both pieces of that result have a plain-English reason.

Why negative

A bounce that pushes the price one way is mechanically prone to being undone by the very next trade — hit the ask, and the next trade is just as likely to hit the bid, pulling the price back. "What bounced one way tends to bounce back" is negative serial covariance, by definition — no real price reversal required.

Why divided by 4

Each trade's bounce is a half-spread, s/2, not a full spread. Covariance is built from a product of two such displacements, so the half-spread gets squared: (s/2)² = s²/4. The ¼ isn't arbitrary — it falls straight out of measuring the bounce in half-spread units.

One more structural fact worth carrying: because q_t has no memory beyond one trade, the model predicts Cov(ΔP_t, ΔP_t−k) = 0 for every k ≥ 2. The bounce is strictly a one-period phenomenon — only the very next price change is mechanically related to this one.

Section 6

Turn the result around, solve for s

One algebraic step: multiply by −4, take a square root.

The estimator
Cov(ΔP_t, ΔP_t−1)
=
−s²/4
↓ rearrange for s ↓
s
=
2 · √( −Cov(ΔP_t, ΔP_t−1) )
Feed in the empirical serial covariance of price changes, computed from nothing but the raw price series, and this formula hands back an estimated effective spread.

Section 8

The catch that matters more than the formula itself

The square root only exists if the covariance actually comes out negative.

Read this twice

Roll's −s²/4 is a statement about what the covariance is on average, under an idealized model — not a guarantee about any one real, finite, noisy sample. In short windows, strongly trending markets, or whenever the "true price is a memoryless random walk" assumption is even mildly violated, the empirical covariance routinely comes out positive instead. When that happens there is no real square root to take — the estimator is undefined, not just noisy. This is a routine, expected outcome of using the method on real short or trending samples, not a rare edge case.

A rolling application of Roll's estimator to real data produces a series with genuine holes in it — windows with no estimate at all. Any honest use of this method needs a plan for what "no answer" means, because a meaningful share of windows will return exactly that.

Section 10

Roll isn't the last word — just the first one

A newer estimator sidesteps Roll's exact failure mode entirely.

Roll (1984)
  • Uses the serial covariance of consecutive transaction prices
  • Undefined whenever that covariance is non-negative
  • Fails routinely in short or trending windows
Corwin & Schultz (2012)
  • Uses each day's high and low price instead
  • Compares one-day range against overlapping two-day range to isolate the spread
  • Never needs a negative covariance to exist — far more robust in practice
Not derived in full here — this deck's depth budget goes to Roll, as the historically foundational, conceptually clearest result. Corwin-Schultz is worth recognizing by name as the modern, more robust successor built for exactly the situations where Roll goes quiet.
Section 11

One phrase, two very different jobs

Don't conflate these

"Bid-ask bounce" shows up elsewhere in this curriculum too — the Hurst/Variance-Ratio and Bipower Variation lessons both mention it. There, it's contamination to filter out of a realized-variance or mean-reversion estimate. Here, that exact same mechanism is the signal being measured — Roll's method exists specifically to extract the bounce's size, not remove its influence from something else. Same phenomenon, opposite role.

Verified against this repo

A repo-wide search finds no computation anywhere of a serial covariance of price changes used specifically for spread estimation, and this curriculum's own Market Microstructure lesson (Glosten-Milgrom, Kyle's Lambda) never mentions Roll's estimator at all — this lesson is genuinely new ground, not a restatement.

Section 12

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

What data does Roll's estimator need?

That's the entire point of the method — recovering the spread from data that never recorded the spread directly.
Question 2

Why does the bid-ask bounce make Cov(ΔP_t, ΔP_t−1) negative?

A trade at the ask followed by one at the bid looks like a drop, and the next bounce back to the ask looks like a rebound — that systematic reversal is negative serial covariance, with no real price movement required.
Question 3

Why −s²/4 and not −s²?

Each trade displaces the price by s/2, not s. Covariance is a product of two such displacements, so (s/2)² = s²/4.
Question 4

A real sample's Cov(ΔP_t, ΔP_t−1) comes out positive. What does Roll's estimator say?

This is a routine outcome in short or trending samples, not a rare failure — report "no estimate," don't force a number out of the formula.
Question 5

What does Corwin-Schultz (2012) use instead of the serial covariance of transaction prices?

Comparing one-day versus overlapping two-day high-low ranges isolates the spread's contribution algebraically — and never needs a negative covariance to exist, which is why it's more robust than Roll's estimator in practice.
Section 13

What to carry forward

01

Roll's estimator recovers an effective spread from transaction prices alone — no quotes needed — by exploiting the bid-ask bounce.

02

Cov(ΔP_t, ΔP_t−1) = −s²/4: negative because a bounce mechanically undoes itself, and divided by 4 because each bounce is a half-spread, squared.

03

s = 2√(−Cov(ΔP_t, ΔP_t−1)) — but only when that covariance is actually negative, as the theory predicts.

04

A non-negative empirical covariance makes the estimator undefined, full stop — a routine, expected outcome in short or trending samples, not a rare failure.

05

Corwin & Schultz (2012) is the more robust modern successor, built from daily high-low ranges instead — worth knowing by name, even without deriving it.
