Lesson 3 · Foundations & Epistemics

Run Enough Tests, and Noise Starts Looking Like Skill

Slice your FX data by pair, by regime, by session, by holding period, by day-of-week, and you're not running one test anymore — you're running dozens. Each one still has only a 5% chance of a false alarm. But the chance that at least one of them fires by pure luck compounds fast, and by the time you've checked seventy slices, finding a "significant" one is no longer a discovery. It's close to guaranteed, whether or not anything real is there.

Section 1

One test's promise is narrower than it sounds

A single, well-designed hypothesis test at α = 0.05 is a reasonable, disciplined thing to run. The question is what that 5% actually covers.

H₀

The null hypothesis

"Nothing interesting is happening" — e.g. this slice's mean return is zero, or this strategy has no edge. Every test starts by assuming this is true.

p

The p-value

If H₀ were really true, the probability of seeing a result at least this extreme, from sampling noise alone. Small p, unlikely under "nothing's there."

α

The significance threshold

Commonly 0.05. If p < α you call the result significant. α is defined as the false-positive rate of one test — exactly 5% of the time, a true H₀ still gets rejected.

1

Per test, not per project

That 5% guarantee is scoped to a single test. It says nothing yet about what happens once you run many of them side by side.

Why this matters here

The natural way to explore an FX/macro idea is exactly the shape that breaks a naive reading of α: slice by pair, slice by regime, slice by session, slice by holding period. Each slice is a separate look at the data — a separate roll of the dice — and nothing about α = 0.05 changes just because you're now taking dozens of those rolls.

Section 2

What "at least one false alarm" does as tests pile up

Step through the derivation — three moves, no calculus required.

Try it
P(no false positive)
=
1 − α
At α = 0.05, a single test correctly stays below the significance bar 95% of the time, when H₀ is genuinely true.
P(no FP in any of m tests)
=
(1 − α)^m
If the m tests are independent, "all of them avoid a false positive" is the product of each one avoiding it — multiply 0.95 by itself m times.
FWER
=
1 − (1 − α)^m
The family-wise error rate is just the complement: at least one false positive is "not zero false positives." α per test never changed — you just gave chance more chances.
This is the mathematical core of the "look-elsewhere effect" — the same correction particle physicists cite when they check a histogram for a bump across many bins before calling it a discovery.

Section 4

Seventy slices isn't a hypothetical — it's how this repo's backtests are built

7 pairs × 5 regimes × 2 holding periods is 70 tests before anyone runs a single day-of-week or session cut.

EURUSD, GBPUSD, AUDUSD, USDJPY — each currency pair checked separately is one more roll of the dice.

🌡

Splitting by volatility regime or risk-on/risk-off multiplies every pair's test count by however many regimes you defined.

⏱

Slice by London vs. NY session, or by day-of-week, and the count of "looks at the data" keeps climbing — with no new data collected.

A 97.2% chance at least one of the 70 slices "clears" the 5% bar from noise alone. The expected count is 70 × 0.05 = 3.5 false positives. So "3 out of 70 slices look significant" isn't a finding — it's almost exactly what you'd see with zero edge anywhere in the data.

Section 5

The blunt fix: shrink the bar per test

Bonferroni holds the family-wise rate at α by dividing the threshold by how many tests you ran.

α_Bonf
=
α / m

At m = 70, α_Bonf = 0.05 / 70 ≈ 0.000714 — a raw p-value would need to land below roughly 0.07% (about 70× stricter than the uncorrected 5%) to count. This is derived from the union bound (Boole's inequality): FWER ≤ m · α_Bonf = α, a guarantee that holds no matter how correlated the tests are — which is Bonferroni's real virtue, and also the seed of its main flaw.

Where this needs qualifying

Bonferroni's guarantee makes no use of how the tests depend on each other — it divides by the full m regardless. Seventy pair/regime slices are not that: EURUSD, GBPUSD and AUDUSD all move partly off a shared USD factor, so a single macro surprise can light up several "independent-looking" slices together. That shared variation means 70 correlated slices carry less independent information than m implies — dividing by the full m ends up needlessly punishing real, shared effects, not just noise.

Section 6

A more forgiving bar: control the rate, not the ban

The False Discovery Rate (Benjamini-Hochberg) accepts a known, bounded fraction of false "winners" instead of trying to ban them outright.

p₍k₎
≤
(k / m) · q

Sort all m p-values smallest to largest. Rank k's p-value only has to beat (k/m)·q — a staircase that gets more lenient the higher the rank. Find the largest k where this holds, and everything ranked 1..k counts as a discovery.

Bonferroni
  • Flat bar for every rank: α/m
  • Controls P(any false positive at all)
  • Safe regardless of correlation — but blunt
  • At m=70: every slice needs p < 0.000714
Benjamini-Hochberg (FDR)
  • Staircase bar: rank k needs p < (k/70)·q
  • Controls the expected proportion of false discoveries
  • More forgiving for exploratory slicing; valid for independent or positively correlated tests (arbitrary dependence: Benjamini–Yekutieli)
  • At q=0.10, m=70: rank 1 needs ≈0.00143, rank 10 needs ≈0.0143
Flagging 10 slices at a 10% target FDR means: on average, roughly 1 of those 10 is expected to be a false positive — not that any specific one is safe, and not that zero false positives exist anywhere in the wider search.

Section 8

This is why the repo has a house rule about it

"Finding a few 'winners' among 70 slices is what noise does." That line, already written into this repo's own project memory, isn't a cautious-sounding guess — it's a direct restatement of the FWER math: at m = 70, roughly 3.5 false positives are expected from pure noise. A survivor has to clear that bar and hold up out-of-sample before it counts as anything.
📊

Beat the chance-baseline

A slice's p-value has to be judged against how many slices were checked, not read alone as if it were the only test run.

🔁

Be IS-consistent

A real edge doesn't depend on one lucky sub-period inside the in-sample window carrying the whole result.

🧩

Lego Principle 5

This is the mechanical basis for the repo's validation discipline — statistical survival plus IS consistency plus genuine OOS confirmation, not any one of those alone.

The correction doesn't manufacture evidence a real effect would produce on fresh data — it manages the false-positive rate of the search you already ran. That's necessary before trusting a slice, not sufficient on its own.

Section 9

How practitioners actually handle this

📋

Log every test you run

Including the ones that failed — a record of "here are the 70 slices I checked" is what lets you compute m honestly later.

📝

Pre-register the discovery rule

Decide what counts as a discovery before looking at results, so "significant" can't quietly get redefined after seeing the winner.

🥇

Distrust the single best slice

Rank #1 of 70 tells you almost nothing alone — the best of many noise draws is expected to look good by construction.

🔬

Prefer real out-of-sample confirmation

Corrections manage false-positive risk in a search you already ran — they don't substitute for fresh-data evidence.

The trap

Being #1 out of 70 slices is not, by itself, evidence of anything — the best of many pure-noise draws is expected to look good. In quant finance this has its own name: backtest overfitting, formalized by López de Prado's Probability of Backtest Overfitting and Deflated Sharpe Ratio — the same Bonferroni/BH logic, aimed at Sharpe ratios instead of p-values.

Section 10 · It actually happened

The factor zoo: hundreds of discoveries, one shared dataset

It actually happened

For decades researchers tested idea after idea on the same US stock returns at the usual bar of t ≈ 2, and journals published the ones that cleared it. John Cochrane called the result a "zoo" of factors. Harvey, Liu & Zhu (2016) counted 316 of them and argued that with that many hidden looks at one dataset, a new factor should clear t ≈ 3, and that many published findings are likely false.

Nobody had to cheat. Each test could be run correctly. The error was in m, which no single paper could report because no single paper knew it.

Section 11

This desk's own version: 54.1% against 54.1%

The claim: confirm a turn in short-term rates, then pre-position in the Nasdaq. Pre-registered with the expectation written as null; 360 days of 15-minute bars.

Desk test: nothing found rates-pivot-lead: 2,430 confirmed pivots; at 1h, −0.005% vs control [−0.021, +0.012]. Null on every cell, and well powered (±0.12 ATR). Up-move after a confirmed low: 54.1%. Base rate: 54.1%.
Where the textbook breaks

Bonferroni and BH both need m. In published research m is unknowable: the failed tests belong to other people and ran on the same shared history, so they are neither counted nor independent. A correction is only as honest as the count fed into it.

15 cells scored. If all were noise you'd expect 15 × 0.05 = 0.75 false passes, with slightly better than even odds of at least one. None passed, and the pre-registered gate (both directions, both halves) meant a lone lucky cell could never have been reported as a finding.
Section 12

Quick check

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

You run 70 independent tests at α = 0.05 each. What does α = 0.05 still guarantee?

α is a per-test guarantee. It never changes — what changes is the family-wise error rate across all 70 tests, which is 1−(1−α)^70 ≈ 97.2%, not 5%.
Question 2

At m = 70 and α = 0.05, roughly how many false positives are expected from pure noise?

Expected false positives = m × α = 70 × 0.05 = 3.5. Finding "3 out of 70" significant is close to exactly what noise alone produces.
Question 3

Why can Bonferroni be too strict for 70 FX pair/regime slices?

Bonferroni's α/m threshold is derived for the worst case and takes no credit for overlap between tests. Correlated slices (EURUSD, GBPUSD, AUDUSD sharing USD exposure) mean the true amount of independent information is less than m, so the flat α/m bar over-punishes real, shared effects.
Question 4

Your best slice out of 70 has a strikingly low p-value. What does that alone tell you?

The minimum p-value among 70 pure-noise draws is expected to look small by construction — that's order statistics, not evidence. It needs to be judged against m, not read in isolation.
Question 5

A slice survives Benjamini-Hochberg at q = 0.10. What is still missing before calling it a tradeable edge?

Surviving a multiple-testing correction only means the result is unlikely to be pure search noise. It says nothing about whether the effect is IS-consistent, survives realistic costs, or holds up on fresh data — Lego Principle 5's own bar.
Section 13

What to carry forward

01

A single test's α = 0.05 is a per-test guarantee, not a per-project one — it never changes, no matter how many tests you run alongside it.

02

The family-wise error rate FWER = 1−(1−α)^m climbs toward 1 fast — at m = 70 it's already ≈97.2%, purely from noise.

03

Bonferroni (α/m) is the safe, correlation-agnostic fix — and often too strict for correlated FX slices sharing a USD factor.

04

Benjamini-Hochberg (FDR) controls the expected proportion of false discoveries with a rank-dependent staircase, p₍k₎ ≤ (k/m)q — usually the more practical tool for exploratory FX slicing, valid when tests are independent or positively correlated (arbitrary dependence needs Benjamini–Yekutieli).

05

The best of many is expected to look good, by construction — rank #1 of 70 alone is close to uninformative.

06

Surviving a correction is necessary, not sufficient — IS consistency and genuine OOS confirmation still decide whether it's tradeable.

07

It actually happened: the factor zoo (316 published factors, hurdle raised to t ≈ 3), and this desk's rates-pivot null, where a 54.1% hit rate matched a 54.1% base rate. Never quote a hit rate without its base rate.

What this now lets you do

Read "3 out of 70 slices are significant" correctly — as a number that needs m attached to mean anything, not a headline. Choose Bonferroni or FDR deliberately, knowing which one fits correlated FX slices better. Recognize the "best of many" trap the moment a backtest report shows a single standout slice with no mention of how many were checked.
