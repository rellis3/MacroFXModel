The Sharpe Ratio Doesn't Know How Many Times You Tried

Sharpe, Sortino and Calmar are three honest ways to divide return by risk — built from the same mean-and-standard-deviation arithmetic every earlier lesson in this series already covers. But all three share one blind spot: a reported ratio looks identical whether it's the only strategy you ever tested, or the single lucky survivor of fifty variations you quietly discarded. The Deflated Sharpe Ratio is the formal fix for that blind spot, and it's the direct Sharpe-ratio cousin of the Multiple Testing lesson already in this curriculum.

Section 1

Reward divided by risk — but which risk?

Sharpe, Sortino and Calmar all answer "how much return per unit of risk." They disagree on what counts as risk.

σ

Sharpe — total wobble

Divides by the full standard deviation of excess returns. Every up-swing and down-swing widens the denominator by exactly the same amount.

↓

Sortino — downside wobble only

Divides by downside deviation — a standard deviation computed only over the periods that lost money. Good surprises stop counting against the strategy.

▽

Calmar — the single worst stretch

Not a dispersion statistic at all: annualized return divided by the single worst peak-to-trough drawdown the equity curve actually lived through.

None of the three is "the" right metric. The honest move is naming which question is being asked before quoting a number — the same "name the benchmark before claiming improvement" discipline this repo already asks for everywhere else.

Section 3

Where Sharpe quietly breaks

≈

Assumes roughly independent returns

The √k derivation needs periods that don't strongly predict each other. It says nothing about what happens when they do.

=

Charges wins and losses equally

A squared deviation from the mean doesn't know which side of the mean it came from — a big up-swing widens σₓ exactly as much as a big down-swing.

⤴

Autocorrelation inflates the annualized number

Trend-following strategies and overlapping-window backtests have genuine serial correlation. Naively annualizing by √k then overstates the true Sharpe — a point Lo (2002) makes formally.

Where this needs qualifying

FX and macro P&L is reliably both skewed and fat-tailed — the "roughly independent, roughly normal" assumption behind a clean √k annualization is a convenience, not a guarantee. That's exactly why the correction later in this deck folds in a track record's own skew and kurtosis instead of assuming Gaussian returns.

Calmar

MaxDD isn't an average of anything — it's one specific realized event, the worst peak-to-trough decline the equity curve actually experienced. Picture two strategies with the same annualized return: Strategy A wobbles moderately most days but has never had a genuinely deep drawdown (a shallow 6%). Strategy B is quieter on typical days — a higher Sharpe — but is exposed to an occasional violent move, producing a brutal 45% drawdown once in the sample. Sharpe barely notices Strategy B's one catastrophic stretch, buried inside a much longer calm average; Calmar flips the ranking completely, because it's built entirely around that one worst outcome.

📊

Reach for Sharpe

The default, general-purpose comparison, when there's no known asymmetry to account for.

📈

Reach for Sortino

When the payoff is known or suspected asymmetric — options-selling, trend-following, convex or concave shapes.

🩹

Reach for Calmar

When the audience — an allocator, a trader who has to live through the drawdown — cares most about the worst realized outcome.

Section 7

The best of 50 random strategies looks great by pure luck

A Sharpe of 1.8 looks identical whether it's the only thing you ever tried, or the single winner you kept after testing 50 variations and discarding the other 49. Those are not the same claim.

If you flip 50 coins, one of them lands heads 8 times in a row purely from volume — that coin isn't secretly a better coin. A backtested Sharpe ratio works the same way: test 50 variations of a signal (different lookbacks, thresholds, exit rules), and the best of the 50 is going to look good even if none of them have any real edge, purely because the search gave luck 50 chances to show up somewhere.

The trap

A Sharpe ratio carries no visible record of how many variations were tried to produce it. A 1.8 that's the only thing ever backtested, and a 1.8 that's the surviving best-of-50, look identical on the page — but they are not the same claim.

This is the exact same shape of problem the Multiple Testing lesson covers for p-values across 70 pair/regime slices — more trials means more chances for one to look significant, or for one Sharpe to look inflated, purely from noise in a finite sample, with zero real edge required anywhere in the search.

Section 9

The Deflated Sharpe Ratio: benchmark against chance, not zero

Two steps: first build SR₀, the chance-expected ceiling; then ask how far above it the reported Sharpe sits.

N

Trials run

How many variations were actually backtested before the best one was reported.

σ_SR

Spread across trials

How dispersed the N trials' own Sharpe ratios were — more spread means more room for one to land far above the pack by chance.

T, γ

Track length & shape

The reported record's own length, skewness and kurtosis — widen the standard error for a lopsided, fat-tailed track record instead of assuming Gaussian.

Section 11

This repo's own discipline — and a claim worth double-checking

This repo's own validation rule already covers half of this problem: a strategy only "wins" if it beats the incumbent on out-of-sample Sharpe with a non-trivial trade count. The full lesson this deck summarizes states that nothing yet tracks how many variations produced a reported Sharpe, and that a Deflated Sharpe Ratio "isn't built" in this project's own metrics stack.

What checking the actual code found

That claim is stale. js/backtestStats.js already exports a working deflatedSharpe(daily, trialSRs) function implementing exactly the SR₀/DSR machinery on this slide — and it isn't sitting unused. It's imported by strategyLabEngine.js, trendFollowEngine.js, forecastAnalyserStore.js and mve/validateInstrument.js, which filters which instruments count as "tradeable" using a minimum-DSR bar. A dated project note (2026-08-29) records a real finding from it: a headline vote-margin config's own Sharpe did not clear its chance-expected ceiling across a 104-trial grid — dsr: 0 — exactly the honest, unflattering answer this correction exists to give.

What's still true from the lesson: undercounting N remains the real risk. That same 2026-08-29 note flags that the vote rule's original search was larger and partly unlogged — 104 trials is only the grid that could be reconstructed, not necessarily the full search that ever ran. DSR being built and wired in doesn't mean every reported Sharpe in this repo has actually been run through it yet.

Section 12

Quick check

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Why does annualizing a Sharpe ratio use √k rather than k?

Annualized mean scales by a full factor of k; annualized variance also scales by k, so annualized standard deviation scales only by √k. Dividing the k-scaled numerator by the √k-scaled denominator leaves a net √k.
Question 2

What specifically does Sortino change relative to Sharpe?

Sortino keeps the same numerator but replaces σₓ with downside deviation — a standard deviation computed only over periods below the minimum acceptable return — so upside surprises stop counting against the strategy.
Question 3

A strategy has a strong Sharpe but a poor Calmar. What does that most likely mean?

Sharpe is built from average dispersion over the whole series; Calmar is built from one specific realized event. A quiet-most-days strategy can still carry a rare, brutal drawdown that variance barely notices but Calmar is built entirely around.
Question 4

In the DSR formula, what happens to SR₀ as the number of trials N increases?

Both terms in the SR₀ approximation increase with N. More variations tried means more chances for one to land far above the pack purely by chance, so the bar a reported Sharpe must clear keeps rising.
Question 5

Does this repo currently have a working Deflated Sharpe Ratio function wired into real strategy validation?

deflatedSharpe(daily, trialSRs) already exists in js/backtestStats.js and is imported by strategyLabEngine.js, trendFollowEngine.js, forecastAnalyserStore.js and mve/validateInstrument.js — the full lesson's "not built yet" framing is stale.
Section 13

What to carry forward

01

Sharpe, Sortino and Calmar divide return by three different kinds of risk — total wobble, downside-only wobble, and the single worst realized drawdown. None subsumes the others.

02

Annualize Sharpe by √k, not k — variance adds linearly across independent periods, standard deviation doesn't, and autocorrelated returns break the assumption behind that scaling.

03

A reported Sharpe carries no visible record of how many variations produced it — a 1.8 that's the only thing ever tested and a 1.8 that's the best of 50 look identical on the page.

04

The Deflated Sharpe Ratio benchmarks a reported Sharpe against SR₀ — the chance-expected ceiling for a best-of-N search — instead of against zero.

05

Undercounting N silently inflates DSR — informal reruns and "nudge it by feel" iterations count as trials too, the same failure mode the Multiple Testing lesson flags for unlogged slice-testing.

06

This repo's deflatedSharpe() is real, wired in, and has already produced an honest negative finding — checking a repo claim against the actual code is worth doing before repeating it.

What this now lets you do

Read a headline Sharpe correctly by asking how many variations were tried before it was reported, not just whether it's above zero. Choose Sharpe, Sortino or Calmar deliberately based on what the audience actually cares about. Recognize the "best of many" trap the moment a backtest report shows one standout result with no mention of the search behind it.
