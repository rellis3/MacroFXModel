Quantile Regression, Aiming Past the Average

Ordinary least squares answers one specific question — "what's the average outcome given these conditions?" — no matter what question you actually asked it. A risk manager rarely wants the average; they want to know how bad the 5th-percentile outcome could get given today's conditions, which is a question about one tail of a distribution, not its center. This guide walks through why that mismatch matters, and how swapping squared-error loss for an asymmetric "pinball loss" lets a regression target a specific quantile directly — closing the loop with the Regression Diagnostics lesson's OLS mechanics and the Cornish-Fisher lesson's static distributional adjustment.

Section 1

What OLS is actually fitting

Not "the relationship" in some vague sense — one very specific conditional quantity.

x̄

The conditional mean, E[Y∣X]

The fitted line ŷ = x⊤β̂ is an estimate of the average value of Y you'd expect given X — not the typical value, not the worst case, the average.

∑²

Squared error picks the mean

Minimizing the sum of squared residuals is what forces the fit toward the average — squared loss punishes over- and under-prediction identically, and the point that minimizes a symmetric penalty is the mean.

◎

One line, one number per x

For every value of X, OLS reports exactly one number: the expected Y. It says nothing about how spread out the actual outcomes are around that number.

✓

Perfectly fine for most questions

"What's the expected return," "what's the expected hedge ratio" — the conditional mean is exactly the right quantity for a huge range of everyday modeling questions.

As covered in Regression Diagnostics, OLS's point estimate β̂ is derived purely by minimizing squared error — no distributional assumption is needed for that minimization itself. What that lesson doesn't dwell on, because it's answering a different question, is what the fitted line represents: the conditional mean, not any particular percentile of the outcome.
Section 2

The question a risk manager actually asks

It is almost never "what's the average outcome." It's "how bad can this get."

What OLS answers

"Given today's volatility, momentum, and macro surprise — what's the expected next-day return?" A perfectly good question. Also not the one risk management is usually trying to answer.

What a risk desk needs

"Given today's conditions — what's my 5th-percentile worst outcome?" That's a direct, VaR-style question about one specific tail quantile of the conditional distribution, not its center.

These are genuinely different quantities, not the same quantity viewed at different confidence levels. Bolting a fixed offset onto a mean forecast only recovers the tail correctly if the spread around the mean stays constant across every condition — and it usually doesn't. When volatility itself rises with the predictors, the gap between "average" and "5th-percentile" widens too, and a mean-based model has no way to see that widening.

Where this needs qualifying

Nothing about OLS is "wrong" here — it was never built to answer a tail question. The mismatch is a mismatch of tool to task, not a flaw in least squares itself.

Section 3

The fix: punish a guess unevenly

Squared error treats over- and under-prediction identically. Pinball loss doesn't — and that asymmetry is the entire mechanism.

The pinball (check) loss, ρτ(u)
u = Y − q
the residual
u ≥ 0 → τ·u
under-prediction
u < 0 → (1−τ)·|u|
over-prediction
At τ = 0.05, over-predicting (guessing the tail isn't that bad) is penalized at weight 0.95 — nineteen times more heavily than under-predicting at weight 0.05. That lopsidedness is what drags the loss-minimizing guess down toward the low tail instead of the center. τ = 0.5 recovers the symmetric special case: equal weights, and the loss-minimizing fit becomes the median.

Nothing about the underlying method changes — it's still "find the line that minimizes total penalty over all observations." Only the shape of the penalty changes, and that shape alone decides whether the fit lands on the mean, the median, or a specific tail quantile. Minimizing the expectation of ρτ(Y − q) over q can be shown to land exactly on q = F⁻¹(τ), the τ-th quantile of Y — the same kind of first-order argument that shows the mean minimizes expected squared error, just applied to this asymmetric loss instead.

Section 7

The practical payoff

A tail estimate that moves with conditions, instead of a fixed correction hoping the tail scales proportionally.

Cornish-Fisher — static

Corrects a Gaussian quantile using the return series's own unconditional skew and kurtosis. One adjustment, computed once from history, applied identically no matter what today's conditions actually look like.

Quantile regression — conditional

Fits qτ(x) directly against live predictors — today's realized volatility, a momentum signal, a regime flag. The fitted tail moves automatically as those predictors move.

In the toy example, the 80%-interval width (τ=0.9 minus τ=0.1) grows from about 0.80 at x=1 to about 4.97 at x=10 — roughly six times wider. A single OLS mean line, or a static Cornish-Fisher offset bolted onto it, has no mechanism to express that the spread itself is widening; both report the same shape of correction at every x. Quantile regression's output tracks how the worst case changes with conditions directly, rather than adjusting an unconditional distribution's mean or moments and hoping the tail follows along proportionally.

Where this needs qualifying

This is a real regression, with real overfitting risk — more predictors and a thin sample can produce a tail estimate that looks sharp in-sample and degrades out-of-sample. Cornish-Fisher's two-number moment correction is cheaper to build and validate; quantile regression trades that cheapness for responsiveness to current conditions.

Section 8

How this actually gets used

🎯

Directly modeling conditional VaR

Fitting qτ(x) at τ=0.01 or τ=0.05 against relevant predictors is a standard way to build a VaR estimate that responds to current conditions, not just a fixed unconditional shape.

📐

Many τ values trace a whole shape

Fitting the same predictors at τ = 0.05, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 0.95 builds a picture of the entire conditional distribution — including how its spread and asymmetry themselves change — without assuming a parametric family.

🌐

Standard well outside finance

Econometrics, epidemiology, and ecology all reach for this exact tool whenever the outcome's spread or shape changes across the predictor range, not just its average.

🤝

Complements the mean, doesn't replace it

A desk reporting both an OLS mean forecast and a quantile-regression tail estimate gets two genuinely different pieces of information from the same predictors.

Section 9

Where it gets over-trusted

Quantile crossing — a real limitation, not an edge case

Each τ is fit as its own separate optimization, so nothing in the basic method guarantees q̂0.1(x) < q̂0.5(x) < q̂0.9(x) holds at every x. Near the edges of the observed data, fitted lines for different τ can actually cross — nonsensically implying a higher quantile predicts a lower value. This is the direct structural cousin of Cornish-Fisher's own non-monotonicity failure.

🔬

Extreme τ needs tail data

A fit at τ=0.01 is pinned down mostly by the small fraction of observations near that extreme — a modest sample can leave it poorly determined even when the median line is comfortable.

📏

Still a linear model

The loss function changed, not the assumption that the quantile is linear in the predictors. A genuinely nonlinear conditional quantile will be misfit, the same way a nonlinear conditional mean misfits OLS.

⚠

More predictors, more overfitting surface

Unlike a two-number moment adjustment, this is a real regression — it needs the same out-of-sample validation discipline any regression does.

Section 10

Checked against this repo's actual code

The full lesson makes a specific claim about what's built here. Verifying it against the current source turned up a nuance worth stating plainly.

Original lesson claim vs. current repo reality

What the full lesson says: its status line reads "CONCEPT — explains a conditional-quantile estimation technique; not built or used anywhere in this repo," and its takeaways repeat that "it is not a claim that quantile regression is fitted or used anywhere in this project."

What the current code actually shows: the pinball loss this lesson derives is not absent — it's implemented and used pervasively as a proper scoring rule: forge/vol.py::pinball_loss, js/volEstimatorAB.js::pinball, js/mve/validation.js::pinball, and js/coneBlend.js::_pinballLoss all score volatility- and cone-forecast calibration with it. forge/drift.py::_fit_linear goes a step further: it grid-searches a drift-adjustment parameter β to minimize out-of-sample pinball loss for a prediction of the form σ·(1+β·d) — a live predictor d (a momentum signal) genuinely shifting the fitted quantile, which is functionally a simple, single-predictor conditional-quantile fit.

What's still accurate: none of this is the general linear quantile regression qτ(x) = x⊤βτ, fit by linear programming against an arbitrary predictor matrix, that Koenker & Bassett describe and this lesson derives. That specific machinery genuinely isn't implemented anywhere in this repo — only the pinball-loss scoring rule and one narrow, single-predictor conditional fit are.

Both things can be true at once: the general technique this lesson teaches is still conceptual here, but the loss function underneath it is already load-bearing in production calibration code. Neither the original claim nor the correction is dropped — both are on this slide.

Section 11

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

What does OLS's fitted line actually estimate?

Squared-error loss is symmetric, and the point minimizing a symmetric penalty is the average — the conditional mean, not any particular percentile.
Question 2

In pinball loss at τ=0.05, which side is penalized more heavily, and by how much?

Under-prediction is weighted τ; over-prediction is weighted 1−τ. At τ=0.05, that's 0.05 vs. 0.95 — over-prediction is punished nineteen times harder, which drags the loss-minimizing guess down toward the low tail.
Question 3

Why can't a single OLS mean line show that the τ=0.1–τ=0.9 band widens with x?

Squared-error loss never distinguishes "the spread was wide" from "the spread was narrow but the mean guess was off" — it only ever reports the single expected value, at every x.
Question 4

What is quantile crossing?

Each τ is its own separate optimization, so nothing guarantees the fitted lines stay correctly ordered everywhere — a real limitation, and the structural cousin of Cornish-Fisher's own non-monotonicity problem.
Question 5

What's the central contrast between Cornish-Fisher and quantile regression?

Cornish-Fisher nudges a Gaussian quantile using the whole sample's unconditional skew and kurtosis — the same correction every day. Quantile regression fits the quantile as a function of today's predictors, so the estimate shifts as conditions shift.
Section 12

What to carry forward

01

OLS fits the conditional mean, E[Y∣X] — the average outcome given the predictors, and nothing else, because squared-error loss is symmetric.

02

A risk manager's real question is usually about a specific tail quantile — "what's my 5th-percentile worst outcome given these conditions" — a VaR-style question the mean can't answer directly.

03

The asymmetric pinball loss ρτ(u) fixes this: minimizing its expectation lands exactly on the τ-th conditional quantile, not the mean.

04

On heteroskedastic data, the fitted quantile lines fan out as the predictor grows — a single OLS mean line structurally cannot show that the spread itself is changing.

05

Quantile regression is conditional, unlike Cornish-Fisher's static moment-based adjustment — the fitted tail moves automatically as live predictors like realized volatility move.

06

Quantile crossing is a real, documented limitation — separately-fit τ lines aren't guaranteed to stay correctly ordered everywhere.

What this now lets you do

Build a model whose output directly tracks how the tail — the worst-case outcome — changes with market conditions, instead of adjusting an unconditional distribution's mean or moments and hoping the tail behaves proportionally. That's a genuinely different, and often more useful, answer to "how bad can this get" than any static correction can offer.
