One FFT Call, an Entire Strike Curve

Heston-style models hand you a closed-form characteristic function but no closed-form price — pricing one strike means numerically integrating that function, which is fine once. It stops being fine the moment a calibration routine needs to reprice a whole observed strike curve, at every candidate parameter set, hundreds or thousands of times before it converges. Carr & Madan's 1999 insight reframes the entire strike curve as one Fourier transform, so a single Fast Fourier Transform call — not one integration per strike — produces the whole thing at once.

How it reached trading

Heston-type prices are integrals of a characteristic function. Calibration reprices hundreds of strikes at every optimiser step, and integrating one strike at a time was the bottleneck.

Carr and Madan (1999) damped the call price so one FFT (Cooley–Tukey, 1965) returns a whole strike grid. That made Heston, Bates and variance gamma practical to fit; the COS method (2008) followed.

A characteristic-function pricing formula turns option pricing into a single one-dimensional integral over a frequency variable — smooth, low-dimensional, exactly where deterministic quadrature (Simpson's rule or similar) is the right tool.

Set up the integral, evaluate it, get one number. Nothing about this is wrong or approximate in a worrying way.

A 50-strike option chain means 50 separate integrations — one per strike, with its own function-evaluation cost, run independently of every other strike.

⏱

And repeats per calibration try

Fitting a model to an observed smile means running that whole 50-integration pass again at every candidate parameter set an optimizer proposes — not once.

The Numerical Integration lesson already covers this exact case: a characteristic-function pricing formula reduces option pricing to a one-dimensional integral, routinely evaluated with Simpson's rule. That is a complete, honest answer — for pricing one option, at one strike, one time. It is not an answer to what calibration actually asks for.

A realistic overnight run

200 strikes — a routine size for a single-name or FX option chain across one expiry — times 500 candidate parameter sets before an optimizer converges is 100,000 separate one-dimensional numerical integrations, each with its own real cost, before a single overnight Heston calibration finishes.

That is the gap Peter Carr and Dilip Madan closed in 1999: a way to price an entire strip of strikes in a single Fast Fourier Transform call, turning what would be N separate integrations into one O(N log N) computation covering the whole grid at once.

Section 5

The snag, and the fix

A Fourier transform needs its input to fade to zero. The raw call price refuses to.

Raw price — C_T(k)

As log-strike k → −∞ (strike toward zero), a call's price approaches the discounted forward price, not zero. It stays large instead of decaying — so C_T(k) is not square-integrable, and directly Fourier-transforming it diverges near the money.

Damped price — c_T(k) = e^(αk)·C_T(k)

Multiply by a decaying exponential in k, tuned by a positive constant α. The damped price actually vanishes on both tails, so its Fourier transform exists and converges cleanly — this, not the raw price, is what gets transformed.

The role of α is purely technical, not economic — it doesn't change what an option is worth. It's a mathematical device that pulls the price toward zero fast enough for the transform to exist. Recovering the true price later just means multiplying back by e^(−αk).
Section 6

The published result: ψ_T(v)

Carr & Madan (1999)'s central contribution — the Fourier transform of the damped price, in closed form.

φ

φ_T(v−(α+1)i) — numerator

The model's characteristic function, evaluated at a shifted, complex argument. This is the one place the specific model — Heston, or any other with a known φ_T — enters the formula.

e

e^(−rT) — discounting

Brings the risk-neutral expected payoff back to a present value, the same discounting role it plays throughout option pricing.

÷

α²+α−v²+i(2α+1)v — denominator

A fixed function of α and the transform variable v, generic machinery that falls out of transforming the damped price — identical for every model plugged in.

Stated here as the correctly cited published result, not re-derived line by line — the same house convention this curriculum uses for other closed forms, including Heston's own semi-closed-form pricing formula in the previous lesson.
Section 7

Inversion is exactly FFT-shaped

Recovering C_T(k) from ψ_T(v) means inverting the transform. Discretized on a grid, that inversion is a discrete Fourier transform.

The same problem, two ways
Once v is put on an evenly-spaced grid, the inversion integral is structurally a discrete Fourier transform — evaluated at every log-strike k the grid implies, simultaneously. That is precisely what the FFT algorithm computes efficiently: one O(N log N) call instead of N separate O(1)-each integrations.

Section 10

Where this lives on a real desk

🌊

Stochastic-volatility calibration

Heston, and jump-diffusion cousins like Merton's or Bates' — fitting five-plus parameters to an observed implied-volatility surface, overnight or intraday.

📐

Any tractable characteristic function

Affine and exponential-Lévy models broadly — a wide, practically important class covering most tractable option-pricing models used in production.

⚡

Real-time repricing

Once a parameter set is fixed, quoting a fresh strike off the same FFT grid is essentially free — the expensive part already happened.

⏳

Feasible vs. infeasible

The difference between an overnight calibration that finishes before the desk opens and one that doesn't converge in time to be useful.

A quant recalibrating Heston's five parameters against that morning's full implied-volatility surface — a few hundred quoted prices, thousands of candidate parameter sets tried by the optimizer — is exactly the workload Carr-Madan targets: not a new source of edge, but the infrastructure that makes running the existing model's calibration at the required scale and speed practically possible at all.
Section 11

Where it doesn't apply

Works when
  • A closed-form characteristic function φ_T exists — Heston, SABR-style, jump-diffusion, affine/exponential-Lévy models
  • Many strikes need repricing repeatedly — calibration, real-time quoting across a chain
Doesn't work when
  • No closed-form φ_T exists — many local-volatility specifications have nothing to plug in
  • Pricing one option, one strike, one time — plain quadrature already does the job with none of the setup
Where this needs qualifying

The damping factor α and the FFT's strike/frequency grid spacing (η, λ) are not free choices — they trade off against each other (a discrete-FFT uncertainty-principle-style constraint), a real tuning step in any implementation. And a fast calibration loop is still curve-fitting: speed says nothing about whether the fitted parameters generalize out-of-sample, the same caveat the Heston lesson raises about a good smile fit.

Section 12

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Why can't the raw call price C_T(k) simply be Fourier-transformed as-is?

A Fourier transform needs its input to fade to zero for the integral to converge. C_T(k) stays large near the money instead of shrinking — Carr & Madan fix this by transforming a damped version, e^(αk)C_T(k), instead.
Question 2

Where does the specific pricing model (e.g. Heston) actually enter ψ_T(v)?

Everything else in ψ_T(v) is generic machinery, identical no matter which model supplied φ_T. Swap in a different model's characteristic function and the rest of the pipeline is unchanged.
Question 3

Why does discretizing the inversion integral over v make it FFT-computable?

Once v sits on a grid, the sum is exactly the summation-of-complex-exponentials shape an FFT computes in O(N log N) — across the whole grid simultaneously, not one point at a time.
Question 4

200 strikes, 500 calibration parameter sets — roughly how do the two routes' operation counts compare?

200 × 500 = 100,000 one-dimensional integrations for the direct route. Carr-Madan needs one FFT call per parameter set — 500 total — since each call already covers the entire strike grid.
Question 5

Does Carr-Madan speed up pricing under a local-volatility model with no known closed-form characteristic function?

The entire construction is built on having φ_T in closed form. Without one, the fallback is Monte Carlo simulation or a PDE solve — the same tools the Numerical Integration lesson names for genuinely hard pricing problems.

What this now lets you do

Recognize when a pricing problem is a Carr-Madan candidate — a tractable characteristic function plus a need to reprice many strikes fast and repeatedly. Understand why an entire calibration loop's runtime hinges on collapsing "one integration per strike" into "one FFT call, whole grid" — and just as importantly, recognize the boundary: no closed-form φ_T means no Carr-Madan speedup, full stop, and Monte Carlo or a PDE solve are the real fallback.
