Risk Parity, or Stop Guessing Returns

The Markowitz & Black-Litterman lesson ended on an honest confession: mean-variance optimization is only as good as its expected-return forecasts, and those forecasts are the least trustworthy number in the whole model. Black-Litterman's answer was to moderate that fragile input with a market prior. Risk parity's answer is more radical — it throws the return forecast out entirely, and asks a completely different question: not "which assets will do best," but "how do I make sure no single asset can dominate my portfolio's risk."

Section 1

Markowitz's one truly fragile input

Everything else in mean-variance optimization is estimable. This one piece isn't.

Σ

Covariance is estimable

Volatilities and correlations, estimated from historical returns, are noisy — but they're reasonably stable from one period to the next. This is the one input Markowitz can lean on with some confidence.

μ

Expected returns are not

Forecasting which asset will outperform is genuinely hard, and small errors in that forecast are not small in their effect — the optimizer treats a barely-more-confident guess as a strong signal worth betting heavily on.

⚡

The optimizer amplifies the noise

Mean-variance optimization is a maximizer — it doesn't just use a slightly-wrong return estimate, it searches out and leans hardest on whichever asset the noise happens to have flattered most that period.

↺

Small input change, huge output change

Nudging one asset's expected return by a fraction of a percentage point can flip an "optimal" portfolio from 70% in that asset to near zero — the hallmark of an ill-conditioned, unstable optimization.

Where the last lesson left off

Black-Litterman's fix was to moderate the fragile expected-return input with a market-equilibrium prior and confidence-weighted views — a way of trusting the forecast less, not abandoning it. Risk parity, this lesson, takes the more radical option still on the table: don't moderate the forecast — remove it from the model altogether.

Section 2

The radical move: allocate on risk alone

Markowitz asks

"Given how much I expect each asset to return, and how they move together, what mix maximizes return for a given level of risk?" — a question that cannot be answered without an expected-return forecast for every asset.

Risk parity asks

"Given only how volatile each asset is and how they move together, what mix makes sure no single asset can dominate my portfolio's total risk?" — a question that never needs a return forecast at all.

This isn't a smaller version of Markowitz — it's a different model with a different goal. Risk parity has no opinion whatsoever about which asset will perform better. It only has an opinion about how much risk each asset brings to the table, which — as the previous lesson established — is the one piece of the model that actually can be estimated with reasonable stability.

Whether that trade is a good one depends entirely on how much genuine signal was sitting in the expected-return forecasts just discarded. That's a real question, and this lesson doesn't pretend to resolve it for you.

Equal capital. But bonds are historically several times calmer than commodities — so on almost any given day, nearly all of the portfolio's actual up-and-down movement is coming from that one-third of the money. The bond sleeve is along for the ride, contributing a rounding error's worth of real risk despite an equal dollar share.

Equal weight is not equal risk — and equal risk, not equal capital, is what risk parity actually targets. To get there, the lower-volatility assets have to be held in larger dollar amounts than the higher-volatility ones, until each one's contribution to the portfolio's total wiggle — not its total dollars — comes out the same.

Risk contribution is a real accounting split of total portfolio risk into per-asset pieces that add back up to the whole, the same way a restaurant bill splits by what each person actually ordered, not by head count.

Subject to the weights summing to 1 (fully invested, no leverage in the base case). With N assets that's N−1 independent equations plus the budget constraint, for N unknown weights — a determined system, but a nonlinear one, since (Σw)ᵢ and σₚ both depend on every entry of w at once.

There is, in general, no algebraic formula that hands back the ERC weights directly — they have to be found by an iterative numerical search that nudges the weights back and forth until every RCᵢ equalizes. There's exactly one tractable special case worth knowing:

The one closed-form shortcut

If every pair of assets happened to share the same correlation ρ, the ERC solution reduces exactly to inverse-volatility weighting, wᵢ ∝ 1/σᵢ — put simply, size each asset in inverse proportion to how jumpy it is, full stop, ignoring correlation altogether. It's a clean, memorable result, and it's the idealization most people picture when they first hear "risk parity." Real markets essentially never have uniform pairwise correlation — the next slide shows exactly what breaks once they don't.

Section 9

The honest trade-off

Dropping the return forecast isn't a free lunch — it's a trade, stated plainly.

What you gain
  • No need to forecast returns — the one Markowitz input that's genuinely hard to estimate reliably
  • No single asset can quietly dominate the portfolio's actual risk, whatever its dollar weight
  • Risk contribution is an exact accounting identity, not a guess — the RCᵢ provably sum to σₚ
  • Depends only on volatility and correlation, which (per the previous lesson) are comparatively stable to estimate
What you give up
  • No opinion at all on which asset is more attractive — a genuinely better expected return gets sized identically to a worse one with the same vol and correlation profile
  • Tends to hold large dollar amounts of historically low-volatility assets like government bonds — a real, if unexamined, tilt
  • Still depends on a covariance estimate that drifts — a regime shift in correlation (bonds and equities decoupling is the textbook case) can leave the book carrying different risk than intended
  • To hit a competitive overall return with so much capital parked in the calm sleeve, real implementations often lever that sleeve up — concentrating tail risk exactly where it's least expected
Dropping expected returns doesn't remove a bet from the portfolio — it just changes which bet is being made, without saying so out loud. It's an unstated wager that volatility and correlation are stable enough to trust, and that whatever real return differences exist across assets aren't worth acting on. Critics call heavy, unlevered bond weightings "an implicit view" for exactly this reason — risk parity claims to have no view, yet its output leans hard on one asset class more than any human allocator, unprompted, ever would.
Section 10

Does this actually exist in this codebase?

The full lesson's own status line calls this "theory only" — that claim was checked against the current source before this slide was written.

What the full lesson says vs. what's actually in the repo now

The full lesson states: "CONCEPT — theory only, no multi-instrument allocator built or tested in this repo," and its Key Takeaways repeat that "this lesson is the theory behind a multi-instrument allocator this repo has not built, tested, or run out-of-sample."

What's actually in the repo today: that claim is stale. js/bookStress.js contains riskParityWeights() — a genuine iterative Equal Risk Contribution solver (a multiplicative fixed-point update on the sample covariance, the same style of algorithm this slide deck's own correlation slider runs in your browser) — feeding an allocationCompare() routine that backtests equal / inverse-vol / risk-parity allocation on real sleeve returns and reports realized vol, Sharpe, max drawdown, and effective bets under each. It's covered by passing unit tests in js/bookStress.test.mjs (weights sum to 1; the equal-risk property holds; it correctly reduces toward inverse-vol weighting for uncorrelated assets), and surfaced live on the book-stress.html page. A simpler sibling, inverseVolWeights() in js/levelAtlasVoteReview.js, is already wired into this project's production trade-vote allocation code.

To be precise about what this does and doesn't establish: this is real, tested code applied to this project's own trade sleeves (a trend book vs. a fade book, or per-pair vote portfolios) rather than a classic multi-asset equity/bond/commodity allocation — but it is the same mathematics this lesson teaches, genuinely implemented, genuinely unit-tested, and already live in a page a reader of this repo can open today. Nothing here is silently corrected in the full lesson's text — both claims are left visible, side by side, exactly as found.

Section 11

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

What is Markowitz's practical Achilles' heel?

Covariance is comparatively stable to estimate; expected returns are not, and the optimizer amplifies small forecast errors into large, unstable weight swings.
Question 2

What does risk parity target instead of expected returns?

Equal Risk Contribution (ERC) sets weights so RCᵢ = RCⱼ for every pair — genuinely different from equal-dollar weighting, which is what most people mean by "balanced" by default.
Question 3

In the three-tab example, why did true ERC differ from simple inverse-volatility weighting?

Inverse-vol weighting is exact ERC only under uniform pairwise correlation. With equities and commodities far more correlated with each other than either was with bonds, the true numerical ERC solve shifted even more weight into bonds to compensate.
Question 4

What's the honest trade-off in dropping expected returns entirely?

Risk parity sizes purely by risk, with zero mechanism to reward a genuinely better expected return — and its bond-heavy tilt is itself an implicit, unexamined bet, according to its critics.
Question 5

Does this repo have code that numerically solves for equal-risk-contribution weights?

js/bookStress.js's riskParityWeights() is a real, tested, iterative ERC solver, wired into allocationCompare() and surfaced on book-stress.html — the full lesson's "no allocator built or tested" claim is stale, as flagged on the earlier slide.

What to carry forward

01

Markowitz's fatal flaw is return-estimate sensitivity — small forecast errors can flip "optimal" weights into extreme corner solutions.

02

Risk parity abandons return forecasting entirely, allocating so that no single asset dominates total portfolio risk.

03

Risk contribution, RCᵢ = wᵢ(Σw)ᵢ/σₚ, is an exact accounting split — every RCᵢ sums exactly to σₚ, for any weights at all.

04

True Equal Risk Contribution (RCᵢ = RCⱼ for every pair) is a genuinely different, correlation-aware target from equal-dollar or naive inverse-vol weighting alone.

05

Only under the idealized uniform-correlation special case does ERC reduce exactly to inverse-volatility weighting; real, uneven correlations need a numerical solve.

06

The trade-off is real: no view on which asset is more attractive, and a real tendency to lean hard on low-vol assets like bonds — itself an implicit bet, by some critics' account.

What this now lets you do

Build a portfolio-weighting scheme that never needs a return forecast — only volatility and correlation, the more stable inputs. Recognize the moment "equal weight" gets silently substituted for "equal risk" in a pitch deck or a fund's marketing. Know precisely when the cheap inverse-vol shortcut is fine (correlations roughly uniform) versus when a full numerical ERC solve is required (they're not) — and know this repo already has that numerical solve built and unit-tested, in js/bookStress.js, ready to point to.
