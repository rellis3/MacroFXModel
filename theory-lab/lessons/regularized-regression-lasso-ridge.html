Regularized Regression: LASSO & Ridge

Plain OLS will happily fit noise if you hand it enough correlated or irrelevant predictors. Ridge and LASSO fix that by penalizing large coefficients — and the shape of the penalty, not just its size, decides whether a weak predictor gets quietly shrunk or deleted outright.

CONCEPT — not built or tested anywhere in this repo ~16 min read Prereqs: Regression Diagnostics, Correlation & Linear Regression primer
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • Plain OLS minimizes only \(\|y-X\beta\|^2\) — with many correlated or irrelevant predictors it can produce wildly unstable, overfit coefficients even while the in-sample fit looks great.
  • Ridge (Hoerl & Kennard, 1970) adds an L2 penalty \(\lambda\|\beta\|_2^2\): it shrinks every coefficient smoothly toward zero but essentially never sets one exactly to zero.
  • LASSO (Tibshirani, 1996) adds an L1 penalty \(\lambda\|\beta\|_1\) instead: its diamond-shaped constraint region has corners sitting exactly on the axes, so the optimum regularly lands with one or more coefficients at exactly zero — automatic feature selection, not just shrinkage.
  • The difference is pure geometry: a circle (L2) almost never touches an elliptical loss contour at an axis point; a diamond (L1) very often does, because its corners are the axis points.
  • Elastic Net (Zou & Hastie, 2005) blends both penalties — LASSO's sparsity plus Ridge's stability when predictors are correlated in groups.
  • The worked example fits a toy 3-predictor regression with one real signal, one near-duplicate of it, and one pure noise column: OLS gives unstable, even sign-flipped coefficients; Ridge shrinks all three smoothly; LASSO zeroes the duplicate and the noise column exactly, keeping only the real signal.
Section 01

Why this matters

The Regression Diagnostics lesson in this Theory Lab derives what can go wrong with the inference a regression produces — the standard error, the t-statistic, the p-value — once the residuals misbehave. This lesson is about a different failure mode, sitting one step earlier: what goes wrong with the coefficients themselves when a regression is handed more predictors than it can cleanly separate, especially predictors that are correlated with each other or simply irrelevant.

Plain ordinary least squares has no defense against this. Its only job is to minimize \(\|y - X\beta\|^2\), the sum of squared residuals, and it will do that job perfectly even when the "best" fit requires enormous, canceling, unstable coefficients on two predictors that are 95% the same signal — or a small but nonzero coefficient on a column that is pure noise, because on this particular sample that noise happened to line up with \(y\) a little. A model built this way often looks impressive in-sample (a high \(R^2\), a tight fit) and then falls apart on new data, because much of what it "learned" was the specific noise pattern of the training sample rather than anything that will repeat.

Regularization is the fix: add a penalty term to the thing being minimized that directly punishes large coefficients, trading a small amount of bias (the fitted coefficients are no longer the unbiased OLS estimates) for a large reduction in variance (how much the fitted coefficients would swing if you re-ran the fit on a slightly different sample). That bias-variance trade is the single idea underneath everything else in this lesson — and the two classic ways of paying it, Ridge and LASSO, differ only in the shape of the penalty, which turns out to matter enormously.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Two ways to rein in a model that's trying too hard

Imagine a regression as a student writing an essay who's been told "longer is better" and has taken that too literally — padding every sentence, restating the same point three different ways, working in details that don't actually support the argument. Regularization is an editor stepping in with a rule: every sentence you keep costs you something, so only keep the ones earning their place.

A Ridge editor trims every sentence a little — tightens the wording throughout the essay, leans on the strong parts less hard, leans on the weak parts less hard too, but never actually deletes a sentence outright. The essay gets uniformly leaner. A LASSO editor is stricter: sentences that aren't pulling real weight get cut entirely, not trimmed. The essay ends up shorter — fewer sentences, full stop — and the ones that survive are the ones that were actually doing something. Both editors improve the same underlying problem (an essay padded with noise); they just make a different kind of edit.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

OLS is a kid packing a suitcase who's been told "bring anything that might be useful" — so they bring everything, including things that don't help at all, and the suitcase is a mess. Ridge is a parent who says "fine, bring it all, but everything gets squeezed smaller." LASSO is a parent who says "no — if it's not actually useful, it's not going in the bag at all."

Section 02

The math, step by step

Start from plain least squares. Given a design matrix \(X\) (rows are observations, columns are predictors) and a target vector \(y\), OLS finds:

\[ \hat\beta_{OLS} = \arg\min_\beta \ \|y - X\beta\|^2 \]

Both Ridge and LASSO keep this same loss term — fit the data — and simply add a second term that penalizes the size of \(\beta\).

Ridge regression — the L2 penalty

Ridge regression (Hoerl & Kennard, 1970, writing specifically to address "nonorthogonal problems" — the technical name for exactly the correlated-predictor situation described above) minimizes:

\[ \hat\beta_{Ridge} = \arg\min_\beta \ \|y - X\beta\|^2 + \lambda\|\beta\|_2^2, \qquad \|\beta\|_2^2 = \sum_j \beta_j^2 \]
‖y−Xβ‖²the ordinary least-squares loss — how well the model fits the data
λthe regularization strength — a dial from 0 (plain OLS) to large (everything shrunk hard toward 0)
‖β‖₂²the sum of squared coefficients — the L2 penalty, punishing size quadratically

Because the penalty is a sum of squares, it punishes a single very large coefficient especially hard (squaring exaggerates large values), so the optimizer's cheapest way to satisfy the penalty is usually to spread a given amount of "explaining power" across several correlated predictors rather than piling it all onto one — which is exactly the fix for the wild, unstable, sign-flipped coefficients OLS can produce when two predictors carry almost the same information. What Ridge does not do is set a coefficient to exactly zero: shrinking a squared term always leaves a diminishing-returns tail, so the optimal \(\beta_j\) approaches zero as \(\lambda\) grows but essentially never reaches it exactly. Ridge shrinks; it does not select.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Ridge is like turning down the volume on every instrument in a band a little bit — the quiet ones get quieter, the loud ones get quieter too, but nobody's instrument gets muted completely. Everyone still plays, just softer.

LASSO — the L1 penalty

LASSO (Tibshirani, 1996 — the name is itself an acronym, "Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator," which describes exactly what it does) swaps the penalty for the sum of absolute values instead of squares:

\[ \hat\beta_{LASSO} = \arg\min_\beta \ \|y - X\beta\|^2 + \lambda\|\beta\|_1, \qquad \|\beta\|_1 = \sum_j |\beta_j| \]
‖y−Xβ‖²same OLS loss term as Ridge — no change here
λsame role — a dial for how hard to penalize coefficient size
‖β‖₁the sum of absolute coefficients — the L1 penalty

That one change — squares to absolute values — sounds cosmetic and is anything but. LASSO's solutions regularly land with one or more coefficients at exactly zero, not just small. That is not a numerical coincidence; it is a direct, provable consequence of the shape of the L1 penalty, and it's worth working through the geometry precisely, because "LASSO does feature selection" is a fact that's easy to memorize and much more useful once you can see why it's true.

Why a diamond's corners create exact zeros and a circle's edge (almost) never does

An equivalent way to write both problems (for a suitable \(t\) depending on \(\lambda\)) is as a constrained minimization: minimize the OLS loss \(\|y-X\beta\|^2\) subject to the penalty term staying inside a budget, \(\|\beta\|_2^2 \le t\) for Ridge or \(\|\beta\|_1 \le t\) for LASSO. Picture the two-coefficient case, \(\beta = (\beta_1, \beta_2)\). The OLS loss, plotted as a function of \(\beta_1\) and \(\beta_2\), forms a set of concentric elliptical contours — like a bowl viewed from above — centered on the unconstrained OLS solution, with the loss growing the further you move away from that center. The penalty budget carves out an allowed region around the origin: for Ridge, \(\|\beta\|_2^2\le t\) is a circle (or, in more than two dimensions, a sphere); for LASSO, \(\|\beta\|_1 \le t\) is a diamond (a square rotated 45°, with its corners sitting exactly on the \(\beta_1\) and \(\beta_2\) axes).

The regularized solution is whichever point inside the allowed region touches the smallest (lowest-loss) elliptical contour — geometrically, the point where the growing ellipse first "kisses" the boundary of the circle or diamond as it expands outward from the OLS center. A circle is smooth everywhere: its boundary has no special points, so the first touch between an arbitrarily-oriented ellipse and a circle can land anywhere along the circle's edge, and for a generic (tilted) ellipse it almost always lands at some interior point of the edge — a point where both \(\beta_1\) and \(\beta_2\) are nonzero. A diamond is different: most of its boundary is flat edges, but it also has four sharp corners, and each corner sits exactly on an axis (one coordinate is exactly zero). Because a corner is a point where the boundary changes direction abruptly, a whole range of possible ellipse orientations and positions will first touch the diamond precisely at that corner, rather than sliding past it onto a flat edge — the corner "catches" the ellipse for a disproportionately large fraction of possible loss shapes. That geometric fact — corners are much more likely tangent points than any single point on a smooth curve is — is the entire reason LASSO tends to zero coefficients out and Ridge does not.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

A circle has no corners, so wherever a balloon squeezed against it happens to touch, it touches — could be anywhere around the edge. A diamond has sharp corners sticking out, and those corners are much easier for a squeezed balloon to catch on than any other point on the diamond's flat sides. LASSO's "budget" is diamond-shaped, and its corners happen to sit exactly where one number is zero — so the balloon (the best-fit point) keeps landing right on a corner, and that number gets deleted, not just shrunk.

Elastic Net — the honest middle ground

LASSO's corner-seeking behavior has a known weak spot: when several predictors are strongly correlated with each other, LASSO tends to pick one of them somewhat arbitrarily and zero out the rest, even if they were all genuinely informative together. Zou & Hastie (2005) proposed Elastic Net specifically to fix this — a penalty that blends both terms:

\[ \hat\beta_{EN} = \arg\min_\beta \ \|y-X\beta\|^2 + \lambda\Big(\rho\|\beta\|_1 + (1-\rho)\|\beta\|_2^2\Big), \qquad \rho \in [0,1] \]
ρthe mixing weight — ρ=1 is pure LASSO, ρ=0 is pure Ridge
λoverall regularization strength, same role as before

With \(0 < \rho < 1\), the L1 piece still zeroes out genuinely useless predictors, while the L2 piece discourages the arbitrary, all-or-nothing choice among a correlated group — a set of correlated useful predictors tends to survive together, shrunk, rather than one being kept at full strength and the rest deleted essentially by coin flip. It is not a free lunch — it adds a second tuning parameter (\(\rho\), on top of \(\lambda\)) that has to be chosen too, usually by cross-validation alongside \(\lambda\) — but it is the standard answer whenever the practical concern is specifically "LASSO's selection is unstable because my predictors come in correlated groups."

\(\beta\)The coefficient vector being estimated — one entry per predictor.
\(X, y\)The design matrix (predictors) and target vector — the data being fit.
\(\lambda\)Regularization strength; \(\lambda=0\) recovers plain OLS, larger \(\lambda\) shrinks harder.
\(\|\beta\|_2^2\)The L2 (Ridge) penalty — sum of squared coefficients, \(\sum_j\beta_j^2\).
\(\|\beta\|_1\)The L1 (LASSO) penalty — sum of absolute coefficients, \(\sum_j|\beta_j|\).
\(t\)The penalty "budget" in the equivalent constrained formulation — smaller \(t\) means a smaller allowed region, corresponds to larger \(\lambda\).
\(\rho\)Elastic Net's L1/L2 mixing weight, from 0 (pure Ridge) to 1 (pure LASSO).

Worked example

Three predictors, one real signal, one near-duplicate, one pure noise

Build a toy dataset with three standardized predictors and a target \(y\) with a true underlying relationship \(y = 2.0\,x_1 + \varepsilon\) (so the true coefficients are \(\beta_1=2.0\), \(\beta_2=0\), \(\beta_3=0\)):

  • \(x_1\) — the real driver of \(y\).
  • \(x_2\) — a near-duplicate of \(x_1\) (correlation \(\rho \approx 0.9\), e.g. a second, highly overlapping proxy for the same underlying factor).
  • \(x_3\) — pure noise, unrelated to \(x_1\), \(x_2\), or \(y\).
1

Plain OLS — unstable, even sign-flipped

Fitting OLS on this sample produces \(\hat\beta_{OLS} = (3.4,\ -1.3,\ 0.4)\). Individually these numbers are close to meaningless: \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) are so collinear that OLS can't tell which one deserves credit for their shared signal, so it assigns an inflated positive weight to one and a compensating negative weight to the other — their sum, \(3.4-1.3=2.1\), is close to the true \(\beta_1=2.0\), but no single coefficient reflects that on its own. \(x_3\), pure noise, still picks up a nonzero \(0.4\) simply because on this particular sample it happened to correlate a little with the residual variation.

2

Ridge — shrinks all three, exactly zero for none

With \(\lambda\) chosen by cross-validation, Ridge gives \(\hat\beta_{Ridge} = (1.4,\ -0.6,\ 0.15)\). All three coefficients moved toward zero — \(x_1\)'s inflated weight came down, \(x_2\)'s compensating negative weight shrank in magnitude too, and \(x_3\)'s spurious weight shrank the most (it had the least real signal to begin with, so the penalty dominates it fastest) — but none of the three is exactly zero. Ridge doesn't know \(x_3\) is useless; it only knows \(x_3\)'s coefficient is small, and shrinks accordingly.

3

LASSO — zeroes the duplicate and the noise, exactly

With its own cross-validated \(\lambda\), LASSO gives \(\hat\beta_{LASSO} = (1.9,\ 0.0,\ 0.0)\). \(x_2\) (the near-duplicate) and \(x_3\) (pure noise) are both set to exactly zero, and the entire real signal is attributed to \(x_1\) alone, close to the true \(\beta_1=2.0\). Which of \(x_1\)/\(x_2\) specifically survives is itself somewhat arbitrary given how correlated they are — rerun on a slightly different sample and LASSO might keep \(x_2\) instead — but the outcome, a sparse model with the noise column gone, is the defining LASSO behavior.

🎯
Real-world trading scenario

An FX macro model with fifteen candidate predictors

A researcher builds a candidate model for a currency pair's monthly return using fifteen macro predictors: yield-curve spreads at several tenors (2y, 5y, 10y — several of which move almost in lockstep), inflation surprises, a commodity index, an equity-volatility index, and a handful of others thrown in "just in case they help." Several of these are highly correlated with each other (the different tenor spreads especially), and several are almost certainly irrelevant to this specific pair's short-horizon moves.

Run through LASSO with \(\lambda\) chosen by cross-validation, roughly nine of the fifteen predictors get zeroed out entirely, leaving a compact, interpretable six-predictor model — easy to explain, easy to monitor, and with far less risk of having fit fifteen predictors' worth of in-sample noise. Run through Ridge instead, all fifteen predictors remain in the model with smaller, more stable coefficients — a reasonable choice if the researcher genuinely believes most of the fifteen carry a little real information and doesn't want to gamble on LASSO's somewhat arbitrary pick among the correlated tenor spreads. Neither answer is "more correct" in the abstract; the choice depends on whether the researcher's honest prior is "most of these matter a little" (favoring Ridge) or "most of these are noise, a few really matter" (favoring LASSO) — and on how much a shorter, more interpretable model is worth relative to squeezing out a little extra fit.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

Choosing \(\lambda\) (and \(\rho\), for Elastic Net) is always done by cross-validation, never by in-sample fit. The in-sample loss is minimized at \(\lambda=0\) by construction — no regularization at all always fits the training data at least as well as some regularization does, so picking \(\lambda\) to maximize in-sample \(R^2\) would always select "don't regularize," defeating the entire point. In practice \(\lambda\) is swept over a grid and picked by whichever value minimizes error on held-out folds, exactly the general cross-validation discipline covered elsewhere in this curriculum, applied here to a single tuning parameter instead of a whole model choice.

Regularization and tree-based methods are two different answers to the same "too many predictors" problem. The Decision Trees & Random Forests and Gradient Boosting lessons in this Theory Lab cover a structurally different route to handling many candidate predictors: a tree only ever splits on the single most useful predictor at each node, so predictors that never help a split are implicitly ignored, and feature importance falls out of how often and how impactfully each predictor gets used across the ensemble. That mechanism handles nonlinearity and interactions between predictors automatically, which LASSO and Ridge — both still fundamentally linear models — do not. The trade-off runs the other way too: a tree-based importance ranking doesn't give you a single clean coefficient per predictor the way regularized regression does, and doesn't have LASSO's precise, provable "exactly zero" sparsity guarantee. Practitioners reach for regularized linear models when the underlying relationship is plausibly linear (or close to it) and interpretable coefficients matter; they reach for trees or boosting when the relationship is likely nonlinear or involves real interaction effects between predictors.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where regularization gets misused in practice

Forgetting to standardize predictors first. Both penalties are computed directly on the coefficient values, and a coefficient's raw magnitude depends entirely on the scale of its predictor — a predictor measured in basis points will naturally get a much smaller raw coefficient than one measured in decimal fractions, for reasons that have nothing to do with which predictor matters more. Standardizing every predictor to mean zero, unit variance before fitting is not optional; skipping it means the penalty punishes arbitrarily-scaled predictors unevenly, for reasons unrelated to their actual importance.

Treating "LASSO selected it" as statistical proof of importance. Which predictor survives among a correlated group can be genuinely unstable — as the worked example notes, a slightly different sample can flip which of two near-duplicate predictors gets kept. A single LASSO fit's selected-variable list is not, on its own, a validated claim that the surviving predictors are the "true" drivers; that instability is exactly what Elastic Net, or checking selection stability across bootstrap resamples, is meant to address.

Both methods stay linear models underneath the penalty. Regularization changes how the coefficients are estimated; it does not add any capacity to capture a genuinely nonlinear relationship or an interaction between two predictors that a straight-line combination can't represent. A LASSO model with a beautifully sparse, clean coefficient list can still be the wrong functional form entirely if the true relationship curves.

Picking \(\lambda\) once and never revisiting it. The right amount of regularization depends on the noise-to-signal ratio in the current data, which can drift — a \(\lambda\) cross-validated on one period's data is not guaranteed to still be the right choice on a later period with a different correlation structure among predictors, the same non-stationarity concern that shows up throughout this project's other validation discipline.

Section 04

Key Takeaways

✓Regularization trades a little bias for a lot less variance. Both Ridge and LASSO add a penalty on coefficient size to plain OLS's loss, shrinking coefficients that would otherwise be unstable.
✓Ridge (L2) shrinks smoothly, never hits exactly zero. Its circular constraint region has no corners, so the tangent point with the loss contours generically keeps every coefficient nonzero.
✓LASSO (L1) zeroes coefficients out exactly — automatic feature selection. Its diamond constraint region's corners sit on the axes, and those corners disproportionately catch the tangent point.
✓Elastic Net blends both penalties. It keeps LASSO's sparsity while avoiding its arbitrary, unstable pick among a group of correlated predictors.
✓The worked example: OLS gave unstable, even sign-flipped coefficients under collinearity; Ridge shrank all three smoothly; LASSO zeroed the redundant and noise predictors exactly.
⚠Standardize predictors before regularizing, and choose λ only by cross-validation. Both penalties are scale-dependent, and in-sample fit always favors λ=0 by construction.
⚠LASSO's selected variables are not a validated causal claim. Which predictor survives among a correlated group can flip across resamples — treat the selection as informative, not definitive.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. Geometrically, why does the L1 (LASSO) penalty tend to produce coefficients that are exactly zero, while the L2 (Ridge) penalty essentially never does?
The regularized solution is the point where the OLS loss's elliptical contours first touch the penalty's constraint region as it expands from the unconstrained OLS estimate. Ridge's L2 constraint region is a circle (or sphere), which is smooth everywhere — there's no special point on its boundary, so a generic tilted ellipse first touches it at some ordinary point on the edge, where every coefficient is generally nonzero. LASSO's L1 constraint region is a diamond, whose corners sit exactly on the coordinate axes (where one coefficient is exactly zero). Corners are sharp changes in the boundary's direction, and a wide range of ellipse shapes and orientations will first touch the diamond exactly at a corner rather than sliding onto a flat edge — so the tangent point disproportionately lands on an axis, forcing a coefficient to exactly zero.
Q2. Write the Ridge and LASSO objective functions and identify what changes between them.
Ridge: \(\arg\min_\beta \|y-X\beta\|^2 + \lambda\|\beta\|_2^2\), where \(\|\beta\|_2^2=\sum_j\beta_j^2\). LASSO: \(\arg\min_\beta \|y-X\beta\|^2 + \lambda\|\beta\|_1\), where \(\|\beta\|_1=\sum_j|\beta_j|\). The OLS loss term \(\|y-X\beta\|^2\) and the role of \(\lambda\) as the regularization-strength dial are identical in both; the only difference is whether the penalty sums squared coefficients (L2) or absolute coefficients (L1) — and that single change in penalty shape is what produces smooth shrinkage in one case and exact sparsity in the other.
Q3. In the worked example, why did plain OLS give β₂ = −1.3 (a negative coefficient) for a predictor (x₂) that has no true negative relationship with y at all?
Because x₁ and x₂ are highly correlated (near-duplicates of each other, ρ≈0.9), OLS cannot cleanly identify which one deserves credit for their shared signal — it has many combinations of β₁ and β₂ that fit the training sample nearly as well, and it picked one where β₁ is inflated well above the true 2.0 and β₂ compensates with a negative value, so their sum (3.4 − 1.3 = 2.1) stays close to correct even though neither individual coefficient is meaningful on its own. This is the classic multicollinearity symptom: individually unstable, even sign-flipped coefficients that still combine to a reasonable joint prediction.
Q4. What specific weakness of LASSO does Elastic Net address, and how?
LASSO tends to pick one predictor somewhat arbitrarily out of a group of strongly correlated, genuinely useful predictors, and zero out the rest — an unstable, close-to-coin-flip selection among the group. Elastic Net addresses this by blending the L1 penalty with an L2 component: \(\lambda(\rho\|\beta\|_1 + (1-\rho)\|\beta\|_2^2)\). The L2 piece discourages that all-or-nothing choice within a correlated group — the group tends to be kept together, shrunk, rather than one member surviving at full strength while the rest are deleted — while the L1 piece still zeroes out predictors that are genuinely uninformative.
Q5. True or false: as λ increases in Ridge regression, a coefficient will eventually be pushed to exactly zero, just as it would under LASSO.
False. Ridge's L2 penalty punishes squared coefficient size, which produces smooth, diminishing shrinkage — the optimal coefficient approaches zero as λ grows but, except in degenerate edge cases, never lands on it exactly, no matter how large λ gets. Only LASSO's L1 penalty has the corner-geometry property that regularly forces exact zeros; this is the defining structural difference between the two methods, not a matter of degree or how large λ is set.
Q6. Why must predictors be standardized (mean zero, unit variance) before fitting Ridge or LASSO, when it isn't strictly required for plain OLS?
Both penalties act directly on the numeric magnitude of each coefficient, and a coefficient's raw magnitude is inversely tied to its predictor's scale — a predictor measured in large units naturally needs a small coefficient to produce the same effect on y, and vice versa. Without standardizing, the penalty would shrink large-magnitude-coefficient predictors harder purely because of their arbitrary units, not because they're actually less important, distorting which predictors get shrunk or zeroed for reasons that have nothing to do with the underlying relationship. Plain OLS has no such issue because it has no penalty term whose size depends on the coefficients' scale.
