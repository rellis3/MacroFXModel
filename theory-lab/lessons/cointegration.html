Cointegration & Statistical Arbitrage

Two series can drift together for years with a 95% correlation and still have no stable relationship at all — cointegration is the specific, testable condition that tells you whether a spread has a home to come back to, or is just two random walks that happened to lean the same way.

CONCEPT — not yet tested in this repo ~16 min read Prereqs: Ornstein-Uhlenbeck & Mean Reversion

Why this matters

A huge amount of cross-asset reasoning in trading — "EUR/USD tracks the US-Germany yield spread," "AUD/USD tracks copper," "credit leads risk-vol" — implicitly assumes two series have a durable relationship worth trading against. The tool almost everyone reaches for first to check that assumption is a correlation coefficient. This lesson exists because that tool answers the wrong question. Correlation tells you whether two series' changes tend to move in the same direction. It says nothing about whether their levels are anchored to each other — whether, if the spread between them gets too wide, some force pulls it back. Two series can be highly correlated in returns and have a spread that wanders off to new extremes forever, never returning. Trading that spread as if it were mean-reverting is not a subtle mistake; it's betting real money on a coin flip you've mislabeled as a law of physics.

Cointegration is the formal statistical condition that separates "these two things happen to be trending together" from "these two things have an equilibrium relationship that reasserts itself." It is the mathematical backbone of pairs trading and, more broadly, of every claim in this repo's own notes that a cross-asset spread is tradeable as mean reversion rather than just narratively appealing. The previous lesson on Ornstein-Uhlenbeck mean reversion described how to model and trade a single stationary, mean-reverting series once you have one. This lesson is about the step before that: how do you know two non-stationary price series combine into a genuinely stationary, mean-reverting spread in the first place, rather than assuming it because a chart looked convincing?

Plain-English intuition

Two drunks and a dog on a leash

A well-known analogy (attributed to Michael Murray's 1994 teaching note "A Drunk and Her Dog") makes the distinction concrete. Imagine a drunk person wandering randomly — each step in an unpredictable direction, with no destination. That's a random walk: at any point in time you genuinely cannot predict which way they'll go next, and over a long enough walk they could end up arbitrarily far from where they started. Now imagine a second drunk, wandering independently, on a different street. Also a random walk. If you happened to record both of their positions over an evening, you might well see the two paths look similar in the sense that both drift generally further from their starting points as time passes — plot "distance traveled" for each and the two lines can look statistically related even though the two people have never met and are doing nothing but stumbling independently. That's the spurious-correlation trap.

Now change the picture: it's the same one drunk, but she has a dog on a leash. The drunk still wanders unpredictably — still a random walk on her own. The dog also wanders unpredictably — also a random walk on its own. But the distance between them is bounded by the leash. It can stretch and slacken, but it cannot grow without limit, because there's an actual mechanical constraint pulling them back together. The drunk's position and the dog's position are each individually unpredictable and non-stationary — but the gap between them is stationary: it has a stable average length (the leash's slack) and it reverts toward that average whenever it stretches too far. That gap is what cointegration is about. The two prices don't have to be individually predictable, and they don't even need identical short-term moves. What matters is whether the specific combination of them — their spread — is tied to something, the way the dog is tied to the leash.

The math, step by step

This section builds up four ideas in order: what "stationary" means, why a non-stationary series creates the spurious-regression trap, what cointegration formally is, and the two-step test (Engle-Granger) for detecting it — plus a conceptual note on its multi-series generalization (Johansen).

1. Stationarity. A time series \(X_t\) is (weakly, or "covariance") stationary if three statistical properties are constant through time — they don't depend on which point in time \(t\) you're looking at, only on how far apart in time two observations are:

\[ E[X_t] = \mu, \qquad \mathrm{Var}(X_t) = \sigma^2, \qquad \mathrm{Cov}(X_t, X_{t+k}) = \gamma(k) \]

In words: the series has one fixed long-run mean \(\mu\) it fluctuates around, one fixed variance \(\sigma^2\) describing how far it typically strays from that mean, and its autocovariance \(\gamma(k)\) — how related an observation is to the one \(k\) periods later — depends only on the lag \(k\), never on the calendar date. Practically: a stationary series has a meaningful "normal level." You can compute a mean or a z-score against it and expect that number to still mean something next year. A daily interest-rate level is usually not stationary (it drifts for years); a well-specified mean-reverting spread, or most return series, typically are (or are close enough, in practice, to be treated that way).

2. The unit root — why a random walk is not stationary. The simplest non-stationary model is the random walk: each step is the previous value plus an unpredictable, independent shock \(\epsilon_t\) (mean zero, constant variance \(\sigma^2\), uncorrelated across time):

\[ X_t = X_{t-1} + \epsilon_t, \qquad \epsilon_t \sim \text{i.i.d.}(0,\sigma^2) \]

Unroll this recursively back to a starting point \(X_0\) and \(X_t\) is just the sum of every shock that's happened since:

\[ X_t = X_0 + \sum_{i=1}^{t}\epsilon_i \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mathrm{Var}(X_t) = t\,\sigma^2 \]

That last step follows because the shocks are independent, so variances of a sum of independent things just add: \(t\) shocks, each contributing variance \(\sigma^2\), gives total variance \(t\sigma^2\). The variance grows linearly with \(t\) — it is not a fixed constant, so the first requirement of stationarity (constant variance) is already violated. There is no fixed "normal level" for a random walk to revert to; the further out in time you look, the wider the range of plausible values becomes, without bound. This is exactly the behavior most raw asset price levels — an FX rate, a bond yield, an equity index — are usually treated as having (an unpredictable, roughly additive increment each period), which is why price levels themselves are generally treated as non-stationary.

This is usually written more generally as an autoregressive model, \(X_t = \phi X_{t-1} + \epsilon_t\). If \(|\phi| < 1\), shocks decay away over time and the series is stationary — it has a fixed mean it keeps returning to (this is the AR(1) case underlying the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process from the previous lesson). If \(\phi = 1\) exactly, you get the random walk above: shocks never decay, they accumulate forever. The name "unit root" comes from writing the model as \((1 - \phi L)X_t = \epsilon_t\) (where \(L\) is the lag operator, \(LX_t = X_{t-1}\)) and noting the characteristic equation \(1-\phi z = 0\) has root \(z = 1/\phi\); when \(\phi=1\) that root equals exactly 1 — a "unit" root. A series requiring one round of differencing (\(\Delta X_t = X_t - X_{t-1}\)) to become stationary is called integrated of order 1, written \(I(1)\); an already-stationary series is \(I(0)\). Most raw FX/rate/index levels are treated as \(I(1)\); their returns (differences) are typically \(I(0)\).

3. Spurious regression — Granger & Newbold (1974). Ordinary least squares (OLS) regression, and the classical machinery built on top of it (R², t-statistics, p-values), assumes the variables involved are stationary. Granger and Clive Newbold showed, first by simulation and later formalized rigorously by Peter Phillips (1986), what happens when you ignore that and regress one independent, unrelated random walk on another in levels: you very often get a high R², a "significant"-looking t-statistic, and a regression that looks like a real relationship — despite the two series having absolutely nothing to do with each other. The reason is that the standard distribution theory behind those t-statistics and R² values is derived assuming stationary inputs; feed it two random walks instead and the t-statistics don't converge to the familiar bell-shaped distribution at all — they diverge as the sample grows, so a "significant" result becomes more, not less, likely with more data, which is the opposite of what a real relationship's evidence should do. Two unrelated random walks tend to look related purely because any random walk trends for stretches, and any two trending lines will show substantial apparent comovement over a shared window, entirely by chance. This single 1974 result is the reason "I regressed X on Y in levels and got R² = 0.90" is not, by itself, evidence of anything when X and Y are both non-stationary price-like series.

4. Correlation vs. cointegration — the crucial distinction. Correlation, as computed in a rolling-window Pearson coefficient, is normally applied to returns (differences) — it measures whether the short-run changes in two series tend to move together. Two series can have highly correlated returns for years — both generally rising, or both whipping around together during risk-off episodes — without their levels ever having a stable relationship at all. Cointegration is a statement about the levels, not the returns. Formally: if \(X_t\) and \(Y_t\) are each individually non-stationary (say, both \(I(1)\)), they are cointegrated if there exists some coefficient \(\beta\) such that the linear combination \(Y_t - \beta X_t\) is itself stationary — \(I(0)\). Two individually unpredictable, unbounded-variance series combine, in the right proportion, into something with a fixed mean and bounded variance — a "leash." That's a much stronger, much rarer condition than return correlation, because it requires an actual structural or economic force — arbitrage, an accounting identity, a shared driver that keeps the ratio in place — anchoring the two together, not just a shared trend over one particular sample window.

5. The Engle-Granger two-step method. Robert Engle and Clive Granger (1987) turned this into a concrete, testable procedure.

\(Y_t, X_t\)The two price/level series being tested, e.g. two FX pairs or a pair and a related macro series. Assumed individually non-stationary, \(I(1)\).
\(\alpha\)The regression intercept — the constant offset between the two series.
\(\beta\)The cointegrating coefficient — the hedge ratio: how many units of \(X\) offset one unit of \(Y\) in the long-run equilibrium.
\(\epsilon_t\)The regression error / residual — if \(X\) and \(Y\) are cointegrated, this is the equilibrium "spread," expected to be stationary.
\(\hat\alpha, \hat\beta, \hat\epsilon_t\)The OLS-estimated versions of the above, obtained from Step 1.
\(\Delta\)The first-difference operator: \(\Delta z_t = z_t - z_{t-1}\).
\(\gamma\)The coefficient in the Step 2 test regression on \(\hat\epsilon_{t-1}\) — its sign and significance is what the whole test hinges on.
\(I(0)\) / \(I(1)\)"Integrated of order 0/1" — already stationary, vs. requiring one differencing to become stationary.
\(H_0\)The null hypothesis being tested in Step 2: \(\gamma = 0\), i.e. "there is a unit root, the spread does not mean-revert, not cointegrated."

Step 1 — regress the levels. Run an ordinary OLS regression of one raw price/level series on the other, in levels (not returns):

\[ Y_t = \alpha + \beta X_t + \epsilon_t \]

This looks exactly like the setup Granger & Newbold warned about in point 3 — and by itself, it is just as vulnerable to a spuriously high R². The crucial move Engle and Granger made is not trusting this regression's R² or t-statistics at all. Instead they proved a separate, useful fact: if \(X_t\) and \(Y_t\) genuinely are cointegrated, OLS applied this way is "super-consistent" — the estimate \(\hat\beta\) converges to the true cointegrating coefficient unusually fast (at rate \(T\), the sample size, rather than the usual \(\sqrt{T}\) of ordinary regression) even though the classical standard errors around it are not reliable. In plain terms: this regression is good enough to get a usable estimate of the hedge ratio \(\beta\), but you cannot yet trust it as evidence that a relationship exists — that's what Step 2 is for.

Step 2 — test the residual for a unit root. Take the fitted residuals from Step 1 — the estimated spread on every day in the sample:

\[ \hat\epsilon_t = Y_t - \hat\alpha - \hat\beta X_t \]

and test this residual series itself for stationarity, using the Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) test. The conceptual idea, without deriving the full test statistic: regress the residual's change on its own lagged level, plus enough lagged differences to soak up any short-run autocorrelation,

\[ \Delta \hat\epsilon_t = \gamma\,\hat\epsilon_{t-1} + \sum_{i=1}^{p}\delta_i\,\Delta \hat\epsilon_{t-i} + u_t \]

and ask: does today's change in the spread depend on yesterday's level of the spread? If the spread mean-reverts, then when it's far above its long-run average, the next change should on average be negative (pulling it back down), and vice versa — that shows up as \(\gamma\) being reliably negative. If the spread is really just a random walk with no memory of a "correct" level, its change should be unrelated to its current level, and \(\gamma\) should be statistically indistinguishable from zero. The test is:

\[ H_0: \gamma = 0 \ \text{(unit root — not cointegrated)} \qquad\text{vs.}\qquad H_1: \gamma < 0 \ \text{(stationary — cointegrated)} \]

A wrinkle that matters in practice: because \(\hat\epsilon_t\) here is not an independently observed series but the leftover residual from a regression that already chose \(\hat\beta\) specifically to make the residual as small (and therefore as "well-behaved") as possible, the ordinary Dickey-Fuller critical values are too lenient — they'd falsely reject the unit-root null too often. Engle and Granger derived their own, stricter critical values for exactly this two-step situation (refined later by James MacKinnon's widely used response-surface tables), and any correct implementation of this test — including, for example, Python's statsmodels.tsa.stattools.coint — uses those adjusted critical values, not the plain ADF ones.

If the test statistic clears that stricter bar (i.e. \(\gamma\) is negative enough to reject \(H_0\)), you have real evidence that \(X_t\) and \(Y_t\) are cointegrated: the spread \(\hat\epsilon_t\) is stationary, has a genuine long-run mean, and behaves like the OU-process-like mean-reverting object covered in the previous lesson — something you can z-score, put bands around, and trade with an actual statistical basis for expecting reversion, rather than a chart that happens to look mean-reverting over one convenient window.

6. Johansen's test — generalizing beyond two series. Engle-Granger has two structural limitations once you move past a single pair: it forces you to arbitrarily pick which series is "\(Y\)" and which is "\(X\)" (regressing \(Y\) on \(X\) is not the same regression as \(X\) on \(Y\) in finite samples, so the choice matters and there's no principled way to make it for more than two series), and it can only ever test for one cointegrating relationship at a time. A basket of three or more series (say, a set of commodity-bloc currencies, or a term structure of multiple yields) can have more than one independent equilibrium relationship living inside it simultaneously. Søren Johansen (1988, 1991) built the proper generalization: instead of a single OLS regression, it works from a Vector Autoregression (VAR) fit to the whole basket of series jointly, rewritten in a form called a Vector Error Correction Model (VECM) that separates short-run dynamics from a matrix \(\Pi\) governing the long-run levels relationship. The rank of that matrix — how many of its eigenvalues are meaningfully different from zero, assessed via the eigenvalues of a matrix built from canonical correlations between the levels and their differences — tells you exactly how many independent stationary linear combinations (cointegrating vectors) exist among the whole basket, via two standard test statistics (the "trace" test and the "maximum eigenvalue" test). You don't need to derive the eigenvalue algebra to use the result correctly: Johansen's test answers "given this whole group of series, how many genuine long-run equilibria are hiding inside it, and what are they," symmetrically across all series at once, instead of Engle-Granger's asymmetric, one-pair-at-a- time answer.

Worked example

AUD/USD and NZD/USD — telling a real spread from a shared trend

AUD/USD and NZD/USD are two commodity-bloc currency pairs that are routinely observed to be highly correlated — both currencies are exposed to similar drivers (Chinese demand, global risk sentiment, commodity export revenue, similar central-bank cycles). Suppose you take two years of daily closing levels for both and run the Engle-Granger procedure.

Step 1. Regress \(\text{AUDUSD}_t = \alpha + \beta\, \text{NZDUSD}_t + \epsilon_t\) in levels. Because both series have genuinely trended together for long stretches of the sample, this regression will very plausibly return something like \(\hat\beta \approx 1.05\)–\(1.15\) and a striking \(R^2\) north of 0.85–0.90. On its own, per Granger & Newbold, that R² is not yet meaningful — it's exactly the number two correlated-but-not-necessarily- cointegrated random walks would tend to produce.

Step 2. Compute the residual spread on every day, \(\hat\epsilon_t = \text{AUDUSD}_t - \hat\alpha - \hat\beta\, \text{NZDUSD}_t\), and look at it as its own time series. This is the step that actually discriminates between the two possible worlds:

World A — not cointegrated. The residual spread drifts to a new multi-month high in year one, sits there for months, then drifts to a new low in year two, never really returning to its early level. Run the ADF test on it and \(\gamma\) comes out close to zero and statistically indistinguishable from it — you fail to reject the unit-root null. The two currencies' high R² in Step 1 was driven by both trending against a common third factor over that particular two-year window (say, broad USD strength through most of it) rather than any structural link tying their ratio to each other. Trading this spread as mean-reverting would mean betting on a leash that, this test says, isn't actually attached to anything.

World B — cointegrated. The residual spread, instead, oscillates in a visibly bounded band — it drifts a few standard deviations away from its average every few weeks and then comes back, repeatedly, throughout the whole two years, never establishing a new permanent level. The ADF statistic on \(\hat\epsilon_t\) is negative enough to clear the (stricter, Engle-Granger) critical value, rejecting the unit-root null. Now the spread is a legitimate stationary, mean-reverting object — you could take its rolling mean and standard deviation, form a z-score, and treat it with exactly the OU-process toolkit from the previous lesson: enter when \(|z|\) exceeds some threshold betting on reversion toward zero, size the trade using the spread's estimated half-life, exit near the mean.

The point of walking through both outcomes is that you cannot tell which world you're in from the Step 1 R² or from eyeballing a correlated-looking chart — that's precisely the trap this lesson opened with. The ADF test on the residual is the step that does the actual discriminating, and this example is illustrative of the procedure, not a reported result — running it for real on live AUD/USD and NZD/USD data (or any other candidate pair) is exactly the kind of check that would need to happen before treating any specific pair as tradeable.

How practitioners actually use this

Pairs trading is the flagship application: find two (or more) instruments with an economically sensible link — two share classes of the same company, an ETF and its underlying basket, two similar commodity producers, related sovereign bonds — test the spread for cointegration rather than assuming it from correlation or a plausible story, and if it passes, trade the z-scored spread as a market-neutral position (long the relatively cheap leg, short the relatively expensive one, sized by the cointegrating \(\beta\)). Gatev, Goetzmann & Rouwenhorst's widely cited 1998/2006 study documented this approach's historical performance in US equities using a simpler distance-based pair-selection method, and the more rigorous cointegration-based version became standard in quant desks through the 2000s.

Index arbitrage and ETF/underlying spreads use the same logic where the cointegrating relationship is close to mechanically guaranteed (an ETF's price is contractually tied to its basket's NAV), making the "is it really cointegrated" question closer to a formality — the interesting part becomes the speed and cost of executing the arbitrage before it closes on its own, not whether the relationship is real.

Macro and cross-asset relative value uses cointegration on genuinely non-obvious pairs where the underlying economic link is a hypothesis, not a guarantee — a currency against a commodity it's correlated to by export exposure, a corporate bond spread against an equity volatility index, one country's yield curve against another's. Here the test is doing real work, distinguishing a durable structural link from two series that have simply been trending together for unrelated reasons over the sample you happened to look at — exactly the distinction this lesson has been building toward.

Error-correction models are the natural next step once cointegration is established: rather than modeling each series' short-run changes independently, an Error Correction Model regresses each series' change partly on how far the previous period's spread was from equilibrium — the same \(\hat\epsilon_{t-1}\) term from Step 2 — formalizing "when the spread is stretched, expect a correction" as an actual forecasting equation rather than just a trading rule layered on top.

Common pitfalls

Where cointegration testing quietly goes wrong

Re-estimating \(\beta\) with data you wouldn't have had yet. The cointegrating coefficient in Step 1 must be estimated only on data available up to the point you'd actually be trading — fitting it once on the full historical sample (including the future relative to any earlier trade) is lookahead, and it will make every historical spread look tighter and more mean-reverting than it would have looked live. Worse, \(\beta\) itself is not guaranteed to be stable — a genuinely cointegrating relationship can still see its hedge ratio drift over time, which is exactly the problem the Kalman Filter lesson's adaptive, time-varying-coefficient approach exists to handle.

Structural breaks silently killing a relationship. A pair can be genuinely cointegrated for years and then permanently stop being so — a currency peg removed, a company delisted or merged, a commodity-currency's export mix shifting. A cointegration test run once on the full historical sample can pass while masking a break that already ended the relationship going forward; the same regime- conditioning discipline the macro-deep-dives-notes.md yield-spread lesson calls "mandatory" for lead-lag relationships applies equally here — test on rolling or recent windows, not just the full history.

Data-mining a large universe of candidate pairs. Testing cointegration across every pair in a large basket (all FX crosses × macro series, say) and keeping only the ones that pass at the 5% significance level is a textbook multiple-comparisons problem — by construction, roughly 1 in 20 genuinely unrelated pairs will pass a 5% test purely by chance. See this repo's own Multiple Testing & the Look-Elsewhere Effect lesson: the number of pairs tested, and a correspondingly stricter significance bar, has to be reported alongside any list of "cointegrated pairs found," not just the survivors.

Engle-Granger's asymmetry. Regressing \(Y\) on \(X\) versus \(X\) on \(Y\) is not guaranteed to give the same residual, the same estimated \(\beta\), or even the same conclusion about cointegration in a finite sample — OLS only minimizes error in one direction. This is a real limitation of the two-step method for more than two series (part of why Johansen exists), and even for a single pair it's worth being aware the choice of dependent variable is not neutral.

A stationary-looking residual is not automatically a tradeable edge. Even a genuinely cointegrated spread can mean-revert so slowly (a very long half-life) that round-trip costs, funding/carry, and slippage eat the edge before reversion completes. And exactly like every other strategy in this repo, an in-sample-passing cointegration test is not itself evidence of a trading edge — it only clears the first bar (a real statistical relationship exists). It still has to clear this repo's full honest-harness discipline — realistic fills, real costs, a true out-of-sample split, ≥30 OOS trades — before being treated as validated, per Lego Principle 5.

Self-test

Q1. Why can two completely unrelated random walks show a high R² when you regress one on the other in levels?
Because the classical distribution theory behind R² and t-statistics assumes stationary inputs, and a random walk isn't stationary — its variance grows with time and it has no fixed mean to anchor to. Granger & Newbold (1974) showed, and Phillips (1986) later proved formally, that regressing one independent random walk on another produces t-statistics that don't converge to the usual distribution at all — they diverge as the sample grows, so "significant"-looking results become more likely with more data, the opposite of a real relationship's behavior. Any two series that each drift for stretches of time will tend to show substantial apparent comovement over a shared window purely by chance, with nothing connecting them.
Q2. What does it formally mean for a linear combination of two non-stationary series to be "stationary," and why does that condition get the name cointegration?
If \(X_t\) and \(Y_t\) are each individually non-stationary (e.g. both \(I(1)\) — each requires one differencing to become stationary), they are cointegrated if there exists a coefficient \(\beta\) such that \(Y_t - \beta X_t\) is itself stationary (\(I(0)\)) — it has a fixed mean and bounded variance even though \(X_t\) and \(Y_t\) individually do not. The name reflects that: the two series are each "integrated" (non-stationary) on their own, but move together closely enough in the right proportion that a specific combination of them cancels out the non-stationarity — they are integrated together, in a way that removes the unit root from the combination even though neither series loses it on its own.
Q3. Why does cointegration matter more than simple return correlation for building a pairs trade?
A pairs trade bets on the spread between two prices reverting to a stable level — that only makes statistical sense if the spread actually has a stable level to revert to, which is exactly what cointegration tests for. Return correlation only tells you the two series' short-term changes tend to move together; two series can have highly correlated returns for years while their levels drift apart without limit (both trending the same direction due to a shared but unrelated driver), leaving no equilibrium for a pairs trade to bet on. Trading a merely-correlated (not cointegrated) spread as if it were mean-reverting is betting on a relationship that has no mechanism pulling it back together.
Q4. In the Engle-Granger method, why is the Step 2 ADF test run on the regression residuals with adjusted (Engle-Granger/MacKinnon) critical values, rather than the standard Dickey-Fuller critical values?
Because the residual series being tested isn't an independently observed series — it's the leftover error from a Step 1 OLS regression that already chose \(\hat\beta\) specifically to make that residual as small (and therefore as well-behaved-looking) as possible. That built-in bias toward a "nicer" residual means the ordinary Dickey-Fuller critical values would reject the unit-root null too easily — falsely finding cointegration more often than the true 5% rate implies. Engle and Granger derived stricter critical values specific to this two-step situation (later refined by MacKinnon's response-surface tables) to correct for that, and correct implementations of the test — e.g. statsmodels's coint function — use those adjusted values.
Q5. What extra capability does Johansen's test give you over Engle-Granger for a basket of more than two series?
Two things Engle-Granger structurally can't do for a basket. First, Engle-Granger forces an arbitrary choice of which series is the regression's dependent variable, and that choice isn't neutral in finite samples — OLS only minimizes residuals in one direction, so regressing Y on X can give a different answer than X on Y. Johansen works symmetrically across the whole basket via a jointly-fit VAR/VECM, with no dependent-variable choice needed. Second, Engle-Granger can only ever test for one cointegrating relationship at a time; Johansen estimates the full rank of the system — via eigenvalues of a matrix built from canonical correlations between the levels and their differences — telling you how many independent cointegrating relationships exist among the whole basket simultaneously, which matters because a group of three or more series can easily have more than one genuine equilibrium relationship living inside it.
Q6. Why isn't a high in-sample R² from Engle-Granger's Step 1 regression, by itself, evidence of cointegration?
Because Step 1 is exactly the levels-on-levels regression Granger & Newbold's spurious-regression result warns about — two non-stationary series can produce a high R² there purely because both happened to trend over the sample window, with nothing structurally tying them together. Engle and Granger's actual insight was narrower: if the two series truly are cointegrated, this regression happens to give a "super-consistent" estimate of the hedge ratio \(\beta\) — but that consistency result doesn't validate the R² or t-statistics as evidence a relationship exists. Only Step 2 — testing whether the residual spread is stationary via the ADF test — actually discriminates a real cointegrating relationship from a spurious one; the R² from Step 1 alone tells you nothing reliable either way.

Further reading

  • Clive W.J. Granger and Paul Newbold, "Spurious Regressions in Econometrics," Journal of Econometrics, 1974 — the original demonstration that regressing unrelated non-stationary series produces misleadingly significant results.
  • David A. Dickey and Wayne A. Fuller, "Distribution of the Estimators for Autoregressive Time Series with a Unit Root," Journal of the American Statistical Association, 1979 — the origin of the (later "augmented") unit-root test used in Engle-Granger's Step 2.
  • Robert F. Engle and Clive W.J. Granger, "Co-integration and Error Correction: Representation, Estimation, and Testing," Econometrica, 1987 — the paper defining cointegration formally and introducing the two-step method; this work contributed to Granger's share of the 2003 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences.
  • Søren Johansen, "Statistical Analysis of Cointegration Vectors," Journal of Economic Dynamics and Control, 1988, and "Estimation and Hypothesis Testing of Cointegration Vectors in Gaussian Vector Autoregressive Models," Econometrica, 1991 — the likelihood-based, multi-series generalization referenced conceptually above.
  • James G. MacKinnon, "Numerical Distribution Functions for Unit Root and Cointegration Tests," Journal of Applied Econometrics, 1996 — the practical response-surface critical values used by most software implementations of these tests.
  • Evan Gatev, William N. Goetzmann, and K. Geert Rouwenhorst, "Pairs Trading: Performance of a Relative-Value Arbitrage Rule," Review of Financial Studies, 2006 — the widely cited practitioner-facing empirical study of pairs-trading performance.
