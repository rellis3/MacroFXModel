Vega & the Volatility Surface, Told As Shape and Consequence

Every option is also a bet on "how much," not just "which way" — vega is the dollar size of that bet. Stretch it across every strike and expiry a market trades at once and a second fact appears: the market never agrees on one volatility number, and that whole grid of disagreement is the volatility surface. One practical fact anchors everything here — the underlying itself has exactly zero vega, so this Greek can only ever be hedged with other options.

The whole idea
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

The stock didn't move, but my option's price did. What happened?

The market changed its mind about how jumpy the stock will be. That's implied volatility, and how much your option cares about it is vega.

How much does it care?

Take a stock at a hundred, an at-the-money option, twenty percent vol, thirty days left. Each point of implied vol is worth about eleven cents on that option. Give it ninety days and it's about twenty cents. A year, about thirty-nine cents.

So longer options carry more vega.

It grows with the square root of time and never decays the way gamma and theta do. And for a fixed expiry it's biggest at the money, where the outcome is still a coin flip. Deep in or out of the money, the vol forecast barely matters.

Is it different for calls and puts?

No. At the same strike and expiry they have exactly the same vega. Direction doesn't come into it.

Can I hedge it by trading the stock?

Never. The stock itself has zero vega. The only thing that offsets an option's vega is another option.

And the volatility surface?

The market doesn't quote one volatility. It quotes one for every strike and every expiry. Across strikes, crash protection costs more: out-of-the-money puts trade at richer vol than the matching calls. Across expiries, vol jumps ahead of events. If a big announcement counts like three normal days in a five-day option, it carries about forty-three percent of that option's expected variance.

Why are the puts always richer?

October nineteen eighty-seven. Big investors had portfolio insurance: they copied a put by selling futures as the market fell. That copy had no vega and needed a smooth market. On the nineteenth the Dow fell twenty-two point six percent in a day, the copy fell behind, and the insurance failed. People with real puts gained on price and on vol. Crash protection has carried a premium ever since.

And the expiry axis?

March twenty twenty. The VIX closed at eighty-two point six nine, a record, and near-dated vol sat above longer-dated vol for weeks.

So the one-line version?

Options are a bet on how much, not just which way. Vega measures that bet, and only another option can hedge it.

Section 2

Why a call and a put share the exact same vega

Put-call parity — C − P = S − Ke⁻ʳᵀ — forces this, and vega's version of the argument is even cleaner than gamma's.

Δ

Delta — 1st derivative w.r.t. S, differs

∂(S−Ke⁻ʳᵀ)/∂S = 1, a nonzero constant. So Δcall − Δput = 1 — a genuine, permanent difference.

Γ

Gamma — 2nd derivative w.r.t. S, identical

The right side is linear in S, so its second derivative is exactly zero. Γcall − Γput = 0 — but it took two derivatives to kill the linear term.

ν

Vega — 1st derivative w.r.t. σ, identical immediately

σ doesn't appear in S−Ke⁻ʳᵀ at all — not even once. So ∂C/∂σ − ∂P/∂σ = 0 on the very first pass. νcall = νput, exactly, at any strike or expiry.

This is the flip side of why delta isn't shared: delta is parity's first derivative w.r.t. S, and that derivative is 1, not 0. Vega skips the whole argument gamma needed, because σ simply never enters the no-arbitrage relationship that defines parity in the first place.

Section 4

Vega peaks at-the-money, in a bell curve

Fixed 90-day expiry, S=100, σ=20%, r=2% — now hold time fixed and walk across strikes instead.

Vega falls off in both directions from the 100 strike — down to 10.11 at the 90 strike (comfortably in the money) and 13.68 at the 110 strike (out of the money). Note the asymmetry: 90 and 110 are equally far from spot in dollars, but not in d₁ terms — d₁ is driven by log-moneyness, not linear distance.

The intuition

A deep in-the-money option behaves almost like owning the underlying outright — its value barely depends on the vol forecast. A deep out-of-the-money option is worth close to nothing regardless of that forecast. The at-the-money strike is the one point where the outcome is genuinely uncertain — exactly where "how much will this move" matters most.

Section 6

Black-Scholes assumes one σ. Markets quote a grid.

A different implied volatility for essentially every (strike, expiry) pair, on the same underlying, same day — σ(K,T), the volatility surface. Two genuinely distinct axes.

Axis 1 — skew / smile

Across strikes, fixed expiry. An empirical, observed market fact — Black-Scholes' constant σ cannot produce a strike-dependent quantity at all. Has to be taken as market data, layered on top of the model.

Axis 2 — term structure

Across expiries, fixed moneyness. A shaped by variance averaging / dilution of event risk (and by vol mean-reverting to a long-run level) — near-dated vol spikes ahead of a known event, longer-dated vol stays comparatively smooth.

Neither axis is reproducible from a single constant σ — a one-parameter model has one degree of freedom, and the surface needs one per strike and a separate one per expiry. That's exactly why stochastic-volatility models (Heston, SABR) exist.

Section 7

The skew: a scar left by October 1987

A stylized equity-index implied volatility smile/skew
Before October 1987, index options traded close to a flat smile. After the crash, out-of-the-money puts have traded at a persistent implied-vol premium over out-of-the-money calls ever since, on essentially every major equity index (Rubinstein, "Implied Binomial Trees," 1994).
📉

Leverage effect

As an equity's price falls, its financial leverage ratio mechanically rises — which itself raises volatility. A real, negative price-volatility correlation that skew pricing reflects.

😨

"Crash-o-phobia"

The market permanently re-priced tail risk higher after 1987 taught it far-left-tail moves are more real than a lognormal model implies — protection stays structurally bid.

Neither story is derived from Black-Scholes

Both are color for why the skew persists, read off what the market actually charges — not proven from first principles.

Section 8

The term structure: event spikes as an averaging effect

Treat variance as roughly additive across trading days. An FOMC decision carries far more expected variance than an ordinary day.

Near-dated: 5 trading days remain

If the event day is expected to contribute variance equivalent to three ordinary days, that one day accounts for roughly 43% (3 of 7 day-equivalents) of the whole period's expected variance. Near-dated implied vol reflects it directly.

Far-dated: 60 trading days out

The same event contributes only a small fraction of that period's total expected variance — the other ~59 ordinary days swamp it, an averaging effect that leaves it comparatively smooth.

That's why term-structure "humps" right around a known event date collapse quickly once the event passes and that expiry is no longer the near-dated one carrying it.

Section 9

The underlying itself has exactly zero vega

The single most important practical fact in this whole lesson.

Delta & gamma

Both hedge cleanly with the underlying — buy or sell shares, futures, or spot FX to flatten the exposure. Fast, liquid, cheap.

Vega

The underlying has no time value and no dependence on σ at all — its vega is exactly zero, not small. No amount of trading it can ever offset a book's vega, not partially, not approximately. Only other options can.

Owning a plain share is like owning a plain photograph — it has no opinion about how shaky the camera was, only about where the picture ended up. An option is a bet on how shaky the camera will be. No amount of trading plain photographs hedges a bet about shakiness.

This is why a vega-exposed desk reaches for a completely different toolkit than a delta-exposed one: calendar spreads, strangles, straddles — combinations designed to isolate vega — because the underlying structurally can't do the job. The cross-Greeks (Vanna, and Charm for delta's time decay) get the full treatment next lesson.

Building the dollar VegaExposure formula, step by step

νₖ × OIₖ × 100 looks arbitrary until you build it up. Step through it.

Try it
νₖ = ν / 100
Raw vega ν = Sφ(d₁)√T is a dollar figure per 1.00 change in σ (100 percentage points). Implied vol moves in vol points (1 percentage point), so divide by 100 to rescale: νₖ is vega per vol point, per share (the "per vol pt" figure used in the table).
νₖ × 100 = ν
Multiply by the standard 100-share contract multiplier and it numerically undoes the /100 rescale: per contract, per vol point, the exposure is just the raw ν. Vega is already denominated in dollars — unlike gamma, which starts as a unit-less "per $1 move" number, so there's no S² rescale needed here at all.
νₖ × OIₖ × 100
Aggregate over the open interest sitting at that strike: the dollar P&L change, for the OI at strike k, from a 1-vol-point move in implied volatility.
As with dealer GEX, this number still needs an assumed long/short sign to say whether a rising or falling vol environment helps or hurts the dealer at that strike — that assumption is layered on separately, not baked into the raw formula.

The full lesson's claim, and what's actually there

The full lesson's meta badge reads "CONCEPT — not built or tested in this repo." That's mostly still true, but not entirely. js/gammaGreeks.js implements Black-Scholes bsVanna and bsCharm as their own closed forms — but there is no standalone vega function (∂C/∂σ = Sφ(d₁)√T) anywhere in the codebase, and no dollar-VegaExposure aggregation (νₖ×OIₖ×100) analogous to the GEX machinery in gammaGreeks.js / fullBookGex.js. On that half, the badge is accurate.

The volatility-surface half is a different story: js/impliedVolCore.js loads a real, static historical CME CVOL dataset — ATM level, skew, up/down variance, and convexity per FX pair (EURUSD, GBPUSD, USDJPY, AUDUSD, USDCAD, USDCHF, XAUUSD) — plus a second CBOE-sourced series (GVZ/VXN) as a cross-check. Its functions (loadCvolSeries, computeVRPSeries, alignCvolToBars, crossCheckSeries) are exercised by a passing test suite via fxVolCarryEngine.test.mjs, feeding a real variance-risk-premium gate in js/fxVolCarryEngine.js. It's a single 30-day tenor per instrument, not a full multi-expiry (K,T) grid or a per-strike smile — so "the volatility surface" in the strict sense still isn't built — but "implied vol / skew data is not built or tested in this repo" is no longer accurate.

Section 13 · It actually happened

October 1987: insurance that had no vega

It actually happened

"Portfolio insurance" replicated puts by selling index futures as prices fell: a delta hedge with zero vega. On 19 October 1987 the Dow fell 22.6% and the S&P 500 about 20% in one session; futures traded at a deep discount and prices gapped. The replication fell behind. Real puts gained on price and on implied vol. The skew has been there ever since. In March 2020 the other axis bent: the VIX closed at a record 82.69 on 16 March and the curve stayed inverted for weeks.

Section 14

Where the textbook breaks, and what this desk found

The tempting claim: an inverted term structure says something about the weeks ahead.

Desk test: held up vix-inversion: first day of VIX above VIX3M → next-5 range: SPX500 +0.76 ATR, NAS100 +0.82, USD/JPY +0.44, XAU/USD +0.43 (86 entries since 2008, all CIs clear of zero). Median inversion: 1 session.
Desk test: held up mv-vixterm-range: next 20 sessions +0.52 [0.38, 0.88] SPX500, +0.44 [0.26, 0.78] NQ, 14 firings, both halves. Direction null: 57% up vs an 80%+ control.
Where the textbook version breaks

One constant σ and a continuous path made a replicated put look like a real one. 1987 broke both. And vega is a parallel-shift number: in a crash the put wing moves far more than at-the-money. The skew itself is untested here as a forward signal.

Section 15

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Which piece of ν = Sφ(d₁)√T is responsible for vega growing as expiry lengthens, and never decaying?

Near the money, φ(d₁) stays close to its maximum across a wide range of T, so the entire time-dependence comes from √T — which never decays, unlike the 1/√T behind gamma or theta.
Question 2

Why is vega exactly the same for a call and a put at the same strike and expiry?

Differentiating C − P = S − Ke⁻ʳᵀ once with respect to σ gives zero on the right immediately, since none of S, K, or e⁻ʳᵀ depend on σ — an even more direct argument than gamma's, which needed a second derivative w.r.t. S.
Question 3

For a fixed expiry, which strike has the highest vega, and why?

φ(·) is the standard normal density, maximized at zero and falling off in both directions — vega peaks exactly where the outcome is genuinely uncertain and fades toward both deep ITM and deep OTM.
Question 4

What's the fundamental difference between the volatility surface's skew axis and its term-structure axis?

The strike-wise smirk has to be taken as observed market data — Black-Scholes' constant σ can't produce it. The expiry-wise term structure has a cleaner mechanical story: one high-variance event day is a large share of a short expiry's total expected variance and a small share of a long expiry's.
Question 5

A dealer has unwanted vega exposure at one strike. Why can't they flatten it by trading the underlying, the way they would for delta or gamma?

A share, future, or spot FX position has no time value and no dependence on σ — its vega is exactly zero, not small. The only positions with nonzero vega are other options, so vega can only be offset by trading other options, at any strike or expiry.
Your call
Scenario drill · 19 October 1987

Your fund's crash insurance is a trading plan

It is the weekend before Monday 19 October 1987, after a week of sharp falls in stocks. You oversee a pension fund whose equity holdings are covered by portfolio insurance: a program that replicates a put by selling stock-index futures as the market falls, tracking the put's delta. It holds no actual options. What do you do before Monday's open?

  1. Nothing. The program does exactly what a put would do, and it was built for a week like this.
    A replicated put is a delta hedge only. It has no vega and it needs a market smooth enough to keep re-hedging. If prices gap, the replication falls behind just when the protection matters, and every other insurer is trying to sell the same futures.
  2. Accept that you are only partly insured: cut the equity exposure directly, or buy listed index puts, even at a higher price.
    Real puts carry vega as well as delta, so they gain from the fall and from the jump in implied volatility, with no re-hedging needed. Cutting exposure removes the risk outright. Both cost something now; neither depends on a smooth path.
  3. Keep the program, but tell the traders to sell futures faster at the open.
    It addresses the lag, which is the right worry. But you would be racing every other insurer to sell the same futures into a falling market, which is how the futures came to trade at a deep discount.
  4. Switch the program off and buy more stocks: the fall is a bargain.
    That turns a partly hedged book into an unhedged, larger one on the eve of a crash. Whatever the long-run value, you have removed the protection the fund was paying for.

What happened. On 19 October 1987 the Dow fell 22.6% and the S&P 500 about 20% in a single session, still the largest one-day percentage falls in their history. Index futures traded at a deep discount to the stocks underneath them, and the Brady Commission found portfolio insurers' futures selling was a large part of the selling pressure that week (how much it caused the fall is still debated). The replication fell behind and the insurance failed when it was needed, while holders of actual puts gained from the fall and from the jump in implied volatility. Out-of-the-money index puts have carried an implied-vol premium ever since.

Sources: Federal Reserve History, "Stock Market Crash of 1987"; Report of the Presidential Task Force on Market Mechanisms (the Brady Commission), January 1988; Rubinstein, "Implied Binomial Trees", Journal of Finance, 1994.

02

Vega grows with √T over normal horizons, and peaks near the money — both fall straight out of the closed form.
