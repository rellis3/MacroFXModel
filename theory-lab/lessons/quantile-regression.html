Quantile Regression

OLS answers "what's the average outcome given these conditions?" Risk questions ask "what's the bad outcome given these conditions?" — a different question that needs a different loss function, not just a different confidence level.

CONCEPT — explains a conditional-quantile estimation technique; not built or used anywhere in this repo ~14 min read Prereqs: Regression Diagnostics (OLS mechanics assumed) and Cornish-Fisher Expansion (the contrasting static approach to the same question)
TL;DR
  • Ordinary least squares fits the conditional mean, \(E[Y\mid X]\) — the single fitted line that minimizes squared error. It has nothing to say about the 5th-percentile outcome given \(X\), only the average one.
  • Quantile regression (Koenker & Bassett, 1978) replaces squared-error loss with the asymmetric pinball loss \(\rho_\tau(u) = u(\tau - \mathbb{1}\{u<0\})\), and minimizing its expectation over \(q\) provably recovers the conditional \(\tau\)-th quantile of \(Y\) given \(X\), not the mean.
  • Fitting at \(\tau=0.05\) directly models the conditional 5th-percentile return given predictors — a direct route to a conditional VaR that Cornish-Fisher cannot offer, because CF only ever adjusts the unconditional distribution's own skew and kurtosis.
  • The central contrast: Cornish-Fisher is a static adjustment — same correction every day, driven by the unconditional sample skew/kurtosis. Quantile regression is conditional — the fitted quantile moves with the predictors, directly capturing changing risk conditions (e.g. today's realized volatility) that a static moment-based correction cannot see.
  • The worked toy example below fits real quantile regression lines at \(\tau=0.1\) and \(\tau=0.9\) alongside an OLS mean line on the same heteroskedastic scatter — the two quantile lines have genuinely different slopes than the mean line and visibly fan apart as the spread widens, exactly what a single mean-regression line can't show.
Section 01

Why this matters

Regression Diagnostics starts from a fact worth repeating here: ordinary least squares gives a point estimate \(\hat\beta\) obtained purely by minimizing the sum of squared residuals, with no distributional assumption required for that minimization itself. What that lesson doesn't dwell on — because it's answering a different question — is what, exactly, OLS's fitted line represents. The answer is the conditional mean: \(\hat{y} = x^\top\hat\beta\) is an estimate of \(E[Y\mid X=x]\), the average value of \(Y\) you'd expect given \(X\). That's the right quantity for a huge range of questions — "what's the expected return," "what's the expected hedge ratio." It is exactly the wrong quantity for a risk question. "What's the 5th-percentile return given today's volatility regime" is not a question about the average outcome at all; it's a question about one specific tail quantile of the conditional distribution, and squared-error loss was never built to answer it. Quantile regression exists because the fix isn't a different confidence interval bolted onto the mean — it's a different loss function that targets the quantile directly.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Punishing a guess unevenly is what makes it aim for something other than the middle

Imagine you have to guess a number, and you're told the penalty for guessing too low is nineteen times worse than the penalty for guessing too high. What number should you guess? Not the average of the possible outcomes — because the penalty isn't symmetric around the average, the penalty-minimizing guess shifts upward, toward a number high enough that you're "too low" only rarely. Push that logic all the way through and the guess that minimizes your expected penalty turns out to be a specific point: the value below which only 5% of the actual outcomes fall — the 95th percentile. Ordinary least squares is the special, perfectly symmetric case of this story: guessing too high and guessing too low are penalized identically (squared error doesn't care about direction), and the penalty-minimizing guess under that symmetric penalty is the plain average. Quantile regression is exactly the asymmetric version of the same story, generalized to a full regression: instead of a flat penalty split 50/50 between over- and under-guessing, you choose a split — say 5% versus 95% — and the fitted line that minimizes that lopsided penalty is no longer the conditional mean, it's a specific conditional quantile. Nothing about the mechanics of "fit a line that minimizes total penalty" changed; only the shape of the penalty did, and that shape alone is what determines whether you're targeting the middle of the distribution or one of its edges.

Section 02

The math, step by step

The pinball (check) loss function

Fix a target quantile level \(\tau \in (0,1)\) (e.g. \(\tau=0.05\) for the 5th percentile). For a candidate predicted value \(q\) and an actual outcome \(Y\), define the residual \(u = Y - q\) and the pinball loss:

\[ \rho_\tau(u) = u\big(\tau - \mathbb{1}\{u < 0\}\big) \]
uthe residual, Y − q (actual minus predicted)
τthe target quantile level, e.g. 0.05
𝟙{u<0}1 if the prediction overshot (q>Y), else 0

Unpacked, this is two different linear penalties depending on which side the error falls on:

u ≥ 0 → τ·u
Under-prediction
Actual exceeded the guess (q too low) — penalized at weight τ
u < 0 → (1−τ)·|u|
Over-prediction
Actual fell short of the guess (q too high) — penalized at weight 1−τ
τ = 0.5
The symmetric special case
Both sides weighted equally at 0.5 — this recovers median regression

For a small \(\tau\) like 0.05, over-prediction is penalized nineteen times more heavily than under-prediction (\(0.95\) vs. \(0.05\)). That lopsidedness is deliberate and is the entire mechanism — it's what pulls the loss-minimizing \(q\) down toward the low tail of the distribution rather than sitting at the center.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Pinball loss is a penalty that's deliberately unfair on purpose. If you're trying to guess the 5th-percentile mark, guessing too high (saying "it won't get that bad") gets punished way harder than guessing too low (being overly cautious). Because the punishment is so lopsided, the smartest strategy shifts away from your normal best-guess average and creeps down toward the pessimistic, cautious end — exactly where a 5th-percentile answer should live.

Why minimizing expected pinball loss recovers exactly the τ-th quantile

Let \(F\) be the cumulative distribution function of \(Y\). Consider choosing the single number \(q\) that minimizes \(E[\rho_\tau(Y-q)]\) — the same kind of first-order argument that shows the mean minimizes expected squared error, applied to this different, asymmetric loss instead.

1

Split the expectation at q

Separate the expectation into the "over" region (\(Y \ge q\)) and the "under" region (\(Y < q\)):

\[ E[\rho_\tau(Y-q)] = \tau\, E\big[(Y-q)\mathbb{1}\{Y\ge q\}\big] + (1-\tau)\, E\big[(q-Y)\mathbb{1}\{Y< q\}\big] \]
2

Differentiate with respect to q

Each term's derivative with respect to \(q\) comes from how the boundary of its indicator region moves as \(q\) moves, giving:

\[ \frac{d}{dq}E[\rho_\tau(Y-q)] = -\tau\big(1-F(q)\big) + (1-\tau)F(q) \]
3

Set the derivative to zero and simplify

\[ -\tau + \tau F(q) + F(q) - \tau F(q) = 0 \ \implies\ F(q) = \tau \]
4

Read off the result

The loss-minimizing \(q\) satisfies \(F(q)=\tau\), i.e. \(q = F^{-1}(\tau)\) — by definition, exactly the \(\tau\)-th quantile of \(Y\). Nothing about \(F\) besides this one equation was used. The asymmetric weights \(\tau\) and \(1-\tau\) are precisely what make the balance point land at the \(\tau\)-quantile instead of the mean (\(\tau=0.5\) gives \(F(q)=0.5\), the median — the symmetric special case).

Linear quantile regression applies this same loss conditionally: instead of a single unconditional \(q\), fit \(q_\tau(x) = x^\top\beta_\tau\) by choosing \(\beta_\tau\) to minimize the sample average of \(\rho_\tau(y_i - x_i^\top\beta_\tau)\) across observations. This is a linear program (the pinball loss is piecewise-linear in \(\beta_\tau\)), solvable exactly with standard linear-programming methods — the same Koenker & Bassett (1978) result, extended from a single number to a full regression line.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Ordinary regression draws one line that tries to run right through the middle of all the dots. Quantile regression instead draws a line that tries to have only 5% of the dots below it (for τ=0.05) or only 10% below it (for τ=0.1) — a line that hugs the bottom edge of the scatter instead of its center, using that same lopsided penalty from before to decide exactly where "the edge" should sit.

\(Y\)The response variable whose conditional quantile is being modeled.
\(X\)The predictor(s)/covariates the quantile is conditioned on.
\(\tau\)The target quantile level, \(\tau\in(0,1)\) — e.g. 0.05 for the 5th percentile.
\(q\)A candidate predicted value; \(u=Y-q\) is the residual against it.
\(\rho_\tau(u)\)The pinball (check) loss — asymmetric, weight \(\tau\) on under-prediction, \(1-\tau\) on over-prediction.
\(F(\cdot)\)The cumulative distribution function of \(Y\) (or of \(Y\mid X\) in the conditional case).
\(\beta_\tau\)The fitted linear quantile-regression coefficients for level \(\tau\) — one full coefficient vector per \(\tau\), generally different from \(\beta_{OLS}\).
\(q_\tau(x)=x^\top\beta_\tau\)The fitted conditional \(\tau\)-quantile of \(Y\) given \(X=x\).

The central contrast: static adjustment vs. conditional, predictor-driven quantile

Cornish-Fisher and quantile regression both target the same underlying question — "what's the bad-outcome threshold?" — from structurally different directions. Cornish-Fisher starts from a single fitted Gaussian VaR and nudges the quantile using the unconditional sample skewness and kurtosis: the same correction applies on every single day, regardless of what's happening in the market right now, because \(S\) and \(K\) are properties of the whole historical sample. Quantile regression instead fits \(q_\tau(x) = x^\top\beta_\tau\) directly against live predictors \(x\) — so the fitted quantile moves as \(x\) moves, capturing changing risk conditions (e.g. today's realized volatility, a regime indicator, a macro surprise) in a way a static, once-computed moment-based correction structurally cannot.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Cornish-Fisher is like a single umbrella-size recommendation printed once a year based on the average rainy season — you use the same umbrella every single day regardless of today's actual forecast. Quantile regression is like checking today's specific weather conditions and picking an umbrella size that adjusts on the spot — bigger on the days that actually look stormy, smaller on the days that look calm, instead of one fixed size forced onto every day alike.

Cornish-Fisher — static distributional adjustment

Corrects the Gaussian quantile using the return series's own unconditional skewness \(S\) and excess kurtosis \(K\). One adjustment, computed once from history, applied identically regardless of today's conditions. Cheap, and a drop-in change to an existing Gaussian VaR formula — but blind to anything that shifts risk day-to-day.

Quantile regression — conditional, predictor-driven

Fits the \(\tau\)-quantile directly as a function of live predictors, \(q_\tau(x)=x^\top\beta_\tau\). The estimated quantile shifts with \(x\) — a period of elevated realized volatility can push the fitted 5th-percentile return further out automatically, without anyone re-estimating a moment by hand. Costs more to build and validate (a real regression, with its own overfitting risk) than a two-number moment adjustment.

Worked example

Fitting OLS and two quantile regressions on the same toy dataset

40 synthetic \((x_i,y_i)\) points were generated with \(x\) spread over roughly 1 to 10 and \(y = 2 + 1.5x + \varepsilon\), where the noise \(\varepsilon\)'s scale grows with \(x\) — a deliberately heteroskedastic toy dataset. OLS was fit by minimizing squared error in closed form; both quantile regressions were fit by solving the linear program that minimizes the sample average of \(\rho_\tau(y_i - \beta_0-\beta_1 x_i)\) exactly, at \(\tau=0.1\) and \(\tau=0.9\).

1

The three fitted lines

OLS (conditional mean): \(\hat{y} = 2.172 + 1.464x\). Quantile regression, \(\tau=0.1\): \(\hat{q}_{0.1}(x) = 2.062 + 1.194x\). Quantile regression, \(\tau=0.9\): \(\hat{q}_{0.9}(x) = 2.395 + 1.658x\).

2

The spread at the low end of X

At \(x=1\): OLS predicts \(3.64\), \(\tau=0.1\) predicts \(3.26\), \(\tau=0.9\) predicts \(4.05\) — an 80%-interval width of about \(0.80\).

3

The spread at the high end of X

At \(x=10\): OLS predicts \(16.81\), \(\tau=0.1\) predicts \(14.00\), \(\tau=0.9\) predicts \(18.98\) — an 80%-interval width of about \(4.97\), roughly six times wider.

The width of the \(\tau=0.1\)–\(\tau=0.9\) band grew from about 0.80 at x=1 to about 4.97 at x=10 — a direct, quantitative read on how the conditional spread changes with X. The single OLS mean line cannot express this at all: it reports one number, the expected value, at every x, with no information about how much that expectation should be trusted to hold.

Real-world trading scenario

A conditional VaR that moves with the volatility regime, instead of a fixed moment-based adjustment

A risk desk currently reports a daily VaR using a Cornish-Fisher adjustment built from the FX pair's full-sample skewness and kurtosis — one fixed correction, applied identically whether today is a quiet range-bound session or a day sitting inside an elevated-volatility cluster. A quant on the desk instead fits a quantile regression of next-day return on today's realized volatility and a short-horizon momentum indicator, at \(\tau=0.05\). On an ordinary, low-volatility day, the fitted 5th-percentile line sits close to what the Cornish-Fisher number already implied — but during a volatility cluster, where today's realized vol is itself elevated, the quantile regression's fitted \(q_{0.05}(x)\) widens automatically, because the fitted coefficient on realized volatility pulls the tail quantile further out exactly when the predictor says conditions have changed. The Cornish-Fisher number, by construction, cannot do this — it only updates when someone re-estimates the sample's unconditional skew and kurtosis, typically on a slow, periodic basis, not day-to-day as conditions actually shift.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

  • Directly modeling conditional VaR. Fitting \(q_\tau(x)\) at, say, \(\tau=0.01\) or \(\tau=0.05\) against relevant predictors is a standard way to build a VaR estimate that responds to current conditions instead of relying on a fixed unconditional distribution shape.
  • A full set of τ values traces out a conditional distribution shape. Fitting the same predictors at many \(\tau\) values (e.g. 0.05, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 0.95) builds up a picture of the entire conditional distribution's shape — including how its spread and asymmetry themselves change with the predictors — without ever assuming a specific parametric family for that distribution.
  • Widely used outside finance too. Quantile regression is standard in econometrics, epidemiology, and ecology for exactly the same reason it matters here: whenever the relationship of interest isn't well summarized by a single average effect, because the spread or shape of the outcome changes across the predictor range.
  • Complements, rather than replaces, mean-based modeling. A desk reporting both an OLS conditional-mean forecast and a quantile-regression-based conditional tail estimate gets two genuinely different pieces of information from the same predictors — the expected outcome, and separately, how bad the outcome could plausibly get.
⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where quantile regression gets over-trusted

  • Quantile crossing. Because each \(\tau\) is fit as its own separate linear program, nothing in the basic method guarantees that \(\hat{q}_{0.1}(x) < \hat{q}_{0.5}(x) < \hat{q}_{0.9}(x)\) holds at every \(x\) — for some predictor values, especially near the edges of the observed data, the fitted lines for different \(\tau\) can actually cross, implying the nonsensical claim that a higher quantile predicts a lower value than a lower one. This is the direct structural cousin of Cornish-Fisher's own non-monotonicity failure: a real, documented limitation of the basic method, not an edge case to ignore. Various extensions (e.g. imposing monotonicity constraints directly in the fit) address it, but the basic linear quantile regression described here doesn't guarantee it on its own.
    🧒 Explain like I'm 5

    Imagine drawing three separate lines for "typically bad," "typical," and "typically great," each one fit completely independently. Nothing forces them to stay in the sensible order — occasionally, at the edges, the "typically bad" line can accidentally cross above the "typical" line, which would nonsensically claim a worse-case outcome is now higher than an average one. It's an honest side effect of fitting each line separately rather than as one coordinated system.

  • Extreme τ needs data in the tail to fit well. A quantile regression at \(\tau=0.01\) is, by definition, being pinned down mostly by the small fraction of observations near that extreme tail — a modest sample can leave that specific fitted line poorly determined even when the median (\(\tau=0.5\)) line is estimated comfortably from the same data.
  • A linear conditional-quantile model is still a linear model. Quantile regression changes the loss function, not the assumption that the quantile is a linear function of the predictors — a genuinely nonlinear relationship between predictors and the tail quantile will be misfit by a linear quantile regression just as a nonlinear conditional mean would be misfit by OLS.
  • More predictors, more overfitting surface. Unlike Cornish-Fisher's two-number moment adjustment, quantile regression is a real regression with real overfitting risk — more predictors and a small sample can produce a fitted tail quantile that looks sharp in-sample and degrades out-of-sample, the same validation discipline any regression needs.
Section 04

Key Takeaways

✓OLS fits the conditional mean; quantile regression fits a conditional quantile. The asymmetric pinball loss ρ_τ, not squared error, is what makes the loss-minimizing fit land on a specific quantile instead of the average.
✓The derivation is clean: minimizing E[ρ_τ(Y−q)] over q gives F(q)=τ exactly. The asymmetric τ vs. 1−τ weighting is the entire mechanism — no distributional assumption needed.
✓Cornish-Fisher is static; quantile regression is conditional. This is the lesson's central contrast — a CF adjustment is fixed from unconditional S and K, while a quantile-regression quantile moves with live predictors like today's realized volatility.
✓The toy example shows it concretely. The τ=0.1–τ=0.9 band width grew roughly sixfold from x=1 to x=10 — information a single OLS mean line structurally cannot carry.
✗Quantile crossing is a real limitation, not an edge case. Separately-fit quantile lines aren't guaranteed to stay correctly ordered at every predictor value — the direct structural cousin of Cornish-Fisher's own non-monotonicity problem.
⚠This lesson is conceptual. It explains the technique and its worked toy example precisely; it is not a claim that quantile regression is fitted or used anywhere in this project.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. What quantity does OLS estimate, and what quantity does quantile regression at τ=0.05 estimate — in your own words?
OLS estimates the conditional mean, \(E[Y\mid X]\) — the average outcome given the predictors. Quantile regression at \(\tau=0.05\) estimates the conditional 5th-percentile of \(Y\) given \(X\) — the outcome level below which only 5% of the conditional distribution falls, a genuinely different quantity from the average.
Q2. In the pinball loss ρ_τ(u) = u(τ − 𝟙{u<0}), what happens to the relative penalty on over- vs. under-prediction as τ gets small (e.g. τ=0.05)?
Under-prediction (the actual value exceeds the guess) is penalized at weight τ, which shrinks toward zero as τ→0. Over-prediction (the guess exceeds the actual value) is penalized at weight 1−τ, which grows toward 1. So for a small τ, over-predicting is punished far more heavily than under-predicting — at τ=0.05, nineteen times more heavily — which pulls the loss-minimizing prediction down toward the low tail of the distribution.
Q3. Walk through, in words, why setting the derivative of E[ρ_τ(Y−q)] to zero produces F(q)=τ.
Splitting the expectation into the region where Y≥q (weighted τ) and Y<q (weighted 1−τ) and differentiating with respect to q gives −τ(1−F(q)) + (1−τ)F(q). Expanding and simplifying, the τ·F(q) terms cancel, leaving −τ + F(q) = 0, i.e. F(q) = τ. Because F is the CDF, the q satisfying F(q)=τ is by definition the τ-th quantile — so the asymmetric weighting directly forces the loss-minimizing q to be that specific quantile rather than the mean.
Q4. State the lesson's central contrast between Cornish-Fisher and quantile regression in one or two sentences.
Cornish-Fisher is a static adjustment: it corrects a Gaussian quantile using the unconditional sample skewness and kurtosis of the whole historical return series, applying the identical correction regardless of today's conditions. Quantile regression is conditional: it fits the target quantile as a function of live predictors, so the estimated quantile moves as those predictors move, directly capturing changing risk conditions that a static, once-computed moment adjustment cannot see.
Q5. In the worked toy example, why does the gap between the τ=0.1 and τ=0.9 lines widen from x=1 to x=10, and why can't the OLS line show this?
The synthetic data was built with noise whose scale grows with x (deliberate heteroskedasticity), so the true conditional spread of Y genuinely widens as x increases — and each quantile regression line is fit to track its own specific quantile of that widening spread, so the τ=0.1 and τ=0.9 lines separate as x grows. OLS fits a single conditional-mean line and reports only one number (the average) at every x — it has no mechanism to represent how much spread surrounds that average, since squared-error loss never distinguishes "the spread was wide" from "the spread was narrow but the mean guess was off."
Q6. What is quantile crossing, and why is it described in this lesson as the structural cousin of Cornish-Fisher's non-monotonicity problem?
Quantile crossing is when separately-fit quantile regression lines for different τ levels cross at some predictor value, implying a higher quantile predicts a lower value than a lower quantile does at that same x — a violation of what a valid quantile function must satisfy. It parallels Cornish-Fisher's non-monotonicity failure because both are cases where a technique meant to describe an ordered quantile structure can, under real conditions (extreme τ separation here, extreme skew/kurtosis there), produce an output that is no longer validly ordered — a genuine limitation of the basic method in both cases, not a hypothetical concern.
