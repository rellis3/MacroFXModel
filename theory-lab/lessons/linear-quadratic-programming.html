Linear & Quadratic Programming

The two specific, extremely well-studied families of convex problem that turn "portfolio construction with real-world constraints" from a research question into a solved, off-the-shelf computation.

CONCEPT — not built or tested in this repo ~16 min read Prereqs: Convex Optimization
TL;DR
  • A linear program (LP) minimizes (or maximizes) a linear objective subject to linear constraints: \(\min c^\top x\) s.t. \(Ax \le b\). A quadratic program (QP) is the same, but with a quadratic objective — and Markowitz mean-variance, \(\min w^\top\Sigma w\) s.t. \(w^\top\mu=\text{target},\ \sum w_i=1\), is exactly a QP.
  • An LP's optimum is always found at a vertex of the feasible region — a direct consequence of the convexity established in the previous lesson. The simplex method exploits this by walking from vertex to adjacent vertex, always improving the objective, until no neighboring vertex is better.
  • Markowitz's textbook closed-form solution (pure linear algebra — invert a matrix, done) only works for the equality-constrained version. The moment you add box constraints — no shorting (\(w_i\ge0\)), position caps (\(w_i\le0.1\)) — you don't know in advance which constraints will bind, and the clean formula breaks.
  • Real portfolio construction always has box constraints, which is exactly why practitioners hand the problem to a QP solver rather than plugging numbers into the closed-form Markowitz formula.
  • The worked example solves a tiny 2-variable LP by evaluating every vertex of its feasible region, and separately writes out a fully-specified 3-asset Markowitz QP with box constraints \(0\le w_i\le0.4\) — a solver's actual input, not something meant to be solved by hand.
Section 01

Why this matters

The previous lesson established that convex problems come with a powerful guarantee — local optimum equals global optimum — but that guarantee is abstract until it's attached to an actual, solvable family of problems. Linear and quadratic programming are that family: two of the most thoroughly studied, most reliably solvable classes of convex optimization problem in existence, with decades of dedicated solver engineering behind them. Anywhere a real trading or portfolio-management workflow needs "the best allocation subject to a list of real-world limits" — capital limits, position caps, no-shorting rules, factor exposure bands — the underlying math is almost always an LP or a QP, not a bespoke algorithm built from scratch.

Plain-English intuition

The best corner of the room, and the best point in a bowl-shaped bucket

Imagine a room whose walls are all flat (every constraint is a straight line or flat plane) — that's the feasible region of a linear program. If you're trying to maximize something that itself changes linearly as you move around the room — walk toward one wall and the quantity increases steadily, walk the other way and it decreases steadily, always at a constant rate — there is never a reason for the best point to be somewhere in the open middle of the floor. You can always do better by continuing to walk in the direction that's improving things, and because the room's walls are flat, that walk only ever gets stopped by a wall, and ultimately by a corner where two or more walls meet. That's the entire intuition behind why an LP's optimal answer is always found at a vertex, and why the classic solution method (the simplex method, below) works by hopping from corner to corner, never bothering to check the empty space in between.

A quadratic program is the same flat-walled room, but instead of a perfectly linear quantity you're optimizing a bowl-shaped one (a convex quadratic, from the previous lesson) — variance, say. Now the best point is not automatically forced to a corner: a bowl's true lowest point might sit comfortably in the open middle of the room, only getting pushed toward a wall if the bowl's own natural minimum happens to sit outside the room's boundary. That's the structural reason QPs need a genuinely different algorithm than "check the corners" — the answer could be anywhere, not just at a vertex.

Section 02

The math, step by step

Linear programming — standard form

A linear program minimizes a linear objective subject to linear inequality constraints (and, optionally, nonnegativity on the variables):

\[ \min_{x} \; c^\top x \quad \text{subject to} \quad Ax \le b, \quad x \ge 0 \]
xthe decision variables — what you're solving for
cthe objective's coefficients — c^Tx is the linear quantity being minimized (or, with a sign flip, maximized)
A, bthe constraint matrix and right-hand side — Ax≤b packs every linear limit into one matrix inequality

Everything in an LP — the objective and every constraint — is a straight line (in two dimensions) or a flat plane (in higher dimensions). The region of points satisfying every constraint simultaneously is a polytope — a many-dimensional generalization of a polygon, bounded by flat faces and sharp vertices, exactly the "flat-walled room" of the intuition above.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

A linear program is like a room with only flat walls, floor and ceiling — every rule you have to obey is a straight boundary. The shape of allowed answers, all squeezed together, is called a polytope: think of a diamond or a many-sided gem instead of a room with curved walls.

Quadratic programming — standard form

A quadratic program keeps the same linear constraints but replaces the objective with a quadratic (still convex, per the previous lesson, whenever its Hessian \(H\) is PSD):

\[ \min_{x} \; \tfrac{1}{2}x^\top H x + c^\top x \quad \text{subject to} \quad Ax \le b \]
Hthe (PSD) Hessian of the quadratic objective
c^Txan optional linear term, alongside the quadratic one
Ax≤bthe same flat, linear-constraint structure as an LP

Markowitz mean-variance is exactly this template with \(H = 2\Sigma\), no linear term, and the constraints \(w^\top\mu = R^\*\) (target return, an equality, which can be written as two opposing inequalities to fit the \(\le\) form) and \(\sum w_i = 1\) (budget) — the worked example below writes the full 3-asset version out explicitly, with box constraints added on top.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

A quadratic program is the same flat-walled room, but now you're trying to find the lowest point of a bowl sitting somewhere inside it, instead of walking toward whichever wall is best. The bowl's true bottom might comfortably sit in the middle of the room, not shoved against a wall — a genuinely different kind of search than an LP needs.

Why an LP's optimum always sits at a vertex

This follows directly from the convexity result proven in the previous lesson, applied to a linear objective over a polytope. A linear function has no curvature at all — it's convex and concave simultaneously (a flat plane satisfies the convexity inequality with equality, not just \(\le\)). Take any point in the feasible region that is not a vertex — it can always be written as a combination of two other feasible points further apart (walk in either direction along some line through it, inside the polytope, until you hit the boundary in both directions). Because the objective is linear, its value changes at a constant rate along that line — so it must be at least as good at one of the two endpoints as it is in the middle. Repeating this argument pushes any candidate optimum outward, along straight lines, until it can go no further — which happens exactly at a vertex, where multiple constraint boundaries meet and there's no direction left to walk without leaving the feasible region. The simplex method (George Dantzig, 1947) turns this fact into an algorithm: start at any vertex, look at each adjacent vertex (reachable by moving along a single edge of the polytope), move to whichever adjacent vertex improves the objective the most, and repeat. Because there are only finitely many vertices and each move strictly improves the objective (barring a technical edge case called degeneracy), the walk is guaranteed to terminate at the true optimal vertex in a finite number of steps — no need to ever inspect a non-vertex point at all.

Why this isn't needed for a QP: a quadratic objective does curve, so the same "always push to the boundary" argument doesn't apply — an unconstrained quadratic minimum can sit anywhere, including deep in the interior of the feasible region. QP solvers (active-set methods, or the interior-point methods mentioned in the practitioner section) are built to search the interior as well as the boundary, which is a structurally different algorithm from simplex's vertex-hopping.
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

If you're maximizing something that only ever changes at a steady rate as you walk (a straight-line objective), there's never a reason to stop in the open middle of the room — you can always do better by continuing toward whichever wall helps you, and you only truly stop once you're wedged into a corner. That's why an LP's best answer always sits at a corner of the room, never floating in open space.

Why real portfolio construction needs a QP solver, not the closed-form formula

The classical Markowitz minimum-variance-for-target-return problem, with only the two equality constraints (budget and target return), has a clean closed-form answer reachable with ordinary linear algebra: write the stationarity condition from the Lagrangian as a system of linear equations in the weights and the multipliers, and solve it directly by matrix inversion — no iteration required, because you know in advance exactly which equations must hold. The moment a box constraint like \(w_i \ge 0\) (no shorting) or \(w_i \le 0.1\) (a position cap) is added, that certainty disappears: you do not know in advance whether any particular box constraint will end up binding at the true optimum — whether asset 7 will want to be negative badly enough that its no-shorting constraint kicks in, for instance — and the constraints that turn out to bind (recall complementary slackness from the previous lesson) determine which system of equations you'd even need to solve. There is no single fixed matrix to invert, because the correct matrix depends on information (which constraints bind) that is only known once you've already solved the problem. A QP solver handles this correctly by iterating — an active-set method repeatedly guesses which constraints are binding, solves the resulting equality-constrained sub-problem, checks whether the guess was self-consistent (no negative multiplier on a supposedly-binding constraint, no violated constraint among the ones left out), and updates the guess until it is. This is exactly why every practical portfolio optimizer with box constraints — which is to say, essentially every real one — runs on a QP solver, never on the textbook closed-form matrix formula alone.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

With no extra rules, finding the minimum-risk portfolio is a one-shot calculation — plug numbers into a formula, done. The moment you add a real-world rule like "no shorting," you don't know in advance which assets will actually want to go negative and get stopped by that rule — so there's no single formula to plug into anymore, and a computer has to try, check, and adjust its way to the answer instead.

Worked example

Solving a tiny LP by evaluating every vertex, then specifying a real 3-asset QP

Part 1 — the LP. A desk splits risk-capital between two strategies, allocating \(x\) units to Strategy A and \(y\) units to Strategy B, and wants to maximize expected P&L (in \$ thousands):

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{maximize} \quad & Z = 3x + 5y \\ \text{subject to} \quad & x \le 4 \quad \text{(Strategy A's operational capacity)} \\ & y \le 6 \quad \text{(Strategy B's operational capacity)} \\ & 3x + 2y \le 18 \quad \text{(shared margin budget)} \\ & x \ge 0, \ y \ge 0 \end{aligned} \]

The feasible region is the pentagon shown in the chart above, with vertices \((0,0)\), \((4,0)\), \((4,3)\) (where \(x=4\) meets the margin line), \((2,6)\) (where the margin line meets \(y=6\)), and \((0,6)\). Per the vertex argument above, the optimum must be at one of these five points — evaluate \(Z\) at each:

\[ \begin{aligned} (0,0): \quad & Z = 3(0)+5(0) = 0 \\ (4,0): \quad & Z = 3(4)+5(0) = 12 \\ (4,3): \quad & Z = 3(4)+5(3) = 27 \\ (2,6): \quad & Z = 3(2)+5(6) = \mathbf{36} \quad \leftarrow \text{maximum} \\ (0,6): \quad & Z = 3(0)+5(6) = 30 \end{aligned} \]
(2,6), Z=36the optimal allocation — 2 units to Strategy A, 6 to Strategy B

The optimal allocation is \(x=2\), \(y=6\), for a maximum expected P&L of 36 (\$36,000) — both the operational cap on Strategy B (\(y\le6\)) and the shared margin budget (\(3x+2y\le18\)) are binding at this vertex simultaneously (check: \(y=6\) exactly, and \(3(2)+2(6)=18\) exactly), while Strategy A's own cap (\(x\le4\)) has slack (\(x=2<4\)) and, per complementary slackness, contributes nothing to pinning down the answer.

Part 2 — the fully-specified QP. A 3-asset Markowitz minimum-variance problem, targeting a 5% expected return, with a no-shorting and a 40%-position-cap box constraint on every weight — exactly the fully-specified input a real QP solver would take (not something meant to be solved by hand):

\[ \mu = \begin{pmatrix} 0.05 \\ 0.07 \\ 0.03 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 0.0400 & 0.0180 & -0.0060 \\ 0.0180 & 0.0625 & -0.0090 \\ -0.0060 & -0.0090 & 0.0225 \end{pmatrix} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} \text{minimize} \quad & w^\top \Sigma w \\ \text{subject to} \quad & w_1 + w_2 + w_3 = 1 \\ & 0.05\,w_1 + 0.07\,w_2 + 0.03\,w_3 = 0.05 \\ & 0 \le w_1 \le 0.40, \quad 0 \le w_2 \le 0.40, \quad 0 \le w_3 \le 0.40 \end{aligned} \]
Σthe 3×3 covariance matrix — variances on the diagonal (4%², 6.25%², 2.25%²), covariances off it
μexpected returns (5%, 7%, 3%) — the target-return equality constraint
0 ≤ wᵢ ≤ 0.40the box constraints that push this out of closed-form territory

This is precisely the input a QP solver expects: a PSD Hessian (\(2\Sigma\)), two linear equality constraints, and six linear inequality constraints (a lower and upper bound for each of the three weights). Solving it numerically is exactly the solver's job, not a pencil-and-paper exercise — the point of writing it out in full is to make concrete exactly how little changes in the problem's specification between the closed-form-solvable version (drop the box constraints) and the QP-solver-required version (keep them): two extra inequality constraints per asset, and the guaranteed closed-form solve is gone.

Real-world trading scenario

A risk-parity fund's overnight rebalance, and why it runs a QP every single day

A systematic multi-asset fund rebalances its portfolio every evening: new return forecasts and a refreshed covariance matrix come in, and the fund needs new target weights that minimize risk for whatever return target the mandate specifies, subject to fixed rules — no instrument may exceed a 15% position, no single asset class may go net short, and the fund's prime broker imposes its own margin-based position caps that can shift day to day as volatility changes.

Every one of those rules is a box or linear constraint, and the covariance matrix guarantees convexity — so the problem is, every single night, a fresh QP. Because which constraints will bind changes from one night to the next (a volatile day might push several positions toward their margin-driven caps; a calm day might leave every box constraint slack, quietly reducing to something close to the closed-form unconstrained answer), there is no fixed formula the operations team could hard-code once and reuse — the solver has to run fresh each night, and its output is only as trustworthy as the correctness of that night's constraint set. A stale or misconfigured position cap (last quarter's margin limit, not today's) doesn't produce an error message — it produces a QP solution that is internally consistent and "optimal," just optimal with respect to the wrong constraints, a failure mode that's silent unless someone checks the constraint inputs against what the mandate and the prime broker actually require that day.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

Almost nobody implements simplex or a QP active-set method from scratch in production — both problem types are served by mature, heavily optimized solver libraries (open-source options like OSQP, CVXOPT, and the GLPK/CLP family for LPs; commercial solvers like Gurobi and CPLEX for large-scale or mixed-integer problems), and a quant researcher's actual job is almost always formulating the business problem correctly as an LP or QP — getting the objective, the constraint matrix, and the bounds right — and handing that specification to the solver, rather than writing solver internals. For large problems (thousands of variables and constraints, common in index-replication or large multi-asset portfolios), interior-point methods are the dominant modern algorithm for both LPs and QPs — rather than walking vertex to vertex like simplex, they cut directly through the interior of the feasible region, using a barrier function that penalizes getting close to a constraint boundary, and converge to the optimum along a smooth path; for the largest, sparsest problems they generally scale better than simplex, though simplex remains highly competitive and is still widely used, particularly when a "warm start" from a previous day's solution is available (a common situation in exactly the daily-rebalance workflow described above).

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where this goes wrong in practice

Forgetting to check the problem is even feasible. A badly specified or overly aggressive set of constraints (target return too high for the box-constrained universe to reach, say) can have no feasible region at all — an empty polytope. A solver reporting "infeasible" is telling you the constraints themselves contradict each other, not that the search failed for some other reason; the fix is to relax a constraint, not to tune the solver.

An unbounded objective with no error until it's too late. If a maximization LP's feasible region is genuinely unbounded in the direction the objective improves (a missing upper-limit constraint, for instance), the "optimal" value is infinite — a modeling bug, not a numerical one, and one that's easy to miss if a constraint that should have capped a variable was accidentally omitted or mis-signed.

Degenerate vertices confusing the interpretation of a solution. When more constraints than strictly necessary are binding at the same vertex simultaneously (a coincidence of the specific numbers chosen), the Lagrange multipliers on the tied constraints are not uniquely determined, even though the optimal \(x\) itself is well defined — a subtlety that matters when trying to read economic meaning ("how much would loosening this cap help?") off a solver's reported multipliers.

A near-singular Hessian in a QP causing numerical trouble. A covariance matrix built from highly correlated or nearly redundant assets can be PSD in theory but numerically very close to singular in practice — small floating-point errors can then produce a solver output that's technically "converged" but numerically unstable, sensitive to tiny input perturbations in a way a well-conditioned problem would not be. Regularizing the covariance matrix (shrinkage, a small ridge term added to the diagonal) is a standard practical fix.

Section 04

Key Takeaways

✓An LP is a linear objective over linear constraints; a QP swaps in a quadratic objective. Markowitz mean-variance, \(\min w^\top\Sigma w\), is exactly a QP.
✓An LP's optimum is always at a vertex of the feasible polytope. A direct consequence of a linear objective having no curvature — the simplex method exploits this by walking vertex to vertex.
⚠A QP's optimum need not sit at a vertex. A quadratic objective curves, so its unconstrained minimum can land anywhere in the feasible region — a structurally different search than simplex's vertex-hopping.
✓Box constraints are exactly why real portfolios need a QP solver, not the closed-form Markowitz formula. You can't know in advance which constraints will bind, so there's no single fixed matrix to invert.
✓Interior-point methods are the dominant modern algorithm for large LPs and QPs. They cut through the interior via a barrier function rather than hopping vertex to vertex, and generally scale better on the largest, sparsest problems.
⚠An "optimal" solver output can be optimal with respect to the wrong constraints. A stale or misconfigured position cap doesn't error out — it silently changes what "optimal" means for that solve.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. Why is an LP's optimal solution always found at a vertex of the feasible region, never at some interior point?
Because the objective is linear, it has no curvature — moving along any straight line inside the feasible region changes the objective at a constant rate. For any interior (non-vertex) point, you can always walk further in whichever direction improves the objective until you hit the boundary — so an interior point can never be strictly better than every point reachable by continuing that walk. Repeating this argument along the boundary itself eventually reaches a vertex, where no further improving direction remains inside the feasible region. A quadratic objective, by contrast, does curve, so this "always push further" argument breaks down and a QP's optimum can genuinely sit in the interior.
Q2. Why does adding a no-shorting constraint (wᵢ≥0) to the classical Markowitz problem eliminate the closed-form solution?
The closed-form Markowitz solution comes from solving a fixed system of linear equations (the stationarity conditions from the Lagrangian with only equality constraints) by direct matrix inversion — which only works because you know in advance exactly which equations must hold. Adding wᵢ≥0 introduces an inequality constraint whose binding status (does the unconstrained optimum actually want a negative weight here, or not) is not known until after solving the problem — different assets might or might not have their constraint active depending on the specific μ and Σ. Since the correct system of equations to solve depends on which constraints turn out to bind, there's no single fixed matrix to invert in advance; an iterative solver that discovers the active set is required instead.
Q3. In the worked 2-variable LP, why are both y≤6 and 3x+2y≤18 binding at the optimal vertex (2,6), while x≤4 is not?
A constraint is binding exactly when it's satisfied with equality at the optimal point. At (2,6): y=6 exactly, so y≤6 is binding; and 3(2)+2(6)=18 exactly, so 3x+2y≤18 is binding too — this vertex is precisely where those two constraint boundaries intersect. Meanwhile x=2, which is strictly less than the x≤4 cap, so that constraint has slack and is not binding — by complementary slackness, its multiplier is zero, meaning it contributes nothing to determining the optimal point; Strategy A's operational capacity was simply never the limiting factor in this particular solution.
Q4. What's the practical difference between simplex and an interior-point method, and why does it matter for a large real-world LP?
Simplex walks from vertex to adjacent vertex along the edges of the feasible polytope, improving the objective at each step, and never examines any point in the polytope's interior. An interior-point method instead moves through the interior of the feasible region, using a barrier function that discourages getting too close to any constraint boundary, and converges to the optimum along a smooth path rather than a sequence of discrete vertex hops. For very large, sparse problems (thousands of variables and constraints, typical of large-scale portfolio or index-replication problems), interior-point methods generally scale better; simplex remains highly competitive and is often preferred when a "warm start" from a nearby previous solution is available, since it can resume close to the new optimal vertex rather than restarting the interior-point search from scratch.
Q5. A QP solver reports a solution to a nightly portfolio rebalance, and it looks internally consistent (all constraints satisfied, KKT conditions hold). Does that guarantee the portfolio is actually appropriate for that day? Why or why not?
No — it only guarantees the solution is the true optimum with respect to whatever objective and constraints were actually fed into the solver that night. If a position cap, margin limit, or return target was stale, mis-specified, or simply wrong for that day's actual mandate, the solver will still return a mathematically optimal, internally consistent answer — just optimal for the wrong problem. The KKT conditions holding confirm the math was solved correctly; they say nothing about whether the inputs handed to the solver were the correct inputs, which is a separate, non-mathematical check that has to happen before trusting the output.
