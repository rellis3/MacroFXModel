Short-Rate Models: Vasicek, CIR & Hull-White

Three models of the instantaneous interest rate, each fixing a specific flaw in the one before it — and each one a direct, named application of the general SDE machinery this sequence has just built.

CONCEPT — the standard textbook progression of one-factor interest-rate models ~18 min read Prereqs: Ornstein-Uhlenbeck & Mean Reversion, Existence & Uniqueness of SDE Solutions, Bootstrapping the Yield Curve, Curve Fitting: Nelson-Siegel & Svensson
TL;DR
  • Vasicek: \(dr_t=a(b-r_t)dt+\sigma\,dW_t\) — an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process applied directly to the short rate, mean-reverting to a long-run level \(b\) at speed \(a\). Its well-known flaw: \(r_t\) is Gaussian, so it can go negative with positive probability.
  • That "flaw" turned into a "feature" in the real world once policy rates in Europe and Japan actually went negative through much of the 2010s and into the 2020s.
  • CIR fixes it with a square-root diffusion, \(dr_t=a(b-r_t)dt+\sigma\sqrt{r_t}\,dW_t\), whose volatility shrinks to zero as \(r_t\to0\) — under the Feller condition \(2ab\ge\sigma^2\), this keeps the process strictly positive.
  • That same \(\sigma\sqrt{r_t}\) term violates the Lipschitz condition from sde-existence-uniqueness.html, needing the more careful Yamada-Watanabe existence argument flagged there.
  • Hull-White extends Vasicek with a time-dependent drift level, \(dr_t=[\theta(t)-a\,r_t]dt+\sigma\,dW_t\), calibrated so the model exactly reproduces today's observed yield curve — the standard fix for the fact that constant-parameter Vasicek/CIR generally cannot.
  • A real simulation in this lesson, using parameters where Vasicek visibly dips negative, confirms CIR stays strictly positive over the identical horizon under a Feller condition that is comfortably satisfied.
Section 01

Why this matters

This lesson is where the whole sequence's machinery lands on a genuinely practical family of models. Vasicek is nothing more than ou-mean-reversion.html's Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, applied to the short-term interest rate instead of a generic mean-reverting price series — the identical SDE, same math, different name because of what it's used for. CIR is the first place in this curriculum a coefficient genuinely fails the Lipschitz condition from sde-existence-uniqueness.html, making that lesson's caveat about the Yamada-Watanabe conditions concrete rather than abstract. And Hull-White is the standard practitioner answer to a problem every constant-parameter model in this lesson runs into: a rates desk needs a model that is consistent with the yield curve observed today (built in yield-curve-construction.html and nelson-siegel-svensson.html), not just one with plausible long-run dynamics.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Three fixes, in sequence, each one solving the last one's problem

Vasicek says: the short rate wanders around a long-run average, pulled back whenever it strays too far — exactly the mean-reversion story ou-mean-reversion.html already tells for any price series. The trouble is that nothing in the model stops the random wandering from occasionally pushing the rate below zero — mathematically fine (a Gaussian variable is just as happy being negative as positive), but for decades treated as an obviously wrong prediction for an interest rate, since nobody imagined being paid to borrow money.

CIR's fix is almost mechanical: make the size of the random wandering itself shrink to nothing as the rate approaches zero, so there's never enough of a random push left to send it negative — like a car whose brakes get progressively stronger the closer it gets to a wall, never quite able to hit it. Hull-White solves a completely different problem: Vasicek and CIR, with fixed parameters, produce some particular average shape for the yield curve, but real yield curves observed on any given day rarely match that shape exactly — Hull-White allows the target level \(b\) to change over time, giving the model enough extra knobs to fit today's curve exactly, on top of whichever positivity or negativity behaviour its diffusion term chooses to keep.

Section 02

The math, step by step

1. Vasicek — Ornstein-Uhlenbeck, renamed and reapplied

\[ dr_t = a(b-r_t)\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t \]
aspeed of mean reversion — identical role to θ in ou-mean-reversion.html
bthe long-run mean level the rate reverts to — identical role to μ in the OU lesson
σconstant volatility — the size of the random wandering, unaffected by the current rate level

This equation is, term for term, ou-mean-reversion.html's \(dX_t=\theta(\mu-X_t)dt+\sigma\,dW_t\) with \(a\leftrightarrow\theta\), \(b\leftrightarrow\mu\), and \(X_t\to r_t\) — Vasicek (1977) is that lesson's OU process, applied specifically to the instantaneous interest rate. Nothing about the derivation, the solution's Gaussian distribution, or the half-life formula \(\ln(2)/a\) changes; only the interpretation of the variable does. This lesson does not re-derive any of that math — ou-mean-reversion.html already has it in full, including the closed-form solution and its stationary variance.

The well-known flaw, stated precisely: because the OU process this lesson borrows is Gaussian at every fixed time \(t\), \(r_t\) has positive probability of being negative, for any choice of \(a\), \(b\), and \(\sigma>0\) — however small that probability is made by picking a large \(b\) relative to \(\sigma\), it is never exactly zero. For decades this was treated as an obvious modelling defect — a policy rate at, say, \(-0.5\%\) looked like nonsense. Then, through the 2010s and into the 2020s, the European Central Bank and the Bank of Japan actually ran policy rates below zero for extended periods. Vasicek's "flaw" turned out to already be a "feature" — it was the CIR- and lognormal-style models (built to rule out negative rates entirely) that had to be patched or abandoned in those regimes, while Vasicek needed no modification to remain internally consistent. Say this plainly rather than picking a side: which behaviour is "correct" depends entirely on which regime the model is being used in, not on which one looks more mathematically elegant.
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Vasicek says the interest rate is a ball on a string, always pulled back gently toward some average level, but the string doesn't stop random jostles from occasionally shoving the ball below zero. For decades that looked like an obvious flaw — how can borrowing money cost less than nothing? Then real-world rates actually did go negative for years, and the "flaw" turned out to just be an honest description of something that really happens.

2. CIR — a diffusion that shrinks to zero at the boundary

\[ dr_t = a(b-r_t)\,dt + \sigma\sqrt{r_t}\,dW_t \]
σ√r_tthe diffusion coefficient — identical drift to Vasicek, but volatility now scales with √(current rate)

The Cox-Ingersoll-Ross (1985) model keeps Vasicek's mean-reverting drift exactly as is, changing only the diffusion term: volatility is no longer a fixed constant \(\sigma\), but \(\sigma\sqrt{r_t}\) — proportional to the square root of the current rate. As \(r_t\to 0^+\), this diffusion term shrinks to zero, so the random wandering that could push the rate negative gets progressively weaker exactly as the rate approaches the boundary it isn't supposed to cross. Combined with a positive drift pulling the rate back up (since \(a(b-r_t)>0\) whenever \(r_t < b\)), this can — under one additional condition — keep the process strictly positive for all time.

That condition is the Feller condition, and it needs to be stated precisely rather than approximately:

\[ 2ab \ge \sigma^2 \]
2ab ≥ σ²if satisfied, r_t never reaches exactly zero; if violated, the process can (and, over a long enough horizon, will) touch zero, though it's typically reflected back up rather than going negative

Read this condition for what it says: the mean-reverting "pull" toward a positive long-run level \(b\), scaled by twice the speed \(a\), has to dominate the variance \(\sigma^2\) of the random shocks. Intuitively, if volatility is large relative to how hard and how quickly the process is pulled back toward a comfortably positive mean, the random shocks can still overwhelm the shrinking-diffusion mechanism and touch the zero boundary — the mechanism helps, but it isn't a free, parameter-independent guarantee.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

CIR is like a car whose brakes get stronger and stronger the closer it gets to a wall — as the rate nears zero, the random jostling shrinks toward nothing, so there's less and less shove available to actually push it through. The Feller condition is just the check that the brakes (the pull back toward a healthy positive average) are strong enough relative to how hard the jostling pushes — if they are, the car never quite touches the wall.

Cross-link to lesson 1's honesty check: \(\sigma\sqrt{r_t}\) is exactly the square-root diffusion flagged in sde-existence-uniqueness.html as a Lipschitz-condition failure at \(r_t=0\) (its local slope, \(\sigma/(2\sqrt{r_t})\), diverges as \(r_t\to0\)) — the standard global Lipschitz + linear growth theorem from that lesson simply does not apply to CIR. Existence and pathwise uniqueness for CIR is instead established via the weaker Yamada-Watanabe (1971) conditions, which are built to handle exactly this kind of Hölder-continuous-but-not-Lipschitz square-root behaviour. This is not a minor technicality being glossed over — it's the direct, concrete payoff of that earlier lesson's caveat, landing on a model this lesson actually uses.
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

The Lipschitz condition from earlier in this sequence is a promise that a coefficient's slope never gets infinitely steep. CIR's \(\sqrt{r}\) term breaks that promise exactly at \(r=0\), where the graph goes nearly vertical — so CIR needs a separate, more forgiving theorem (Yamada-Watanabe) to guarantee it's still a well-defined, genuine process, rather than the standard one used everywhere else in this sequence.

\(r_t\)The instantaneous short rate — the interest rate for an infinitesimally short borrowing/lending horizon, the fundamental building block every bond price in yield-curve-construction.html is derived from.
\(a\)Mean-reversion speed — how quickly the rate is pulled back toward its long-run level \(b\).
\(b\)The long-run (unconditional) mean level the short rate reverts to.
\(\sigma\)The volatility scale — constant in Vasicek and Hull-White, scaled by \(\sqrt{r_t}\) in CIR.
Feller condition, \(2ab\ge\sigma^2\)The precise condition under which CIR's square-root diffusion keeps \(r_t\) strictly positive for all time.
\(\theta(t)\)Hull-White's time-dependent drift level — replaces Vasicek's constant \(ab\) with a function of time, calibrated to fit today's observed yield curve exactly.

3. Hull-White — fitting today's curve exactly

\[ dr_t = \big[\theta(t) - a\,r_t\big]\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t \]
θ(t)a deterministic, time-varying function replacing Vasicek's constant term ab — the entire extension, in one substitution

Hull-White (1990) is Vasicek with exactly one structural change: the constant product \(a\,b\) (mean-reversion speed times long-run level) is replaced by an arbitrary, time-dependent function \(\theta(t)\). Everything else — constant \(a\), constant \(\sigma\), Gaussian dynamics, the same potential-negativity behaviour Vasicek has — carries over unchanged. The entire motivation for this one substitution is a practical limitation: a Vasicek model with fixed \(a\), \(b\), \(\sigma\) produces one specific theoretical shape for the yield curve implied by its dynamics, and that shape generally will not match the actual curve bootstrapped from today's market prices in yield-curve-construction.html, or the smooth parametric fit nelson-siegel-svensson.html builds from it. A rates desk pricing a new instrument off this model needs the model to at least agree with the market on instruments that already trade — that is a hard, non-negotiable calibration constraint, and \(\theta(t)\) is the standard degree of freedom introduced specifically to satisfy it: choose \(\theta(t)\) (in closed form, given the initial forward-rate curve \(f(0,t)\) from the observed curve) so that the model's own theoretical bond prices reproduce today's observed curve exactly, then let the dynamics evolve forward from there. This is the standard practitioner fix for the general fact that a constant-parameter one-factor model essentially never fits an arbitrary observed initial term structure exactly.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Vasicek and CIR each predict one fixed "natural" shape for the whole yield curve, and real curves almost never match that shape exactly on any given day. Hull-White's fix is to stop insisting on one fixed target level and instead let that target level change over time, dialed in precisely so the model's prediction lines up with today's actual market curve before letting the randomness take over from there.

Worked example

Checking the Feller condition for the parameters used in the chart above

The simulation above uses \(a=0.20\), \(b=0.005\) (0.5%), \(\sigma=0.010\) (1%). Check the Feller condition directly:

\[ 2ab = 2(0.20)(0.005) = 0.00200 \qquad \sigma^2 = (0.010)^2 = 0.00010 \]

\(0.00200 \ge 0.00010\) — the Feller condition is satisfied, comfortably (by a factor of 20), which is exactly why the CIR path in the chart above never touches zero despite the underlying random shocks being strong enough to push the Vasicek path (using the identical \(\sigma\)) to \(-2\%\) at one point. It's worth being honest about what "comfortably satisfied" is doing here: these particular parameters were chosen specifically to produce a case where Vasicek visibly misbehaves while CIR visibly does not — a real desk's actual short-rate parameters, estimated from data rather than chosen for illustration, might sit much closer to the Feller boundary, where the distinction between "CIR stays positive" and "CIR occasionally touches zero" becomes a genuinely live, parameter-sensitive question rather than a clean-cut demonstration.

🎯
Real-world trading scenario

2015: negative Swiss and Eurozone policy rates break the "rates can't go negative" assumption

In January 2015, the Swiss National Bank's policy rate fell to -0.75%, and the European Central Bank's deposit rate had already gone negative the previous year — both parts of a broader move into negative-rate territory across several major central banks that persisted for the rest of that decade. Any desk still marking short-rate risk off a model that structurally assumed nonnegativity (a lognormal short-rate model, or a naive CIR implementation without a careful boundary treatment) had to either patch the model or abandon it for those currencies — while a Vasicek-family model, built on the assumption that had looked like its obvious weakness for decades, needed no structural change at all to remain consistent with the observed data. This is the honest version of the "flaw becomes feature" point from step 1: it isn't that Vasicek was secretly right all along and CIR was wrong, it's that neither model's boundary behaviour is universally "correct" — which one is appropriate depends entirely on the rate regime actually being modeled, and 2015 is a real, dated instance where that choice mattered financially, not just theoretically.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

📐

Vasicek/CIR for intuition and risk-factor modelling

Rarely used directly for pricing exotic rate derivatives on a modern desk, but still the standard teaching and stress-testing baseline for understanding mean-reverting rate dynamics and choosing between Gaussian and positivity-preserving assumptions.

🎯

Hull-White for actual derivatives pricing

Hull-White's exact fit to today's observed curve is what makes it (and its extensions) genuinely usable for pricing interest-rate derivatives — a model that already disagrees with today's market on plain vanilla instruments cannot be trusted on anything more exotic built on top of it.

🧮

The Feller condition as a parameter-admissibility check

Whenever CIR (or CIR-type variance processes, like Heston's) are calibrated to data, checking 2ab≥σ² against the fitted parameters is standard due diligence — a fit that violates it is still usable, but the process's zero-touching behaviour then needs explicit handling rather than being assumed away.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where this gets misapplied

Simulating CIR with a naive Euler-Maruyama scheme and no boundary handling. A plain discretization of \(\sigma\sqrt{r_t}\) can produce a negative \(r_n\) at some step purely from discretization error, even when the Feller condition holds for the true continuous-time process — the square root of a negative number is undefined, crashing the simulation. Standard practical fixes (full truncation, reflection at zero) are needed regardless of whether Feller holds theoretically; this lesson's own CIR chart uses exactly a full-truncation scheme for this reason.

Assuming Hull-White's exact curve fit means the model is "more correct" than Vasicek or CIR. Hull-White fits today's curve exactly by construction, not because its underlying dynamics are a better description of how rates actually evolve — the extra \(\theta(t)\) flexibility is a calibration convenience, not evidence about the true stochastic process generating rates.

Treating the Feller condition as a strict pass/fail gate on model validity. A CIR fit that mildly violates \(2ab\ge\sigma^2\) is not automatically an invalid model — the process is still well-defined (via Yamada-Watanabe existence), it simply touches zero occasionally rather than never, which may or may not matter depending on the application.

Picking parameters (as this lesson's own chart does, honestly flagged) to make one model look obviously better than another. The chart above uses σ large enough, relative to b, to make Vasicek's negativity visually dramatic — real fitted short-rate parameters are often far closer to the boundary where the distinction is subtle, not dramatic, and a demonstration chart should never be mistaken for a general claim about how different these models look under realistic calibration.

Section 05

Self-test

Q1. How does the Vasicek short-rate model relate to the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process covered elsewhere in this curriculum?
They are the identical SDE. Vasicek's dr_t=a(b−r_t)dt+σdW_t is ou-mean-reversion.html's dX_t=θ(μ−X_t)dt+σdW_t with a↔θ, b↔μ, and X_t renamed r_t. All of the OU lesson's derivations — the closed-form Gaussian solution, the half-life ln(2)/a, the stationary variance — carry over unchanged; Vasicek is simply that math applied specifically to the instantaneous interest rate.
Q2. State the Feller condition precisely, and explain in plain terms what it guarantees.
The Feller condition is 2ab≥σ², where a is CIR's mean-reversion speed, b its long-run mean level, and σ its volatility scale. When satisfied, it guarantees the CIR process r_t never reaches exactly zero — the mean-reverting pull toward a positive long-run level, scaled by twice the reversion speed, dominates the variance of the random shocks, so the shrinking-diffusion mechanism (volatility scaling with √r_t) is strong enough to keep the process strictly positive for all time.
Q3. Why does CIR's diffusion coefficient, σ√r_t, fail the global Lipschitz condition from the existence-and-uniqueness lesson, and what's the practical consequence?
Checking the ratio |σ√r_t)-σ√0|/|r_t-0| = σ/√r_t as r_t→0 diverges to infinity — no finite Lipschitz constant K can bound it near the origin. The practical consequence is that the standard global-Lipschitz-plus-linear-growth existence theorem simply doesn't apply to CIR; existence and pathwise uniqueness are instead established via the separate, weaker Yamada-Watanabe (1971) conditions, built specifically to handle this kind of Hölder-continuous (but not Lipschitz) square-root behaviour.
Q4. What single substitution turns Vasicek into Hull-White, and what problem is that substitution solving?
Hull-White replaces Vasicek's constant drift term ab with a time-dependent function θ(t): dr_t=[θ(t)−a·r_t]dt+σdW_t. Everything else (constant a, constant σ, Gaussian dynamics) is unchanged. The substitution solves the calibration problem that a constant-parameter Vasicek generally cannot exactly reproduce an arbitrary observed initial yield curve — θ(t) gives the model exactly enough extra degrees of freedom, chosen from the observed forward-rate curve, to fit today's market prices exactly before letting the dynamics evolve forward.
Q5. A CIR model is fit to short-rate data and the Feller condition comes out only mildly violated (2ab slightly less than σ²). Is the model unusable?
No. The process is still well-defined via the Yamada-Watanabe existence conditions even when Feller is violated — the practical consequence is only that the process can occasionally touch zero (typically reflected back up) rather than staying strictly positive for all time. Whether that matters depends on the application: it's a real behavioural difference worth being aware of and explicitly handling in simulation, not evidence the calibration or the model itself is broken.
