Breeden-Litzenberger & Variance Swaps

A continuum of European option prices secretly encodes two separate things at once: the market's entire risk-neutral probability distribution for the underlying, and a model-free fair price for realized variance itself — both pulled out of the same cross-section of quotes, without ever having to pick a volatility model first.

TL;DR
  • Breeden-Litzenberger (1978): the risk-neutral probability density of the underlying's price at expiry is \(f_Q(K) = e^{rT}\,\partial^2 C/\partial K^2\) — the second derivative of the call price with respect to strike, evaluated at \(K\), discounted back to today. A call spread approximates a digital payout; a butterfly spread approximates a spike at the middle strike; shrinking the spacing to zero turns the discrete butterfly price into exactly this second derivative.
  • Variance swaps: the risk-neutral expected realized variance of the underlying can be replicated by a "strip" of out-of-the-money puts and calls across every strike, each weighted by \(2/K^2\) — the famous log-contract result. You never need to forecast future volatility; today's entire strike-by-strike option prices already imply the answer.
  • Both results share one deep reason for their kinship: they both extract information that lives in the entire cross-section of option prices, without assuming any specific model for how volatility behaves — genuinely different in kind from the parametric machinery in the sibling Bates Model and Dupire Local Volatility lessons, both of which commit to a specific model structure before fitting it to the surface.
  • The exact same quantity, \(\partial^2 C/\partial K^2\), sits in the denominator of the Dupire local-volatility formula (sibling lesson) — not a coincidence: it's the arbitrage-free risk-neutral density, and any formula that needs "how much probability mass sits near this strike" needs this object.
  • Neither result survives contact with real markets without an honest caveat: both need a genuine continuum of strikes in theory, and both are computed in practice on a finite, truncated set of actual listed strikes — introducing interpolation risk (Breeden-Litzenberger) and truncation error (variance-swap replication) that this lesson quantifies with worked toy examples.
  • This is the actual theoretical basis for how the real-world VIX index is computed — CBOE's published methodology is a discretized version of exactly the variance-swap replication formula derived here.
Section 01

Why this matters

Black-Scholes-Merton hands you \(N(d_2)\): the risk-neutral probability that a single option, at a single strike, finishes in the money. That's genuinely useful, but it's also a very narrow slice of information — one number, about one strike. The market, on any given day, is quoting calls and puts at dozens of strikes on the same underlying and the same expiry simultaneously. A natural question follows immediately: if one strike's option price encodes one probability, does the entire set of prices across every strike encode the entire probability distribution? The answer, due to Breeden and Litzenberger in 1978, is yes — and it comes with an explicit formula, not just a plausibility argument.

A second, seemingly unrelated question turns out to have almost the identical answer. A variance swap is a contract that pays out based on how much the underlying actually moved around over some period — its realized variance — rather than on its direction. Pricing that contract sounds like it should require forecasting future volatility, which nobody can do with confidence. It doesn't. A classical replication result shows the risk-neutral expected realized variance can be built today, exactly, from a portfolio of options at every strike, weighted in a specific way — no volatility forecast required, only today's already-observed prices. This lesson covers both results together because the second is built on the same idea as the first: an entire cross-section of option prices, not any single price, is the thing that actually contains the information.

💡
Plain-English intuition

A call spread is a bet on "above"; a butterfly is a bet on "exactly here"

A single call option pays off however far above its strike the underlying ends up — an open-ended amount. A call spread — long one call, short another at a slightly higher strike — caps that payoff, and as the two strikes get closer together, the payoff starts looking less like "a lot if we're way above" and more like "exactly $1 if we finish above this level, $0 otherwise": a digital, or binary, bet. The price of that spread, divided by the strike gap, approximates the market's probability of finishing above that level.

Push one step further: a butterfly spread — long a call at a lower strike, short two calls at the middle strike, long one more call at a higher strike, all evenly spaced — pays off only in a narrow band around the middle strike, tapering to zero on both sides. As the three strikes are squeezed together toward one point, that tent-shaped payoff gets narrower and taller, converging toward an idealized payout that fires only if the underlying lands exactly at that one strike — a Dirac-delta-like payoff. The market price of that shrinking butterfly, appropriately rescaled, converges to exactly the risk-neutral probability density at that point. That limit, made precise, is Breeden-Litzenberger's second derivative.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine you can't ask people directly "how likely do you think it is the temperature tomorrow is exactly 70 degrees?" — but you can watch what they're willing to pay for a bet that only wins in a very narrow band around 70. If you keep narrowing that band and watch how the bet's price scales, you can back out exactly how likely people think 70 degrees is, without ever asking the question directly. That's the whole trick: option prices are bets, and squeezing three nearby bets together (a butterfly) reveals a probability that was hiding inside the prices the whole time.

Section 02

Part 1 — The math, step by step: Breeden-Litzenberger

Let \(C(K)\) denote the price today of a European call option struck at \(K\), expiring at time \(T\), on an underlying with risk-neutral density \(f_Q(\cdot)\) for its terminal price \(S_T\). Its price is the discounted risk-neutral expectation of the payoff:

\[ C(K) = e^{-rT}\,E^Q\big[(S_T-K)^+\big] = e^{-rT}\int_K^\infty (s-K)\,f_Q(s)\,ds \]
f_Q(s)the risk-neutral probability density of S_T — the unknown object this whole section recovers
(S_T-K)^+the call's payoff — zero below the strike, S_T−K above it
e^{-rT}discounts the risk-neutral expected payoff back to today's price

Differentiate both sides with respect to \(K\) once (Leibniz's rule for differentiating under the integral sign, since the lower limit of integration itself depends on \(K\)):

\[ \frac{\partial C}{\partial K} = -e^{-rT}\int_K^\infty f_Q(s)\,ds = -e^{-rT}\,P(S_T>K) \]

This first derivative already has a clean reading: it's (minus, and discounted) the risk-neutral probability of finishing above \(K\) — the same flavor of object as Black-Scholes' \(N(d_2)\), just written as a slope of the call-price curve instead of a closed form. Differentiate a second time with respect to \(K\):

\[ \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2} = e^{-rT}\,f_Q(K) \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \boxed{f_Q(K) = e^{rT}\,\frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2}} \]
∂²C/∂K²the curvature of the call-price curve at strike K, as a function of strike
f_Q(K)the Breeden-Litzenberger result: the risk-neutral density, recovered directly from observed call prices

That boxed identity is the entire Breeden-Litzenberger (1978) result. It requires no assumption about which model generated the call prices in the first place — no Black-Scholes, no stochastic volatility, nothing. It is a purely mechanical statement: whatever arbitrage-free call-price curve the market actually quotes, its curvature in strike is the risk-neutral density, up to the discount factor. This is exactly what makes it "model-free" in the sense this lesson keeps coming back to.

Why a butterfly spread is the discrete version of this second derivative. A butterfly centered at \(K\) with wing spacing \(h\) — long one call at \(K-h\), short two calls at \(K\), long one call at \(K+h\) — has price \(C(K-h)-2C(K)+C(K+h)\). Divide by \(h^2\) and this is exactly the standard central finite-difference approximation to the second derivative:

\[ \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2}\bigg|_{K} \approx \frac{C(K-h)-2C(K)+C(K+h)}{h^2}, \qquad f_Q(K) \approx e^{rT}\,\frac{C(K-h)-2C(K)+C(K+h)}{h^2} \]

As \(h\to 0\) this converges to the exact second derivative; at any finite \(h\) — the only case that ever exists in a real market, where strikes are listed at fixed intervals — it's an approximation, and Section 08 returns to exactly how that approximation can go wrong.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

If you know the price of a butterfly bet centered at every possible strike, and you know that as the wings of the butterfly get pulled in tight it turns into a bet on "exactly this one number," then you already know how likely the market thinks every single outcome is — strike by strike, all the way across. That's the whole idea: derivatives (in the calculus sense) of the derivative-security (option) prices hand you the probabilities directly.

\(C(K)\)Price today of a European call struck at \(K\), expiring at \(T\).
\(P(K)\)Price today of a European put struck at \(K\), expiring at \(T\).
\(f_Q(K)\)The risk-neutral probability density of \(S_T\), evaluated at \(K\) — the Breeden-Litzenberger result.
\(r\), \(T\)Risk-free rate and time to expiry.
\(F\)Forward price, \(F = S_0 e^{rT}\).
\(h\)Strike spacing used in a discrete butterfly / finite-difference approximation.
\(K_0\)Reference strike closest to (at or below) the forward — the CBOE convention used in Part 2.
\(Q(K_i)\)The out-of-the-money option price used at strike \(K_i\) in the variance-swap strip (a put below \(K_0\), a call above, the average of both at \(K_0\)).

Note what this recovers: not "the probability the option finishes in the money" (a single number, from a single strike, the way \(N(d_2)\) works) but the actual height of the probability curve at \(S_T=100\) — a genuinely richer object, extracted from three market prices and nothing else.

Section 04

Part 2 — The math, step by step: variance swaps and the options strip

A variance swap pays the buyer, at expiry, the difference between the underlying's actual realized variance over the contract's life and a fixed "strike" agreed at inception — so pricing the swap fairly means finding the strike that makes the contract worth zero today: the risk-neutral expected realized variance. The genuinely surprising classical result (going back to work by Neuberger, and formalized by Carr and Madan, and by Demeterfi, Derman, Kamal and Zou) is that this expectation can be built today, exactly, from a static portfolio of options — no path simulation, no volatility forecast, just today's prices.

The key building block is the log contract: a claim that pays \(-2\ln(S_T/F)\) at expiry, where \(F\) is the forward price. Under standard continuous-diffusion assumptions (no jumps — Section 08 flags exactly where this matters), Itô's lemma applied to \(\ln S_t\) shows that the risk-neutral expectation of this log-contract payoff equals exactly the expected integrated variance over the contract's life. In other words: if you can find a way to buy this exact log payoff today, you've bought a variance swap.

You can't trade \(\ln(S_T)\) directly — no such instrument exists. But any smooth payoff \(g(S_T)\) can be exactly spanned (replicated) by a position in the forward plus a continuum of out-of-the-money puts and calls, using the identity (a standard result in option-payoff replication, sometimes called the Carr-Madan spanning formula):

\[ g(S_T) = g(F) + g'(F)(S_T-F) + \int_0^F g''(K)(K-S_T)^+\,dK + \int_F^\infty g''(K)(S_T-K)^+\,dK \]

Apply this to \(g(x) = -2\ln(x/F)\). Its second derivative is \(g''(K) = 2/K^2\) — and that \(2/K^2\) weight is exactly the famous weighting scheme for the option strip. Taking risk-neutral expectations of both sides, discounting, and dividing by \(T\) to annualize gives the model-free variance formula:

\[ E^Q\!\left[\frac{1}{T}\int_0^T \sigma_t^2\,dt\right] \approx \frac{2}{T}\left[\int_0^{K_0} \frac{e^{rT}P(K)}{K^2}\,dK + \int_{K_0}^\infty \frac{e^{rT}C(K)}{K^2}\,dK\right] - \frac{1}{T}\left(\frac{F}{K_0}-1\right)^2 \]
P(K), C(K)out-of-the-money put and call prices at every strike — puts below the reference strike K₀, calls above it
1/K²the log-contract's own weighting — a direct consequence of g''(K)=2/K² for g(x)=−2ln(x/F)
(F/K₀−1)²a small correction term for using a reference strike K₀ near, but not exactly at, the forward F

Because this is exactly the same "differentiate/reconstruct a payoff from a strip of options" machinery as Part 1 — the second derivative of a payoff function, weighted across every strike — it should feel like a close cousin of Breeden-Litzenberger, not a coincidence. Section 06 makes that connection explicit. In practice, the integrals above become a discrete sum over the actual listed strikes:

\[ \text{Var} \approx \frac{2}{T}\sum_i \frac{\Delta K_i}{K_i^2}\,e^{rT}\,Q(K_i) \;-\; \frac{1}{T}\left(\frac{F}{K_0}-1\right)^2 \]

where \(Q(K_i)\) is the out-of-the-money option price at strike \(K_i\) (put below \(K_0\), call above, the average of both at \(K_0\) itself) and \(\Delta K_i\) is the spacing around that strike (typically the average distance to its two neighbors). This discretized form is — not coincidentally — essentially identical in structure to the published CBOE VIX methodology, which computes the VIX index precisely this way from real S&P 500 option quotes.

💡
Plain-English intuition

You don't need to know the future — you need today's whole surface

Forecasting future volatility is genuinely hard; nobody has a reliable crystal ball for it. The variance-swap replication result sidesteps that problem entirely by asking a different question: not "what will volatility be," but "what does the entire current market of option prices, across every strike, already imply the fair break-even variance should be, right now." Buying the right strip of options today — a lot of far out-of-the-money puts and calls weighted down by \(1/K^2\), progressively fewer as strikes move away from the current price — literally locks in that answer as a static, unchanging position, with no forecast anywhere in the construction.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine wanting to bet on "how bumpy will the road be" without knowing which specific bumps are coming. Instead of guessing the bumps, you buy a big spread-out set of small side bets — a little bit on "it swerves left a lot," a little on "it swerves right a lot," smaller bets the further out those swerves get — and it turns out that if you buy exactly the right mix, today, the total payout of that mix automatically matches how bumpy the road actually turns out to be. You never had to predict the road. You just had to buy the right basket of side bets on it, all at once, today.

This is a real, well-known fact worth stating plainly: this replication idea is the actual theoretical basis for how the CBOE computes the VIX index. The VIX is not a survey of opinions about future volatility — it is calculated directly from a snapshot of real S&P 500 option prices across a strip of strikes, using a discretized version of the exact \(1/K^2\)-weighted formula derived above (with CBOE's own specific strike-inclusion and truncation rules, discussed honestly in Section 08).

Section 06

Why these two results are actually connected

It would be easy to teach Breeden-Litzenberger and variance-swap replication as two unrelated party tricks that both happen to involve second derivatives. They're not unrelated — they're the same underlying idea, applied to two different target payoffs. Breeden-Litzenberger asks "what payoff, spanned across a continuum of strikes, recovers the probability density itself?" and the answer is the second derivative of the call price. Variance-swap replication asks "what payoff, spanned across a continuum of strikes, recovers expected realized variance?" and the answer again comes from a second derivative — this time of the log-contract payoff \(g(x)=-2\ln(x/F)\), which is exactly what produces the \(2/K^2\) weighting. Both are instances of the identical spanning principle: any smooth function of \(S_T\) can be rebuilt from a continuum of options via its own second derivative, weighted across strikes — Breeden-Litzenberger is that principle applied to the indicator-like limit of a butterfly, variance-swap replication is the same principle applied to the log payoff.

The exact quantity at the center of Part 1, \(\partial^2 C/\partial K^2\), is not unique to this lesson — it is also, not coincidentally, the same object that sits in the denominator of the Dupire local volatility formula (covered in the sibling Dupire Local Volatility lesson in this category). That's a genuine, structural connection, not a coincidence of notation: Dupire's formula needs "how much risk-neutral probability mass sits near this strike" as an ingredient for turning an entire implied-volatility surface into a single local-volatility function, and that ingredient is the Breeden-Litzenberger density. Both lessons ultimately depend on the same arbitrage-free object; they just use it for different purposes once they have it.

What this lesson's two results have in common

Both extract a well-defined, model-independent quantity — a probability density, or an expected variance — directly from the observed cross-section of option prices. Neither requires committing to a specific stochastic process for the underlying's volatility first. If the observed prices are arbitrage-free, the extraction works regardless of what "true" model, if any, generated them.

What the sibling Bates and Dupire lessons do differently

The Bates Model commits to a specific parametric structure — stochastic volatility plus price jumps, with a fixed number of parameters to calibrate — before it can price anything. Dupire Local Volatility is a genuine step toward "model-free" in that it's consistent with any full implied-vol surface by construction, but it still commits to a specific structural assumption: that volatility is a deterministic function of price and time alone (no separate stochastic-volatility factor). This lesson's two results commit to neither kind of assumption — they only assume the observed option prices themselves are arbitrage-free.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Some tools in this category are like building a specific machine first — decide exactly how it works inside, tune its knobs to match what you see, then use it to make predictions (that's Bates and Dupire). This lesson's two tools are different: they're more like a ruler you hold up directly against the market's own prices, and read the answer straight off — no machine to build first, just a very clever way of measuring what's already there.

Section 07

How practitioners actually use this

📊

Extracting an implied distribution from the vol surface

Rather than assuming a distributional shape up front, a desk can interpolate/smooth the observed strike-by-strike implied vols into a continuous call-price curve, then apply Breeden-Litzenberger to read off the market's own implied distribution — used to sanity-check tail pricing or to compare against a house view.

🎯

Trading realized-vs-implied variance

The fair variance-swap strike from the strip replication is, by construction, the market's break-even for realized variance. A desk with a view that realized variance will differ can take a position accordingly — the replication formula is what defines the fair line to trade against.

🧭

The VIX index itself

CBOE publishes the exact strike-selection, weighting, and interpolation rules it uses to compute the VIX this way, in real time, from actual listed S&P 500 option quotes — a real-world, continuously-running instance of the formula in Section 04.

🩹

A model-free cross-check on parametric models

Because Breeden-Litzenberger needs no model assumption, a density extracted this way can serve as an independent check on whatever a Bates or Dupire-style calibration produces — if the two disagree sharply, that's a flag to look harder at the parametric model's fit, not at this lesson's math.

🎯
Real-world trading scenario

A skew steepens overnight — what does the density say happened?

Imagine an index's implied-vol skew steepens sharply overnight — OTM puts get noticeably more expensive relative to OTM calls, with the at-the-money vol barely moving. A Breeden-Litzenberger density extraction, run before and after, makes the shift concrete rather than qualitative: the left tail of the implied distribution visibly thickens (more risk-neutral probability mass priced into a sharp downside move), while the body of the distribution near the current price is largely unchanged. That's a genuinely different, more specific statement than "vol went up" — it says where the market's fear actually concentrated, which is exactly the kind of detail a single implied-vol number, or even the ATM vol alone, cannot show.

Section 08

Common pitfalls

⚠️
Where both results quietly need real-world caveats

A continuum of strikes never actually trades

Breeden-Litzenberger needs a continuum of strikes; only a finite, discrete set actually trades. Turning a handful of listed strikes into the smooth call-price curve the second derivative needs requires interpolation or smoothing across the gaps — and the resulting density is only as good as that interpolation. This is exactly the same numerical-stability concern the sibling Dupire Local Volatility lesson raises about its own use of \(\partial^2 C/\partial K^2\): both formulas amplify small wiggles or noise in the input curve, since differentiating twice is inherently noise-sensitive — a smooth-looking implied-vol curve can still produce a jagged, unreliable second derivative if the underlying option quotes are thinly traded or stale.

A negative "density" from real market quotes is a red flag about the data, not a real negative probability. A genuine probability density can never be negative. If a discrete second-derivative calculation on real quoted prices produces a negative number, that is a diagnostic signal that the input call prices are internally inconsistent — noisy, stale, or genuinely arbitrageable (violating the convexity-in-strike condition that arbitrage-free call prices must satisfy) — not evidence that probabilities can go negative. The fix is to clean or re-check the input prices, never to report a negative density as a real result.

Variance-swap replication has the identical continuum problem, in a different guise: truncation. The theoretical formula integrates over every strike from 0 to infinity; real markets only list strikes out to some finite range before liquidity disappears entirely. Cutting the sum off at the widest actually-traded strikes, as this lesson's own worked example did at 80 and 120, introduces a real approximation error — this lesson's own numbers showed roughly a 3% relative vol error from using just five strikes, shrinking to about 1% on a much wider, finer grid. CBOE's actual VIX methodology has to handle this with explicit, published cutoff rules (excluding strikes once two consecutive listed strikes have zero bids, among others) — a real, engineering-level detail this lesson's toy example simplifies away rather than reproduces exactly.

The log-contract derivation assumes continuous price paths — no jumps. The step connecting realized variance to the log payoff via Itô's lemma relies on the underlying following a continuous diffusion. Genuine price jumps (the exact phenomenon the sibling Bates Model lesson exists to handle) introduce a residual jump-variance term the pure options-strip replication does not fully capture — a known, published limitation of the model-free result, not an oversight unique to this lesson.

Section 09

Key Takeaways

✓Breeden-Litzenberger: \(f_Q(K)=e^{rT}\partial^2C/\partial K^2\). A butterfly spread's price, in the limit of infinitesimal wing spacing, is exactly the risk-neutral density — a purely model-free consequence of arbitrage-free call prices.
✓Variance swaps can be replicated, model-free, by a strip of OTM options weighted by \(2/K^2\). The fair variance-swap strike needs no volatility forecast — only today's full cross-section of option prices, via the log-contract spanning result.
✓Both results are the same spanning principle, applied to two different target payoffs — a density-revealing limit for Part 1, a log-contract payoff for Part 2 — and both share the exact \(\partial^2C/\partial K^2\) object that also drives the sibling Dupire local-vol formula.
⚠Real markets only ever supply a finite, discrete set of strikes. Breeden-Litzenberger needs interpolation/smoothing across the gaps; variance-swap replication needs a truncation cutoff at the widest listed strikes — both introduce real, quantifiable approximation error, illustrated with this lesson's own worked examples.
✗A negative computed "density" from real quotes is a data-quality red flag, never a real negative probability. It signals noisy, stale, or arbitrageable input prices — the fix is to clean the inputs, not to report the negative number.
✗The log-contract replication assumes continuous price paths. Genuine jumps leave a residual jump-variance term the pure options-strip result does not fully capture — a published, known limitation, not a lesson-specific oversight.
Section 10

Self-test

Q1. State the Breeden-Litzenberger formula, and explain in one sentence why a butterfly spread is the natural discrete analogue of it.
\(f_Q(K) = e^{rT}\,\partial^2 C/\partial K^2\) — the risk-neutral density at \(K\) is the discounted second derivative of the call price with respect to strike. A butterfly spread's price, \(C(K-h)-2C(K)+C(K+h)\), is exactly the standard central finite-difference approximation to that second derivative, so its price (divided by \(h^2\) and discounted) approximates the density, converging to it exactly as the wing spacing \(h\to 0\).
Q2. In the worked Breeden-Litzenberger example, the discrete butterfly approximation gave 0.027926 against a true density of 0.028209. What does that gap represent, and would it shrink if the strike spacing h were made smaller?
The gap (about 1%) is finite-difference discretization error — the approximation \((C(K-h)-2C(K)+C(K+h))/h^2\) only converges to the true second derivative as \(h\to 0\); at any positive \(h\) it carries leading-order error proportional to \(h^2\). Yes, shrinking \(h\) would reduce the gap, exactly the way it did going from the five-strike variance example to the wider/finer grid in Section 05.
Q3. What payoff does the "log contract" pay, and why does its second derivative in strike produce the famous 1/K² weighting used in variance-swap replication?
The log contract pays \(-2\ln(S_T/F)\) at expiry, and its risk-neutral expectation equals expected realized variance under continuous-path assumptions (via Itô's lemma). Spanning this payoff with a continuum of options requires \(g''(K)\) where \(g(x)=-2\ln(x/F)\); since \(g'(x)=-2/x\) and \(g''(x)=2/x^2\), the spanning portfolio weights each strike's OTM option by exactly \(2/K^2\) — the log payoff's own curvature is literally what produces that weighting scheme.
Q4. Why are Breeden-Litzenberger and variance-swap replication described in this lesson as "the same underlying idea," rather than two unrelated results that happen to both use second derivatives?
Both are instances of the same general spanning principle: any smooth function of \(S_T\) can be rebuilt from a continuum of options via its own second derivative, weighted across strikes. Breeden-Litzenberger applies this to the indicator-like limiting payoff of a shrinking butterfly to recover the density itself; variance-swap replication applies the identical spanning machinery to the log-contract payoff to recover expected variance. Same mechanism, two different target payoffs — not a coincidence of both involving "a second derivative."
Q5. A discrete Breeden-Litzenberger calculation on real, quoted call prices returns a negative number for f_Q(K). What should you conclude?
Not that probabilities can be negative — a genuine risk-neutral density is never negative. A negative result signals that the input call prices are internally inconsistent: noisy, stale, thinly traded, or genuinely arbitrageable (violating the convexity-in-strike condition arbitrage-free call prices must satisfy). The correct response is to investigate and clean the input data, not to report the negative value as a real finding.
Q6. In what specific, structural sense do the two results in this lesson qualify as "model-free," in contrast to the sibling Bates and Dupire lessons in this same category?
Both results depend only on the observed option prices being arbitrage-free — they require no assumption about the specific stochastic process generating those prices. The Bates model commits to a specific parametric structure (stochastic volatility plus jumps) before it can price anything; Dupire local volatility, while consistent with any given full implied-vol surface, still commits to the structural assumption that volatility is a deterministic function of price and time alone. Breeden-Litzenberger and variance-swap replication make neither kind of commitment — they extract their result directly from the prices themselves.
