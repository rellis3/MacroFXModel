Lesson 2 · Foundations & Epistemics

Bayesian Inference: Evidence Becomes Belief, Not Certainty

A rule for turning "here is what I believed" plus "here is what I just observed" into "here is what I should believe now." Every strategy in this repo eventually asks the same question in disguise — given what just happened, how much should I believe X? — and the honest answer is never just yes or no. It's a number between 0 and 1, and that number depends on two things people routinely conflate: how rare X was before the evidence showed up, and how strong the evidence actually is.

Section 1

A belief exists before you ever look outside

Bayesian inference starts with an uncomfortable admission: you already believed something, before any evidence showed up.

Say you think there's a 20% chance of rain today — your prior, what you believed before checking anything. You glance outside and see dark clouds. Dark clouds are more common on rainy days than dry ones, so seeing them should raise your belief. But it shouldn't jump straight to 100% — clouds pass without rain constantly, and in most climates it still doesn't rain on most cloudy days. Bayesian inference is the arithmetic for exactly how much to move your belief — not all the way, not by gut feel, but by a precise amount.

Swap "rain" for "trend day," "this level holds," or "this classifier's BUY flag is real," and the shape of the question doesn't change. A prior is usually just a base rate — how often the thing is true in general — and skipping it quietly underlies most of what goes wrong later in this series: p-hacking, an overfit classifier, a backtest's win rate taken at face value. All three are, underneath, a missing prior.

The one-line rule

A piece of evidence is not the same thing as the probability it implies. How much it should move your belief depends on how rare the thing was to begin with — that's the entire lesson; everything below is just the arithmetic for it.

Section 2

Bayes' theorem isn't assumed — it falls out of one definition

Step through the algebra. Every line below uses nothing but the definition of conditional probability.

Try it
P(H|E)
=
P(H∩E) / P(E)
This is conditional probability — nothing assumed yet. Out of every time E happens, what fraction of those times does H also happen?
P(H∩E)
=
P(H|E) · P(E)
Multiply both sides of the definition by P(E) — the "multiplication rule." Still nothing but algebra.
P(H∩E)
=
P(E|H) · P(H)
"H and E" is the same event as "E and H" — apply the exact same definition again, the other way around.
P(E|H)·P(H)
=
P(H|E)·P(E)
P(H|E)
=
P(E|H)·P(H) / P(E)
Both expressions equal the same P(H∩E), so they equal each other. Divide by P(E) and Bayes' theorem falls out — derived, not assumed.
Every result later in this lesson is just this equation, run with real numbers.

This matters for the same reason rearranging a formula mattered in Lesson 1: knowing the theorem is a rearrangement of something more basic means it can't secretly be "wrong for this case" the way an unfamiliar formula might feel — it's conditional probability, twice, and a division.

Section 3

Four terms, four jobs

P(H)

Prior

What you believed before seeing this evidence — usually the base rate: how often is H true in general?

P(E|H)

Likelihood

If H were true, how probable is this exact evidence? Not "how probable is H" — a common, costly mix-up.

P(E)

Evidence

The overall probability of seeing E at all, averaged over every way H could be true or false.

P(H|E)

Posterior

Your updated belief in H after seeing E. This is the answer everything above was built to reach.

Every term gets computed from a different source in practice — P(H) is usually a historical base rate, P(E|H) comes from a test's validation report, and P(E) has to be built, not read off a sheet. Which is which matters, because mixing up P(E|H) with P(H|E) is the single most common way this goes wrong — the entire next few slides are about that one mix-up.
Section 4

The term nobody hands you: P(E)

If H is binary, P(E) has to be built from every way E could happen — the law of total probability.

The law of total probability
P(E|H)·P(H)
+
P(E|¬H)·P(¬H)
=
P(E)
The total chance of seeing the evidence is the chance of seeing it when H is true, weighted by how often H is true, plus the chance of seeing it when H is false, weighted by how often H is false.

Most intuitive reasoning anchors on P(E|H) — how good the test is — and quietly forgets P(E|¬H): how often you'd see the identical evidence for the wrong reason entirely. That single omission is the whole mechanism behind the base-rate fallacy, two slides from here.

Where this needs qualifying

P(¬H) = 1 − P(H), always — if 20% of days are trend days, 80% aren't. That's not an extra assumption, it's what "the complement" means. It's also exactly the number people forget to weight the false-positive rate by.

Section 5

"80% accurate" is a claim about the test, not about today

A regime classifier flags "trend day." Validation says it's 80% accurate. Only 20% of days actually are trend days. It just fired. How confident should you be?

Try it

H = "today is a trend day" → P(H) = 0.20, P(¬H) = 0.80.
E = "the classifier fires the flag."
P(E|H) = 0.80 — true positive rate.
P(E|¬H) = 0.20 — false positive rate (accuracy is symmetric here).

The intuitive, wrong answer is "80%" — that's the number that got quoted. The correct answer needs the prior too.
0.80×0.20 = 0.16
+
0.20×0.80 = 0.16
=
P(E) = 0.32
True positives (real trend days correctly flagged) and false positives (reversion days flagged anyway) come out exactly equal — 0.16 each.
0.16
÷
0.32
=
50%
P(trend day | flagged) = 50% — a coin flip, not 80%. Half of every flagged day is a false alarm.

Nothing about the classifier is broken — 80% accuracy is real. What broke is the assumption that "accurate" and "trustworthy on any single flag" mean the same thing. They don't, once the thing being flagged is rare.

Section 6

The same arithmetic, as things you can count

Percentages hide this. Counting out of 1,000 days doesn't — Gigerenzer's "natural frequencies" trick.

Out of 1,000 days, 200 really are trend days and 800 aren't. The 80%-accurate classifier correctly flags 160 of the real 200 — but it also incorrectly flags 160 of the 800 reversion days. Those two highlighted flagged boxes are the same size. Of the 320 total days that get flagged, only 160 — half — are the real thing.

Section 7

Watch the posterior swing — drag the base rate

Same classifier throughout. Only the base rate — how rare the flagged thing actually is — changes. Watch what that alone does to your confidence in a single flagged day.

Try it
Base rate P(trend day)20%
Classifier accuracy80%
Drag the base rate down toward 5% and the posterior collapses well below 50%, even though accuracy never moved. Drag it up past 50% and the posterior starts to exceed the accuracy figure. The classifier didn't change — your confidence in any one flag should, because it was never really about the classifier alone.

Section 9

Today's posterior is tomorrow's prior

Bayes' theorem doesn't care whether P(H) came from a textbook base rate or from yesterday's posterior.

Chaining evidence
P(H) = 20%
evidence E₁ →
P(H|E₁) = 50%
= new prior
evidence E₂ →
P(H|E₁,E₂) = ?
That's the entire trick of doing this repeatedly: multiply the old belief by how much more, or less, likely the new data makes it. Nothing is thrown out and re-derived from zero.

This is formally what "the market updates on news" or "a classifier updates on each new bar" means. It underlies Kalman filters — rolling estimates of a drifting parameter like beta or volatility — and it's the justification for the rule "never trust a signal in isolation": weight it by how rare or common the regime it claims to detect actually is, then let the next bar update that weight again rather than resetting to a blank slate.

Where this needs qualifying

The simplified chaining above assumes each new piece of evidence is independent of the others, given H. Two correlated signals — say two "levels" that are both really just re-detecting the same daily pivot — get double-counted as if they were separate confirmations, inflating the posterior further than the evidence actually earns.

Section 10

"My strategy has an 80% win rate" is the same fallacy, different costume

The naive read

An 80% accurate classifier or an 80% win-rate strategy sounds like "4 times out of 5, trust it." That's quietly reporting P(E|H) — how the system behaves when the underlying claim is true — as if it were P(H|E) — how likely the claim is true given the system fired. Old enough to have a name: the prosecutor's fallacy.

The Bayesian read

How much a flagged day, or a winning-looking setup, should move your belief depends on the base rate of that setup actually working — plus the false-positive rate you never asked about. A Bayes factor of 4 (0.80/0.20 here) is real, "substantial" evidence on Jeffreys' scale — it just isn't the same claim as an 80% posterior.

🩺

Medical & diagnostic testing

A rare condition plus an imperfect test almost always means a positive result is less certain than it feels — the textbook version of this lesson's worked example.

📧

Spam filters & fraud detection

Built as literal Bayesian classifiers (naive Bayes): each feature is evidence that updates the posterior multiplicatively, one at a time.

🧪

A/B testing

Reports "87% probability variant B is better" instead of a p-value, because that's the statement people actually want and a p-value doesn't say it.

Where this needs qualifying

A large Bayes factor applied to a very unlikely prior can still land at a modest posterior — "30 times more likely" is not the same claim as "30 times more probable to be true." And a Bayesian confluence score is a hypothesis this project hasn't built or validated — it would have to beat the incumbent weighted score out-of-sample before replacing anything live, not just sound more principled.

Section 11

Quick check

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

A test is "80% accurate" at detecting a rare condition. What does that number actually describe?

Accuracy figures are almost always the likelihood, P(E|H) — not the posterior P(H|E) you actually want. Confusing the two is the entire mechanism behind the base-rate fallacy, and it has a name: the prosecutor's fallacy.
Question 2

In the trend-day example (20% prior, 80%-accurate classifier), why did the posterior land at 50% instead of 80%?

0.80×0.20 (true positives) = 0.20×0.80 (false positives) = 0.16 each. The flag alone can't tell you which pool a given alarm came from, so it splits 50/50.
Question 3

What does "sequential updating" mean in practice?

Bayes' theorem doesn't care where P(H) came from — a textbook base rate or yesterday's posterior work identically. The old belief is preserved and adjusted, never discarded and re-derived from a blank slate.
Question 4

A Bayes factor of 30 means...

A Bayes factor isolates what the evidence itself is worth, with the prior completely factored out. It is not a posterior probability — a large factor applied to a very unlikely prior can still land at a modest posterior.
Question 5

What's the core disagreement between Bayesian and frequentist statistics?

A frequentist p-value answers "how surprising is this data if the null were true?" A Bayesian posterior answers "what should I now believe about the hypothesis?" — genuinely different questions, not two routes to the same number.
Section 12

What to carry forward

01

Bayes' theorem is derived, not assumed — it falls straight out of the definition of conditional probability, applied two ways to the same quantity.

02

A piece of evidence is not the probability it implies — how much it should move your belief depends on the prior, the base rate of the thing before you saw anything.

03

The base-rate fallacy is the single most common failure — an "80% accurate" test on a rare (20%) event gives only a 50% posterior, not 80%, because false alarms from the much larger opposite population catch up to true alarms.

04

Sequential updating is just reusing yesterday's posterior as today's prior — multiply by the new likelihood ratio; nothing gets re-derived from scratch.

05

A Bayes factor isolates what the evidence itself is worth, separate from the prior — 1–3 barely worth mentioning, 3–10 "substantial," 10–30 "strong," 100+ "decisive" (Jeffreys' scale).

What this now lets you do

Read any "X% accurate" claim — a classifier, a strategy's win rate, a screening test — and ask for the base rate before believing the headline number. Recognise when a flag has moved your belief versus resolved it. Chain evidence correctly across multiple bars or signals without silently double-counting correlated ones.
