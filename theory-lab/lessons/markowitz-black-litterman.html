Markowitz & Black-Litterman

Harry Markowitz gave portfolio construction its first real mathematics — and, almost immediately, practitioners discovered the mathematics was so sensitive to its own inputs that it was barely usable as written. Black-Litterman is the fix everyone actually uses: start from what the market already believes, and nudge it, rather than trying to specify the whole truth yourself.

CONCEPT — the theory behind this repo's diversification tooling ~18 min read Prereqs: Linear Algebra & Matrices, Correlation & Linear Regression primer
TL;DR
  • Portfolio variance is a quadratic form \(w^\top \Sigma w\), not a weighted average — the cross-covariance terms are exactly where diversification benefit lives, the same idea this project's own diversification research measures via effective-bets metrics rather than prescribes weights for.
  • Solving for the minimum-variance combination at every target return traces the efficient frontier; its leftmost point is the minimum-variance portfolio.
  • Markowitz's optimizer is extremely sensitive to the expected-return inputs specifically (far more than to the covariance matrix) — small forecast nudges can flip "optimal" weights into extreme long/short corner solutions, the "error-maximization" flaw that kept it from being used naively for decades.
  • Black-Litterman fixes this by starting from a market-equilibrium prior \(\Pi = \delta\Sigma w_{mkt}\) and Bayesian-blending it with a handful of confidence-weighted investor views, rather than asking for a precise forecast for every instrument.
  • Nothing in this repo currently runs either optimizer — every strategy here is single-instrument; this is the theory a future multi-instrument portfolio-construction layer, built on this project's own existing covariance machinery, would sit on.
Section 01

Why this matters

Every strategy in this repo, so far, answers a single-instrument question: given this pair, this level, this regime, what's the edge? Nothing in the codebase currently answers the next question up — given a basket of positions across many instruments, how much of each should you actually hold at once? That second question is portfolio construction, and it has had a formal mathematical answer since 1952: Harry Markowitz's mean-variance framework, the theory that a portfolio's risk is not the average of its holdings' individual risks but a function of how they move together, and that there's a mathematically identifiable "best" trade-off between expected return and risk for any given set of assets and beliefs about them.

This repo already has real infrastructure one layer below this question: this project already computes correlation matrices and their eigenvalue structure to answer "how many genuinely independent bets does this basket have" (the subject of the PCA & Factor Regimes lesson). Markowitz's framework is the natural next layer on top of exactly that correlation matrix — instead of just describing how concentrated a basket is, it would prescribe what weights to hold. This lesson builds that framework from the ground up, is honest about the well-documented practical flaw that kept it from being used naively for decades, and explains the fix — Black-Litterman — that turned it into something real asset managers actually run. None of this is built here yet; this lesson is the theory a future portfolio-construction layer would sit on.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Not "how much do I own," but "how much do I own together"

Imagine you're told two currency pairs each have the same expected return and the same volatility, and asked which is the better portfolio holding. The question as posed is unanswerable — it depends entirely on what else is already in the portfolio. If the rest of your book is long commodity currencies and short-dollar, and pair A tends to move with that book while pair B tends to move against it, then B is doing real work — it's dampening the swings of everything else — even though, in isolation, A and B look identical. Markowitz's core insight, in one sentence, is that a position's risk contribution to a portfolio is not its own volatility — it's its volatility filtered through how it co-moves with everything else you hold. A portfolio's variance is not a weighted average of its parts' variances; it's a weighted sum that also depends on every pairwise covariance between those parts, and that second term is where diversification benefit — or its absence — actually lives.

The practical punch line follows immediately: for any target level of expected return, there is one specific combination of weights across your available instruments that achieves it with the least possible risk — because some combinations happen to cancel more of each other's noise than others. Find that combination for every possible target return, and you've traced out a curve: the efficient frontier. Everything below and to the right of that curve is a portfolio someone could improve — more return for the same risk, or less risk for the same return — just by reweighting. Everything on the curve is, given your inputs, as good as it gets.

Section 02

The math, step by step

This builds directly on the matrix-vector notation from the Linear Algebra & Matrices lesson — a portfolio's risk and return are both, quite literally, a vector and a matrix acting on that vector, and nothing more exotic than that.

Step 1 — portfolio expected return

Say you're choosing weights across \(N\) instruments — \(w_i\) is the fraction of the portfolio (as a share of capital, or of risk budget) held in instrument \(i\), and \(E[R_i]\) is your belief about instrument \(i\)'s expected return over the horizon you care about. A portfolio's expected return is simply the weighted average of its parts:

\[ E[R_p] = \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i\, E[R_i] \]
wᵢthe fraction of the portfolio held in instrument i
E[Rᵢ]your belief about instrument i's expected return
E[R_p] = w^T μportfolio expected return — a dot product of weights and the expected-return vector

Nothing subtle here — if you're 60% long EURUSD with an expected return of 2% and 40% long AUDJPY with an expected return of 5%, the portfolio's expected return is \(0.6(2\%) + 0.4(5\%) = 3.2\%\). Written as a matrix expression, with \(w\) the column vector of weights and \(\mu\) the column vector of expected returns, this is just \(E[R_p] = w^\top \mu\) — a dot product, the simplest possible matrix-vector operation.

Step 2 — portfolio variance (the part that isn't just an average)

Portfolio risk does not work the same simple way, and this is the entire reason Markowitz's paper mattered. Let \(\Sigma\) be the covariance matrix of the instruments' returns (the same object built in the PCA lesson: an \(N \times N\) grid whose diagonal holds each instrument's own variance and whose off-diagonal entries hold the pairwise covariances). Portfolio variance is the quadratic form:

\[ \text{Var}(R_p) = w^\top \Sigma w \]
wportfolio weight vector
Σcovariance matrix of instrument returns
w^T Σ wportfolio variance — a quadratic form, not an average

Read this the same way the Linear Algebra lesson reads \(Av\): first \(\Sigma w\) is the covariance matrix acting on the weight vector — a new vector describing how much each instrument's variance-and-covariance exposure gets scaled by the weights — and then \(w^\top(\cdot)\) takes the dot product of that result with the weights again, folding everything into one number. Expand it for two instruments and the two-term structure becomes visible directly:

\[ \text{Var}(R_p) = w_1^2 \sigma_1^2 + w_2^2 \sigma_2^2 + 2 w_1 w_2\, \text{Cov}(R_1, R_2) \]
w₁²σ₁², w₂²σ₂²each instrument's own variance, scaled by its weight squared
2w₁w₂Cov(R₁,R₂)the cross term — where diversification benefit actually lives
signcan be negative — a negative covariance actively subtracts from portfolio variance

The first two terms are exactly what you'd expect — each instrument's own variance, scaled by its weight squared. The third term is the one a naive "risk is just a weighted average" intuition misses entirely: it can be zero (uncorrelated instruments, no interaction), positive (correlated instruments amplifying each other's risk), or negative — if \(\text{Cov}(R_1,R_2)\) is negative, that cross term actively subtracts from total portfolio variance, meaning the combined position can genuinely be less risky than either instrument held alone. That's diversification benefit, made numerically explicit rather than asserted qualitatively — and it's precisely the same covariance matrix machinery this project already builds and eigen-decomposes for its effective-bets metrics.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Adding up risk isn't like adding up weights on a scale — it's more like packing umbrellas for a trip. If everyone brings the exact same umbrella, you're carrying N times the bulk of one umbrella. But if half the group brings umbrellas and the other half brings sunscreen (things useful in opposite weather), the group as a whole is prepared for more with less total stuff, because the two kinds of gear partly substitute for each other. That "partly substitute for each other" effect is exactly the cross term in the portfolio-variance formula, and it's the entire mathematical reason diversification works.

Step 3 — the efficient frontier

Fix a target expected return and ask: among every combination of weights that achieves it, which one has the smallest \(w^\top \Sigma w\)? Solve that question for every achievable target return in turn (this is a constrained optimization — minimize variance subject to hitting a given return and the weights summing to 1 — whose full derivation isn't needed to use the result, so it's not repeated here) and the collection of answers traces a curve in expected-return-versus-risk space: the efficient frontier.

The frontier has a characteristic shape: it curves, rather than forming a straight line, because of exactly the cross-covariance term from Step 2 — diversification benefit means you can often get more expected return for a given increase in risk than a naive linear trade-off would suggest, especially near the low-risk end. The single leftmost point on the curve — the portfolio with the lowest possible variance achievable from any combination of the available instruments, full stop, regardless of what return it happens to deliver — is called the minimum-variance portfolio. Every point on the curve above and to the right of it is "efficient" (more return, deliberately accepting more risk to get it); the mirror-image lower branch of the same curve (same risk, but lower return than the minimum-variance point already achieves at that risk) is mathematically reachable but never rational to hold — it's strictly dominated by the minimum-variance point itself, which gets the same risk for a better return.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Picture every possible mix of your instruments plotted as a dot on a "how much risk, how much return" map. Most dots are wasteful — you could get more return for the same risk, or less risk for the same return, just by mixing differently. The efficient frontier is the outer edge of that whole cloud of dots: the best deal available at every risk level, with nothing better sitting further out.

Step 4 — Markowitz's famous practical flaw

Everything above is clean, correct mathematics, and for decades it was also, in practice, close to unusable — for a specific, well-documented reason that is worth stating plainly rather than glossing over: the optimizer is extremely sensitive to the expected-return inputs, \(E[R_i]\), far more sensitive than to the covariance matrix \(\Sigma\). Covariances can be estimated reasonably stably from historical data (that's exactly the correlation-matrix machinery this repo already has); expected returns cannot — a return forecast is inherently the least reliable, most opinion-laden number in the whole model, yet it's the input the optimizer's output depends on most violently.

Concretely: nudge one instrument's expected-return forecast by a fraction of a percentage point — well within any honest forecaster's margin of error — and the "optimal" weights can swing from a modest long position to an enormous long, or flip sign entirely into a large short, often paired with an equally extreme offsetting position elsewhere to keep the target return and budget constraints satisfied. The optimizer isn't malfunctioning; it's doing exactly what it was asked to do — find the corner of the feasible region that squeezes the most return-per-unit-of-variance out of whatever numbers it was given, taken as if they were exact. But because real return forecasts are never exact, the result is a portfolio that looks like a confident, extreme bet built on top of what was actually a vague, low-conviction estimate. This isn't a minor footnote — it's the single most cited practical criticism of mean-variance optimization (sometimes called the optimizer's "error-maximization" property: it doesn't just fail to filter out estimation error, it actively concentrates the portfolio in exactly the assets where your errors happen to look most favorable), and it's the reason "just run Markowitz" was, for a long time, something academics recommended and desks quietly avoided doing literally.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Tell a hyper-literal genie "give me the best possible outcome using exactly these numbers, no questions asked," and a tiny nudge to one number can send the genie's answer to a wildly different, extreme place — because the genie treats every number you gave it as gospel truth, not as a rough guess with a fuzzy margin of error. That's the raw optimizer: it isn't broken, it's just taking your uncertain forecasts far more literally than you ever meant them.

Step 5 — Black-Litterman: start from the market, not from a blank page

Fischer Black and Robert Litterman's 1992 fix does not abandon mean-variance optimization — the frontier, the covariance matrix, the quadratic-form variance are all still exactly what's described above. What changes is where the expected-return vector comes from. Instead of asking an investor to specify a precise expected return for every single instrument (the step that made the optimizer so fragile), Black- Litterman starts from the assumption that current market prices already embed a reasonable estimate of expected returns — a direct extension of the same "take the market seriously as a strong prior, unless you have a specific reason not to" framing from the EMH lesson. If everyone's capital, in aggregate, is currently allocated according to today's market-cap (or, in an FX context, position/flow) weights, then those weights themselves imply a specific vector of expected returns — the returns that would make the current allocation the mean-variance-optimal one. That implied vector, called \(\Pi\) (capital pi), is recovered by literally reversing the optimization:

\[ \Pi = \delta\, \Sigma\, w_{mkt} \]
Π(capital pi) the equilibrium expected-return vector — the Bayesian prior
δrisk-aversion scalar, calibrated from the market portfolio's own return/variance
Σ w_mktthe same covariance-acting-on-weights operation from Step 2, run in reverse
w_mktcurrent market-equilibrium (or flow/position) weights
Σ w_mkt
The market's own risk
Covariance acting on today's equilibrium weights, same operation as Step 2's w^TΣw

where \(w_{mkt}\) is the vector of current market-equilibrium weights and \(\delta\) is a single risk-aversion scalar calibrated from the market portfolio's own observed return and variance. Note the right-hand side: it's the covariance matrix acting on a weight vector, the exact same \(\Sigma w\) operation from Step 2 — reverse-engineering equilibrium returns from equilibrium weights uses nothing more exotic than the forward calculation run backwards.

This equilibrium vector \(\Pi\) becomes the prior. The investor is then asked for something far smaller and more honest than "the expected return of every asset in the basket": a handful of specific views, each with its own stated confidence — for example, "I believe EURUSD will outperform GBPUSD by 2% over the next quarter, and I hold that view with moderate confidence." A view like that is the likelihood. Black-Litterman then blends the equilibrium prior with the investor's views to produce a posterior expected-return vector — the number that actually gets fed into the optimizer from Step 3, in place of raw, unmoderated forecasts.

That blend is explicitly Bayesian, in exactly the sense built up in the Bayesian Inference lesson: prior belief, updated by new evidence, in proportion to how confident each side is, produces a posterior belief. The cleanest way to see the mechanics is the two-normal-distributions case that Bayesian updating reduces to when both the prior and the "evidence" are treated as noisy estimates of the same unknown quantity: if the prior estimate is \(\mu_0\) with variance \(\sigma_0^2\), and a new, independent estimate comes in at \(\mu_1\) with variance \(\sigma_1^2\), the standard result for combining them (precision-weighted averaging, precision being the inverse of variance) is:

\[ \mu_{\text{post}} = \frac{\mu_0/\sigma_0^2 + \mu_1/\sigma_1^2}{1/\sigma_0^2 + 1/\sigma_1^2}, \qquad \sigma_{\text{post}}^2 = \frac{1}{1/\sigma_0^2 + 1/\sigma_1^2} \]
μ₀, σ₀²the prior estimate and its variance (uncertainty)
μ₁, σ₁²the new estimate — the view — and its own variance
1/σ²precision — inverse variance; each side pulls in proportion to it
μ_postthe precision-weighted blended posterior mean

Each side's pull on the posterior mean is weighted by its own precision — a low-uncertainty estimate (small variance, large \(1/\sigma^2\)) pulls the blended answer strongly toward itself; a high-uncertainty estimate barely moves it. This is precisely the logic Black-Litterman applies at portfolio scale: the equilibrium prior \(\Pi\) carries an uncertainty (scaled by a parameter usually called \(\tau\), applied to \(\Sigma\) itself), and each investor view carries its own stated uncertainty (the matrix \(\Omega\), literally the numerical expression of "moderate confidence" versus "very confident"). The full multi-asset, multi-view version of the same precision-weighting idea is the model's published master formula:

\[ E[R]_{BL} = \left[(\tau\Sigma)^{-1} + P^\top \Omega^{-1} P\right]^{-1} \left[(\tau\Sigma)^{-1}\Pi + P^\top \Omega^{-1} Q\right] \]
τΣthe prior's uncertainty — a scaling of the covariance matrix
P, Qthe pick matrix (which instruments each view touches) and the view-return vector
Ω(capital omega) the confidence matrix — how sure the investor is in each view
E[R]_BLthe posterior expected-return vector actually fed to the optimizer

where \(Q\) is the vector of view returns ("EURUSD outperforms GBPUSD by 2%"), \(P\) is a "pick matrix" that encodes which instruments each view touches, and \(\Omega\) is the confidence matrix for those views. It looks more intimidating than it is: it is the exact matrix generalization of the scalar precision-weighted average two equations above — the equilibrium prior and the investor's views, each weighted by how much uncertainty is attached to it, blended into one posterior expected-return vector. That posterior — never the raw, unmoderated \(\Pi\) alone, and never the raw view alone — is what gets handed to the Step 3 optimizer.

This is exactly what tames the Step 4 instability. A single strong view on one pair no longer gets fed to the optimizer as if it were an exact, isolated fact about that one instrument in a vacuum — it gets blended with the market's own equilibrium belief about that instrument and everything correlated with it, moderated by exactly how confident the investor said they were. A modest-confidence view produces a modest pull away from equilibrium; the market prior, not the investor's raw forecast, still dominates unless the view is both large and stated with high confidence. The corner-solution, wildly-extreme-weight failure mode of naive Markowitz largely disappears, not because the underlying optimization math changed, but because the input feeding it is no longer a fragile, ungrounded guess — it's a market-anchored belief nudged by a small, explicitly-quantified amount of genuinely new information.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Black-Litterman starts from "the market's already-existing best guess" instead of a blank page, then lets you nudge that guess only as hard as you're actually willing to bet on your own opinion. A whispered hunch barely moves the needle; a loudly, confidently stated view moves it a lot further — but even the loudest view is still just pulling on a sensible starting point, not replacing it outright, which is exactly what keeps the final answer from swinging to an extreme.

Worked example

How small an input change it takes to flip the optimizer's answer

Take a simplified two-instrument portfolio — a "safe" pair with expected return 3% and a "risky" pair with expected return 4%, annualized volatilities of 8% and 15%, and a modest positive correlation of 0.3 between them. Solving for the maximum-return-per-unit-of-variance portfolio (the tangency portfolio, the standard mean-variance target) with these exact inputs might come back with something intuitively sensible — say, roughly 65% in the safe pair and 35% in the risky one.

Now nudge only the risky pair's expected return from 4% to 4.5% — a half-percentage-point change, easily within the noise of any realistic forecasting process, and arguably not even a meaningfully different belief about the asset. Because the optimizer is solving a ratio (expected excess return divided by risk contribution) rather than an absolute comparison, that small change can be enough to swing the optimal weight in the risky pair sharply higher — plausibly past 60-70%, sometimes further if a short position on the safe leg is permitted, in order to squeeze more of the now-slightly-more-attractive risky return into the portfolio. Nothing about the real-world relationship between these two instruments changed; a rounding-level adjustment to one input the investor was never confident about to begin with produced a dramatically different "optimal" allocation. Scale this up to a basket of eight or ten correlated FX pairs, where small return forecasts differ from each other by fractions of a percent and the covariance structure has several near-offsetting relationships, and the instability compounds — this is exactly the mechanism behind the notorious extreme long/short corner solutions naive Markowitz optimization is known to produce.

Black-Litterman defuses exactly this failure mode by construction: if that same "risky pair, +4.5%" belief is expressed as a single, explicit view with moderate (not absolute) confidence, and blended with an equilibrium prior built from the whole basket's actual market weights, the posterior expected return for the risky pair moves only partway from its equilibrium value toward 4.5% — precision-weighted by how confident the view was stated to be — rather than being taken as exact. The resulting weight shift is a moderate tilt, not a corner solution.

Real-world trading scenario

One modest AUD/NZD view, two very different portfolios

Suppose a trader holds a specific, limited view: "I think AUD will outperform NZD by about 1.5% over the next quarter, with moderate confidence" — a perfectly ordinary, realistic piece of conviction, the kind any G10-macro desk forms routinely. Now consider two different ways of turning that single view into a portfolio.

Fed into a raw Markowitz optimizer — with every other instrument's expected return left at zero, or at a naive historical average, since the trader has no other specific views — that one modest, hedged-sounding statement can produce startlingly immoderate output: a large long AUD position, a large offsetting short NZD position, sized far beyond what "moderate confidence in a 1.5% difference" should imply. This isn't a bug in the arithmetic; it's exactly the Step 4 sensitivity problem playing out on a single-view basket — with almost nothing else in the input vector to compete against it, the optimizer treats the one specific number it was given as if it were certain, and sizes the position to fully exploit it, unconstrained by anything resembling the trader's actual, moderate conviction.

Fed into Black-Litterman instead, the same view is blended with the market-implied equilibrium returns for the whole currency basket — not just AUD and NZD in isolation — and the blend is weighted by the confidence the trader actually stated. The result is a materially more moderate tilt: a real, directional lean toward long AUD / short NZD, appropriately larger than doing nothing, but nowhere near the raw optimizer's extreme corner solution — because the view was treated as one moderately-confident update to a reasonable market-anchored starting point, not as the entire, exact truth about the world. The size of the resulting position scales sensibly with the stated confidence: a "high confidence" version of the same view would produce a larger tilt than a "low confidence" version, in a way the raw optimizer — which has no native concept of "confidence," only point estimates — cannot represent at all.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

In institutional asset management, essentially nobody runs unconstrained naive Markowitz mean-variance optimization on raw analyst forecasts and trades the output directly — the extreme-weight problem from Step 4 is too well known and too easily demonstrated for that to survive a risk committee. What actually gets used is some combination of: Black-Litterman or a similar Bayesian-shrinkage approach to moderate the expected-return inputs; hard position and turnover constraints layered on top of the optimizer (capping any single position's weight, capping how far the optimizer can move from a benchmark or from last period's holdings); and, very commonly, simply not optimizing on expected returns at all — running "risk parity" or minimum-variance-only portfolios that use just the (comparatively stable) covariance matrix and skip the fragile expected-return vector entirely, accepting a less "optimal" but far more robust allocation.

🧭

Black-Litterman / Bayesian shrinkage

Moderate the expected-return inputs themselves before they ever reach the optimizer — the fix this lesson builds in detail above.

🔒

Hard position & turnover constraints

Cap any single position's weight, or how far the optimizer can move from a benchmark or last period's holdings, regardless of what the raw math wants.

⚖️

Risk-parity / minimum-variance-only

Skip the fragile expected-return vector entirely and optimize only on the comparatively stable covariance matrix — less "optimal," far more robust.

Black-Litterman specifically is widely used at multi-asset and macro-overlay desks for exactly the reason built up above: it lets a portfolio manager express a small number of genuine, specific convictions — the two or three trades they actually have a view on this quarter — without being forced to fabricate a precise, false-precision forecast for every other instrument in the universe just to make the optimizer run. Everything the manager has no view on simply stays at its market-implied equilibrium value, which is a far more defensible default than an arbitrary "flat" or "zero" assumption. The confidence parameter (\(\Omega\)) is also a genuine, separate lever from the view itself — the same directional belief, stated with low versus high confidence, produces a smaller versus larger tilt away from equilibrium, which maps naturally onto how position sizing already ought to scale with conviction (the same theme the Kelly Criterion lesson develops from a different angle — sizing should track how sure you actually are, not just the sign of your view).

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where Markowitz/Black-Litterman goes wrong in practice

Trusting the raw optimizer's output as if the inputs were exact. This is Step 4 restated as a warning rather than a description: an unconstrained Markowitz optimization is, by construction, maximally exploiting whatever estimation error is hiding in your expected-return inputs. If the output allocation looks surprisingly extreme or concentrated, the correct read is almost always "the inputs were more uncertain than the model was told," not "the model found a hidden edge."

Treating Black-Litterman's moderation as a free pass on the view quality. Blending with an equilibrium prior tames the optimizer's instability — it does not validate the view itself. A confidently-stated but wrong view still pulls the posterior in the wrong direction; Black-Litterman just prevents that pull from being amplified into an absurd position size. The model is honest about uncertainty in the blending step, not about whether the view is correct in the first place.

Getting the confidence matrix (\(\Omega\)) wrong in either direction. Set view confidence too high (\(\Omega\) too small) and Black-Litterman degenerates back toward raw Markowitz's instability for that view; set it too low and the view barely moves the portfolio at all, making the whole exercise pointless. There's no universal "correct" \(\Omega\) — it has to reflect the investor's genuine conviction, and different reasonable choices of \(\tau\) and \(\Omega\) can produce meaningfully different posteriors from the same stated view, a real practical calibration burden the model doesn't remove.

Ignoring transaction costs and turnover. Both frameworks, as derived above, are single-period and frictionless — they say nothing about how expensive it is to trade from today's actual holdings to the newly "optimal" weights. Re-optimizing every period on updated inputs, without a turnover penalty or rebalancing band, can produce a portfolio that chases small, noisy shifts in the frontier at real cost, eroding exactly the theoretical edge the optimization was meant to capture.

An FX-specific wrinkle: there is no obvious "market portfolio" to reverse-engineer \(\Pi\) from. Black-Litterman's equilibrium step leans on equity markets' natural market-cap weighting — a real, observable, economically meaningful quantity. FX has no equivalent single number; net global positioning, central-bank reserve composition, or simple equal-weighting across a chosen basket are all workable stand-ins, but each embeds its own assumption about what "equilibrium" means for currencies, and that choice should be made deliberately and stated, not treated as a solved, off-the-shelf input the way it effectively is in equities.

✓Markowitz's optimizer is extremely sensitive to the expected-return inputs specifically. A fraction-of-a-percent forecast nudge can flip "optimal" weights into extreme long/short corners — the well-documented "error-maximization" flaw.
✓Black-Litterman fixes this by starting from a market-equilibrium prior. \(\Pi = \delta\Sigma w_{mkt}\), Bayesian-blended with a handful of confidence-weighted investor views, replaces the fragile raw forecast vector.
✓Nobody runs unconstrained naive Markowitz in practice. Real desks use Black-Litterman or similar shrinkage, hard position/turnover constraints, or skip expected returns entirely with risk-parity/min-variance-only portfolios.
⚠None of this is built in this repo yet. Every strategy here is single-instrument; a portfolio-construction layer would be genuinely new infrastructure, not a quick extension of the existing diversification tooling.
⚠Black-Litterman moderates the optimizer's instability — it does not validate the view. A confidently-stated but wrong view still pulls the posterior in the wrong direction; the model just prevents that pull from becoming an absurd position size.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. Why does portfolio variance, \(w^\top \Sigma w\), depend on more than just each instrument's own variance and its weight?
Because the quadratic form \(w^\top \Sigma w\) includes cross terms — \(2 w_i w_j \text{Cov}(R_i, R_j)\) for every pair of instruments — that capture how the instruments move together, not just how much each moves on its own. Those cross terms can be positive (correlated instruments compounding each other's variance), negative (anti-correlated instruments partially canceling each other's variance, i.e. genuine diversification benefit), or near zero (independent instruments, variances simply add). A weighted average of individual variances would miss all of this — it's exactly the covariance structure, not just the individual risks, that determines whether a combination of positions is more or less risky than its parts.
Q2. Why is the raw Markowitz optimizer so sensitive to the expected-return inputs specifically, more than to the covariance matrix?
Covariances can be estimated with reasonable stability from historical return data — that's exactly the kind of matrix this project already builds. Expected returns cannot be estimated nearly as reliably; a forecast of future return is inherently the least certain input in the model, often little more than an informed guess with a wide error band. Yet the optimizer is solving for the combination that maximizes return-per-unit-of-risk, a ratio that is acutely sensitive to small changes in the numerator (expected return) whenever assets have similar risk characteristics. The result is that a fraction-of-a-percent nudge to an uncertain, low-conviction number can flip the "optimal" weights dramatically — the model is amplifying noise in exactly the input that has the most noise to begin with.
Q3. What role does the "market equilibrium prior" play in Black-Litterman, and why is that a genuinely Bayesian idea rather than just a convenient default?
The equilibrium prior \(\Pi\) — the expected returns reverse-engineered from current market weights via \(\Pi = \delta \Sigma w_{mkt}\) — plays the role of the starting belief that gets updated, exactly as a prior does in Bayesian inference: it's the answer you'd give before incorporating any new, specific information. It's genuinely Bayesian, not just a convenient placeholder, because the model then combines it with new evidence (the investor's views) via precision weighting — each side pulling the posterior in proportion to how confident it is — which is the same structural logic as combining a prior probability with new evidence via Bayes' rule. Using market prices as that starting belief is also a direct application of the EMH lesson's framing: taking the market as a reasonable prior, worth deviating from only when you have a specific, evidenced reason to (a stated view), not by default.
Q4. In the AUD/NZD scenario, why does the raw Markowitz optimizer produce an extreme position from a "moderate confidence" view, while Black-Litterman produces a moderate one?
The raw optimizer has no way to represent "moderate confidence" at all — it only accepts a point estimate (AUD outperforms NZD by 1.5%) and, with little else in the input vector to compete against that number, it treats it as exact and sizes the position to fully exploit it. Black- Litterman, by contrast, requires the view to come with an explicit confidence (\(\Omega\)), and blends it with the market-equilibrium prior for the whole basket via precision weighting — a moderate-confidence view pulls the posterior only partway from equilibrium toward the view's stated number, producing a real but proportionate tilt rather than a corner solution. The size of the resulting position scales with the stated confidence in a way the raw optimizer structurally cannot represent.
Q5. What, specifically, would need to be built for this repo to go from its current correlation/eigenvalue diversification tooling to an actual Black-Litterman-style portfolio optimizer, and why is that a large step rather than a small one?
The covariance-matrix piece — the correlation-matrix and eigenvalue computation this project already has — is already there and is exactly the \(\Sigma\) both the variance formula and the equilibrium-return formula need. What's missing is substantial: a source for expected-return views (or a principled way to source/estimate them per instrument), a defensible definition of "market-equilibrium weights" in an FX context (which has no natural market-cap analogue the way equities do), the actual constrained- optimization solver, and — per this repo's standard discipline — a real IS/OOS validation of whatever the combined portfolio-construction layer produces, since the way signals get combined is its own claim, separate from whether the underlying signals individually have edge. It's a new, meaningfully sized piece of infrastructure sitting on top of an existing foundation, not a small extension of it.
Q6. Why does the efficient frontier's lower branch (same risk as the minimum-variance portfolio's neighborhood, but lower return) never actually get held, even though it's mathematically reachable?
For any given level of risk on that lower branch, the minimum-variance portfolio (or a nearby point on the upper branch) achieves the same risk with a strictly higher expected return — the lower branch is "dominated" in the sense that there's always a reachable alternative that's at least as good on risk and strictly better on return. Rational portfolio choice, by definition, never accepts a dominated option when an equal-risk, higher-return alternative is available, which is exactly why only the upper branch of the curve is called the "efficient" frontier — the lower branch is mathematically part of the same curve but economically irrelevant.
