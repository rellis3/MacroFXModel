Neural SDEs & Neural SPDEs

Every model in this sequence hand-specifies μ and σ — a drift and a diffusion someone wrote down because it seemed to describe the world. The last step this curriculum's SDE thread can take: what if a neural network learns μ and σ instead?

CONCEPT — current machine-learning research, not built, tested, or validated anywhere in this repo ~14 min read Prereqs: Existence & Uniqueness of SDE Solutions, Numerical Schemes: Euler-Maruyama & Milstein
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • A neural SDE replaces the hand-specified drift and diffusion, \(\mu(x,t)\) and \(\sigma(x,t)\), with neural networks \(\mu_\theta(x,t)\) and \(\sigma_\theta(x,t)\), trained so the SDE's simulated paths (or distributions) match observed data.
  • The idea traces to Tzen & Raginsky (2019) and was formalized as a full generative-modelling framework — "neural SDEs as infinite-dimensional GANs" — by Kidger, Foster, Li & Lyons (2021).
  • Naively backpropagating through every simulated Euler-Maruyama step is memory-expensive (it has to store every intermediate state); the fix, the stochastic adjoint sensitivity method (Li, Wong, Chen & Duvenaud, 2020), computes gradients by solving a second, backward SDE instead — the direct stochastic analogue of the Neural-ODE adjoint method (Chen et al., 2018).
  • Neural SPDEs generalize this further, to infinite-dimensional, spatio-temporal fields (Salvi, Lemercier & Gerasimovics, 2021) — an implied-volatility surface evolving stochastically over time is exactly the kind of object (a field indexed by strike and tenor, evolving in time) an SPDE models.
  • This is current, active machine-learning research machinery — considerably further from a testable trading signal than anything else in this curriculum. Nothing in this lesson claims it has been built, tested, or shown to produce edge anywhere in this repo.
  • How it reached trading: deep hedging (Buehler et al., 2019) and, from around 2020, neural SDEs calibrated to option prices brought the idea into quant finance; it is still research, not a desk standard.

Every model in this sequence has someone deciding the drift and the volatility. Square roots, mean reversion, power laws. Why trust their choice?

You shouldn't trust it blindly. That choice is a real commitment, and it can be wrong in ways you only find out later. A neural SDE asks: what if the data chooses instead?

How does that work?

Keep the exact same skeleton: a drift term plus a noise term. But instead of writing a formula for each, you plug in two small neural networks. Their weights are the knobs.

And you tune the knobs how?

Simulate paths from the current networks. Compare them with real market paths. Nudge the weights so the fakes look more like the real thing. Kidger and co-authors framed this in 2021 as a forger and a detective: one network makes paths, another tries to tell them from real ones.

Sounds expensive to train.

The obvious way is. Ordinary training has to remember every step of every simulated path. Take a curve of fifty points simulated over a thousand steps. That is fifty thousand numbers to store, before you count the networks' own workings. Double the steps and you double the memory.

So what's the trick?

The adjoint method, from Li and colleagues in 2020. Instead of storing the forward path, you solve a second random equation that runs backward in time and hands you the gradients directly. Its memory does not grow with the number of steps.

And the neural partial differential version?

Same idea for a whole field. Think of an implied volatility surface: a number for every strike and every tenor, moving every day. A neural version tries to learn how the whole surface moves at once.

Has it reached a trading desk?

Mostly as research. Deep hedging, from Buehler and colleagues in 2019, trained networks to hedge with costs. From around 2020 groups calibrated neural SDEs to option prices. It is not a desk standard yet.

What's the catch?

Flexibility cuts both ways. A model that can fit anything will fit the noise in your training data too. A close fit in sample proves nothing until it survives data it has never seen.

One line to remember?

Letting data write the drift and volatility removes one assumption and adds a lot of knobs, so judge it on out-of-sample evidence, not on how well it fits.

Section 01

Why this matters

Look back across this entire five-lesson sequence and notice the pattern: every model — GBM, OU, Vasicek, CIR, Heston — starts with a human writing down a specific functional form for \(\mu(x,t)\) and \(\sigma(x,t)\), chosen for tractability, for intuition, or for a specific stylized fact it was designed to reproduce (mean reversion, positivity, a volatility smile). That choice is a real modelling commitment, and it can be wrong in ways that are hard to detect until the model is used somewhere its assumed functional form doesn't hold. The final, most speculative step this sequence can take is to ask: what if the functional form itself isn't chosen by a human at all, but learned directly from data, the same way a neural network learns an image classifier's weights? That question is what "neural SDEs" answers — and it's worth being upfront that this lesson sits at a very different maturity level than the previous four. Vasicek, CIR, and Hull-White are decades-old, industry-standard tools. What follows is active research, much of it published within the last five years, with no established track record as a production trading or pricing tool anywhere, including in this repo.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Same equation shape, but the coefficients are learned, not written down

Every SDE in this curriculum has the same skeleton: \(dX_t = \mu(X_t,t)\,dt + \sigma(X_t,t)\,dW_t\). What changes from model to model is only what goes inside \(\mu(\cdot)\) and \(\sigma(\cdot)\) — a constant, a linear function of \(X_t\), a square root of \(X_t\). A neural SDE keeps the exact same skeleton and replaces those functions with small neural networks, \(\mu_\theta(X_t,t)\) and \(\sigma_\theta(X_t,t)\), where \(\theta\) stands for the network's weights — the same kind of adjustable numbers a network learns in image recognition or language modelling, just here controlling the shape of a drift and a diffusion instead of the shape of a decision boundary. Training the model means simulating paths from the current \(\mu_\theta,\sigma_\theta\), comparing the simulated paths (or their statistics) against real observed data, and adjusting \(\theta\) — via gradient descent, exactly as any other neural network is trained — to make the simulated behaviour look more like the real thing. The appeal is flexibility: instead of committing to "this process is exactly Ornstein-Uhlenbeck" or "exactly CIR," the model can, in principle, learn whatever functional shape the data actually calls for. The honest cost is exactly the cost of any flexible learned model: more parameters, more data required to fit them reliably, and much less interpretability than a named, textbook coefficient function.

How it reached trading

Letting the data choose the dynamics

Neural SDEs in finance · deep hedging (Buehler et al., 2019); neural-SDE calibration from c. 2020

Every classical model fixes the shape of its dynamics in advance: Heston's square root, SABR's power law. When the smile or the path behaviour disagrees with that shape, a desk can only add parameters or patch the model.

Machine learning offered another route. "Deep hedging" by Buehler, Gonon, Teichmann and Wood (2019), written with J.P. Morgan quants, trained networks to hedge under trading frictions directly from simulated paths. From around 2020, research groups (for example Gierjatowicz, Sabate-Vidales, Šiška, Szpruch & Žurič, and Cuchiero, Khosrawi & Teichmann) made an SDE's drift and volatility neural networks and calibrated them to vanilla option prices. The building block, the neural ODE (2018), came from deep learning.

On a desk this is still research: a candidate for calibration and for generating realistic market scenarios, not a standard pricing model. Its practical value today is showing how much a price or hedge depends on the model shape someone assumed.

Section 02

The math, step by step

Watch the formula build
From a hand-written SDE to a learned one, one term at a time
\[ dX_t = \color{#fbbf24}{\mu(X_t,t)}\,dt + \color{#fbbf24}{\sigma(X_t,t)}\,dW_t \]

The skeleton every model in this sequence shares. Until now a person filled in \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\): a constant, a line, a square root.

\[ dX_t = \color{#fbbf24}{\mu_\theta(X_t,t)}\,dt + \sigma(X_t,t)\,dW_t \]

Hand the drift to a neural network. It takes the current state and time and outputs a drift; its weights are part of \(\theta\).

\[ dX_t = \mu_\theta(X_t,t)\,dt + \color{#fbbf24}{\sigma_\theta(X_t,t)}\,dW_t \]

Hand the diffusion to a second network, or a second output of the same one. This is a neural SDE: the skeleton is unchanged, only the coefficients are learned.

\[ \color{#10b981}{\theta^* = \arg\min_\theta\ \text{distance}\big(\text{simulated paths}_\theta,\ \text{real paths}\big)} \]

Train it: simulate paths, score how distinguishable they are from real data, and move \(\theta\) downhill. The gradient comes from a backward adjoint SDE, so the forward path never has to be stored.

1. The neural SDE specification

\[ dX_t = \mu_\theta(X_t,t)\,dt + \sigma_\theta(X_t,t)\,dW_t \]
μ_θ(X_t,t)a neural network taking the current state and time, outputting a drift vector
σ_θ(X_t,t)a second neural network (or a shared network with two output heads) outputting a diffusion matrix
θthe combined set of learnable weights across both networks — what "training" actually adjusts

This idea traces to Tzen & Raginsky, "Neural Stochastic Differential Equations: Deep Latent Gaussian Models in the Diffusion Limit" (2019), which framed a neural SDE as the continuous-time limit of a deep latent variable model — connecting this machinery back to standard generative modelling ideas. Kidger, Foster, Li & Lyons, "Neural SDEs as Infinite-Dimensional GANs" (ICML, 2021), formalized this into a complete generative-adversarial framework: the neural SDE plays the role of a generator, producing simulated paths, while a separate discriminator network is trained to distinguish those simulated paths from real observed paths — exactly the generator/discriminator structure of an ordinary GAN, except the generator here is an entire stochastic process rather than a single generated image.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Every model so far in this sequence had a human write down the exact formula for the drift and the wobble. A neural SDE keeps the same skeleton but hands those two jobs to a neural network instead, letting it learn whatever shape best matches real data — the same way an image classifier learns what makes a cat look like a cat, except here it's learning what makes a price path look like a real price path.

2. Training: why naive backpropagation is expensive, and the adjoint fix

Training any neural network means computing the gradient of a loss function with respect to every weight in \(\theta\), then updating \(\theta\) to reduce the loss. For a neural SDE, the loss depends on simulated paths built from potentially hundreds or thousands of Euler-Maruyama steps (sde-numerical-schemes.html built this recursion in full). The most direct way to get gradients — ordinary backpropagation through every single simulated step, storing every intermediate state along the way to reuse during the backward pass — is exactly what standard deep-learning frameworks do automatically, and it works, but its memory cost scales directly with the number of simulated steps: a long simulation with a fine time grid can require storing an enormous number of intermediate states simultaneously.

The fix, due to Li, Wong, Chen & Duvenaud, "Scalable Gradients for Stochastic Differential Equations" (AISTATS, 2020), is the stochastic adjoint sensitivity method. Rather than storing every forward step, it derives a second SDE — the "adjoint" process — that runs backward in time from the end of the simulation, and whose solution directly gives the gradients needed for training, without needing the entire forward trajectory kept in memory. This is the direct stochastic generalization of the adjoint method for Neural ODEs (Chen, Rubanova, Bettencourt & Duvenaud, "Neural Ordinary Differential Equations," NeurIPS, 2018), which established the same trick — compute gradients via a second, backward differential equation instead of storing the whole forward solve — for deterministic (non-stochastic) neural differential equations first. The stochastic version needs real additional care that the deterministic version doesn't: naively reversing a Brownian path's time direction is not itself a valid Brownian motion in the reversed direction, so the backward adjoint SDE has to be derived properly to account for this, rather than simply running the forward equation's noise term backward.

\(\theta\)The learnable parameters (weights) of the drift and diffusion neural networks — what gradient descent actually updates during training.
Adjoint processA second, backward-running SDE whose solution gives the gradient of the training loss with respect to \(\theta\), without requiring every forward simulation step to be stored in memory.
Generator / discriminatorThe two-network structure Kidger et al. (2021) use to train a neural SDE: the SDE itself generates simulated paths, and a separate discriminator network is trained to distinguish them from real data.
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Training the ordinary way means remembering every single step of a long simulated walk so you can retrace it backward later — expensive if the walk has thousands of steps. The adjoint trick instead solves a separate "replay" equation that runs backward on its own, recovering the information you need without ever having kept the full walk in memory. It's the difference between saving a video of the whole trip versus having a rule that can reconstruct where you were at any moment, computed fresh.

3. Neural SPDEs: the field-valued generalization

A neural SDE learns the dynamics of a finite-dimensional state \(X_t \in \mathbb{R}^n\) — a handful of numbers evolving through time. A neural stochastic partial differential equation (SPDE) generalizes this to an infinite-dimensional object: a whole field, indexed by both time and one or more spatial (or spatial-like) coordinates, evolving stochastically. Salvi, Lemercier & Gerasimovics, "Neural Stochastic PDEs: Resolution-Invariant Learning of Continuous Spatiotemporal Dynamics" (NeurIPS, 2021), build a neural network architecture for learning exactly this kind of dynamics directly from data, with the specific advantage (the paper's central contribution) of being resolution-invariant — trained at one spatial/temporal resolution, the learned model can be evaluated at a different, finer or coarser resolution without retraining, unlike a naive grid-based neural network architecture.

A concrete, honestly-scoped financial framing of what an SPDE could model: an implied-volatility surface — a value for every combination of strike and tenor, changing from day to day — is precisely a field indexed by two spatial-like coordinates (strike, tenor) evolving stochastically in a third dimension (time). Instead of the standard practitioner approach of modelling the surface's parameters (a local-vol grid, or a small set of factor loadings) evolving independently, a neural SPDE would attempt to learn the entire surface's joint stochastic evolution directly, as one continuous object. This is a natural extension of the SDE machinery this curriculum has built — but it is also a genuinely advanced, unvalidated-in-this-repo research question, not a description of an existing tool. Framing it as a natural question worth knowing about is different from framing it as something ready to build.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

A neural SDE learns how one wiggly number, or a short list of them, behaves over time. A neural SPDE stretches that same idea to learn how an entire wiggly surface — like every point of an option's implied-volatility surface, all at once — behaves over time, without a human first deciding what shape that surface's motion should take.

Worked example

Why "just backpropagate through Euler-Maruyama" gets expensive, in concrete numbers

Consider training a neural SDE by simulating a path with \(N=1{,}000\) Euler-Maruyama steps (a fine daily-to-intraday-scale grid over a year), where the state \(X_t\) at each step has dimension \(d=50\) (say, 50 points on a yield curve or volatility surface, from multivariate-correlated-sdes.html's vector-SDE machinery), and the drift/diffusion networks together have on the order of \(P=100{,}000\) parameters. Ordinary reverse-mode backpropagation needs to retain, at minimum, every intermediate state \(X_n\) (dimension \(d\)) computed during the forward pass, since the backward pass walks through them in reverse order to accumulate gradients — that's \(N\times d = 1{,}000\times 50 = 50{,}000\) floating-point numbers of state alone, before accounting for the networks' own internal activations at every one of those 1,000 forward evaluations. Doubling the path length to \(N=2{,}000\) steps (a finer grid, or a longer horizon) roughly doubles this memory requirement, directly, with no way around it under naive backpropagation. The stochastic adjoint method's entire value proposition is replacing this direct, path-length-scaling memory cost with a second, backward SDE solve whose memory footprint does not grow with the number of forward steps — a genuine engineering necessity once \(N\) or \(d\) get large, not a marginal optimization.

Say this plainly, as the closing word on this entire five-lesson sequence: everything in this lesson — neural SDEs, the stochastic adjoint method, neural SPDEs applied to an implied-volatility surface — is current, active machine-learning research machinery, published within the last five to seven years, with genuine open research questions still being worked out in the literature. It is considerably further from a testable trading signal than anything else in this curriculum, including the CIR and Hull-White models three lessons prior, which are decades-old and industry-standard. This lesson is a knowledge lesson about what the technique is and how it works conceptually — it is explicitly not a claim that any of this has been built, tested, or shown to produce trading edge anywhere in this repo. Treat the distinction between "this is a real, interesting piece of machinery" and "this machinery has been shown to work here" as load-bearing, not a formality.
Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

🔬

Almost entirely a research-lab activity today

Neural SDEs remain overwhelmingly an academic and research-lab topic — published at NeurIPS, ICML, AISTATS — with limited, still-emerging adoption in production quantitative finance compared to the decades of track record behind Vasicek, CIR, or Heston.

🎲

Generative modelling for synthetic market data

One genuinely active practitioner use case: training a neural SDE to generate realistic synthetic price paths for stress-testing or data augmentation, where the goal is statistically realistic paths rather than an interpretable, named model.

📚

A frontier worth knowing about, not a tool to reach for

Useful to understand conceptually so a "neural SDE" claim in a paper or vendor pitch can be evaluated honestly — what it can plausibly do, what training it actually requires, and how far it is from a validated signal.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where this gets oversold

Treating "the model is more flexible" as "the model is more accurate." A neural SDE's drift and diffusion networks can fit the training data's observed statistics very closely — that is a statement about in-sample fit, not about whether the learned dynamics generalize to new market regimes the training data didn't include. A hand-specified model like CIR is inflexible by design, which is itself a form of regularization; a neural SDE's extra flexibility is exactly the extra overfitting risk that flexibility always buys, unless demonstrated otherwise out-of-sample.

Assuming "neural" implies validated. The presence of a neural network in a model's description carries no evidence, by itself, about whether the model has been tested honestly, with a genuine out-of-sample split and realistic costs — the same discipline this entire curriculum applies to a plain moving-average crossover applies identically here, and arguably more urgently given how many more parameters there are to overfit.

Confusing "resolution-invariant" (a genuine, specific claim about neural SPDEs) with "accurate at any resolution." Resolution invariance, as Salvi et al. (2021) define it, means a trained model can be evaluated at a different spatial/temporal grid without retraining — a real architectural property. It says nothing about whether the underlying learned dynamics are actually correct at any given resolution; those are two separate claims, easy to conflate.

Scenario drill · an illustrative vendor pitch

"Our neural SDE fits the surface far better than Heston"

A vendor shows you a neural SDE calibrated to two years of your market's option prices. Its in-sample fit to the volatility surface is far tighter than your Heston calibration, and it produces beautiful simulated paths. They want you to price and hedge with it from next month. What do you do?

  1. Adopt it. A tighter fit means a better model.
    In-sample fit is what flexibility always buys. A model with many more knobs will fit two years of data more closely whether or not its dynamics generalise. That is a statement about the past sample, not about next month.
  2. Ask for out-of-sample tests: fit on one period, then price and hedge on later data it never saw, including a stressed regime, with realistic costs, compared against Heston.
    This is the same discipline you would apply to a simple moving-average rule, applied more urgently because there are far more parameters to overfit. If it still wins out of sample, the flexibility is earning its keep.
  3. Use it only to generate extra market scenarios for stress tests, not for pricing.
    A reasonable, lower-stakes use, and one of the few active practitioner uses today. It still needs checking that the scenarios are realistic, but a bad scenario costs less than a bad hedge.
  4. Reject it outright, because neural networks cannot be trusted in finance.
    "Neural" carries no evidence either way. Rejecting it on the label skips the test just as surely as adopting it on the label. The question is out-of-sample evidence.

The lesson. This is an illustrative situation, not a dated event. Neural SDEs are active research, with deep hedging (2019) and neural-SDE calibration (from around 2020) the main routes into finance and no desk standard yet. A close fit says nothing until it holds on data the model never saw.

Section 05

Self-test

Q1. What, precisely, does a neural SDE replace compared to every other model in this five-lesson sequence?
It keeps the identical SDE skeleton, dX_t=μ(X_t,t)dt+σ(X_t,t)dW_t, and replaces the hand-specified functional forms for μ and σ — a constant, a linear function, a square root — with neural networks μ_θ(X_t,t) and σ_θ(X_t,t), whose weights θ are learned from data via gradient descent, rather than chosen by a modeller ahead of time.
Q2. Why is naive backpropagation through a simulated Euler-Maruyama path memory-expensive, and what does the stochastic adjoint method do instead?
Naive backpropagation needs to retain every intermediate simulated state (and the networks' internal activations at each step) from the forward pass, since the backward pass walks through them in reverse to accumulate gradients — this memory cost scales directly with the number of simulated steps. The stochastic adjoint method instead derives a second, backward-running SDE whose solution directly yields the training gradients, without needing the entire forward trajectory stored in memory — the stochastic analogue of the Neural-ODE adjoint method.
Q3. How does a neural SPDE generalize a neural SDE, and what financial object is given as a concrete example of what it could model?
A neural SDE learns the dynamics of a finite-dimensional state (a handful of numbers evolving in time). A neural SPDE generalizes this to an infinite-dimensional field — an object indexed by both time and one or more spatial (or spatial-like) coordinates, evolving stochastically. The concrete financial example given is an implied-volatility surface: a value for every combination of strike and tenor, changing from day to day — precisely a field indexed by two spatial-like coordinates evolving in time.
Q4. This lesson describes Kidger, Foster, Li & Lyons' framing of neural SDE training as generator/discriminator. What plays the role of the "generator" and what plays the role of the "discriminator"?
The neural SDE itself (its drift and diffusion networks, simulating paths forward via Euler-Maruyama) plays the role of the generator, producing simulated paths. A separate discriminator network is trained to distinguish those simulated paths from real observed paths/statistics — exactly the generator/discriminator structure of an ordinary GAN, except the generator here produces entire stochastic paths rather than a single static output like an image.
Q5. Is anything described in this lesson built, tested, or validated as a trading tool anywhere in this repo?
No. This lesson is explicitly a knowledge lesson about current, active machine-learning research machinery — neural SDEs, the stochastic adjoint method, and neural SPDEs — not a claim that any of it has been implemented, tested, or shown to produce trading edge anywhere in this repo. It is considerably further from a testable trading signal than any other model in this five-lesson sequence, including the decades-old, industry-standard Vasicek, CIR, and Hull-White models.
