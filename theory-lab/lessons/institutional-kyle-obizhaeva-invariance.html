Kyle-Obizhaeva: Market Microstructure Invariance

A $2 billion mega-cap and a $200 million micro-cap trade at utterly different costs, in utterly different sizes, on utterly different clocks. Invariance is the claim that this apparent chaos is an illusion of the wrong ruler — that once "bet size" is measured in units of risk transferred rather than shares or dollars, the same statistical law governs every stock, every currency pair, and every era.

TL;DR
  • Trading costs, typical order sizes, and price impact all look wildly different across a mega-cap and a micro-cap — but Kyle & Obizhaeva (2016) argue that's an artifact of measuring in the wrong units. Measure a trade's size in risk transferred — dollar size times volatility — instead of shares or dollars, and the same statistical distribution of "bet sizes" shows up across essentially every stock, venue, and era.
  • The activity measure is \(W = P \cdot V \cdot \sigma\) (price × daily share volume × daily volatility) — a single number summarizing how much dollar risk a market transfers per day. The central invariance hypothesis: the distribution of bet sizes, standardized by \(W^{2/3}\), is approximately the same everywhere.
  • That exponent produces a real, checkable prediction: doubling an asset's trading activity does not double its typical bet size — it scales the typical bet size by only \(2^{2/3}\approx1.587\times\). In this lesson's worked example, two assets differ in activity by roughly 1,042×, yet their invariance-implied typical bet sizes differ by only about 103×.
  • This extends, rather than replaces, Kyle's (1985) single-asset Lambda — already covered in the Market Microstructure lesson. Kyle prices one asset's impact given that asset's own exogenous \(\sigma_v,\sigma_u\); invariance is the cross-sectional theory of how those parameters, and the bet sizes they imply, should systematically differ from one asset to the next.
  • Practical payoff: because the standardized distribution is shared across assets, a desk that has calibrated it on data-rich, liquid names can predict expected costs and sensible order-slicing sizes for a new or illiquid instrument — an FX cross, a thin small-cap — from nothing but that instrument's own price, volume, and volatility, with no separate history-hungry calibration required.
  • Invariance is an empirical regularity with real scatter around it, not an exact physical law, and it is explicitly silent on short-run transient price-impact dynamics — the subject of the next lesson in this category, Obizhaeva-Wang Transient Price Impact.
Section 01

Why this matters

Look at execution costs and typical trade sizes across almost any two instruments with very different trading activity — a heavily traded mega-cap equity and a thin small-cap, or a G10 FX pair and an emerging-market cross — and the numbers look like they belong to two different universes. The mega-cap trades in blocks of hundreds of thousands of shares at a few basis points of cost; the small-cap moves the same percentage of its own volume at many times that cost, in absolute share counts that are a rounding error next to the mega-cap's. The obvious read is "these are just different markets, calibrate each one separately." Kyle and Obizhaeva's 2016 paper asks a much sharper question: are they actually different, or is that apparent chaos purely an artifact of measuring trade size in the wrong units — shares, or raw dollars — when the right unit was sitting there all along?

This repo's own Market Microstructure lesson already covers the classical answer to "why does size cost money at all": Kyle's (1985) linear price-impact model, \(\Delta p = \lambda Q\) with \(\lambda = \sigma_v/(2\sigma_u)\). That model is single-asset and single-market — it takes one instrument's fundamental-value uncertainty \(\sigma_v\) and noise-trading depth \(\sigma_u\) as given, exogenous inputs, and derives that instrument's own price-impact coefficient. It says nothing at all about how \(\lambda\), or the sizes informed traders actually choose to trade, should systematically compare across a universe of different instruments with different volumes, prices, and volatilities. Invariance is exactly the cross-sectional theory that fills that gap: not "how does size affect cost for this one asset," but "given everything we know about how big this asset's own trading activity is, what does that alone imply about the sizes and costs we should expect to see trade there?"

Kyle's Lambda (already covered)

One insider, one market, one linear price-impact coefficient \(\lambda=\sigma_v/(2\sigma_u)\), with \(\sigma_v\) and \(\sigma_u\) taken as given for that specific asset. Answers: "for this market, how much does trading more cost?"

Market Microstructure Invariance (this lesson)

A population-level, cross-sectional claim spanning every asset, venue, and era at once: once trade size is measured in risk-transfer units and rescaled by an asset's own trading activity \(W\), the distribution of those standardized sizes is the same everywhere. Answers: "given how active this market is, what should its typical bet size and cost look like — with no separate, asset-specific calibration at all?"

💡
Plain-English intuition

The wrong ruler makes every market look unique

Imagine trying to compare how "big" a splash two different objects make when dropped into water, but you're only allowed to measure the splash in inches of water displaced, never accounting for how big or dense the object itself was. A bowling ball dropped into a bathtub and a pebble dropped into a swimming pool might, purely by coincidence of scale, produce splashes of wildly different heights that tell you almost nothing comparable about the two events — the ruler you're using (raw inches) isn't the right unit for the comparison you actually want to make.

Kyle and Obizhaeva's claim is that trading data has exactly this problem. Comparing raw share counts, or even raw dollar sizes, across a mega-cap and a micro-cap is comparing splash heights without accounting for the size of the pool. Their fix is to build a ruler that already has the pool's size baked in: measure a trade not in shares or dollars, but in dollars of risk actually transferred — size times price times volatility — and measure the market's own capacity to absorb that risk the same way. Once both sides of the comparison are expressed in that shared currency, the invariance hypothesis says the bowling ball and the pebble stop looking so different after all.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

An elephant's heartbeat and a mouse's heartbeat look totally different if you just count beats per minute — the mouse's heart races many times faster. But biologists noticed that once you account for body size the right way, most mammals' hearts beat roughly the same total number of times across a full lifetime, big or small. Invariance is that same kind of trick, applied to trading: two stocks that look nothing alike in raw share counts or dollar costs can turn out to follow the exact same rule once you measure their trades in the right "body-size-adjusted" unit instead of the raw one.

Section 02

The math, step by step

Three ingredients build the whole hypothesis: a way to measure the size of a single trade ("bet") in risk-transfer units, a way to measure how active a market is overall, and the specific power-law relationship linking the two that Kyle and Obizhaeva argue must hold.

1. A "bet" measured in units of risk transferred

A trader deciding to buy or sell some quantity \(Q\) of an asset is executing what Kyle and Obizhaeva call a bet — a single decision to acquire or shed a position, which in practice often gets sliced into many smaller child orders over time (exactly the slicing covered in the Market Impact Models & Almgren-Chriss and Execution Algorithms lessons). Measured in raw shares, \(Q\) alone says almost nothing comparable across assets — 10,000 shares of a $5 penny stock and 10,000 shares of a $500 mega-cap are not remotely the same economic bet. The first fix is obvious: measure in dollars, \(Q\cdot P\). But even that misses something. A $1 million position in a stable, low-volatility utility and a $1 million position in a violently swinging small-cap carry very different amounts of actual risk — and it's risk, not raw notional, that a market maker or a counterparty is ultimately being asked to absorb. Kyle and Obizhaeva's bet-size measure folds volatility in directly:

\[ L = Q \cdot P \cdot \sigma \]
Lbet size, in dollars of risk transferred — this lesson's central object
Qthe bet's size in shares (or contracts, units)
Pthe asset's price, converting shares to dollars
σthe asset's own volatility (this lesson uses daily volatility throughout)

Read \(L\) as "the dollar standard deviation of value this bet exposes someone to" — not the notional itself, but the notional scaled by how much that notional typically moves. Two bets with identical \(L\) put an identical amount of genuine price risk on the table, even if their share counts, dollar notionals, and underlying instruments are nothing alike.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Ten thousand dollars sitting in a sleepy savings account and ten thousand dollars riding on a single spin of a roulette wheel are the same "size" in dollars, but not remotely the same size in risk. Multiplying a trade's dollar size by how much the asset actually wiggles turns "how much money is involved" into "how much genuine uncertainty is actually being taken on" — which is the number that matters for comparing across totally different assets.

2. \(W\): how much risk a market transfers per day

The mirror-image question is how to measure a whole market's capacity to absorb bets like \(L\). Kyle and Obizhaeva define a single "trading activity" number for each asset, built from exactly the same price/volatility logic, but applied to the market's total daily volume instead of one trader's bet:

\[ W = P \cdot V \cdot \sigma \]
Wtrading activity — the dollar rate at which this market transfers risk, per day
Vaverage daily share (or contract) volume
P, σthe same price and volatility used to build a single bet's L above

\(W\) is what Kyle and Obizhaeva sometimes describe informally as the speed of the market's own "business-time clock": a high-\(W\) name (a heavily traded, reasonably volatile mega-cap) transfers an enormous amount of dollar risk every single day purely through ordinary trading; a low-\(W\) name (a thin, low-volatility utility, or an illiquid small-cap) transfers comparatively little. Every quantity this lesson discusses — typical bet size, the number of distinct bets placed per day, and the cost of trading a given bet — is hypothesized to be some specific, predictable function of this one number.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

W is a single number for "how much of a fire hose is this market" — how much dollar risk flows through it in an ordinary day just from everyone's regular buying and selling. A firehose market can swallow a huge bet without much fuss; a garden-hose market chokes on the same bet, and W is exactly the number that tells you, in advance, which kind of hose you're dealing with.

3. The scaling law: typical bet size grows as \(W^{2/3}\), not \(W\)

The headline empirical result is that the typical bet size in a market — the scale that anchors the whole distribution of bet sizes traded there — does not grow in direct proportion to \(W\). It grows more slowly, as a specific fractional power:

\[ L^{*} \;\propto\; W^{2/3} \]
L*the invariance-implied typical (scale of the) bet size for this market
W^(2/3)trading activity raised to the two-thirds power — the headline invariance exponent

The practical bite of this is entirely in the exponent being less than one: it is a concave, sub-linear relationship, exactly the same qualitative shape as the square-root law of price impact already covered in the Market Impact Models & Almgren-Chriss lesson (impact growing as \(\sqrt{Q/\text{ADV}}\) rather than linearly in \(Q\)) — but applied here to a different quantity: not how a single asset's own cost scales with the size of one trade, but how the typical bet size traders actually choose scales across different assets with different activity levels. Concretely, doubling \(W\) does not double the typical bet size — it scales it by only \(2^{2/3}\approx 1.587\times\); a hundred-fold jump in \(W\) scales typical bet size by only \(100^{2/3}\approx 21.5\times\). Trading activity can differ by orders of magnitude between two instruments while their typical bet sizes differ by something much smaller — the worked example below makes this concrete with numbers.

Where the 2/3 actually comes from — stated honestly. Kyle and Obizhaeva derive this exponent from a formal argument they call leverage neutrality: rescale an asset's price, share count, and volatility the way a stock split, a currency redenomination, or a switch from shares to round lots would — and require that the whole theory (what counts as "one bet," and how many bets have occurred) give back an identical prediction regardless of that relabeling. Applying that neutrality requirement to how a bet's trading cost depends on its size relative to volume pins the exponent down; the number of distinct bets placed per day is then forced to scale as the complementary \(W^{1/3}\), so that bet size times bet count remains consistent with total volume, which by definition scales as \(W\) itself. This is a genuine formal result in Kyle & Obizhaeva (2016) — not an arithmetic identity this lesson can safely re-derive from scratch — so it is stated here as the paper's result, with the intuition above, rather than worked out as invented algebra. See the Further Reading section for the paper the full argument comes from.
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

If a market gets ten times busier overall, common sense might say "typical trades should get about ten times bigger too." Invariance says something more surprising: typical trades only get about 4.6 times bigger (10 to the two-thirds power), while the number of separate trades happening per day picks up the rest of the slack, growing by about 2.15 times (10 to the one-third power). Busier markets split the extra activity between "somewhat bigger typical trades" and "quite a few more of them" — not purely one or the other.

4. The invariance hypothesis proper: one shared distribution, everywhere

The scaling law above only pins down where the typical bet size sits for a given market — it says nothing yet about the whole shape of the distribution around it (how often unusually large or small bets occur, how fat the tails are). The full invariance hypothesis is the stronger, more striking claim: define the standardized bet size

\[ z = \frac{L}{W^{2/3}} \]
zstandardized bet size — a specific bet's L, rescaled by its own market's W^(2/3)

Kyle and Obizhaeva's central empirical hypothesis is that the distribution of \(z\) across the whole population of bets placed in a market — its shape, spread, and tail behavior, not just its central tendency — is approximately the same across different stocks, different trading venues, and different historical periods, once every bet is standardized this way. This is a much stronger claim than "the average bet size follows a power law" — it says the entire statistical texture of how trade sizes vary is a shared, universal property of markets in general, not a bespoke fact about any one instrument. A market's own \(W\) is the only asset-specific ingredient needed to translate that one universal distribution into predictions for that specific market.

The same logic extends directly to trading cost. The square-root law from the Market Impact Models & Almgren-Chriss lesson, \(I \approx Y\sigma\sqrt{Q/\text{ADV}}\), already expresses expected cost as a function of trade size relative to volume for a single asset. Invariance adds the cross-sectional layer on top: because \(z\) is meant to occupy the same statistical position in every market's own distribution, a bet with a given \(z\) should carry a broadly comparable expected cost — in properly risk-normalized terms — no matter which asset it was placed in. That, and not the square-root law itself (already established elsewhere in this Theory Lab), is the specifically new claim this lesson is about.

🧮
Worked example

Two assets, ~1,000× apart in activity, ~100× apart in typical bet size

Asset A — a liquid mega-cap. Price \(P=\$150\), average daily volume \(V=50{,}000{,}000\) shares, annualized volatility 25% (daily \(\sigma = 0.25/\sqrt{252} \approx 1.575\%\)):

\[ W_A = 150 \times 50{,}000{,}000 \times 0.01575 \approx 1.181\times 10^{8} \]

Asset B — a thin, volatile small-cap. Price \(P=\$20\), average daily volume \(V=200{,}000\) shares, annualized volatility 45% (daily \(\sigma = 0.45/\sqrt{252}\approx 2.835\%\)):

\[ W_B = 20 \times 200{,}000 \times 0.02835 \approx 1.134\times 10^{5} \]
W_A / W_B ≈ 1,042×Asset A's trading activity is over a thousand times Asset B's

Raise both to the \(2/3\) power to get each market's invariance-implied typical bet-size index (an illustrative scale, not a calibrated dollar figure — see the note below):

\[ L^{*}_A = W_A^{2/3} \approx 2.407\times 10^{5} \qquad\qquad L^{*}_B = W_B^{2/3} \approx 2.343\times 10^{3} \]
L*_A / L*_B ≈ 102.8×the typical-bet-size gap the invariance scaling law predicts

Trading activity differs by roughly 1,042× between the two assets, yet the invariance-implied typical bet size differs by only about 103× — visibly compressed relative to the raw activity gap, exactly as the sub-linear \(W^{2/3}\) exponent predicts (\(1{,}042^{2/3}\approx 102.8\), confirming the two numbers are the same calculation viewed two ways). This lesson does not have Kyle & Obizhaeva's own calibrated overall constant, so \(L^{*}_A\) and \(L^{*}_B\) above are an internally consistent scaling index, not literal dollar predictions — the ratio between them is the honest, real content of the example, not the absolute numbers themselves.

Now compare expected trading cost using the already-established square-root law, for the same trade size relative to volume (1% of ADV) in both markets:

\[ \text{Cost}_A \approx 10{,}000\times 0.01575\times\sqrt{0.01} \approx 15.7\text{ bps} \qquad \text{Cost}_B \approx 10{,}000\times 0.02835\times\sqrt{0.01} \approx 28.3\text{ bps} \]

Even at the same percentage of ADV, the thinner, more volatile small-cap costs roughly 1.8× more per the square-root law alone — before invariance even enters. The added insight invariance contributes is that a rational bet-sizer would not, in fact, choose to trade the same percentage of ADV in both names: the invariance-implied "typical" bet for Asset B is a much smaller share of its own volume than the invariance-implied "typical" bet for Asset A is of its volume — precisely because the whole point of the standardized measure \(z=L/W^{2/3}\) is that a "typical," unremarkable-sized bet occupies the same statistical position in both markets' own distributions, even though it corresponds to very different raw share counts, dollar sizes, and percentages of ADV in each.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

The single most useful practical consequence of invariance is exactly what its name promises: it lets a desk carry conclusions across assets instead of re-deriving them from scratch for every single one. Estimating a bespoke cost model or a sensible order-slicing schedule for an instrument normally requires a meaningful history of that instrument's own trades — exactly the kind of data a brand-new listing, a thinly-traded small-cap, or an emerging-market FX cross often doesn't have enough of. Invariance offers a shortcut: if the shape of the standardized bet-size (and cost) distribution really is shared across markets, then a desk that has carefully calibrated that shared shape once, on liquid, data-rich instruments, can transport it to a new instrument using nothing but that instrument's own \(P\), \(V\), and \(\sigma\) — the three ingredients of \(W\) — with no separate, history-hungry calibration of the new instrument required at all.

🔀

Cross-asset cost prediction

Given only an instrument's price, volume, and volatility, invariance implies an expected order-size distribution and cost profile without needing that specific name's own trading history to calibrate one.

✂️

Sizing order slices sensibly

Rather than slicing a large order to a fixed percentage of ADV regardless of the name, invariance suggests the "unremarkable," typical-sized clip should itself scale as \(W^{2/3}\) — smaller relative to volume in already-thin markets, not the same ratio everywhere.

🌍

Extending Kyle's Lambda across a universe

Where Kyle's model treats each asset's \(\sigma_v,\sigma_u\) (and therefore \(\lambda\)) as separately given, invariance is a specific, falsifiable theory of how those quantities — and the bet sizes they support — should compare from one asset to the next.

📊

A sanity check on outlier trades

A trade's standardized size \(z\) flags whether it's a routine, typically-sized bet for that market (\(z\) near the market's own historical center) or a genuinely unusual one for that specific level of activity — a diagnostic raw share count or dollar size alone can't give.

🎯
Real-world trading scenario

Sizing a first trade in a newly-added, thinly-traded instrument

A desk's mandate expands to include a newly-added small-cap or an emerging-market FX cross it has never traded before. There is no internal execution history for this specific name — no calibrated cost curve, no learned "typical" clip size, nothing but the standard reference data every instrument has on day one: its price, its recent average daily volume, and its recent realized volatility. A desk relying purely on name-by-name calibration is stuck guessing, or forced to trade tiny, uninformative test sizes first purely to learn the market's behavior the hard way.

A desk that has instead calibrated the invariant, standardized distribution once — on the deep history of dozens of liquid, well-understood names — can immediately compute this new instrument's own \(W\) from its price, volume, and volatility, and read off a sensible starting order size and an expected-cost benchmark straight from the shared distribution, before ever placing a single trade in the name. That is not a guarantee of being exactly right on day one — it is a principled starting point instead of a blind guess, precisely because the theory claims the relevant statistical shape was never actually asset-specific in the first place.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where invariance is misread or over-applied

Treating invariance as an exact physical law. It is an empirical regularity, documented with real, substantial scatter around it in Kyle and Obizhaeva's own data — a statistical tendency for the standardized distribution to look similar across markets, not a guarantee that any two specific instruments will match it precisely. Genuine outliers, idiosyncratic microstructure quirks, and small-sample noise in a thinly-traded name's own \(W\) estimate can all produce a standardized bet size that departs meaningfully from what the "universal" distribution would predict.

Confusing invariance with the square-root law of impact. They are related but distinct claims. The square-root law (Market Impact Models & Almgren-Chriss) is about how one asset's own cost scales with the size of a single trade relative to its own volume. Invariance is the cross-sectional claim about how typical bet sizes and the whole distribution around them compare across different assets once properly standardized by \(W\). This lesson leans on the square-root law as an input, but does not re-derive or replace it.

It says nothing about short-run, transient dynamics. Invariance describes a statistical regularity in bet sizes and their average costs — it is silent on how price impact actually decays moment to moment once a bet stops trading, how quickly a temporary concession reverts, or what an optimal intraday execution trajectory looks like within a single bet. That is a genuinely separate question, the subject of the next lesson in this category, Obizhaeva-Wang Transient Price Impact — invariance sets the scale of a typical bet; that lesson is about the shape of its impact over time once it starts trading.

Most of the underlying empirical evidence is from equities. The original invariance tests were run primarily on U.S. equity market data. Applying the same standardized distribution uncritically to FX, fixed income, or other genuinely different market structures — with different venue fragmentation, different informed-trading dynamics, and in FX specifically no single centralized volume figure the way an exchange-listed equity has — requires real caution, not a blind assumption that the equity-calibrated shape simply transfers unchanged.

Key Takeaways

✓Measuring in the wrong unit makes markets look falsely unique. Raw share counts and dollar sizes obscure a shared pattern that shows up once bet size is measured as risk transferred: \(L=Q\cdot P\cdot\sigma\).
✓\(W=P\cdot V\cdot\sigma\) is the one number summarizing a market's trading activity — the dollar rate at which it transfers risk per day, and the sole asset-specific input the invariance hypothesis needs.
⚠Typical bet size scales as \(W^{2/3}\), not \(W\) itself. A sub-linear, concave relationship: a 1,000× gap in trading activity implies only roughly a 100× gap in typical bet size, not a 1,000× gap.
✓The full hypothesis is stronger than the scaling law alone: the entire distribution of standardized bet size \(z=L/W^{2/3}\) — not just its center — is claimed to be approximately shared across assets, venues, and eras.
✓This extends Kyle's (1985) single-asset Lambda into a cross-sectional theory. Kyle takes \(\sigma_v,\sigma_u\) as exogenous per asset; invariance is a specific, falsifiable prediction for how those quantities compare across a whole universe of assets.
⚠It is an empirical regularity with real scatter, not an exact law, and it is silent on short-run transient impact dynamics — the next lesson in this category, Obizhaeva-Wang Transient Price Impact, is exactly the piece invariance leaves out.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. What specifically does "bet size measured in risk-transfer units" mean, and why isn't raw dollar notional good enough?
Bet size is defined as \(L=Q\cdot P\cdot\sigma\) — quantity times price times the asset's own volatility — rather than just \(Q\cdot P\) (dollar notional). Two positions of identical dollar notional in a stable, low-volatility instrument and a violently swinging one expose a counterparty to very different amounts of actual price risk. Folding volatility directly into the size measure means two bets with the same \(L\) carry a genuinely comparable amount of risk, even if their share counts, dollar notionals, and underlying instruments look nothing alike.
Q2. Define \(W\), and explain what it means for it to be "the sole asset-specific input" invariance needs.
\(W=P\cdot V\cdot\sigma\) — price times average daily volume times volatility — is a single number summarizing how much dollar risk a market transfers through ordinary trading each day. The invariance hypothesis claims that, given a market's own \(W\), the entire shape of its standardized bet-size distribution can be predicted from a single shared, universal distribution — no separate, name-specific calibration of that shape is needed. \(W\) is the one number that translates the universal distribution into a specific prediction for that specific market.
Q3. Why does a 1,000× difference in trading activity \(W\) between two assets imply only roughly a 100× difference in typical bet size, not a 1,000× difference?
Because typical bet size scales as \(W^{2/3}\), a sub-linear (concave) power law, not as \(W\) itself. \(1{,}000^{2/3}=100\) exactly (since \(1{,}000=10^3\) and \((10^3)^{2/3}=10^2=100\)) — the worked example's own numbers, \(1{,}042\times\) in activity versus roughly \(103\times\) in typical bet size, are the same calculation applied to slightly less round inputs. The 2/3 exponent is what compresses a very large gap in raw activity into a much smaller (though still large) gap in typical bet size.
Q4. How does this lesson's invariance framework relate to Kyle's (1985) linear price-impact model already covered in the Market Microstructure lesson — is it a replacement, a contradiction, or something else?
Neither a replacement nor a contradiction — an extension into a different dimension. Kyle's model is single-asset: it takes one instrument's fundamental-value uncertainty \(\sigma_v\) and noise-trading depth \(\sigma_u\) as given and derives that instrument's own linear impact coefficient \(\lambda=\sigma_v/(2\sigma_u)\). Invariance is cross-sectional: it is a specific, falsifiable theory of how bet sizes (and, by extension, the parameters feeding a Kyle-style impact model) should systematically compare across many different assets with different trading activity, once measured in the right units — a question Kyle's own single-market model is not built to answer at all.
Q5. What does the invariance hypothesis not say anything about, and which lesson in this same category picks up exactly that gap?
Invariance is silent on short-run, transient price-impact dynamics — how a bet's price impact actually decays moment to moment once trading stops, how quickly a temporary concession reverts, or what an optimal intraday execution trajectory looks like within a single bet. It sets the statistical scale of a "typical" bet and its average cost, not the time-path of impact within one. The next lesson in this category, Obizhaeva-Wang Transient Price Impact, is specifically about that missing piece.
Q6. A desk wants to trade a newly-added small-cap it has no execution history for. How would it use invariance, rather than name-by-name calibration, to size a sensible first trade — and what caveat should temper that confidence?
Compute the new instrument's own \(W=P\cdot V\cdot\sigma\) from publicly available price, volume, and volatility data, then apply the shared standardized distribution — already calibrated once on data-rich, liquid names — to translate that \(W\) into an expected typical bet size and cost benchmark, with no separate history-hungry calibration of this specific new name required. The caveat: invariance is an empirical regularity with real scatter, most of its original supporting evidence comes from U.S. equities, and it says nothing about short-run transient dynamics — so this gives a principled starting point for a first trade, not a guarantee that this specific instrument will match the universal distribution precisely.
