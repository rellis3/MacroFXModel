Lesson 30 · Advanced Portfolio & Market Structure

Random Matrix Theory, or How to Tell a Factor From a Fluke

The PCA lesson showed that sorting a correlation matrix's eigenvalues surfaces genuine shared factors — a dollar factor, a risk-on/risk-off axis. What it left unanswered: with only a finite, noisy sample of returns, how many of those eigenvalues are real, and how many are exactly what pure chance produces when you don't have enough data? Random Matrix Theory answers that with a precise, computable line in the sand.

Section 1 · How it reached trading

Most of a correlation matrix is noise

Random matrix theory · Laloux, Cizeau, Bouchaud & Potters and Plerou et al., 1999

How it reached trading

An optimiser inverts a correlation matrix with more parameters than the data can pin down, and loads up on spurious low-risk combinations. In 1999 Laloux, Cizeau, Bouchaud & Potters and Plerou et al. compared stock correlation matrices with the Marchenko–Pastur band.

Only a handful of eigenvalues, the market and some sectors, stood clearly above the noise. Cleaning the rest, alongside Ledoit–Wolf shrinkage, became a standard step before mean-variance or risk-parity optimisation.

Section 2

Even zero correlation doesn't look like zero

A correlation matrix built from finite history always spreads its eigenvalues away from 1 — even when the true, underlying correlations are exactly zero.

∞

Zero correlation is a limit, not a reading

A correlation matrix converges to the identity — every eigenvalue exactly 1 — only as history goes to infinity. You never have infinite history.

📅

250 days is not infinity

One calendar year of daily returns is a perfectly ordinary lookback window, and also a small, finite sample that lets sample correlations drift from their true value purely by chance.

📊

The drift shows up as eigenvalue spread

Build a matrix out of these coincidentally-nonzero sample correlations and its eigenvalues scatter above and below 1 — even with zero real structure anywhere.

?

No cutoff, no way to tell

Without knowing what pure-noise spread looks like in advance, there's no principled way to separate a real second factor from an unlucky sample window.

Flip a fair coin 20 times and you'll almost never land exactly 10 heads, 10 tails — 12-8, 8-12, sometimes 15-5. None of that means the coin is rigged; it's what "no real pattern, finite sample" looks like. Random Matrix Theory hands correlation-matrix eigenvalues the exact equivalent of that "how much wobble is normal" band.
Section 3

The thought experiment: correlate pure noise with itself

Take it literally, not abstractly.

What happens
N = 30 independent series
→
T = 250 draws each

eigenvalues spread away from 1
Zero true correlation was built into the data by construction. The spread is entirely finite-sample noise — the specific, characteristic footprint noise leaves on a correlation matrix's eigenvalues.

Generate 30 completely independent random return series with no shared driver of any kind, draw 250 observations of each — exactly the shape of one year of daily returns across a 30-pair FX basket. The true correlation between any two of them is exactly 0. But the sample correlation over those 250 draws won't land at exactly 0.00 — some pairs will show 0.06, others −0.11, others 0.03, purely from the same finite-draw drift that keeps 20 coin flips off 10-10. Build a 30×30 matrix out of those coincidences and its eigenvalues will not all equal 1.

Plain-English version

Ask 30 strangers "did you happen to wear the same shirt color more often than not this month?" With only a month of data, some pairs will look coincidentally matched purely by luck. A "who matches whom" grid built from those coincidences won't look flat and random either — it'll show fake clusters, purely from too few days to average the coincidences away.

Section 4

The Marchenko-Pastur law names the exact noise band

Marchenko & Pastur (1967) proved the spread from Section 3 isn't just "some spread" — it converges to a specific, computable distribution with a hard edge.

N/T

q — the shape parameter

q = N/T: instruments relative to independent observations. The single ratio the entire noise band depends on — not N alone, not T alone.

↓

λ_min — the floor

λ_min = (1 − √q)². The lowest eigenvalue a pure-noise correlation matrix is expected to produce at this N/T.

↑

λ_max — the ceiling

λ_max = (1 + √q)². The highest eigenvalue pure noise alone could plausibly produce. Clear this and you likely have a real factor.

Only the ratio q = N/T matters, not N or T individually. A basket of 100 instruments over 100,000 days and a basket of 3 instruments over 3,000 days can share the same q — and therefore the exact same noise band — even though their N and T look nothing alike.

A smaller q — more history per instrument — pushes √q toward 0, so both edges converge on 1: the band tightens because sample correlations converge more tightly to their true (zero) value. A larger q pushes the band wide open: λ_min falls toward 0, λ_max climbs well above 1, and a much bigger eigenvalue is needed before it's distinguishable from chance.

Section 8

Thirty FX pairs, one year of daily returns

q = 30/250 = 0.12 → noise band ≈ [0.43, 1.81]. Now check the real eigenvalues against it.

λ₁ = 8.2
  • Sits enormously far above 1.81 — more than 4× the upper edge
  • An overwhelming, easy call: this is a real, genuine common factor
  • In practice, almost certainly the same dollar/risk-on factor the PCA lesson calls PC1
λ₂, λ₃, λ₄ ≈ 0.5 – 1.7
  • Squarely inside the 0.43–1.81 noise band
  • At N=30, T=250, this basket doesn't carry enough information to tell PC2/PC3 from pure randomness
  • Treating them as "the euro-specific factor" or "the commodity-bloc factor" would be fitting noise, not signal

That doesn't mean PC2 is definitely meaningless in reality — it means this sample, at this size, cannot tell the difference. Building on top of it as if it could is overfitting to noise while believing it's structure.

Section 9

A binary screen, or a full cleanup

Practitioners use this idea two ways — one this lesson covers, one it only names.

Binary screen — covered here

Decide how many of a basket's eigenvalues are worth treating as real factors at all, before doing anything more sophisticated — running a factor model, sizing a diversification metric, deciding how many independent bets a book really has.

Eigenvalue cleaning — named, not built

Replace every eigenvalue inside the noise band with a single common value (often their average), leave eigenvalues above λ_max untouched, and reconstruct a "cleaned" correlation matrix — a more honest input to a covariance-based optimizer.

The landmark real-world application: Laloux, Cizeau, Bouchaud & Potters (1999) found that the great majority of eigenvalues in a real stock-market correlation matrix, built across several hundred U.S. stocks, fell inside the Marchenko-Pastur band computed from their actual N and T — while a small handful, led by one dominant market factor, sat far outside it. That paper is generally credited with launching "RMT filtering" as a standard risk-management technique.
Section 10

Where this plugs into the rest of the curriculum

This lesson builds directly on PCA & Factor Models: same N instruments, same T observations, same correlation matrix, same ranked eigenvalues λ₁ ≥ λ₂ ≥ … ≥ λ_N. PCA tells you how to rank them and read off a candidate factor; the Marchenko-Pastur band is the missing step that tells you which of those candidates have actually earned the name "factor," versus which ones are exactly what an unlucky sample window would produce anyway.

Verified against this repo's current code

js/diversificationCore.js already computes a correlation matrix's eigenvalues (symmetricEigenvalues) and feeds them into three effective-number-of-bets metrics — effectiveBetsPCA, effectiveBetsEntropy, effectiveBetsWeighted. As of this lesson, none of them compares a single eigenvalue against a Marchenko-Pastur λ_min/λ_max band — there is no noise-band check anywhere in that file, or elsewhere in the codebase, against the spectrum it already produces. The full lesson's claim that this check "is not currently run in this repo" still holds; nothing here is stale.

Practically: a basket's effective-bets score can look reassuringly close to N while partly resting on eigenvalues that are noise-band artifacts, not real diversification. Flagging which eigenvalues clear the band would add an honest caveat to that number without changing the formula itself — a concrete, unbuilt guardrail, not a claim about what the repo currently does.

Section 11

Where the rule gets misapplied

🚫

"Inside the band" ≠ proof of nothing

It's a statistical-power limit, not a certainty — a real, modest factor can still hide there at small T.

♾️

It's an asymptotic result

Finite N, T means edge effects (Tracy-Widom fluctuations) — treat values right at λ_max as ambiguous, not a clean pass/fail.

📉

Real returns aren't i.i.d.

Time-varying volatility and fat tails aren't in the textbook derivation, but the band still holds up as a strong practical benchmark.

🔢

Use the true independent T

Overlapping windows inflate the effective T you'd plug in, making the band look narrower — and any factor look more significant — than the data supports.

🧹

Binary screen ≠ denoised matrix

Flagging in/out of band is not the same as building a cleaned, shrinkage-adjusted correlation matrix ready for an optimizer.

Section 12

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Why does a correlation matrix built from truly independent series still produce a spread of nonzero eigenvalues, instead of a flat spectrum at exactly 1?

The spread is finite-sample noise — the same way 20 fair coin flips rarely land exactly 10-10 even though the coin is unbiased.
Question 2

What is q, the Marchenko-Pastur shape parameter?

q = N/T is the single ratio the entire noise band's width depends on — not N or T individually.
Question 3

More history relative to instruments (a smaller q) does what to the noise band?

λ_min=(1−√q)² and λ_max=(1+√q)² both move toward 1 as √q→0 — more data per relationship means sample correlations converge more tightly to their true value.
Question 4

N=30, T=250. λ₂ = 1.1 falls inside the ≈[0.43, 1.81] noise band. What does that prove?

"Inside the band" is a statistical-power statement, not a certainty. A real but modest factor can hide there at small T — more history is the remedy, not a reinterpretation of the same sample.
Question 5

Why is an eigenvalue several multiples above λ_max a stronger signal than one sitting just barely above it?

λ₁=8.2 against λ_max≈1.81 is far outside any plausible noise fluctuation at that edge; a value of, say, 2.0 against the same 1.81 bound would not be.
Section 13

What to carry forward

01

Finite samples always spread eigenvalues away from 1, even for truly independent series — that's pure sampling noise, not evidence of structure.

02

The Marchenko-Pastur law gives the exact noise band: [λ_min, λ_max] = [(1−√q)², (1+√q)²], where q = N/T.

03

Only the ratio q matters, not N or T alone — more history per instrument narrows the band; more instruments relative to history widens it.

04

The decision rule: an eigenvalue inside the band is suspect; one sitting several multiples above λ_max is a strong factor candidate.

05

"Inside the band" is a power limit, not proof of zero structure — and edge effects mean a value just above λ_max is a weaker signal than one far above it.

06

This repo's diversificationCore.js already has the eigenvalues this check would need — running it is a concrete, unbuilt guardrail on the effective-bets metric, not something it currently does.

07

RMT reached markets in 1999 (Laloux et al.; Plerou et al.): most eigenvalues of a stock correlation matrix are noise, so a cleaned matrix, not the raw one, goes into the optimiser.

What this now lets you do

Before trusting PC2, PC3, or any component beyond a basket's dominant factor, compute q = N/T for your actual window, derive λ_min and λ_max, and check each real eigenvalue against that band instead of assuming every principal component past PC1 is meaningful. It turns "how diversified is this book, really?" from a guess into a testable claim.
