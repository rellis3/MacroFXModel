Order Flow Toxicity & VPIN, Read by the Bucket

A market maker doesn't need one catastrophic trade to get hurt — a long enough run of quietly one-sided, informed flow does the same damage, one small fill at a time. VPIN is an attempt to catch that pattern live: bucket the tape by traded volume instead of by the clock, guess how one-sided each bucket's flow was, and average the result into a single number. It is also one of market microstructure's most publicly argued metrics — famous for a warning it allegedly gave before the 2010 Flash Crash, and just as famous for the researchers who later picked that claim apart.

The whole idea
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

People talk about toxic order flow. What is that number actually measuring?

How one-sided the trading looks. A market maker can't tell a trader who knows something from one who is just rebalancing. VPIN tries to read that mix live, straight from the tape.

So what's the trick?

A different clock. Instead of cutting the day into five-minute bars, you cut it into buckets of equal volume. A bucket closes every thousand contracts, however long that takes. On a quiet afternoon one bucket might take twenty minutes. After a headline they fly past.

Why is that better?

Because people with news trade more, not on a timetable. Sampling by volume keeps roughly the same amount of information in each observation.

And how do you know who was buying in each bucket?

You don't, really. You guess from the price. If the price rose a lot over the bucket, compared with a normal move, call most of the volume buying. If it closed flat, call it fifty-fifty. That guess is called bulk volume classification.

Give me numbers.

Five buckets of a thousand contracts. The fourth one rose two normal moves, so the guess calls about nine hundred and seventy-seven contracts buying and twenty-three selling. That's an imbalance of nine hundred and fifty-four. The third bucket closed flat, so its imbalance is zero. Add up all five imbalances, divide by the five thousand contracts in the window, and you get about zero point five eight.

So fifty-eight percent one-sided. Is that high?

Only compared with the same market's own history. And despite the name, it isn't a probability. It's an average imbalance.

The famous story is the Flash Crash.

The sixth of May, twenty ten. VPIN on the E-mini future was said to be high in the hours before the crash. Andersen and Bondarenko pushed back hard. Change the bucket size or the window and the warning moves, and VPIN is high at plenty of times when nothing crashes.

What was the flow that day, really?

The regulators' report says the big seller was a fund hedging, selling seventy-five thousand contracts, about four point one billion dollars, at a fixed share of volume. Then fast traders passed contracts back and forth: more than twenty-seven thousand in fourteen seconds, for a net change of about two hundred.

So the flow was huge and one-sided, but nobody knew anything.

Exactly. Toxic to market makers, but not informed. Buy-side depth fell below one percent of its morning level.

The one-line version?

VPIN tells you how lopsided recent volume looked. It doesn't tell you who knows what, or whether a crash is coming.

Section 1

Toxicity doesn't need one big trade

A market maker can be picked off slowly — a hundred ordinary-looking fills, all the same direction, adding up to a real loss nobody sees coming until it's already there.

The mechanism
Many small trades, one direction
→
Quotes get filled, again and again
→
Losses accumulate, unnoticed
No individual trade looks unusual. The market maker only recognizes the pattern in hindsight — after a run of fills that, in isolation, each looked like ordinary two-sided business.

The Market Microstructure lesson builds the bid-ask spread from exactly this risk: a market maker can't tell an informed trader from a noise trader, and prices that uncertainty into the spread via a belief parameter, α — the assumed fraction of order flow that's informed. That lesson treats α as a fixed input. VPIN exists to answer the obvious follow-up: where does a number like that actually come from, moment to moment, off the real tape?

↔

One trade tells you almost nothing

A single buy could be anyone — a hedger, an index fund rebalancing, someone who simply changed their mind. Direction alone isn't evidence of information.

⏳

Persistence is the tell

A short run of one-sided flow is noise. A long run, sustained across many trades, starts looking like someone trading on information the market maker doesn't have yet.

🎭

Intent is invisible; flow isn't

A market maker can't ask a counterparty why they're trading. All that's observable is the tape itself — who lifted the offer, who hit the bid, and how often.

📉

VPIN tries to score the pattern

Not any one trade, but the flow's recent shape: has buying and selling been roughly balanced, or has it been running mostly one direction?

This is the entire motivation behind VPIN in one sentence: it isn't trying to catch a single bad trade — it's trying to catch a mood.
Section 2

A different clock: sample by volume, not by time

Markets don't generate information at a constant rate — a quiet Tuesday afternoon and the thirty seconds after a surprise headline are not the same five minutes.

Illustrative
Illustrative, not fitted to real data. The clock-time row (top) forces every five-minute window to count as one observation, whatever it contained. The volume-time row (bottom) closes a bucket the instant a fixed amount of volume has traded — keeping roughly the same amount of information per bucket instead of the same amount of time.

VPIN's actual mechanical innovation is exactly this and only this: pick a fixed bucket size V (often around 1/50th of an average day's volume, so roughly 50 buckets span a normal session), then walk the tape and close a new bucket every time cumulative volume since the last boundary reaches exactly V. A single trade that would push volume past the boundary gets split across the two buckets, so every bucket's total volume is exactly V — only its duration in real time varies.

Why it matters

Informed traders reveal themselves by trading more when they have something to act on, not by trading on a schedule. Sampling in volume time is a bet that this synchronizes far better with the true rate information is arriving than any fixed clock interval could.

Section 3

Splitting a bucket into buy and sell, without asking a trader

Bulk Volume Classification (BVC) never checks an individual trade against the quote — it reads the bucket's overall price move and guesses.

The formula
V_buy = V × Z(ΔP / σ)
→
V_sell = V − V_buy
Z(·) is the standard normal CDF — the same "how many typical moves was this" idea as a z-score. A flat bucket (ΔP = 0) gets Z(0) = 0.500 → an even 50/50 split.
Bucket closes flat

ΔP ≈ 0, standardized to a z-score of 0. Z(0) = 0.500 → V_buy = 50% of V, V_sell = 50% of V → order imbalance = 0. Read as: no net directional evidence in the price move.

Bucket has a big up move

Standardized price change of +2.0. Z(2.0) = 0.977 → V_buy ≈ 97.7% of V, V_sell ≈ 2.3% → order imbalance ≈ 95.4% of V. Read as: almost entirely buy-initiated.

Where this needs qualifying

BVC classifies an entire bucket at once, from one aggregate number — it never actually asks whether any specific trade was a buy or a sell. A single large resting order getting lifted by one buyer can produce the exact same price move, and get classified identically, as many small informed trades all pushing the same direction. The method genuinely cannot tell the two apart — a limitation the academic critique later in this lesson leans on directly.

Section 6

The VPIN formula, in three pieces

VPIN = (1 / n·V) × Σ |V_buy,τ − V_sell,τ| — a rolling, normalized average of order imbalance.

τ

OI_τ / V — one bucket's toxicity

How one-sided a single bucket's classified flow was — 0 at perfectly balanced, 1 at fully one-sided.

n

The averaging window

Smooths out single-bucket noise, at a real cost: a bigger window lags further behind a genuine, sudden change in flow.

∴

VPIN — the result

A single number, roughly 0 to 1, meant to track how informed recent flow looks right now — not a literal, calibrated probability.

VPIN is not a Bayesian probability

The name says "probability of informed trading," but VPIN is a normalized order-imbalance average, not a probability produced by a fitted likelihood model the way a genuine Bayesian posterior (like the Glosten-Milgrom belief update) is. A VPIN of 0.6 does not mean "there's a 60% chance the next trade is informed" — it means recent buckets, as classified by BVC, were on average 60% one-sided.

Section 8

The Flash Crash claim — and the fight over it

VPIN's public reputation rests almost entirely on one event, argued from both directions ever since.

Illustrative
Illustrative, not real market data. A rise into a stress event followed by a spike and slow decay is the shape of the original claim — but whether a rise like this is genuinely predictive, or simply what VPIN mechanically does whenever prices move a lot, is exactly the disputed part.

On May 6, 2010, U.S. equity index futures and a wide swath of individual stocks fell roughly 5–9% in minutes, then largely recovered within the same session. VPIN entered the public conversation almost entirely because of this event.

The claim

Easley, López de Prado & O'Hara reported VPIN, computed on E-mini S&P 500 futures order flow, rising to unusually high levels in the hours before the crash — framed as evidence that a real-time toxicity monitor could have flagged the danger building.

The critique

Andersen & Bondarenko re-examined the same data and argued the apparent predictive power was considerably weaker than portrayed, and highly sensitive to the BVC method and to the bucket-size (V) and window-length (n) choices — different reasonable parameters produced meaningfully different VPIN paths around the same event.

Genuinely unresolved, not settled

VPIN measures something real and mechanically well-defined — rolling, volume-bucketed order imbalance — and a rise in it around this stress event is a fact about the data. Whether that rise was genuine advance warning, as opposed to a coincident or mildly-lagging symptom of volatility that was already building, remains an open, actively debated question. Treat "VPIN predicted the Flash Crash" as a claim you should be able to argue both sides of, not a settled fact to repeat.

Section 9

Where this shows up on a desk — and where this repo stands

🛡️

Market-making risk controls

A market maker watching a toxicity proxy rise can mechanically widen quotes or shrink size — the same Glosten-Milgrom logic, applied live.

🐌

Slowing an execution schedule

A desk working a large order that sees toxicity spike may pause or slow participation, on the theory that trading into unusually one-sided flow right now is more likely to get run over.

📊

One input among several

In practice, a toxicity proxy like this typically sits alongside realized volatility, spread widening, and order-book depth — not a standalone signal fired in isolation.

Verified against this repo (September 2026)

The full lesson tags this concept "not built or tested in this repo," and that check holds up: nothing in this codebase implements VPIN, Bulk Volume Classification, or any genuine volume-bucketed order-imbalance signal. Two things worth being precise about while checking: the repo's oi_recon/, oi-dashboard.html, and related oi_* files are Open Interest — options positioning data — an entirely different, unrelated codebase despite the name overlap. Separately, js/cogExecutionTrigger.js defines its own "orderflowProxy" used by the COG engine's trigger gate — but it computes something much simpler (where a bar's close sits within that bar's own high-low range) and isn't a buy/sell volume split, a volume bucket, or an informed-trading estimate. Neither one is VPIN; the concept tag stands.

Section 10 · It actually happened

What the flow really was on 6 May 2010

Both sides of the VPIN argument take the tape as given. The SEC/CFTC staff report reconstructed it.

It actually happened

The big seller was a mutual fund complex hedging: 75,000 E-mini contracts (about $4.1 billion), sold at a fixed share of recent volume with no price limit. In Glosten-Milgrom terms, a very large noise trader. Intermediaries bought, then had to unload, much of it to each other: in one 14-second stretch they traded more than 27,000 contracts (about 49% of volume) for a net change of about 200. The report called it "hot potato" trading.

Section 11

Where the textbook breaks, and what's untested here

Where the textbook version breaks

VPIN assumes one-sided flow means informed flow, and that volume tracks information arriving. On 6 May the biggest one-sided flow was an uninformed hedge, and hot-potato volume carried no new information at all. A measure that rises mechanically with big one-way moves and recycled volume will read high in a crash whether or not it warned in advance. That's the gap the Andersen & Bondarenko critique points at.

Not tested here "A high VPIN warns of a rougher tape ahead": not tested on this desk, which has no trade-level futures tape. A fair test fixes V, n and a threshold in advance, computes VPIN on years of E-mini trades, and compares the next hour's range after a first crossing with periods matched on prior volatility (VPIN rises with volatility mechanically). It reports false alarms next to hits and needs both halves of the sample to agree.
Section 12

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

What's the core mechanical difference between a VPIN bucket and an ordinary time bar?

A time bar closes on a fixed clock interval no matter how much volume traded. A VPIN bucket closes the instant cumulative volume hits a fixed size V — spanning twenty minutes on a quiet day, thirty seconds during a burst.
Question 2

In Bulk Volume Classification, why does a bucket that closes exactly flat get a 50/50 buy/sell split?

BVC sets V_buy = V·Z(ΔP/σ). A price change of zero standardizes to a z-score of 0, and the standard normal CDF at 0 is exactly 0.5 — an even split, whether or not that reflects what individual trades actually did.
Question 3

A bucket has V = 1,000 and is classified as entirely buy-initiated (V_buy = 1,000, V_sell = 0). What is its order imbalance?

OI_τ = |V_buy − V_sell| = |1,000 − 0| = 1,000, the maximum possible value since OI_τ can never exceed V.
Question 4

What does the Andersen & Bondarenko critique argue about VPIN's role in the Flash Crash?

They re-examined the same data and found the pattern sensitive to the Bulk Volume Classification method and to bucket-size/window choices, with comparably elevated VPIN readings occurring at other times without a crash following — a real, unresolved challenge, not a claim that VPIN never moved.
Question 5

Does a VPIN reading of 0.6 mean "there is a 60% chance the next trade is informed"?

Despite the name "probability of informed trading," VPIN is not produced by a fitted likelihood model. A VPIN of 0.6 means recent buckets, as classified by BVC, were on average 60% one-sided — not a rigorous probability statement about any single next trade.
Your call
Scenario drill · 6 May 2010

Your toxicity monitor is pinned high, and the selling will not stop

You make markets in E-mini S&P 500 futures. It is the afternoon of 6 May 2010. Volume is exploding, almost every recent bucket closes lower, and your VPIN-style monitor is near the top of its range. You cannot see who is selling or why. What do you do with your quotes?

  1. Keep quoting full size at your usual spread. VPIN's track record is disputed, so ignore it.
    The forecasting claim is disputed. What the monitor describes is not: the flow hitting you is one-sided right now, and one-sided flow runs over whoever is providing liquidity, informed or not. Quoting full size into it is how a market maker gets run over.
  2. Read it as informed selling and go short in size.
    VPIN measures lopsidedness, not knowledge. A huge one-way hedge or recycled volume pushes it up just as well as someone with news. Shorting a falling, thin market on that reading is a bet on information that may not exist.
  3. Widen your spread and cut your quoted size, reading the monitor as a description of the flow now, not a forecast.
    This is the Glosten-Milgrom response the lesson describes: protect yourself against one-sided flow while it is there, and stay in the market. It does not need VPIN to predict anything, only to describe what is hitting you.
  4. Pull all your quotes and step away until it calms down.
    It protects your book, and liquidity providers did pull back that day. But when everyone does it, nobody is left to absorb the selling, and that is how depth vanishes. Defensible for one firm, but not the measured response.

What happened. The SEC/CFTC staff report found that the big seller was a fund complex hedging an equity position, not trading on information. It sold 75,000 E-mini contracts, about $4.1 billion, through an algorithm set to sell a fixed share of the previous minute's volume, with no limit on price or time. Intermediaries passed contracts back and forth, more than 27,000 in 14 seconds for a net change of about 200. Buy-side depth in the E-mini fell to less than 1% of its morning level, and index futures fell roughly 5–9% in minutes before largely recovering the same session. The flow was toxic, but nobody in it knew anything.

Section 13

What to carry forward

01

Order flow toxicity is death by a thousand cuts — a market maker picked off by persistently one-sided, informed flow, with no single trade unusual enough to raise the alarm on its own.

02

VPIN's real innovation is the volume clock, not a new theory — bucketing by traded volume instead of elapsed time better tracks the rate new information actually arrives.

03

Bulk Volume Classification turns each bucket's price change into an assumed buy/sell split via the normal CDF — a statistical proxy for direction, not a verified per-trade tag.

04

VPIN = the rolling average of each bucket's |buy − sell| imbalance, normalized by volume — bounded between 0 (balanced) and 1 (fully one-sided).

05

The claim that VPIN gave advance warning of the 2010 Flash Crash is genuinely disputed — Andersen & Bondarenko showed sensitivity to BVC and to the bucket-size/window parameters.

06

Treat VPIN as one input among several — spread, depth, realized volatility — feeding a broader "is the market stressed right now" read, not a standalone crash predictor.

07

Toxic isn't the same as informed. On 6 May 2010 the big seller was hedging, and intermediaries traded 27,000+ contracts in 14 seconds for a net change of about 200: both push VPIN up mechanically. Whether VPIN warns of a rougher tape is untested here.

What this now lets you do

Read a volume-imbalance signal correctly: know exactly what it's built from (equal-volume buckets, a normal-CDF classification, a rolling normalized average), why volume-time sampling was chosen over clock-time, and how much confidence any single claim that it "predicts" a crash actually deserves — plus the guardrails (parameter sensitivity, no literal probability) worth applying before trusting one operationally.
