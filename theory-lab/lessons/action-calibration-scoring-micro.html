Scoring Your Own Calls

"I'm usually right" is a feeling. "My 70% calls come true 55% of the time" is a measurement you can size from. Here is how to turn a journal into a score.

The whole idea
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

I write a confidence on every call now. Seventy percent, eighty percent. Is that number worth anything?

Only if it comes true about as often as you say. That is calibration. If your seventy percent calls come true seven times in ten, your seventy means seventy.

But I can't grade one call. If it fails, maybe I was just unlucky.

Right. One call proves nothing. So you collect them. Every seventy percent call goes in one bucket, every eighty in another, and you count how many came true in each.

Like a weather forecaster.

That is exactly where it came from. Glenn Brier proposed his score in nineteen fifty to grade rain forecasts. You take the gap between what you said and what happened, square it, and average it over all your calls. Zero is perfect. Lower is better.

Why square it?

So confident misses hurt. A seventy percent call that comes true costs you nine hundredths. One that fails costs forty-nine hundredths. More than five times as much.

So what counts as a good score?

Don't compare it with zero. Compare it with someone who quotes your overall hit rate on every single call. They have no skill at telling one call from another, but they know the average. If you can't beat them, your numbers add nothing.

Give me a real example.

Forty calls in a quarter. On average you wrote about sixty-six percent. Twenty-two came true, which is fifty-five percent. Your score comes out at about twenty-eight hundredths. Quoting fifty-five percent every time would have scored about twenty-five hundredths. You lost to the plain benchmark by about twelve percent.

Ouch. Where did it go wrong?

The high-confidence calls. Your eighty and ninety percent calls came true half the time, no better than your fifty percent calls. The score splits into parts, and they show both problems: the numbers were inflated, and they barely separated good calls from bad ones.

Does that matter if I still made money?

It matters for size. At even odds, if you believe you are right sixty-six percent of the time, Kelly says risk a third of the bankroll. Your record supports a tenth. That is more than three times too big, past the point where growth turns negative.

And I can't just shade my numbers to score better?

No. The score is built so that writing your honest belief always does best on average. Any shading costs you, call after call.

So what changes next quarter?

Swap your felt numbers for your measured ones. And keep one simple rule: until your eighty and ninety percent calls beat your sixty percent calls, they all get the same size.

The one-line version?

Write a number on every call, count the hits, and size from what you measured, not what you felt.

Section 1

A way to grade a call that said "70%"

Brier (1950); Murphy (1973); Tetlock's forecasting research (2005–2015).

How it reached trading

A journal of reasons can't be graded: one outcome never proves a "probably" right or wrong. So traders graded by P&L — or not at all.

Brier's 1950 score for weather forecasts fixed that, and Murphy split it into reliability, resolution and uncertainty. Tetlock's expert studies and the Good Judgment Project's tournament results, all Brier-scored, made "keep score in probabilities" standard for discretionary analysts; probability-output models are checked the same way.

Section 2

Grade the numbers, not the day

One rainy day can't grade a "70% rain" forecast. Every day she said 70%, together, can: did it rain on about seven in ten?

Calibrated

Her 70% days: rain on ~70% of them. Her 30% days: ~30%. Her numbers mean what they say.

Calibrated but useless

She says "30%" every day in a town where it rains 30% of days. Perfect calibration, zero information.

You need both: numbers that mean what they say, and numbers that move when the evidence does.

Marked by the rule at the deadline — not by whether the trade made money.

Section 5

Honesty is the best policy — exactly

If your true hit rate is p and you write f, your expected score is p(1−f)² + (1−p)f². It's lowest at f = p. Shading your number costs exactly (f − p)² per call.

Section 8

It takes many calls

If you really are calibrated at f, a bucket of n calls lands within f ± 1.96·√(f(1−f)/n) about 95% of the time.

Ten calls at 70% tell you almost nothing. Pool buckets (sum of hits vs sum of stated probabilities) and wait for numbers.

Section 10 · It actually happened

The people who keep score

Weather forecasters. Murphy and Winkler found US National Weather Service precipitation forecasts remarkably well calibrated in the 1970s: many similar calls, fast outcomes, every one scored. Nate Silver later described a "wet bias" in some TV forecasts — rain chances overstated on purpose. Different incentive, worse calibration.

Tetlock's forecasters. Two decades of scored expert predictions (2005) found most experts barely beat simple rules. In the IARPA tournament from 2011, the Good Judgment Project's best "superforecasters", Brier-scored, kept outperforming year after year — skill, not luck. Their shared habit: precise probabilities and keeping score.

Section 12

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Three calls at 80%: two happen, one doesn't. Brier score?

(0.04 + 0.04 + 0.64) / 3 = 0.24. Barely better than always saying 50% — and three calls say almost nothing.
Question 2

60% of your calls come true. What's the benchmark Brier to beat?

0.6 × 0.4 = 0.24: what someone who knows the base rate but can't tell calls apart would score.
Question 3

Reliability 0.001, resolution 0.000. Good forecaster?

Calibrated but useless: zero resolution means her score is the base-rate score or worse. Resolution is what makes calls worth acting on.
Question 4

Twenty calls at 90%, fifteen came true. Overconfident?

Expected 18, SE √(20·0.9·0.1) = 1.34, z = −3/1.34 = −2.24. Beyond what luck usually produces.
Your call
Scenario drill · an illustrative quarterly review

Your next call feels like a 90%

You have just scored a quarter of 40 calls, the same tally as the worked example. Your ten calls written at 80% or 90% came true five times. Your twelve 60% calls came true seven times. The next morning a setup appears that feels like a 90% call, at an even payoff. How do you size it?

  1. Size it as a 90% call. This one really is different.
    At even payoff, Kelly from 90% is 2(0.9) − 1 = 80% of the bankroll. Your 90% calls have come true about half the time, so that is many times the size your record supports. Every high-confidence call felt different too.
  2. Use the corrected number from your table, about 54%, and size it the same as your ordinary calls.
    The shrunk hit rate for the 90% bucket is 53.6%, which gives a Kelly fraction of about 7%. This follows the rule the review produced: until your 80% and 90% calls beat your 60% calls, they get the same size.
  3. Halve what the 90% would say, to be safe.
    Half of 80% is 40% of the bankroll. That still sizes off the felt number. Against a measured edge near 54%, it is more than five times the Kelly fraction, deep in the zone where growth turns negative.
  4. Skip the trade until you have more calls on record.
    It cannot over-bet, but the call still looks better than a coin flip, and you only learn your calibration by making and scoring calls. Taking it at the corrected size does both.

What the numbers say. This is an illustrative review built on the lesson's worked example, not a real trade. The felt 90% would size at 80% of the bankroll; the measured 53.6% sizes at about 7%. The gap between those two numbers is what an unscored confidence costs. Keep scoring: if the next 40 calls show your high-confidence calls pulling ahead, the table will let them earn more size.

Section 13

What to carry forward

01

Write every call as an event, a deadline and a rounded probability.

02

Brier = reliability − resolution + uncertainty. Beat the base-rate score, not 0.25.

03

The score is proper: shading your number costs (f − p)².

04

Size from the measured hit rate. Stated 66.5% vs real 55% is Kelly 33% vs 10%.

05

Desk tests: an unscored conviction vote was anti-predictive; a 54.1% hit rate matched its base rate. Score confidence before it sets size.
