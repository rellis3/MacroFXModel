Risk & Sizing · Trade management

Managing the Trade, or Every Decision After Entry Is an Exit

Entries get the attention, but once you are in, every choice is about leaving: where the stop sits, when to take profit, whether to trail, when to give up. This guide measures all of it in R, shows where stops belong, proves that no exit rule can create an edge from noise, and counts what moved stops and early profits really cost.

The whole idea
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

I spend hours on entries. Once I'm in, I just sort of manage it by feel.

Then most of your results are being decided by feel. Every choice after the entry is an exit choice: where the stop goes, whether to take profit, whether to trail, when to give up.

So how do I even compare trades? One was fifty pips, one was two hundred.

Measure them in R. One R is the loss you planned when you placed the stop. A trade that makes twice its planned risk is plus two R. A stopped-out trade is minus one R. Now every trade speaks the same language.

And then?

Then expectancy is simple. Say you win forty percent of the time, winners make two R, losers lose one R. Forty percent of two is zero point eight. Sixty percent of one is zero point six. You make a fifth of an R per trade, on average.

Only forty percent winners, and it still makes money.

Yes, because the winners are bigger. Now the bad news. Close half your winners early at one R, and let one loser in five run to minus three because you moved the stop. Same entries. You now lose about a quarter of an R per trade.

Ouch. So where should the stop go?

Where the reason for the trade is wrong. Not where it starts to hurt. And outside ordinary noise. If a day's typical move is fifty pips, a twenty pip stop gets touched by pure noise most days, about seven times in ten on a random walk.

What about targets versus letting winners run? Everyone argues about that.

Here's the uncomfortable part. If prices were pure noise, no exit rule would help. A two R target before a one R stop gets hit one time in three, and that works out to exactly zero. Exits only shape the outcomes. The edge has to come from the idea.

And partial exits? Taking half off feels safer.

It is smoother. You win more often and the swings are smaller. But the expectancy is just the average of the two halves. Scaling out can't beat the better half.

And the stop always protects me?

A stop is an order, not a guarantee. In January twenty fifteen the Swiss central bank dropped its floor under the franc without warning. The euro fell from one twenty to below ninety centimes in minutes. Stops just under one twenty filled far lower. A fifty pip stop filled a thousand pips away is a twenty one R loss.

So the one-line version?

Count in R, put the stop where the idea dies, size for the gap, and never move a stop further away.

Section 1

Five exit tools, one question

Each tool answers "when do I leave?" differently. None of them answers "is there an edge?"

🛑

Stop

Where the reason for the trade is wrong. It defines 1R, your unit of risk.

🎯

Target

A fixed profit level. High hit rate, capped winners.

🪢

Trailing stop

Follows the best price at a set distance. Lets winners run, but always hands back that distance from the peak.

⏱

Time stop

Leave after N days if nothing has happened. Ends exposure once the reason has expired.

✂️

Partial exit

Take some off early, let the rest run. A blend of two exits: smoother, never better than the better half.

The entry decides whether you have an edge. The exits decide how that edge is paid out, and how much of it leaks away.
Section 2

Count in R, judge by expectancy

1R is the loss you planned at entry. Every result becomes a multiple of it.

Follow it through
R = (exit − entry) / (entry − stop)
Long at 1.0850, stop 1.0775: 1R = 75 pips. Exit at 1.1000 is +150 pips = +2R. Stopped out is −1R. A gap fill at 1.0700 is −2R. R divides out size and instrument, so every trade in the journal speaks the same language.
E = p·W̄ − (1 − p)·L̄ − c
Win 40% of the time at +2R, lose 1R otherwise: E = 0.8 − 0.6 = +0.20R a trade. A 70% hit rate with +0.4R winners has E = 0.28 − 0.30 = −0.02R. Hit rate is what feels good; expectancy is what pays.
p* = (L̄ + c) / (W̄ + L̄)
With +2R winners and −1R losers, p* = 1/3: win more than one trade in three and you make money. Add 2 pips of cost on a 75-pip stop (0.027R) and p* rises to 34.2%.

One trade's result swings far more than its average: for the 40% / +2R system the swing per trade is 1.47R against an average of 0.20R. You need about (2 × 1.47 / 0.20)² ≈ 216 trades to see that edge two standard errors clear of zero.

Section 4

A stop is an invalidation point, not a pain threshold

Put it where the idea is wrong, beyond ordinary noise. How often does noise alone touch a stop? Drag to find out.

Try it
Stop distance (daily σ)0.40σ
Touched within 1 day 68.9% of the time, within 5 days 85.8%.

With a daily σ of 50 pips, a "safe" 20-pip stop is 0.4σ: noise touches it within a day about 69% of the time. A 75-pip stop (1.5σ) is touched within a day 13% of the time. Scale stops with volatility (a multiple of σ or ATR, where ATR ≈ 1.6σ on a random walk) and the stop keeps its meaning in calm and busy markets. Fear belongs in the position size, never in the stop.

Section 5

Exits reshape outcomes; they do not create edge

On a random walk, a target b above entry is hit before a stop a below it with probability a / (a + b).

+2R target, −1R stop: P = 1/3
→
E = ⅓×2 − ⅔×1 = 0
+0.5R target, −1R stop: P = 2/3
→
E = ⅔×0.5 − ⅓×1 = 0

The hit rate moved from 33% to 67%. The expectancy did not move at all. The general rule is Wald's identity: expected profit per trade = drift × average holding time. No drift, no profit, whatever the exit.

When exits cannot help
  • Prices are pure noise after your entry
  • Every rule then earns zero before costs, and less after
  • Under a random walk a stop-loss rule always lowers expected return (Kaminski and Lo, 2014)
When exits matter
  • The edge fades: a time stop ends exposure when it is gone
  • Adverse moves mean "probably wrong": a stop cuts wrong ideas early
  • Moves persist: letting winners run pays; ranges reward taking profit
Section 6

Targets, trailing stops and time stops: what each one costs

🎯

Fixed target

Caps every winner. Pays when prices tend to stall or reverse after your entry; leaves money behind in trends.

🪢

Trailing stop

Exits a set distance below the best price, so it always gives that distance back. Too tight and noise ends trades before a trend can form; too wide and each good trade returns a lot at the end. On a random walk the best price before a pullback of d averages exactly d above entry, so the trail earns zero.

⏱

Time stop

Once an edge's window has passed, each extra day adds variance and no expected profit. A time stop also frees the risk budget for the next idea.

Which exit wins is an empirical question about your entries and your market, not a matter of taste. Test it on hundreds of trades, with costs.
Section 7

Partial exits: smoother, never better

Expectancy is linear in size, so a blend's expectancy is the weighted average of its parts. Three plans on a random walk, −1R stop:

Same entries, three exit plans
All out at +2R: hit rate 33% · expectancy 0 · swing 1.41R
Half at +1R, half at +3R: hit rate 25%, plus 25% scratched at 0 · expectancy 0 · swing 1.22R
Half at +1R, then stop to break-even, half at +3R: hit rate 50% · expectancy 0 · swing 1.12R

Scaling out wins more often and swings less, at exactly the same expectancy here. With a real edge it trades some expectancy for smoothness: worth it only if smoothness keeps you on the plan. Scaling in is the mirror image: after adding, recompute the whole position's risk; it is often more than 1R.

Section 10 · It actually happened

15 January 2015: the stop that was not an exit

It actually happened

From September 2011 the Swiss National Bank held a minimum of 1.20 francs per euro, promising unlimited purchases. Traders bought EUR/CHF near 1.20 with stops just below. On the morning of 15 January 2015 the SNB scrapped the floor without warning and cut its deposit rate to −0.75%. EUR/CHF fell below 0.90 within minutes, with almost no trading in between. Stops filled far below their levels; some clients ended owing their brokers money, FXCM needed a $300 million rescue loan, and Alpari UK went into insolvency.

Section 11

The two leaks: cut winners, moved stops

Same entries, same market. Only the management changes.

Plan: 40% × 2R − 60% × 1R = +0.20R
Close half the winners at +1R: W̄ = 1.5 → 0.00R
Also let 1 loser in 5 run to −3R: L̄ = 1.4 → −0.24R

Both habits are the disposition effect acted out inside a single trade: selling winners too early and riding losers too long (Shefrin and Statman, 1985; Odean, 1998). The cure is mechanical: write the stop and the exit rule before entry, and let stops move only in the direction that reduces risk.

Where this needs qualifying

The stop, target and trailing results all assume prices move continuously, so a stop fills at its level. Real prices jump at weekends, announcements and policy changes. And MAE studies overfit easily: choose exits on one sample, test them on another, include costs, and judge them on hundreds of trades, not twenty.

Section 12

Quick check

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

You win 40% of trades at +2R and lose 1R on the rest. What is the expectancy per trade?

E = 0.40 × 2 − 0.60 × 1 = 0.80 − 0.60 = +0.20R. Losing more often is fine when the winners are bigger: break-even is a 33% hit rate.
Question 2

Where should a stop go?

A stop is an invalidation point, not a pain threshold. The money at risk is controlled by position size. A stop inside the noise (say 0.4σ) is touched by noise alone within a day about 69% of the time.
Question 3

On a pure random walk, you switch from a +2R target to a +0.5R target, keeping a −1R stop. What happens?

P(target first) = a/(a+b). Both plans have E = 0: exits reshape outcomes but cannot create edge from noise. Expected profit = drift × holding time.
Question 4

What does taking half off at +1R do to a trade plan?

Expectancy is linear in size. Partials change the shape (more small wins, smaller swings), not the average beyond the better part.
Question 5

Long EUR/CHF at 1.2050, stop 1.2000, on 15 January 2015. The stop fills at 1.1000. What is the result in R?

(1.1000 − 1.2050) / (1.2050 − 1.2000) = −1,050 / 50 = −21R. A stop fills at the next available price. Only the size, chosen with the gap in mind, could have limited this.
Your call
Scenario drill · 23 June 2016

Referendum night, and your trade is in profit

The UK votes on leaving the European Union today, and polls close at 10pm. Earlier in the week you bought GBP/USD at 1.4700 (an illustrative position) with a stop at 1.4550, 150 pips, so 1R = 150 pips. Late in the evening sterling trades around 1.50 as markets lean towards Remain: you are up about 2R. Results will arrive overnight, in thin markets. What do you do with the position before the results?

  1. Move the stop up to entry at 1.4700. Now the trade can't lose.
    A break-even stop is an order, not a floor. If the result shocks the market, price can fall far through 1.4700 before a fill. The stop changes the hit rate on a normal night; it does not cap the loss on an abnormal one.
  2. Add to the position. The polls point to Remain, and adding to winners is how trends pay.
    Scaling in only pays when moves persist. A binary event is not a trend: adding raises the size just before the largest gap risk of the year, and the combined position's real risk is now several times 1R.
  3. Close the trade, or cut it to a size whose loss you could accept if the price gapped many hundreds of pips.
    The trade was not built to hold a binary vote result. An event rule, like a time stop, ends exposure the plan never priced. If you keep any, size it for the gap line, not for the stop.
  4. Keep the trade but buy a short-dated sterling put as protection.
    Defensible: an option caps the loss whatever the gap. But protection is expensive the night everyone wants it, and it does not answer whether the trade belongs in the book at all.

What happened. Sterling traded around 1.50 against the dollar shortly after the polls closed. As results came in overnight showing a vote to leave, it fell to about 1.32 on 24 June, its lowest level since 1985, a fall of more than 10% within hours. A long from 1.4700 faced a move of roughly 1,500 pips, about 10R on a 150-pip stop; how much of that a stop saved depended on where it filled in a fast, thin market.

Section 13

What to carry forward

01

Count every trade in R. Expectancy E = p·W̄ − (1 − p)·L̄ − c, not the hit rate, decides whether a plan pays: 40% at +2R earns +0.20R a trade.

02

Stops go where the idea is wrong, beyond the noise, scaled by volatility. Fear belongs in the size, never in the stop.

03

Exits reshape outcomes; they do not create edge. On noise every rule earns zero before costs (profit = drift × time held).

04

Partials and break-even stops smooth the ride but never beat the better part. Set stops and targets from your own MAE and MFE, tested out of sample.

05

Cutting winners and moving stops take the same entries from +0.20R to −0.24R. A stop is an order, not a guarantee: SNB, 15 January 2015, a 50-pip stop filled at 1.10 is −21R.

What this now lets you do

Read a trade journal in R and compute its expectancy, break-even win rate and how many trades it needs. Place a stop at invalidation and check how often noise alone would hit it. See through any exit rule that claims to turn a coin flip into an edge, and price the two habits that quietly drain most traders' results.
