Random Matrix Theory & the Marchenko-Pastur Law

The PCA lesson showed how to split a correlation matrix into ranked eigenvalues. It never asked the harder question: with only a finite sample of history, how many of those eigenvalues are real, and how many are exactly what pure noise looks like when you don't have enough data?

CONCEPT — extends js/diversificationCore.js's existing eigenvalue math ~18 min read Prereqs: Principal Component Analysis & Factor Regimes, Linear Algebra & Matrices

Why this matters

The PCA lesson in this Theory Lab ends on a clean, useful result: sort a correlation matrix's eigenvalues from largest to smallest, and the biggest one usually corresponds to a genuine, interpretable common factor — for an FX basket, "risk-on/risk-off," a broad dollar factor that most pairs load onto together. It's a satisfying story, and for the very first eigenvalue it's usually a true one. But the lesson quietly skipped a harder question that shows up the moment you look past PC1: how do you know the second-largest eigenvalue is a real factor too, rather than nothing at all — just an artifact of the fact that you only had, say, 250 days of history to estimate a 30-instrument correlation matrix from?

This is not a hypothetical worry. Even a correlation matrix built from instruments that are, by construction, completely independent of each other — no shared factor, no real structure whatsoever — will not produce a flat, boring eigenvalue spectrum when you only have a finite number of observations to estimate it from. It will produce a spread of eigenvalues, some above 1, some below, purely from sampling noise. If you don't know what that noise-only spread is supposed to look like, you have no principled way to tell a real second factor from an unlucky roll of the dice in your sample window. Random Matrix Theory (RMT) — specifically the Marchenko-Pastur law — is the tool that tells you exactly what the noise-only spread looks like, as a precise, derivable band of values, so you can check any real eigenvalue against it before you believe it.

Plain-English intuition

Flipping coins tells you what "no pattern" looks like, too

Flip a fair coin 20 times and you will almost never get exactly 10 heads and 10 tails. You might get 12-8, or 8-12, or occasionally 15-5. None of those outcomes mean the coin is biased — they're exactly what "no real pattern, finite sample" looks like, and statistics gives you a precise band (a confidence interval) around 50/50 that tells you how much wobble is normal before you'd start suspecting a rigged coin. Random Matrix Theory does the same job for correlation-matrix eigenvalues. If every instrument in your basket were truly independent — no shared factor at all, the statistical equivalent of a fair coin — a correlation matrix estimated from a finite number of days would still show eigenvalues scattered above and below 1, purely from sampling noise, the same way 20 fair coin flips scatter around but not exactly on 10-10. The Marchenko-Pastur law is the "how much wobble is normal" band for eigenvalues: a precise, math-derived range that pure noise is expected to live inside, given how many instruments you have relative to how many days of data you used. An eigenvalue inside that range isn't obviously a real factor, any more than 12 heads out of 20 is obviously a biased coin. An eigenvalue far outside it is a different story — that's the equivalent of flipping 19 heads out of 20.

The math, step by step

Everything below builds directly on the PCA lesson's setup: \(N\) instruments, \(T\) days of returns, arranged into a correlation matrix \(\Sigma\) whose eigenvalues \(\lambda_1 \geq \lambda_2 \geq \dots \geq \lambda_N\) sum to \(N\) (the trace of a correlation matrix — every diagonal entry is 1). Nothing about that machinery changes here. What changes is what you're allowed to conclude from a given eigenvalue's size.

Step 1 — why a genuinely flat spectrum is only a limit, never a real observation

If your \(N\) instruments were truly, perfectly independent, and you had infinite history (\(T \to \infty\)) to estimate their correlation matrix from, that matrix would converge exactly to the identity matrix — every off-diagonal correlation exactly 0, every eigenvalue exactly 1. A perfectly flat spectrum at 1 is what "no shared structure, in the limit of infinite data" looks like. But you never have infinite data. \(T\) is always some finite number — 250 trading days in a year, maybe a few thousand across a decade of history if the instrument's even existed that long — and \(N\), the instrument count in a basket you actually care about, is not always small next to that. That gap between the idealized infinite-data case and the finite sample you actually have is exactly where the trouble starts.

Step 2 — a thought experiment: correlate pure noise with itself

Take this literally rather than abstractly. Generate \(N = 30\) completely independent random return series — no shared driver of any kind built in, by construction — and draw \(T = 250\) observations of each, exactly the shape of one year of daily returns across a 30-pair FX basket. Because these are independent by construction, the true (population) correlation between any two of them is exactly 0. But compute the sample correlation between any two of these series over those 250 draws, and it will not come out at exactly 0.00 — by pure chance, some pairs will show a sample correlation of 0.06, others -0.11, others 0.03, purely because 250 finite draws of two truly unrelated series will happen to drift together on some days and apart on others, the same way 20 fair coin flips don't land exactly 10-10. Build the full \(30 \times 30\) sample correlation matrix out of these purely coincidental sample correlations, and its eigenvalues will not all equal 1. They will spread out — some noticeably above 1, some below — even though there is, by construction, zero genuine correlation structure anywhere in the data that generated them. This spread is not evidence of anything real; it is the specific, characteristic footprint that finite-sample noise leaves on a correlation matrix's eigenvalues, and it is exactly as knowable, in advance, as the spread of outcomes from flipping a coin 20 times.

Step 3 — the Marchenko-Pastur law

Marchenko and Pastur (1967) proved something remarkable about exactly this thought experiment: it doesn't just produce "some spread" — the spread converges to a specific, fully derivable probability distribution. Formally, take an \(N \times N\) sample correlation matrix built from \(N\) mutually independent series, each with \(T\) i.i.d. observations (mean zero, unit variance). In the limit \(N, T \to \infty\) with the ratio held fixed at:

\[ q = \frac{N}{T} \]

the empirical distribution of that matrix's eigenvalues converges to the Marchenko-Pastur distribution — and critically, that distribution has bounded support: in the idealized limit, no eigenvalue of a pure-noise correlation matrix falls outside a specific, computable band:

\[ \lambda_{\min} = (1 - \sqrt{q})^2 \qquad\qquad \lambda_{\max} = (1 + \sqrt{q})^2 \]

Read \(q = N/T\) exactly as "how many instruments you're trying to estimate simultaneous pairwise relationships for, relative to how many independent days of data you have to estimate them from." That single ratio is the entire input the noise band depends on — not \(N\) alone, not \(T\) alone, but their relationship. Within the band, the density is not uniform; it is highest somewhere in the middle of \([\lambda_{\min}, \lambda_{\max}]\) and tapers toward zero at both edges, which is exactly why a histogram of a real basket's noise-only eigenvalues tends to look like a hump sitting inside the band, not a flat plateau — and, in the idealized limit, the density is exactly zero everywhere outside the band.

A smaller \(q\) — meaning relatively more history for a given number of instruments, either because \(N\) is small or \(T\) is large — pushes \(\sqrt{q}\) toward 0, which pushes both \(\lambda_{\min}\) and \(\lambda_{\max}\) toward 1: the noise band narrows and tightens around 1, because more data per instrument means sample correlations converge more tightly to their true (zero) values, so a pure-noise matrix's eigenvalues cluster closer to the flat, no-structure spectrum described in Step 1. A larger \(q\) — more instruments relative to history — pushes the band wider: \(\lambda_{\min}\) falls toward 0 and \(\lambda_{\max}\) rises well above 1, meaning noise alone can produce a much bigger spread of eigenvalues, so a much larger eigenvalue is needed before it's distinguishable from what chance alone could produce. In the extreme case \(q \to 1\) (nearly as many instruments as observations), \(\lambda_{\min} \to 0\); if \(q > 1\) (literally more instruments than days of data, \(N > T\)), the sample correlation matrix becomes mathematically singular — rank at most \(T\) — and a fraction \((1 - 1/q)\) of its eigenvalues sit at exactly zero, a separate, harder failure mode worth flagging on its own (a wide basket estimated over a short window doesn't just have a wide noise band, it has structurally unrecoverable directions).

Step 4 — the practical decision rule

Put a real basket's real sample correlation matrix's eigenvalues next to this band, and the rule is direct. Compute \(q = N/T\) for your actual basket and window, compute \(\lambda_{\min}\) and \(\lambda_{\max}\) from the formula above, then look at each observed eigenvalue \(\lambda_i\):

  • \(\lambda_i\) inside \([\lambda_{\min}, \lambda_{\max}]\): statistically indistinguishable from what a matrix of pure, uncorrelated noise would produce at this same \(N\) and \(T\). This does not prove there is no real structure behind that component — it means your current sample size doesn't give you enough evidence to tell the two apart, which for practical purposes should be treated with real suspicion before being called a genuine factor.
  • \(\lambda_i\) clearly above \(\lambda_{\max}\): evidence that this component explains more shared variance than pure chance could produce at this \(N\), \(T\) — a strong candidate for a genuine common factor. This is exactly why the PCA lesson's dollar/ risk-on PC1, which routinely explains 50-70%+ of an FX basket's variance, survives this test so comfortably: an eigenvalue that large sits nowhere near the noise band at any realistic \(N/T\), while PC2 and PC3 — the components the PCA lesson was already more cautious about — are exactly the ones most likely to land inside it.
\(N\)Number of instruments in the basket — the number of rows/columns of the correlation matrix, and the number of eigenvalues it has.
\(T\)Number of independent observations (e.g. trading days) used to estimate the correlation matrix — the amount of information available per instrument.
\(q\)\(= N/T\), the Marchenko-Pastur shape parameter — instruments relative to history. Smaller \(q\) (more history per instrument) narrows the noise band; larger \(q\) widens it.
\(\lambda_i\)The \(i\)-th eigenvalue of the real, observed sample correlation matrix (same object as in the PCA lesson) — what gets checked against the noise band.
\(\lambda_{\min}, \lambda_{\max}\)\((1 \mp \sqrt{q})^2\) — the lower and upper edge of the Marchenko-Pastur noise band: the range a pure-noise correlation matrix's eigenvalues are expected to occupy at this \(N/T\).
"noise band"Informal shorthand used throughout this lesson for \([\lambda_{\min}, \lambda_{\max}]\) — the region where an eigenvalue cannot be confidently distinguished from sampling noise.
Real-world trading scenario

Thirty FX pairs, one year of daily returns

A researcher builds a correlation matrix across \(N = 30\) FX pairs using \(T = 250\) days of daily returns — one calendar year, a perfectly ordinary lookback window. First, the shape ratio:

\[ q = \frac{N}{T} = \frac{30}{250} = 0.12 \]

Then the noise band, worked through exactly:

\[ \sqrt{q} = \sqrt{0.12} \approx 0.3464 \] \[ \lambda_{\min} = (1 - 0.3464)^2 = (0.6536)^2 \approx 0.4272 \qquad \lambda_{\max} = (1 + 0.3464)^2 = (1.3464)^2 \approx 1.8128 \]

So at this \(N/T\), any eigenvalue between roughly 0.43 and 1.81 is inside the noise band — indistinguishable from what 30 completely independent random series over 250 days would produce by chance alone. The researcher then looks at the real eigenvalues from the actual 30-pair basket: the largest, \(\lambda_1 = 8.2\), sits enormously far above 1.81 — an overwhelming, easy call: this is a real, genuine common factor (in practice, almost certainly the same dollar/risk-on factor the PCA lesson describes as PC1). But the next several eigenvalues — \(\lambda_2, \lambda_3, \lambda_4, \dots\) — all come back somewhere between 0.5 and 1.7, squarely inside the 0.43-1.81 noise band.

That places the researcher in front of a genuinely consequential modeling decision: treating that second or third principal component as a meaningful additional "factor" in a diversification or risk model — something to trade against, hedge separately, or report as "the euro-specific factor" or "the commodity-bloc factor" — would be fitting statistical noise, not signal. At \(N=30\) instruments and \(T=250\) days, this basket simply does not carry enough information to distinguish PC2 or PC3 from what pure randomness would have produced anyway. Only the first component clears the bar. That doesn't mean PC2 is definitely meaningless in reality — it means this sample, at this size, cannot tell the difference, and building on top of it as if it could would be overfitting to noise while believing it's structure.

How practitioners actually use this

The best-known application of this exact idea to real markets is Laloux, Cizeau, Bouchaud & Potters' 1999 study of the correlation matrix of several hundred U.S. stocks: they found that the overwhelming majority of the eigenvalues of a real stock-market correlation matrix fell inside the Marchenko-Pastur band computed from the actual \(N\) and \(T\) of their dataset — meaning most of what a naive PCA would report as "factor structure" in that basket was statistically indistinguishable from noise — while a small handful of the largest eigenvalues (led by one dominant "market factor," directly analogous to the FX dollar factor) sat far outside it and were judged to be genuinely non-random. That paper is generally credited with launching "RMT filtering" or "RMT denoising" as a standard risk-management technique, particularly for large-N problems like equity covariance estimation for portfolio optimization, where naively trusting every eigenvalue (and every eigenvector) of a sample covariance matrix is well known to produce badly overfit, unstable optimal portfolios — Markowitz optimization is notoriously sensitive to estimation error in exactly this way, and RMT gives a principled first cut at separating the estimation error from the real signal before optimizing on top of it.

In practice the technique is used in two related ways. The first, covered fully by this lesson, is a binary screen: decide how many of a basket's eigenvalues are worth treating as real factors at all, before doing anything more sophisticated with them (running a factor model, sizing a diversification metric, deciding how many independent bets a book really has). The second, more advanced use — eigenvalue cleaning or shrinkage — goes a step further: rather than a hard binary cut, it replaces every eigenvalue inside the noise band with a single common value (often their average), leaving the eigenvalues above \(\lambda_{\max}\) untouched, and reconstructs a "cleaned" correlation matrix from that adjusted spectrum. That cleaned matrix still has \(N\) eigenvalues and is still a valid correlation matrix, but it no longer pretends the noise-band eigenvalues carry information they can't actually support — a materially more honest input to a covariance-based optimizer than the raw sample matrix. This lesson focuses on the simpler binary version of the idea; eigenvalue cleaning is a real, well-established extension worth knowing exists, not something built or claimed here.

Where this could plug into MacroFXModel

A concrete, unbuilt guardrail for the existing effective-bets metric

As the PCA lesson's repo box lays out, js/diversificationCore.js already computes symmetricEigenvalues — the full eigenvalue spectrum of a correlation matrix, via a from-scratch Jacobi rotation — and feeds it into effectiveBetsPCA(corr), the \((\sum_i \lambda_i)^2 / \sum_i \lambda_i^2\) inverse-participation-ratio metric that diversification.html displays live as "effective number of bets." That metric already uses the entire eigenvalue spectrum this lesson is about — every \(\lambda_i\) it computes goes into the formula, with no distinction made between an eigenvalue that is an overwhelming, obvious common factor and one that is statistically indistinguishable from noise at the basket's actual \(N\) and \(T\).

To be explicit about what is and isn't already there: the Marchenko-Pastur noise-filtering step described in this lesson is not currently applied anywhere in this repo. It is a specific, well-defined, low-risk enhancement candidate, not a claim about existing behavior. Concretely, it would mean: given the eigenvalues symmetricEigenvalues already returns, and the \(N\) and \(T\) already known from the basket and the lookback window used to build the correlation matrix, compute \(q = N/T\) and the \((\lambda_{\min}, \lambda_{\max})\) band from the two-line formula in this lesson, and flag which of the returned eigenvalues fall inside it. That flag doesn't have to change the effective-bets number itself — it could ship first as a simple annotation on diversification.html ("2 of 5 eigenvalues distinguishable from noise at this window length") — a direct, honest caveat on a metric that currently has none, and a guardrail against a very specific overstatement: a basket whose effective-bets score looks reassuringly close to \(N\) only because several of its "distinct" eigenvalues are actually noise-band clutter that happens to sum correctly, not because the basket is genuinely that diversified.

This is a buildable next step, not a validated one. Any change to how effectiveBetsPCA itself treats noise-band eigenvalues (excluding them, shrinking them, reweighting the formula around only the "real" ones) would be a new piece of decision logic on top of an existing metric, and per Lego Principle 5 it would need its own honest before/after comparison — does flagging or filtering noise-band eigenvalues actually produce a more decision-useful diversification read in practice, on real baskets, not just a theoretically cleaner one — before being trusted, and it should be registered as a candidate in LEGO_MODULES.md §2 before it's built, not assumed to help because the math is elegant.

Common pitfalls

Where the noise-band rule is misapplied

Treating "inside the band" as proof of no structure. The Marchenko-Pastur test is a statistical power statement, not a certainty. An eigenvalue inside \([\lambda_{\min}, \lambda_{\max}]\) means your sample cannot currently distinguish that component from noise — it does not prove the underlying economic relationship is zero. A real but modest factor can easily hide inside the noise band at a small \(T\) or a large \(N\); more history (a bigger \(T\), which shrinks \(q\) and narrows the band) is the direct remedy, not a reinterpretation of the same sample.

Forgetting the theorem is an asymptotic (\(N, T \to \infty\)) result being applied to a finite basket. \(N=30\), \(T=250\) is nowhere near infinity. The Marchenko-Pastur band is the right and widely-used practical benchmark at finite sample sizes too — that's exactly what the Laloux et al. finance application relies on — but edge effects exist: real finite-\(N,T\) spectra show some spread right around \(\lambda_{\max}\) itself (formally, fluctuations of the largest "noise" eigenvalue follow a separate Tracy-Widom distribution, not a hard wall), so an eigenvalue sitting just barely above \(\lambda_{\max}\) is a much weaker signal than one sitting several multiples above it, the way \(\lambda_1 = 8.2\) does against \(\lambda_{\max} \approx 1.81\) in the worked scenario. Treat values right at the edge as ambiguous, not as a clean pass/fail.

Applying the raw i.i.d. assumption to real returns without caveat. The theorem assumes the underlying series are independent and identically distributed with fixed variance. Real asset returns have time-varying volatility (the exact subject of this repo's GARCH/HV lessons), fat tails, and autocorrelation — none of which the textbook Marchenko-Pastur derivation assumes. In practice this mostly affects how tightly real noise hugs the theoretical band (studies generally find it's still a good practical benchmark, per Laloux et al. and the large RMT-in-finance literature that followed), but it means the band should be read as "the right order of magnitude and a strong first cut," not a court-admissible p-value.

Using the wrong \(N\) or \(T\) in the formula. \(q = N/T\) only means what this lesson says if \(T\) is genuinely the number of independent observations used to build the correlation matrix. Overlapping windows (e.g. rolling weekly returns computed from daily data with a 1-day step) inflate the effective \(T\) you'd plug in relative to the true amount of independent information, understating \(q\) and making the noise band look narrower — and any real factor look more significant — than the data actually supports. Sample degrees of freedom, not raw row count, is the correct \(T\).

Stopping at the binary rule and calling it "denoised." This lesson deliberately covers only the in-band/out-of-band screen, the simplest and most teachable slice of RMT-in-finance. Reporting "2 of 30 eigenvalues are real" is a genuine, useful statement; it is not the same as having built a cleaned, shrinkage-adjusted correlation matrix ready to feed a portfolio optimizer — that is a separate, more involved technique (mentioned above, not built here) with its own literature and its own validation requirements.

Self-test

Q1. Why does a correlation matrix built from truly independent random series still produce a spread of nonzero, non-identical eigenvalues, instead of a flat spectrum at exactly 1?
Because a flat spectrum at exactly 1 is only what "no shared structure" looks like with infinite data. With any finite number of observations \(T\), the sample correlation between two truly independent series will not land exactly on the true (zero) correlation — it will drift above or below zero purely by chance, the same way a finite number of fair coin flips rarely lands exactly 50/50. Building a correlation matrix out of these coincidentally-nonzero sample correlations and computing its eigenvalues necessarily produces a spread away from 1, even though the underlying population has zero real correlation anywhere. That spread is finite-sample noise, not evidence of any real relationship.
Q2. What does a smaller q = N/T do to the width of the Marchenko-Pastur noise band, and why does more history relative to instruments narrow it?
A smaller \(q\) narrows the band: \(\lambda_{\min} = (1-\sqrt{q})^2\) and \(\lambda_{\max} = (1+\sqrt{q})^2\) both move toward 1 as \(\sqrt{q} \to 0\). This happens because \(q = N/T\) shrinks either by lowering \(N\) (fewer instruments to estimate simultaneous relationships for) or raising \(T\) (more independent observations to estimate each pairwise correlation from). More data per relationship means each sample correlation converges more tightly to its true value (here, zero, for genuinely independent series), so the finite-sample noise described in Q1 is smaller in magnitude, and the resulting eigenvalue spread tightens correspondingly. Less history relative to instruments does the opposite: each sample correlation is noisier, so a pure-noise matrix's eigenvalues spread further from 1 in both directions.
Q3. In the worked scenario (N=30, T=250), the second-largest eigenvalue falls inside the noise band. Does that prove there is no real second factor in the data?
No. It means this sample, at this \(N\) and \(T\), cannot statistically distinguish that eigenvalue's size from what a matrix built from pure, uncorrelated noise would produce at the same \(N/T\) ratio. There might be a real, but modest, second factor whose true effect is simply too small to clear the noise band at only 250 observations — a bigger \(T\) (more history) would narrow the band and could reveal it. What the result does support is a specific, narrower claim: treating that eigenvalue as a confirmed factor right now, on this sample, is not statistically justified — a materially different statement from "there is definitely nothing there."
Q4. Why is an eigenvalue sitting just barely above λ_max a weaker signal than one sitting several multiples above it, even though both are technically "outside the band"?
The Marchenko-Pastur bound is an asymptotic (infinite N, T) result; at finite sample sizes, the largest eigenvalue produced by pure noise alone doesn't stop precisely at \(\lambda_{\max}\) — it has its own fluctuation distribution around that edge (Tracy-Widom fluctuations, a standard refinement in the random-matrix literature). So a real eigenvalue only modestly above \(\lambda_{\max}\) could still plausibly be an unusually large draw from the noise distribution's own edge wobble, not a genuine factor. An eigenvalue several multiples above \(\lambda_{\max}\) — like 8.2 against a \(\lambda_{\max}\) of 1.81 in the worked scenario — is far outside any plausible noise fluctuation at that edge, which is why it reads as an unambiguous genuine factor while a value of, say, 2.0 against the same 1.81 bound would not.
Q5. Why is q = N/T, rather than N or T individually, the parameter that determines the noise band's width?
Because what determines how noisy a sample correlation matrix's eigenvalues are is not the absolute size of the basket or the absolute length of history, but how much information you have per relationship you're estimating. A basket of 100 instruments over 100,000 days of history and a basket of 3 instruments over 3,000 days can have the same \(q\) and therefore the same noise band width, even though their \(N\) and \(T\) individually look nothing alike — what matters is that both have comparably abundant data relative to the number of pairwise relationships being estimated. \(N\) alone tells you how many eigenvalues exist; \(T\) alone tells you how much data exists; only their ratio tells you how reliably each individual eigenvalue can be estimated.
Q6. js/diversificationCore.js already computes eigenvalues via symmetricEigenvalues and feeds them into an effective-bets metric. What specific step is missing to apply this lesson's noise-band test, and why would it matter?
What's missing is comparing each returned eigenvalue against a Marchenko-Pastur band computed from the basket's actual \(N\) (number of instruments) and \(T\) (number of observations in the lookback window used to build the correlation matrix) — a two-line calculation (\(q = N/T\), then \((1 \mp \sqrt{q})^2\)) that isn't currently run anywhere against the eigenvalues symmetricEigenvalues already produces. It matters because the effective-bets formula currently treats every eigenvalue as equally informative regardless of whether it's an obvious common factor or a value indistinguishable from pure sampling noise at that window length — so a basket could report a reassuring, close-to-\(N\) effective-bets score that is partly an artifact of noise-band eigenvalues summing up correctly, not evidence the basket is genuinely that diversified. Flagging which eigenvalues clear the noise band would add an honest caveat to that number without requiring the metric's formula itself to change.
