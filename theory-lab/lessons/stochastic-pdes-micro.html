Stochastic PDEs: When a Whole Curve Wiggles, Not Just a Point

Every SDE so far in this sequence has moved a single number, or a short finite list of numbers, randomly through time. The Fokker-Planck equation, right before this lesson, showed how the probability density of one of those numbers spreads out deterministically over time. This lesson takes the next step, and it's a genuine jump: what happens when the object evolving randomly stops being a finite list at all, and becomes an entire curve — the whole interest-rate forward curve, at every maturity simultaneously — or an entire probability density that's also being nudged by continuous noisy observations? That object is a stochastic PDE, and this lesson introduces it through two concrete, named examples: the HJM/Musiela forward-curve framework, which connects directly back to the yield-curve-construction and Nelson-Siegel-Svensson lessons earlier in this curriculum, and the Zakai equation from filtering theory.

Section 1

One number, a few numbers, then a whole curve

Everything in this sequence so far has lived in a small, finite space. This lesson is what happens when it stops being finite.

1

A single SDE — one number

An FX rate, a short rate r_t, a single stock price: one number, wiggling randomly through time. This is where the sequence started.

n

A vector SDE — a handful of numbers

Multivariate & correlated SDEs stacked several such numbers together — a basket of FX pairs, or a price-and-variance pair — into one finite vector, still a list you could count on your fingers if n were small enough.

∞

An SPDE — an entire curve or field

Not "the 1-year rate and the 10-year rate" as two numbers, but the rate at every maturity at once, as one continuous, infinite-dimensional object. That whole object is what an SPDE describes evolving randomly.

Picture the entire forward interest-rate curve as a long rubber band stretched from "today" out to "30 years from now." A short-rate SDE watches one single point on that band bounce around. An SPDE describes the entire band flexing and rippling along its whole length at once — with nearby points on the band moving alike, but not identically.

Same randomness, two entirely different kinds of object being moved by it.

This is the entire conceptual jump this lesson is built around: finite-dimensional (ℝⁿ) versus infinite-dimensional (a space of functions). Everything below — the noise, the solution formula, the two named examples — is what has to change once the object being moved is a curve instead of a list of numbers.
Section 3

The noise driving it is stranger than dW_t

The canonical, simplest SPDE — the stochastic heat equation — makes the new difficulty concrete.

The equation
∂u/∂t
=
½∂²u/∂x²
+
σ·Ẇ(t,x)
The first two pieces are the ordinary (non-random) heat equation — how heat spreads along a rod, no chance involved. The last piece, space-time white noise, is new: independent randomness injected at every point in space, at every instant in time.

Here is the single hardest conceptual jump in the whole subject. Ẇ(t,x) is not a function you can evaluate at a point — plugging in one specific (t,x) gives nothing meaningful; it is infinitely rough there. It is rigorously defined only through what it does once integrated against a smooth test shape — the same technical sense in which the Dirac delta is a distribution, not a function. An ordinary SDE's dW_t has no such problem: at any single time t it is a genuine, if rough, random value. That's precisely why an SPDE needs the more careful infinite-dimensional machinery (a Q-Wiener process, built from a countable family of ordinary 1-D Brownian motions, one per spatial frequency) that an ordinary SDE never has to reach for.

Where this needs qualifying

The full, rigorous construction of that machinery — exactly which function spaces it lives in, and the technical conditions that make it converge — is genuine graduate-level functional analysis (Da Prato & Zabczyk's Stochastic Equations in Infinite Dimensions is the standard reference). What matters here is the working picture: the noise is a distribution, not a function, and that single fact is why everything downstream needs more care.

Section 4

The same solution formula, one dimension up

SPDEs aren't solved from scratch — the practical solution concept is a direct echo of a formula already used earlier in this sequence.

Ordinary SDE — a number

For dX_t = aX_t dt + σ dW_t, the explicit solution is:

X_t = e^(at) X_0 + ∫₀ᵗ e^(a(t−s)) σ dW_s

A scalar exponential e^(at) carries the starting value forward; the integral adds up every past random push, each one grown forward by that same exponential.

SPDE — a whole field

For the analogous linear SPDE, the "mild solution" is:

u(t) = S(t) u(0) + ∫₀ᵗ S(t−s) dW(s)

A semigroup operator S(t) — the deterministic, noise-free evolution — replaces the scalar exponential; the integral again adds up every past random push, now propagated forward by S(t−s) instead.

Same shape, same idea — "multiply by a growth factor and add up the random pushes along the way" — done for an entire field instead of a single number. e^(at) becomes S(t); the ordinary Itô integral becomes a stochastic integral against a Q-Wiener process. Nothing about the logic changes, only the space it happens in.
Section 5

HJM/Musiela: one SPDE for the whole forward curve

The most finance-relevant named example — and the direct generalization of the short-rate models earlier in this sequence.

The equation
df(t,x)
=
[∂f/∂x + α(t,x)]dt
+
σ(t,x)dW(t)
f(t,x) is the forward rate, at calendar time t, for a loan starting x years from now — the entire curve, indexed by time-to-maturity x, evolving as one object.
↷

∂f/∂x — just bookkeeping

Present even with zero randomness — a consequence of re-indexing by time-to-maturity instead of calendar date, the same effect traders call "rolling down the curve."

⚖

α(t,x) — not a free choice

The no-arbitrage drift condition pins it down completely once the volatility σ(t,x) is chosen: α(t,x) = σ(t,x)·∫₀ˣσ(t,u)du. Specify the vol, and the drift follows — it isn't chosen independently.

•

Vasicek/CIR/Hull-White = x=0 only

Those single-number short-rate models track exactly one tenor point of this curve, x=0, and collapse everything else into one mean-reversion parameter.

The earlier yield-curve-construction and Nelson-Siegel-Svensson lessons in this curriculum fit a single static snapshot of f(t,·) at one moment — a photograph of the rubber band, not a model of how it moves. HJM/Musiela is what models the motion itself.

Section 9

The Zakai equation: filtering's own SPDE

A second, completely different named SPDE — this one from continuous-time filtering, not term-structure modelling.

The equation
dπ_t
=
L*π_t dt
+
π_t h(x_t) dy_t
π_t is the unnormalized conditional density of a hidden state x_t, given a noisy, continuously-observed process y_t — a whole probability curve, not one number, updating as each new observation arrives.
Kalman filter

The exact closed-form solution — when the hidden-state dynamics are linear and every noise term Gaussian, π_t stays Gaussian at every instant, so the whole infinite-dimensional density collapses to just tracking a mean and a covariance. That finite system of update equations is the Kalman filter.

Particle filter

A Monte Carlo approximation — outside the linear-Gaussian case, π_t has no closed form. The particle filter approximates it with a weighted swarm of simulated trajectories, converging to the true Zakai solution as the particle count grows.

Neither the Kalman filter nor the particle filter lesson earlier in this curriculum names the object both are — in different senses — solving. This is it: one linear SPDE governing how confidence about a hidden state evolves, given noisy continuous evidence.
Section 10

Genuinely advanced — and genuinely not what's running in this repo

Worth being explicit about where this machinery actually gets used, and where it doesn't.

🏦

Specialist curve-modelling desks

The HJM/Musiela framework, in something close to its full generality, is genuinely used where a book's exposure to curve shape — not just level — is large enough to justify the complexity.

📚

Academic filtering research

The Zakai equation is standard machinery in the nonlinear-filtering literature; the algorithms it motivates (Kalman, particle filters) are what actually reach production, not a direct SPDE solve.

🧭

A frontier worth recognizing, not a default tool

Knowing the SPDE framing is most useful for judging when a finite-dimensional simplification is adequate — and when its assumptions are being stretched.

Verified against this repo's actual code

Checked directly rather than assumed: nothing in this codebase implements HJM/Musiela, a Zakai equation, or any whole-curve stochastic model — a repo-wide search turns up no such code anywhere. This curriculum's own js/yieldCurveEngine.js models the yield curve only as a single 2s10s slope number plus a z-score trend versus its own history — a level-and-trend signal, not even a static multi-tenor snapshot, let alone a stochastic model of the whole curve moving through time. That's fully consistent with this lesson's framing below: full SPDE machinery is real, rigorous, and used elsewhere, but nowhere in this repo, and it isn't meant to be — this is background theory for reading specialist material and the next lesson's neural-SPDE extension, not a build spec.

Section 11

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

What is the core structural difference between an SDE and an SPDE?

An SDE lives in a finite-dimensional space ℝⁿ. An SPDE's unknown, u(t,x), lives in an infinite-dimensional space — a space of functions — because it's a whole curve or field at every instant, not a fixed-length list.
Question 2

Discretizing the HJM/Musiela SPDE and keeping only the tenor point x=0 collapses it into what?

Tracking only the short rate r_t = f(t,0) and folding everything else about the curve into one mean-reversion term is exactly what a short-rate model does — the finite-dimensional special case of the full curve SPDE.
Question 3

Once a volatility structure σ(t,x) is specified for the forward curve, is the drift α(t,x) a free modelling choice?

The HJM drift condition pins α down completely as a function of the already-chosen σ. Picking σ and then an arbitrary α produces an arbitrage, not a valid model.
Question 4

What does the Zakai equation describe the evolution of?

π_t in the Zakai equation is a whole density — a field-valued object — updating continuously as new noisy observations y_t arrive, in exactly the filtering setting the Kalman and particle filter lessons build algorithms for.
Question 5

How does the Kalman filter relate to the Zakai equation?

When dynamics are linear and noise Gaussian, the Zakai solution stays Gaussian, collapsing to a mean and covariance — the Kalman filter's update equations. The particle filter, by contrast, is the Monte Carlo approximation used outside that special case.
Section 12

What to carry forward

01

An SDE moves a finite vector of numbers randomly through time; an SPDE moves an entire curve or field — the direct infinite-dimensional generalization of everything else in this sequence.

02

Space-time white noise, the canonical SPDE's driving randomness, is a distribution, not a pointwise function — it's rigorously defined only through integration against a test shape.

03

The mild solution u(t) = S(t)u(0) + ∫S(t−s)dW(s) is the same "growth factor plus accumulated random pushes" idea as an ordinary linear SDE's closed form — a semigroup replacing a scalar exponential.

04

HJM/Musiela writes the whole forward-rate curve's random motion as one SPDE; the no-arbitrage drift condition forces the drift the moment volatility is chosen — it's never a second, independent choice.

05

Vasicek, CIR, and Hull-White are the x=0-only special case of that same curve SPDE — a single short-rate SDE is what's left once every other tenor is discarded.

06

The Zakai equation is the SPDE an unnormalized filtering density evolves by — the Kalman filter solves it exactly in the linear-Gaussian case, a particle filter approximates it by Monte Carlo otherwise.

What this now lets you do

Recognize when a finite-dimensional model in this curriculum — a Hull-White short rate, a Kalman filter — is a deliberate simplification of a richer curve- or field-valued object, and know exactly what it's a simplification of. Name the two SPDEs that connect that richer object to concrete finance and filtering problems: HJM/Musiela for the whole forward curve, Zakai for continuous-time state estimation. That's enough grounding to read specialist term-structure or filtering material, or the next lesson's neural-network extension of this same machinery, without starting from zero.
