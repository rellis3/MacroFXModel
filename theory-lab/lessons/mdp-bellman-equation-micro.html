Reinforcement Learning 1 · Reinforcement Learning

Value Is Just Reward Now, Plus a Discounted Guess at Later

Every strategy this curriculum has covered so far applies a static rule regardless of what happens next. Reinforcement learning is built for the opposite case: problems where today's action changes tomorrow's situation, so the "right" move can only be judged by its effect on the whole future path. The Markov Decision Process is the formal object underneath every RL method, and the Bellman equation — value now equals immediate reward plus the discounted value of what comes next — is the one recursive trick that makes it solvable at all.

Section 1

The five pieces of a Markov Decision Process

Formally, an MDP is the tuple (S, A, P, R, γ). Nothing more is added later — everything in this lesson is built from just these five things.

S

States

Every distinct situation the process can be in — flat, long, or short a position, say.

A

Actions

The choices available in a given state — hold, close, open — possibly a different set per state.

P

Transition probabilities — P(s′|s,a)

The chance of landing in state s′, given action a was taken in state s. Not always certain — opening a position doesn't guarantee which way the market moves next.

R

Rewards — R(s,a,s′)

The immediate payoff or cost of one specific transition.

γ

Discount factor

A number between 0 and 1 saying how much a reward one step in the future is worth today — the subject of Section 4.

A rule for choosing actions — possibly randomized — is called a policy, written π(a|s): the probability of taking action a while in state s. Everything downstream in RL is either evaluating a policy (how good is this way of acting?) or improving one (is there a better way to act?).

Section 3

"Markov" means the past stops mattering

The simplifying assumption that makes an MDP tractable at all.

Full history
  • What happens next depends on everything that's happened so far
  • Momentum, regime memory, and path dependence all count
  • Realistic for real markets — and computationally hopeless to model exactly
Markov state
  • What happens next depends only on the current state and action
  • The state is assumed to already summarize everything relevant about the past
  • Deliberately idealized — but it's what turns an intractable problem into a solvable one
This is the same move as assuming you know your edge exactly in the Kelly derivation, or ignoring fat tails in a normal-distribution primer: build the exact math on a clean idealization first, then reason carefully about where the real world will diverge from it. Real price processes carry momentum and regime memory that a small state (just "current position," say) doesn't capture — the usual fix is enlarging the state to include recent history, at the cost of a much bigger state space.
Section 4

The discount factor sets how patient the agent is

Drag the slider. Each bar is γ raised to the power of how many steps in the future it is — the weight that reward gets in today's value.

Try it
Discount factorγ = 0.90
Effective horizon (≈ 1/(1−γ), roughly how many future periods still carry meaningful weight): ≈ 10.0 periods

A low γ makes an agent myopic — only the next reward or two really counts, and the bars collapse to almost nothing after a few steps. A high γ makes it far-sighted — rewards many steps away still carry real weight today. γ is doing two jobs at once: economically, a reward tomorrow is worth less than the same reward today (the same logic behind discounting a bond's future coupons); mathematically, it's what keeps an infinite sum of rewards from adding up to infinity.

Section 5

The value function compresses a whole future into one number

Fix a policy π. Vπ(s) is the expected sum of every future reward, each shrunk by how far away it is.

V

Vπ(s)

Expected total discounted future reward, starting in state s and following policy π forever after.

γᵗ

γᵗ rt

The reward received t steps from now, shrunk by γ raised to the t-th power — far-future rewards count for less.

π

s₀=s, π

Conditioned on: the starting state is s, and every action along the way is chosen by policy π.

This single number compresses an entire infinite future into one comparable quantity — exactly the same compression a bond's price performs on a stream of future coupons, or that the Kelly criterion's log-growth objective performs on an entire sequence of compounding bets. Reduce a whole future to one number you can actually maximize, and comparing two situations becomes comparing two numbers.
Section 6

Where the Bellman equation actually comes from

It isn't a new assumption bolted onto the value function — it falls straight out of splitting an infinite sum. Step through it.

Try it
V(s) = r₀ + γr₁ + γ²r₂ + ⋯
An infinite sum, conditioned on starting at s. Looks hopeless to compute directly — you'd need to sum forever.
V(s) = r₀ + γ(r₁ + γr₂ + ⋯)
Just algebra — factor γ out of every term after the first. Nothing new assumed yet, just re-grouped.
V(s) = r + γ · V(s′)
The part in parentheses is V again — the exact same infinite-sum shape, just starting one step later at whatever state s′ comes next. That's the Bellman equation: value now equals immediate reward plus the discounted value of what comes next.
This is what turns an infinite-horizon problem into a system of equations relating each state only to its immediate neighbors — solvable by substitution, by iteration, or by a learning rule that only ever looks one step ahead.

Section 9

Grading a state versus grading one specific move

Everything so far evaluated a fixed policy. Optimality asks a sharper question: what's the best possible value, over every policy?

V*(s) — grade the state

V*(s) = maxa Σs′ P(s′|s,a)[R(s,a,s′) + γV*(s′)]. Averaging over the policy's actions becomes maximizing over actions — the best policy, by definition, always takes whichever action leads to the highest expected value.

Q*(s,a) — grade the action

Q*(s,a) = Σs′ P(s′|s,a)[R(s,a,s') + γ·maxa′Q*(s′,a′)]. The value of taking action a right now, then playing optimally forever after. Note V*(s) = maxa Q*(s,a) — a state's best value is just the value of its best action.

Knowing the score for every individual action is more directly useful than knowing only a state's overall score, because it tells you exactly which move to make — not just how happy to be. This particular rewriting, Q* in terms of itself one step later, is the exact identity the next lesson's Q-learning update rule is built from directly.
Section 10

Where the MDP framing quietly goes wrong

⚠

Assuming a market is actually Markov

Real price processes carry momentum, volatility clustering, and regime memory that a naively small state doesn't capture — the state has to be enlarged to compensate, at the cost of a much bigger state space.

⚠

Treating Vπ as achievable, not just expected

The value function is an expectation over an entire distribution of future paths. One realized trajectory can and will look nothing like the average — the same trap the Kelly lesson raises about expected value versus long-run compounded growth.

⚠

Picking γ without thinking about it

γ isn't a free technical knob — it encodes how much future reward genuinely matters, and the optimal policy can change qualitatively depending on it, as Section 4's slider showed even in this 3-state toy.

⚠

The model has to actually be known

Value iteration needs P(s′|s,a) and R(s,a,s′) for every state-action pair up front. Real environments — a market, a robot, a game — rarely hand you that table explicitly.

That last gap is exactly the reason the field moved beyond this chapter: learning values from sampled experience, without ever needing an explicit P or R, is the entire subject of the next lesson.

Full lesson's claim, and today's reality

What the full lesson says: "CONCEPT — explained here, not built or tested anywhere in this repo." Still true, for what this lesson covers: nothing in the trading bot solves an explicit discrete state/action/reward table by value iteration or Q-learning — there is no MDP-shaped decision engine here. One close mathematical cousin does exist, though: Beta Idea/BETA_STOCHASTIC_CONTROL_README.md documents a live "beta stochastic control" system built on Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman optimal control — the continuous-time relative of the exact "value now = reward now + discounted value of what's next" identity from this lesson — used to size an inaction band around target factor exposures. By its own documented design it is monitoring and alerting only: it does not place trades, size positions, or veto entry signals, so "not used for decision-making here" also still holds.

Worth carrying forward: the same recursive idea shows up under different names — discrete Bellman equation here, continuous HJB equation there — well before anything in this repo calls itself "reinforcement learning."

Section 12

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

What does the Markov property assume?

"Markov" is exactly this simplifying assumption — the current state is assumed to already capture everything relevant about the past.
Question 2

In V(s) = r + γV(s′), what job does γ do?

γ (between 0 and 1) discounts the future — economically, a reward later is worth less than now; mathematically, it stops an infinite sum from diverging.
Question 3

In the 3-state MDP at γ=0.9, why does the optimal policy Hold at Long but Close at Short?

Same discounting math, opposite-signed rewards: +1 repeated forever compounds to a much bigger number under γ=0.9 than −0.5 repeated forever does — the sign and size of the per-period reward decides the policy, not any special-casing of action names.
Question 4

What is the one change that turns the policy-evaluating Bellman equation into the Bellman optimality equation?

Averaging describes how good a fixed policy is; maximizing describes the best value achievable over every possible policy, since the optimal policy always picks the highest-value action.
Question 5

r = 2, γ = 0.5, V(s′) = 4. What is V(s) = r + γV(s′)?

2 + 0.5 × 4 = 2 + 2 = 4.
Section 13

What to carry forward

01

An MDP is the tuple (S, A, P, R, γ) — states, actions, transition probabilities, rewards, and a discount factor. It's the standard formalism for any sequential decision problem.

02

The Markov property assumes the current state already captures everything relevant about the past — a deliberate simplification, not a claim about how real markets actually behave.

03

Vπ(s) compresses an entire infinite, discounted future into one comparable number, under a fixed way of acting.

04

The Bellman equation — value now equals reward now plus γ times the value of what's next — is a recursive identity, not a new assumption. It falls straight out of splitting an infinite sum.

05

The Bellman optimality equation swaps "average over the policy's actions" for "take the best one," turning policy evaluation into policy search.

06

Value iteration converges to a fixed point that also encodes the optimal policy — often the greedy policy stabilizes long before the numbers themselves stop moving.

What this now lets you do

Read any reinforcement-learning method — Q-learning, policy gradients, deep Q-networks — as a different way of approximating this one recursive identity, rather than an unrelated algorithm to memorize. Make "how patient is this decision rule" an explicit, tunable number (γ) instead of a vague feeling. Recognize the same value-now-equals-reward-plus-discounted-later idea when it shows up under a different name, like the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman control already running in this repo's beta-monitoring system.
