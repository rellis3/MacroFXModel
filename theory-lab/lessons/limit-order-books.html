Limit Order Books & Queue Dynamics

Every quoted price hides a whole ladder of resting orders underneath it — how that ladder is organized, what happens when a market order tears through it, and why the order sitting first in line at a price gets treated completely differently from the order sitting last.

CONCEPT — not built or tested in this repo ~15 min read Prereqs: Market Microstructure
TL;DR
  • A limit order book is two ladders of resting orders — bids below the market, asks above it — each level holding a queue of orders at one exact price, ranked strictly by arrival time within that price (price-time priority, first-in-first-out).
  • The best bid and best ask define the spread; the total size resting at and near those prices is the book's depth — together they measure how much size can trade before the price has to move.
  • A limit order rests and waits, providing liquidity at a chosen price; a market order takes immediately, consuming whatever size is resting, walking through successively worse price levels if one level alone isn't enough to fill it.
  • Queue position is not a detail — it is often the entire economics of a passive order. Two identical resting orders at the identical price can have completely different fill probabilities purely because of how many shares/contracts are queued ahead of one versus the other.
  • Worked example: a 600-unit market buy order against a small three-level ask ladder walks through all of level 1, all of level 2, and part of level 3, landing at a volume-weighted average price about 0.8 pips worse than the best quoted ask — a concrete illustration of "walking the book."
Section 01

Why this matters

The Market Microstructure lesson derived why a bid-ask spread exists (Glosten-Milgrom: compensation for adverse selection) and why a bigger order costs more to execute (Kyle's Lambda: price impact scales with size and inversely with liquidity depth). Both of those results treat the market as a single number — one spread, one impact coefficient. Neither one describes the actual mechanical object those numbers are being read off of: a real limit order book, which is not one price but a whole ladder of prices, each with its own queue of waiting orders.

This lesson opens up that object. Once you can see the ladder itself — price levels, sizes at each level, and the strict first-in-first-out rule that governs who gets filled first at a given price — Kyle's Lambda stops being an abstract coefficient and becomes something you can compute directly by walking down the book level by level, exactly as the worked example below does. It is also the necessary foundation for the next lesson in this sequence, Market Impact Models & Almgren-Chriss, which is entirely about optimizing how to execute a large order against a book like this one over time.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Think of it as a deli counter with a numbered ticket at every price

Imagine a deli counter that sells one item, but at several different prices simultaneously, each price having its own separate line of customers. At $5.00 there's a line of three people; at $5.05 there's a line of seven; at $5.10 a line of two. A new customer joining the $5.05 line doesn't cut in — they take a ticket and go to the back of that specific line. If you want to be served next at $5.05, you need everyone ahead of you in that specific line to either get served or walk away first. That's queue position, and it's exactly how a real exchange's matching engine treats resting limit orders at the same price: strict, first-come-first-served, no cutting.

Now imagine a customer who doesn't want to wait in any line at all — they walk up and say "I'll pay whatever it costs, serve me now." The counter fills them from the cheapest available line first ($5.00), and if that line runs out of stock before the order is filled, moves on to the next cheapest line ($5.05), and so on, until the whole order is filled. That impatient customer is a market order: it "walks the book," and the price it actually pays, averaged across everyone it bought from, is worse than the very first price it touched, precisely because it kept having to reach for progressively less favorable lines to get fully served. Everyone who was willing to simply wait in a line instead — the resting limit orders — is called liquidity; the impatient order that eats through those lines is said to consume it.

Section 02

The math, step by step

1. The structure of the book: two ladders, many levels, one queue per level

A limit order book (LOB) is organized around two sides. The bid side holds every resting order to buy, sorted from the highest price (the best bid, closest to the market) down to progressively lower, less attractive prices. The ask side (also called the offer side) holds every resting order to sell, sorted from the lowest price (the best ask) up to progressively higher prices. Each distinct price on either side is called a level, and a level's size is the total quantity resting there, summed across every order queued at that exact price.

Critically, a level is not one order — it is a queue of potentially many separate orders from many separate participants, all willing to trade at that exact price, waiting their turn. How that queue is ordered internally is the subject of the next section.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Picture a parking garage with several floors, each floor a different price to park. The cheapest floor for buyers fills up first, then the next-cheapest, and so on. A "level" isn't one car, it's a whole floor's worth of cars all willing to park at that exact price, lined up in the order they arrived.

A tight spread with deep resting size on both sides is the signature of a liquid market — small orders barely move the price at all. A wide spread with thin size is the signature of an illiquid one — precisely the low-\(\sigma_u\), high-\(\lambda\) regime from the Kyle's Lambda derivation in the prior lesson. Depth and the spread are two different numbers measuring the same underlying thing — how expensive it is, right now, to trade a given amount immediately — and a market maker's decision about how far to space each level, and how much size to post at each one, is exactly the practical implementation of the adverse-selection and price-impact logic from that lesson.

2. Price-time priority: the matching rule almost every modern exchange uses

When a market order (or an aggressively priced limit order) arrives and needs to be matched against resting liquidity, the exchange's matching engine has to decide which specific resting orders get filled first. Two rules apply, in strict order:

1

Price priority

Better prices are always filled before worse ones. An incoming market buy always takes the lowest resting ask price first, in full, before it is ever allowed to touch the next-lowest ask price — no incoming order can "skip" a better-priced resting order to reach a worse-priced one.

2

Time priority, within a price

Among every order resting at the exact same price, the one that arrived first is filled first — strictly first-in-first-out (FIFO). An order placed a millisecond earlier at the same price is filled completely ahead of one placed a millisecond later, regardless of size, account type, or anything else about either order.

Together these two rules are called price-time priority (also "FIFO matching"), and it is the default matching algorithm at the large majority of modern electronic exchanges and ECNs across equities, futures, and FX. A very small number of venues use variants (e.g. pro-rata matching, common in some futures markets, which allocates fills proportionally to order size at a price level rather than strictly by arrival time), but price-time priority is the rule to know first, because it is both the most common and the one every other variant is defined as a departure from.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

It's exactly like a checkout line at a store: the register only serves whoever's cheapest overall first (price priority), and among people willing to pay that same price, whoever got in line first gets served first (time priority) — no cutting, no matter how big your cart is.

The consequence that matters in practice: under strict FIFO, when you joined a price level is as economically important as what price you joined at. An order resting at the very back of a long queue at the best price can, in a slow market, wait through the entire rest of the trading session without a single fill, while a newer, smaller order at a slightly worse price two ticks away gets filled first simply because its own queue happened to clear faster. Price alone does not tell you your fill probability — position in the queue does too, and often dominates.

3. Market orders vs. limit orders: taking liquidity vs. providing it

Limit order — provides liquidity

Specifies a price (and side) and rests in the book until it either gets matched against an incoming order, is cancelled, or expires. The trader who posts it accepts uncertainty about whether and when it fills, in exchange for a better price than crossing the spread would give them, and in exchange for not moving the price against themselves the way a market order does.

Market order — takes liquidity

Specifies only a size (and side), no price, and demands to trade immediately against whatever is currently resting, at whatever price that requires. It accepts price uncertainty (how far will I have to walk down the book?) in exchange for certainty of immediate execution.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

A limit order is like posting "I'll sell my bike for exactly $200" and waiting for someone to agree — you might wait a while, but you get your price. A market order is like walking into a bike shop and saying "give me a bike right now, whatever it costs" — you get it instantly, but you don't control what you pay.

A market order large enough to exceed the size resting at the best price does not fail or wait — it "walks the book": it fills against the entire best level, then the entire next level, and so on, at successively worse prices, until the full requested size is filled or the book is exhausted. The volume-weighted average price (VWAP) actually paid across all of those levels is the real execution price, and it is always at least as bad as the best price the order started at — worked through with real numbers below.

4. Queue position and fill probability

For a resting limit order, the single most useful number to reason about is not just "is my price close to the market" but "how much size is ahead of me in my own queue." Call that ahead-of-you size \(A\) (measured in the same units as order size), and the pace at which orders at that level get matched or cancelled the level's attrition rate. Your order cannot receive a single unit of fill until \(A\) units directly ahead of you have first been matched away or cancelled — your own size only starts filling once \(A\) reaches zero.

\[ A_{\text{new}} = A_{\text{old}} - (\text{matched against incoming orders}) - (\text{cancelled by the orders ahead of you}) \]
Atotal size resting ahead of your order, at your exact price
matchedshrinks only when an incoming order actually trades against that queue
cancelledalso shrinks it — orders ahead of you leaving voluntarily helps you too

A small numeric illustration: suppose your order joins the back of the best-bid queue with 800 units already resting ahead of you (\(A=800\)). A market sell order for 300 units arrives and matches against that level. Because of strict time priority, every one of those 300 units is taken from the front of the queue — from the orders that arrived before yours — so your ahead-of-you size drops from 800 to 500. You have moved 300 units closer to being filled, but you are still 500 units away from receiving a single fill yourself, and nothing about your own order changed at all. This is why queue position is sometimes described as "waiting for other people's trades to happen to you" — your fill depends entirely on order flow arriving that is unrelated to, and ahead of, your own order.

This is precisely why queue position matters enormously for passive (liquidity-providing) strategies specifically: a market-making or resting-limit strategy's realized fill rate — and therefore its realized profitability — can differ substantially between two orders posted at the identical price, purely as a function of when each order joined the queue and how much size has since queued up ahead of it. A strategy that only ever measures "did the market trade through my price" without also tracking queue position will systematically overestimate how often it would actually have been filled.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Your fill chances depend on how many people are ahead of you in line, not just whether you're in the right line at all. If 800 people are ahead of you at the return counter and only 300 get helped before the store closes, you're not getting helped today — even though you're standing in exactly the right spot. Nothing about your own order matters here; it's entirely about who's in front of you.

Worked example

Walking the book: the VWAP execution price of a 600-unit market buy order

Using the ask-side snapshot from the table above, a trader submits a market buy order for 600 units. Level 1 only has 200 units — nowhere near enough — so the order has to walk through multiple levels. Work through it level by level.

1

Level 1 — 1.1005, size 200

The order takes the entire 200 units resting here — it is not remotely filled yet, so it must continue to the next level.

\[ \text{filled so far} = 200,\quad \text{remaining} = 600-200 = 400 \]
2

Level 2 — 1.1006, size 300

The order takes the entire 300 units resting here too — still not fully filled.

\[ \text{filled so far} = 200+300=500,\quad \text{remaining} = 600-500 = 100 \]
3

Level 3 — 1.1007, size 500

Only 100 units are needed to complete the order, so it takes just 100 of the 500 resting here and stops — the remaining 400 units at this level are untouched, still resting in the book for the next order.

\[ \text{filled so far} = 500+100=600 = \text{done} \]
4

Volume-weighted average execution price

Weight each level's price by how many units were actually filled there, divide by the total size:

\[ \text{VWAP} = \frac{200(1.1005) + 300(1.1006) + 100(1.1007)}{600} = \frac{220.10+330.18+110.07}{600} = \frac{660.35}{600} \approx 1.10058 \]
200(1.1005)units filled at level 1, times that level's price
300(1.1006)units filled at level 2, times that level's price
100(1.1007)units filled at level 3, times that level's price
÷ 600total units filled — turns the weighted sum back into an average price

The order's real average execution price, 1.10058, is about 0.8 pips worse than the 1.1005 best ask it started at (\((1.10058-1.10050)/0.0001 \approx 0.83\) pips) — the exact, quantifiable cost of walking the book, computed directly from the ladder rather than inferred from an abstract impact coefficient. This is the mechanical, level-by-level reality that Kyle's Lambda, \(\Delta p = \lambda Q\), is a smooth, tractable approximation of.

Real-world trading scenario

Two market makers, same price, very different economics

A market-making desk posts a resting limit buy order at the current best bid the moment the book opens for the session, when the queue at that price is empty — their order becomes the very first in line. Thirty seconds later, a second desk posts an identically-sized limit buy order at the exact same price. By now, a wave of other resting orders has already queued up ahead of the second desk's order at that price — call it 2,000 units of ahead-of-you size.

Over the next few minutes, only 1,500 units of market sell orders arrive and trade against that price level. Because of strict price-time priority, every one of those 1,500 units is matched against the queue from front to back: the first desk's order — first in line — is filled completely and immediately captures the spread it was quoting for. The second desk's order has moved from 2,000 units behind to 500 units behind, and has not received a single unit of fill. Both desks quoted the identical price, took the identical directional risk, and posted the identical size — but one of them made money on the trade and the other is still waiting, purely as a function of thirty seconds of queue position. This is not a hypothetical edge case; it is the everyday reality of running a passive, liquidity-providing strategy on any exchange that uses price-time priority, and it is exactly why sophisticated market-making operations invest heavily in being first to a new price level rather than only in picking a good price.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

Understanding the book as a live, level-by-level structure — rather than as a single quoted price — changes how a desk actually routes and manages orders:

📊

Reading depth before sizing an order

A desk checks how much size is actually resting at and near the touch before sending a market order, precisely to estimate in advance how many levels it will have to walk and what the resulting VWAP execution price is likely to be — the same arithmetic as the worked example above, run live against the current book.

🧭

Choosing limit vs. market deliberately

A trader who can tolerate waiting posts a limit order to avoid paying the spread and walking the book; a trader who needs certainty of execution accepts the worse expected price of a market order. Most professional execution algorithms are, at their core, a policy for deciding this trade-off order by order rather than committing to one type for an entire parent order.

⏱️

Racing to be first in queue

Because queue position at a good price can matter more than the price itself, market-making and passive-execution operations invest heavily in being among the first to join a new or improved price level, and in estimating their own current queue position in real time to judge how likely a resting order is to fill soon versus needing to be cancelled and replaced.

🧩

Splitting an order across levels deliberately

Rather than sending one market order that mechanically walks the book, execution logic can choose to rest part of a large order as limit orders at or near the touch, only crossing the spread with a market order for the urgent remainder — trading off certainty of execution against the added cost of walking multiple levels, one level at a time.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where this goes wrong in practice

Assuming the quoted best price is the price you'll actually pay. The best bid/ask only tells you the price for the first unit of size. Any order larger than what's resting at that single level will walk the book and pay a worse VWAP, exactly as in the worked example — quoting "the price" without reference to size is quietly assuming an order small enough to never touch a second level.

Treating displayed depth as static or fully reliable. A book snapshot is a photograph of one instant; resting orders are cancelled and replaced constantly (this is sometimes called "phantom" or "flickering" liquidity when it happens especially fast), and the size resting a moment before an order arrives is not a guarantee of the size still there when it's actually matched. Backtests or execution-cost estimates that treat a single stale book snapshot as available liquidity for the whole trade will systematically overstate how much size was really there to trade against.

Ignoring queue position when estimating a passive strategy's fill rate. A common and costly modeling mistake is assuming any resting limit order fills whenever the market subsequently trades through its price, with no accounting for how much size was queued ahead of it first. As the scenario box above shows, two orders at the identical price can have wildly different real fill outcomes — a backtest that ignores queue position will overstate a passive strategy's realized fill rate, sometimes dramatically.

Confusing price-time priority with the only possible matching rule. It is the most common rule, but not universal — some futures markets use pro-rata matching (allocating fills proportional to order size at a level rather than strictly by arrival time), which changes the queue-position logic above substantially. Always confirm which matching rule a specific venue actually uses before assuming FIFO applies.

Section 04

Key Takeaways

✓The book is two ladders of price levels, each holding a queue. Bids sorted highest-to-lowest, asks sorted lowest-to-highest, and every level's size is the sum of possibly many separate resting orders.
✓Price-time priority governs who fills first. Better prices always fill before worse ones; at the identical price, strictly first-arrived, first-filled (FIFO) — the dominant matching rule across modern electronic exchanges.
✓Market orders walk the book; limit orders provide liquidity and wait. A market order large enough to exceed the best level's size pays a volume-weighted average price across every level it had to consume — always at least as bad as the first price it touched.
✓Queue position can dominate price for a passive order's real economics. Two identical resting orders at the identical price can have very different fill probabilities purely due to how much size queued up ahead of each one.
⚠Depth and the spread are two different, complementary liquidity measures. A tight spread with thin depth can still be expensive to trade in any real size — always check both, not just the quoted spread.
⚠A book snapshot is a photograph, not a guarantee. Resting size can be cancelled or replaced before an order actually reaches it — treating a stale snapshot as available liquidity overstates what a trade will really get filled against.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. What does "price-time priority" actually guarantee, in order?
Two rules, applied strictly in this order. First, price priority: a better price always fills before a worse one — an incoming market buy must fully consume the lowest resting ask price before it is ever allowed to trade against the next-lowest ask price. Second, time priority within an identical price: among all the resting orders queued at the exact same price, the one that arrived earliest is filled first, strictly first-in-first-out, regardless of size, account, or anything else about the competing orders. It does not guarantee any particular order will fill at all — only the sequence in which resting orders get matched if and when trading reaches their price and their position in the queue.
Q2. Using this lesson's ask-side snapshot (200 @ 1.1005, 300 @ 1.1006, 500 @ 1.1007), what VWAP would a market buy order for only 450 units pay, and why is that different from the 600-unit example worked through above?
450 units: take all 200 at level 1, all 300 at level 2 — wait, that's already 500, more than needed. Correctly: take all 200 at level 1 (remaining 250), then 250 of the 300 at level 2 (remaining 0) — fully filled without ever touching level 3. VWAP = [200(1.1005) + 250(1.1006)] / 450 = [220.10 + 275.15] / 450 = 495.25 / 450 ≈ 1.10056. This is closer to the best ask than the 600-unit example's 1.10058, because a smaller order that stops within level 2 never pays level 3's worse price at all — illustrating directly that execution cost rises with order size, exactly the qualitative claim Kyle's Lambda makes, now visible as simple arithmetic over the actual ladder rather than an abstract coefficient.
Q3. Two limit buy orders rest at the identical best bid price. Order A joined when the queue was empty; Order B joined later behind 2,000 units of size already queued. What single number best explains why they can have very different fill outcomes, and what does that number depend on?
The ahead-of-you size, \(A\) — how much size is queued in front of your own order at that exact price. Order A has \(A=0\) and fills immediately against the very next matching sell order; Order B has \(A=2{,}000\) and cannot receive a single fill until that entire 2,000 units has first been matched away by incoming sell orders or cancelled by the traders who placed it. That number depends purely on arrival time relative to other orders at the same price and on how quickly the orders ahead get matched or cancelled (the level's attrition rate) — not on anything about Order B's own price, size, or quality as an order.
Q4. Why does a market order's true execution price always look at least as bad as the best quoted price it started at, and when does that gap become large?
Because price priority forces the order to consume the best-priced level completely before it is allowed to trade at any worse price — the very first units always fill at the best available price, and every unit after that, if any is needed, can only fill at that price or worse (never better). The volume-weighted average across all levels touched is therefore a mix of the best price and some number of worse prices, which can only pull the average away from (never toward) the best price. The gap becomes large exactly when the order size is large relative to the size resting at the first few levels — a small order relative to depth barely walks the book at all (see Q2's 450-unit example), while a large order relative to depth walks through many levels and its VWAP diverges substantially from the best quoted price, as in the 600-unit worked example.
Q5. Why might a matching engine that uses pro-rata priority instead of strict price-time priority change how a trader should think about queue position?
Under strict FIFO, being first at a price is what matters — a huge order placed later at the same price still waits behind a tiny order placed earlier, in full. Under pro-rata matching, incoming size is instead allocated across all resting orders at a price in proportion to their size, regardless of arrival order — a large resting order gets a proportionally large slice of any incoming fill immediately, without needing to "wait its turn" the way FIFO requires. This flips the practical strategy: FIFO rewards being early to a price; pro-rata rewards being large at a price. A trader who assumes FIFO-style queue mechanics on a venue that actually uses pro-rata matching will misjudge their own expected fill rate.
