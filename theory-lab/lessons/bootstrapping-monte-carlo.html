Bootstrapping & Monte Carlo Simulation

Two ways to manufacture "many possible outcomes" out of a single dataset — and the one, easy-to-miss reason neither one tells you whether a signal will work on data it has never seen.

CONCEPT — formalizes a validation discipline used throughout this project ~15 min read Prereqs: Probability & Random Variables primer
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • The bootstrap resamples your one real dataset with replacement, thousands of times, to approximate the sampling distribution of a statistic (a Sharpe ratio, a mean, a max drawdown) with no assumption about its shape.
  • For time series (volatility clustering, serial correlation), a plain bootstrap shuffles that structure away and understates uncertainty — the block bootstrap resamples contiguous blocks instead, so short-term dependence survives in the resample.
  • Monte Carlo simulation is a different move: fit a parametric model (GARCH, GBM) to the data and generate brand-new synthetic paths from it, rather than resampling real observations.
  • Both tools only describe what's plausible given what you already have — the one sample, or a model fitted to that sample — never what would happen against market conditions that sample never contained.
  • As this project's own working philosophy states: "Don't let Monte Carlo stand in for OOS. Resampling from a strategy's own realized return distribution shows the range of outcomes consistent with that mean/std — it says nothing about whether the signal generalizes to data it hasn't seen. It's a legitimate expectation-setting tool; label it as that, not as robustness evidence."
Section 01

Why this matters

A backtest produces exactly one number for "the Sharpe ratio" and exactly one equity curve, computed from exactly one historical sample. That immediately raises an honest question: if history had unfolded even slightly differently — a few different trades, a slightly different ordering of the same market moves — how much would that Sharpe ratio have moved around? Bootstrapping and Monte Carlo simulation are the two standard tools for answering variants of that question without needing a second, independent dataset. They are genuinely useful, widely used in finance, and completely legitimate techniques. They are also, this project's own house discipline states plainly, not a substitute for testing whether a signal generalizes:

"Don't let Monte Carlo stand in for OOS. Resampling from a strategy's own realized return distribution shows the range of outcomes consistent with that mean/std — it says nothing about whether the signal generalizes to data it hasn't seen. It's a legitimate expectation-setting tool; label it as that, not as robustness evidence."

That sentence is asserted in this repo's house rules without derivation. This lesson is the from-scratch statistical explanation of exactly why it's correct — what the bootstrap and Monte Carlo simulation each mechanically do, what question each one actually answers, and why that question is a different question from "does this signal have real, generalizable edge." Status badge above is set to CONCEPT rather than ALREADY IN USE for a specific reason: this lesson formalizes a rule the repo already follows in prose (the quoted paragraph above), rather than describing a new brick or module.

💡
Plain-English intuition

One bag of marbles, reached into many times — versus a dice machine run many times

Imagine you have exactly one bag of marbles pulled from some larger, unknown population, and you want to know how much the average marble weight in "a bag like this" would bounce around if you'd happened to draw a different bag. You don't have access to more bags. The bootstrap's trick is to pretend your one bag is the population: reach into it, pull out a marble, note its weight, put it back, and repeat until you've "filled" a new bag the same size as the original. Compute the average weight of that new bag. Do this thousands of times and you get thousands of averages — a whole distribution of plausible averages, built entirely out of the one bag you actually have. That's the bootstrap, and the same idea applies word-for-word to a strategy's historical trades: reach into your one set of realized trades, with replacement, over and over, and see how much the Sharpe ratio (or the mean, or the max drawdown) wobbles across those reaches.

Monte Carlo simulation starts from a different place. Instead of reaching into a bag of real observations, you build a small machine — an assumed model, like a GARCH process or geometric Brownian motion, fit with parameters estimated from your data — and you run that machine thousands of times, each run generating a brand-new, entirely synthetic path. Neither approach requires new, real, unseen data. That's exactly their appeal, and exactly their limit: both describe what's plausible given what you already have — the one sample, or the one fitted model built from that sample — never what would happen against a market condition that sample never contained.

Section 02

The math, step by step

The setup. You observe one sample of \(n\) values, \(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n\) — daily returns, closed-trade P&Ls, whatever the unit of the strategy is — drawn from some true, unknown distribution \(F\). You compute a statistic of interest, \(T\) — the sample mean, a Sharpe ratio, a max drawdown, a hit rate — and you get one number, \(T(x_1,\dots,x_n)\). The question that matters for judging that number is: how much would \(T\) itself have varied if you'd drawn a different sample of size \(n\) from \(F\)? That's asking for the sampling distribution of \(T\) — and classically, answering it required either assuming \(F\) is some known shape (normal, and lean on the CLT from the Probability primer for an analytic standard error) or deriving a statistic-specific formula by hand. Neither option exists cleanly for something like a Sharpe ratio or a max drawdown computed on a short, lumpy, possibly non-normal trade sequence.

Efron's bootstrap (1979). Bradley Efron's insight was to stop trying to know \(F\) at all. Instead, treat the empirical distribution of the sample you already have — written \(\hat F_n\), which just means "put probability \(1/n\) on each of your \(n\) observed values" — as a stand-in for the true \(F\):

\[ \hat{F}_n(x) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbb{1}(x_i \le x) \]
F̂ₙempirical distribution — the bootstrap's stand-in for the true F
1/nprobability mass put on each observed value

A bootstrap resample is then built by drawing \(n\) new observations independently from \(\hat F_n\) — which, mechanically, just means drawing \(n\) values with replacement from your original \(n\) observations, each one equally likely to be picked every time:

\[ x_1^{*},\, x_2^{*},\, \dots,\, x_n^{*} \;\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}\; \hat{F}_n \]
xᵢ*one value in a resample — drawn with replacement from the originals

Because the draw is with replacement, a resample of size \(n\) will typically contain some original observations more than once and leave some out entirely — that's expected and is exactly what makes the resample a genuinely different sample from the original, rather than a reshuffled copy of it. Compute the same statistic \(T\) on this resample to get one bootstrap replicate, \(T^*\). Repeat the whole draw-and-compute step \(B\) times (in practice, \(B\) = 1,000 to 10,000 or more, since it's cheap to do on a computer):

\[ T^*_b = T\big(x_1^{*(b)}, \dots, x_n^{*(b)}\big), \qquad b = 1, \dots, B \]
T*ᵦone bootstrap replicate — T computed on the b-th resample
Bhow many resamples you draw, typically 1,000–10,000+
x*
Resample
n values drawn with replacement from the original data
T*ᵦ
Replicate
The statistic recomputed on that resample
SEᵦₒₒₜ
Bootstrap SE

The collection \(\{T^*_1, \dots, T^*_B\}\) is the bootstrap's answer: an approximation to the sampling distribution of \(T\), built entirely out of the one dataset you actually had, with no assumption about \(F\)'s shape. From that collection you get two of the most commonly reported outputs. The bootstrap standard error is simply the standard deviation of the replicates:

\[ SE_{boot} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{B-1}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\big(T^*_b - \bar{T}^{*}\big)^2}\,, \qquad \bar{T}^{*} = \frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B} T^*_b \]
T̄*the average replicate across all B resamples
SEᵦₒₒₜordinary standard deviation of the replicates

And a percentile confidence interval is read straight off the sorted replicates — for a 95% interval, take the value at the 2.5th percentile and the value at the 97.5th percentile of \(\{T^*_1,\dots,T^*_B\}\):

\[ \text{95\% bootstrap CI} \;=\; \big[\, T^{*}_{(0.025)},\; T^{*}_{(0.975)} \,\big] \]
T*₍₀.₀₂₅₎, T*₍₀.₉₇₅₎read straight off the sorted replicates — no formula needed

Notice what this does and does not require. It requires only the original \(n\) observations and a rule for computing \(T\) — it works identically whether \(T\) is a simple mean with a known analytic formula or a Sharpe ratio, a max-drawdown, or a Deflated Sharpe Ratio with no clean closed-form sampling distribution at all. That generality, not needing to assume a parametric shape for \(F\) or derive a statistic-specific formula, is the entire reason the bootstrap became one of the most widely used tools in applied statistics after 1979.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

You have one hand of cards. To get a feel for "how different could my hand have been," you shuffle those exact same cards back into the deck, redraw the same number of cards (some might come up twice, some might not come up at all), and see what hand you get. Do that thousands of times and you build up a real sense of how much your hand's "score" wobbles — all without ever getting a genuinely new card you didn't already have.

The block bootstrap — for data that isn't independent. The plain bootstrap above assumes each \(x_i\) is drawn independently, and that assumption is baked into the resampling step: picking \(x_7^{*}\) has no effect on the chance of picking \(x_{12}^{*}\) next. Financial time series routinely violate that assumption in exactly the way this repo's other lessons describe elsewhere in this tier: adjacent observations carry real serial correlation — volatility clustering, trending runs, mean- reverting bounces — so treating each day or each trade as an interchangeable, independent marble in the bag destroys information that was actually in the data. Shuffling a volatility-clustered return series into independent single-day draws and resampling from that shuffled bag produces synthetic paths with the clustering structure erased, which understates how much a real block of bad days can compound together — the plain bootstrap's confidence interval ends up too narrow, quietly overconfident, for exactly the reason a naive CLT-based calculation does (a point this tier's ARIMA/VAR material develops further under autocorrelation).

The fix, due to Hans Künsch (1989), is the block bootstrap: instead of resampling single, independent observations, resample contiguous blocks of \(l\) consecutive observations at a time. Concretely: fix a block length \(l\) (say, 10 trading days), form every overlapping contiguous block of that length from the original series (\(\{x_1,\dots,x_l\}, \{x_2,\dots,x_{l+1}\}\), and so on), draw \(k = \lceil n/l \rceil\) of those blocks with replacement, and concatenate them (trimming to length \(n\)) to build one resampled series. Because each block is a real, intact, consecutive run lifted straight out of history, whatever short-term serial dependence existed inside that run — the day-to-day correlation the plain bootstrap would have shuffled away — survives inside the resample. Compute \(T\) on the resampled series exactly as before, repeat \(B\) times, and read off \(SE_{boot}\) or the percentile CI the same way. (A related refinement, Politis & Romano's 1994 "stationary bootstrap," draws block lengths themselves at random from a geometric distribution rather than fixing one \(l\), which avoids the plain block bootstrap's sensitivity to picking exactly the right block length — too short and the correlation still leaks out at the block boundaries, too long and there are too few distinct blocks left to resample meaningfully.)

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Plain bootstrapping tears a movie into individual frames and shuffles them randomly — which destroys the story, because what happens in frame 50 is closely tied to what happened in frame 49. The block bootstrap instead cuts the movie into short scenes and shuffles those scenes around instead. Each scene still plays out in its original order internally, so the "this leads to that" structure — the stuff that actually mattered — survives, even though the overall movie is now a novel remix of real scenes.

Monte Carlo simulation. The bootstrap resamples the actual observed values, over and over, with replacement. Monte Carlo simulation is a different move: instead of resampling real observations, fit a parametric model to the data — a GARCH(1,1) process for volatility, geometric Brownian motion for a price path, an assumed return distribution with an estimated mean and standard deviation — and then generate brand-new, entirely synthetic observations directly from that fitted model, using freshly drawn random innovations at each step:

\[ r_t^{(sim)} = \hat\mu + \hat\sigma_t \, z_t, \qquad z_t \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0,1) \ \text{(or a fitted residual distribution)} \]
μ̂, σ̂ₜparameters fitted to the same historical sample
zₜa freshly drawn random innovation at each step

Running that recursion forward many times, independently, produces many synthetic return/price/equity paths, \(S^{(1)}, S^{(2)}, \dots, S^{(M)}\), each one a plausible trajectory under the assumed model. Looking across those \(M\) paths at the spread of terminal outcomes, drawdowns, or any other path-dependent statistic gives a Monte Carlo estimate of the range of outcomes the model considers plausible — exactly analogous to what the bootstrap's replicates give you, but generated from a fitted model's assumed mechanics rather than resampled from the raw data itself.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Instead of reshuffling real cards like the bootstrap, Monte Carlo builds a little dice-rolling machine that's designed to behave like your data does — same average, same typical swings — and then rolls those dice thousands of times to generate entirely made-up, but statistically similar, outcomes. It's powerful because you can generate as many "what if" paths as you want, but it's only as trustworthy as the little machine's design: if the dice were built wrong, every path it spits out inherits that same wrongness.

The critical distinction — read this paragraph twice. Both techniques answer some version of the same question: "given what I already have — one historical sample, or one fitted model built from that sample — what range of outcomes is consistent with it?" That is a real, useful, well-posed statistical question, and both tools answer it validly. It is not the same question as "will this signal work on a market, or a time period, it has never seen?" Every bootstrap resample is built from the same \(n\) original observations, generated by the same signal, over the same historical period — reshuffling and re-drawing from that fixed pool cannot create information about a regime that pool never contained. Every Monte Carlo path is generated from a model whose parameters were themselves estimated from that same historical sample — if the fitted \(\hat\mu\), \(\hat\sigma\), or GARCH parameters are wrong for tomorrow's regime, every synthetic path inherits that same mis-specification, no matter how many thousands of paths you generate. Neither procedure has any mechanism for learning about data outside the sample it started from. That is exactly why this repo's own house discipline states the rule quoted at the top of this lesson, and the rest of this page exists to make precisely why that rule is true, not just asserted, fully explicit.

The trap: a narrow bootstrap or Monte Carlo interval proves the sample (or the fitted model) is internally consistent — it proves nothing about whether the signal that generated it will work on data it has never seen. Neither tool can leave the sample it started from.
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine studying for a test by rewriting your own class notes over and over in slightly different orders. No matter how many times you do it, you're still only ever reviewing what you already wrote down — you can't accidentally learn something the teacher never covered. Bootstrapping and Monte Carlo are exactly this: incredibly useful for understanding how solid your existing notes are, but structurally incapable of testing you on material you haven't seen, which is the only thing that tells you if you're actually ready for a real exam.

Worked example

Bootstrapping the mean of five trade returns, by hand

Suppose a strategy has closed exactly five trades, with percentage returns \(x = \{2,\, -1,\, 3,\, -2,\, 1\}\) (in percent). The sample mean is:

\[ \bar{x} = \frac{2 + (-1) + 3 + (-2) + 1}{5} = \frac{3}{5} = 0.6 \]
0.6%the one number a backtest report would show — nothing about its stability

That's the one number a backtest report would show: "average trade return = 0.6%." The bootstrap asks: how much would that 0.6% wobble if the five trades had come out slightly differently? Draw three resamples by hand, each one five values pulled with replacement from the same five original numbers:

\[ \begin{aligned} x^{*(1)} &= \{2,\, 2,\, -1,\, 1,\, 3\} &&\Rightarrow \bar{x}^{*(1)} = \tfrac{2+2-1+1+3}{5} = \tfrac{7}{5} = 1.4 \\ x^{*(2)} &= \{-2,\, -2,\, 1,\, -1,\, -2\} &&\Rightarrow \bar{x}^{*(2)} = \tfrac{-2-2+1-1-2}{5} = \tfrac{-6}{5} = -1.2 \\ x^{*(3)} &= \{3,\, 1,\, 3,\, 2,\, -1\} &&\Rightarrow \bar{x}^{*(3)} = \tfrac{3+1+3+2-1}{5} = \tfrac{8}{5} = 1.6 \end{aligned} \]
1.4, −1.2, 1.6three resample means, from the exact same five original values

Notice each resample reuses the same five original values — nothing new has entered — yet the resample means already range from \(-1.2\) to \(1.6\), swinging well past the original \(0.6\) in both directions, just from which values happened to repeat and which happened to be left out. In practice you would not stop at three: you would draw \(B = 10{,}000\) such resamples, collect all 10,000 resample means \(\bar{x}^{*}_1, \dots, \bar{x}^{*}_{10{,}000}\), and then read off \(SE_{boot}\) as the standard deviation of that whole collection, and a 95% confidence interval as its 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. The exact same mechanical procedure, with \(T\) swapped from "sample mean" to "Sharpe ratio computed on the resampled sequence of trades," is precisely how a bootstrap confidence interval for a strategy's Sharpe ratio is built — and precisely the technique the Repo Connection box below warns against over-trusting. Five trades is a deliberately extreme example to make the mechanism vivid; the same logic, and the same limitation, apply identically whether \(n\) is 5 or 500 — resampling can characterize the spread within the sample you have, but it cannot conjure a sixth, genuinely new trade that never happened.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

📏

Performance-metric confidence intervals

Bootstrapping a Sharpe ratio, max drawdown, or hit rate — metrics with no clean analytic sampling distribution — is a standard, legitimate risk-desk request.

🎲

Monte Carlo option pricing & risk

Pricing path-dependent derivatives and computing portfolio VaR by simulating thousands of joint scenarios rather than a single closed-form formula.

🧱

Block bootstrapping in econometrics

The standard fix wherever a time series' own serial correlation would otherwise be silently discarded — GARCH/ARIMA coefficient standard errors, for instance.

🏖️

Retirement planning

Simulating thousands of possible future market paths to show a saver the range of outcomes — an expectation-setting exercise, never a guarantee.

Performance-metric confidence intervals. Efron & Tibshirani's own textbook (Further Reading) popularized using the bootstrap for exactly the metrics that don't have clean analytic sampling distributions — a Sharpe ratio, a max drawdown, a hit rate on a modest number of trades. A bootstrap CI around a reported Sharpe ratio is a standard, legitimate risk-desk request, precisely because it's honest about sampling uncertainty in a way a single point estimate never is. Monte Carlo option pricing and risk is one of finance's oldest applied uses of simulation: pricing a path-dependent derivative (an Asian option, a barrier option) by simulating thousands of price paths under a risk-neutral process and averaging the discounted payoff, and computing portfolio Value-at-Risk by simulating thousands of joint scenarios across a book's risk factors rather than relying on a single closed-form formula. Block bootstrapping in econometrics is the standard fix wherever a time series' own serial correlation would otherwise be silently discarded — bootstrapping the standard errors of a fitted GARCH or ARIMA coefficient, for instance, needs the block variant for exactly the reason described above. Sequence-of-returns / retirement planning is a widely visible use of Monte Carlo outside trading proper: simulating thousands of possible future market paths to show a saver the range of retirement-portfolio outcomes consistent with an assumed return and volatility — a legitimate expectation-setting exercise that, just as this project's own rule states, is never sold as a guarantee the future will resemble any one simulated path.

🎯
Real-world trading scenario

The reassuringly narrow bootstrap interval that proved nothing about tomorrow

A researcher backtests a strategy and reports an attractive Sharpe ratio. Wanting to check how solid that number is, they bootstrap the strategy's trade returns 10,000 times, compute the Sharpe ratio on each resample, and get back a tight, reassuring-looking 95% confidence interval — the resampled Sharpe barely moves across all 10,000 draws. "The range of outcomes is narrow," the researcher concludes, "this looks robust."

It doesn't, or at least not in the way that conclusion implies. Every one of those 10,000 resamples was built by reaching into the exact same bag of trades — generated by the exact same signal, over the exact same historical period. A tight bootstrap interval is genuine evidence that the sample is internally consistent: the trades don't include one or two outlier events so extreme that resampling them in and out wildly swings the Sharpe ratio. That is a real, useful, and legitimately reassuring fact about data quality and outlier sensitivity. It is a completely different fact from whether the signal that generated those trades would produce anything like that same Sharpe ratio on a market, or a regime, or a time period it has never traded through — a question the bootstrap has no way to touch, because it never leaves the historical sample it started from.

A narrow, confidence-inspiring bootstrap band and genuine out-of-sample validity are two different questions with two different answers, and conflating them — treating "the resample is stable" as if it meant "the strategy is robust" — is a documented, easy-to-fall-into way backtests mislead their own authors. The fix is not to distrust the bootstrap; the fix is to be precise about which of the two questions it just answered.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where these ideas quietly get misapplied

Reporting a tight bootstrap or Monte Carlo interval as "robustness evidence." This is the scenario above, generalized: a narrow interval says the original sample is internally consistent, not that the underlying signal has out-of-sample edge. State plainly which claim a given number supports — sample consistency, or generalization — and never let the first stand in for the second.

Using a plain (i.i.d.) bootstrap on serially correlated data. Resampling single, independent observations from a return series that exhibits volatility clustering or autocorrelation destroys exactly the dependence structure that mattered, and understates the true variability — the resulting confidence interval looks tighter and more confident than it should be, for the same underlying reason a naive CLT-based standard error is too optimistic on dependent data. Use the block bootstrap (or the stationary bootstrap) for time series, and choose a block length long enough to capture the relevant serial correlation.

Treating a Monte Carlo model's assumptions as settled fact. Every synthetic path in a Monte Carlo simulation inherits whatever mis-specification exists in the fitted model — a GARCH model that understates tail risk, a GBM assumption that ignores jumps, drift or volatility parameters estimated over a calm period. Thousands of paths generated from a wrong model produce a precise-looking, wrong answer; more simulated paths reduce simulation noise around the model's own implied distribution, they do not correct the model's mis-specification.

Expecting the bootstrap to manufacture information that isn't in a small original sample. Resampling 20 real trades 10,000 times produces 10,000 numbers, but every one of them is built from the same 20 underlying data points. A small \(n\) means the empirical distribution \(\hat F_n\) is itself a noisy, coarse approximation of the true \(F\) — the bootstrap faithfully reflects the uncertainty already present in a small sample, it cannot shrink that uncertainty below what 20 real observations can actually support, however large \(B\) is set.

Section 04

Key Takeaways

✓The bootstrap resamples your one dataset with replacement. Thousands of resamples approximate a statistic's sampling distribution — a Sharpe ratio, a mean, a max drawdown — with no assumption about its shape.
✓Use the block bootstrap for time series. A plain bootstrap shuffles away volatility clustering and serial correlation, understating true uncertainty; resampling contiguous blocks preserves it.
✓Monte Carlo simulates from a fitted model, not real observations. It generates brand-new synthetic paths from an assumed process (GARCH, GBM) rather than resampling actual data.
⚠Neither tool can leave the sample it started from. Both describe what's plausible given what you already have — never what would happen under conditions that sample never contained.
⚠A narrow bootstrap interval is not robustness evidence. It proves the sample is internally consistent, not that the underlying signal generalizes — conflating the two is a documented, easy way backtests mislead their own authors.
✓Walk-forward validation is the tool that actually tests generalization. It splits real history into a fitting period and a genuinely separate later testing period the signal never touched — bootstrapping and Monte Carlo cannot substitute for that, no matter how many replicates are run.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. Why does bootstrapping a strategy's own historical trades NOT prove it will work on new, unseen data?
Because every bootstrap resample is built by drawing, with replacement, from that same fixed set of original trades — generated by the same signal, over the same historical period. No amount of reshuffling or re-drawing from a fixed pool of observations can introduce information about a market condition, regime, or time period that pool never contained. The bootstrap characterizes sampling variability within the sample you have; it has no mechanism for testing generalization to data outside that sample, which is a structurally different question that only a true out-of-sample test (walk-forward validation, a held-out period the signal was never fit to) can answer.
Q2. Why do you need a BLOCK bootstrap instead of a plain bootstrap for time series data?
The plain bootstrap draws single observations independently, which implicitly assumes the original observations were independent of each other. Financial time series routinely violate that assumption — volatility clustering and other serial correlation mean adjacent observations carry real information about each other. Resampling single points independently shuffles that structure away, so the resampled series understates how a real, contiguous run of bad days can compound — producing a confidence interval that's falsely narrow. The block bootstrap resamples contiguous blocks of consecutive observations instead of single independent points, so whatever short-term serial dependence existed inside each block survives intact in the resample.
Q3. What is Monte Carlo simulation actually simulating, and what does that make it a legitimate test of — versus not a test of?
Monte Carlo simulation generates synthetic paths from an assumed, fitted model (e.g. GARCH or GBM with parameters estimated from the historical data) rather than resampling the real observations directly. It is a legitimate test of "what range of outcomes is plausible if the fitted model's assumptions hold" — an expectation-setting exercise. It is not a test of whether the model's assumptions themselves are correct for the future, and it is not a test of whether the underlying trading signal generalizes to conditions the historical data used to fit the model never contained — the model was estimated from the same sample the strategy was built and tested on.
Q4. If you bootstrap 10,000 resamples from only 20 original trades, what have you actually learned, and what's the limit of what you can learn?
You've learned an approximation of how much a statistic (say, the mean return or the Sharpe ratio) would plausibly vary if drawn repeatedly from a population that looks like those 20 trades — a genuine, useful measure of sampling uncertainty given a small sample. The limit is that all 10,000 resamples are still built from the same 20 underlying data points; the empirical distribution of 20 observations is itself a coarse, noisy stand-in for the true underlying distribution, and no amount of resampling can shrink that underlying coarseness — it is not equivalent to having collected 10,000 (or even 1,000) genuinely new, independent trades.
Q5. Why would you prefer a bootstrap standard error over an analytic, CLT-based standard error for something like a Sharpe ratio or a max drawdown?
A CLT-based analytic standard error typically requires assuming a specific distributional shape (often normality) and, for many statistics of practical interest — a Sharpe ratio, a max drawdown, a hit rate on a modest trade count — a clean closed-form formula for the sampling distribution simply doesn't exist, or exists only under assumptions that don't hold for real, often skewed or fat-tailed, trading returns. The bootstrap sidesteps both problems: it makes no distributional assumption about the underlying data and works identically regardless of how complicated the statistic \(T\) is, because it never needs an analytic formula for T's sampling distribution — it estimates that distribution empirically, by resampling.
Q6. Given this project's own validation rule, what tool should actually be used to test whether a signal generalizes, and how does it differ structurally from bootstrapping or Monte Carlo?
Walk-forward validation with a purged cross-validation split (this tier's previous lesson) is the tool built for that question. It differs structurally in exactly the way that matters: instead of resampling or simulating from the same historical sample the signal was fit to, it splits real history into a fitting period and a genuinely separate, later testing period (with a purge gap to prevent information leaking across the boundary), refits or re-runs the strategy only on the fitting side, and evaluates performance strictly on the held-out side the strategy never touched. Bootstrapping and Monte Carlo never leave the original sample; walk-forward validation's entire purpose is to test performance on data structurally outside it.

Further reading
  • Bradley Efron, "Bootstrap Methods: Another Look at the Jackknife," Annals of Statistics, 1979 — the original paper introducing the bootstrap.
  • Bradley Efron & Robert J. Tibshirani, An Introduction to the Bootstrap, Chapman & Hall/CRC, 1993 — the standard textbook treatment, covering standard errors, confidence intervals, and the bootstrap's theoretical justification in far more depth than this lesson.
  • Hans R. Künsch, "The Jackknife and the Bootstrap for General Stationary Observations," Annals of Statistics, 1989 — the original moving block bootstrap for dependent, time-series data.
  • Dimitris N. Politis & Joseph P. Romano, "The Stationary Bootstrap," Journal of the American Statistical Association, 1994 — the random block-length refinement of the block bootstrap.
  • Oliver Ledoit & Michael Wolf, "Robust Performance Hypothesis Testing with the Sharpe Ratio," Journal of Empirical Finance, 2008 — a direct finance application of resampling-based inference to Sharpe ratio comparisons, including the serial-correlation caveats covered above.
