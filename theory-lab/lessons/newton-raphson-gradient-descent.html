Newton-Raphson & Gradient Descent

Two ways to let a computer search for the number you can't solve for by hand — one climbs down a slope one step at a time, the other uses the slope's own curvature to leap straight at the answer.

CONCEPT — not built or tested in this repo ~17 min read Prereqs: Optimization Basics, Calculus for Finance
TL;DR
  • Newton-Raphson finds where a function crosses zero by repeatedly following its tangent line down to the axis: \(x_{n+1} = x_n - f(x_n)/f'(x_n)\). Near a well-behaved root it converges quadratically — the number of correct digits roughly doubles every iteration.
  • That speed is bought with fragility: a bad starting guess, a derivative near zero, or a function with multiple roots can make it overshoot wildly or never settle at all.
  • Solving for a bond's yield-to-maturity and solving for an option's implied volatility are the same task in disguise — "find the input that makes a pricing formula reproduce an observed market price" — and both are textbook Newton-Raphson applications.
  • Gradient descent solves a different but related problem: not "where does \(f\) cross zero" but "where is \(f\) smallest." It repeatedly steps a fixed fraction \(\alpha\) (the learning rate) down the negative gradient: \(x_{n+1} = x_n - \alpha\nabla f(x_n)\).
  • Too large a learning rate overshoots the minimum and can oscillate or diverge; too small a rate converges reliably but slowly. There is no free lunch — the rate has to be tuned to the problem.
  • Newton's method for optimization, \(x_{n+1} = x_n - H^{-1}\nabla f(x_n)\), is the bridge between the two ideas: it uses the Hessian \(H\) (curvature, a second derivative) to pick both the step's direction and its size automatically — faster convergence than gradient descent, at the cost of computing and inverting \(H\).
Section 01

Why this matters

A huge share of quantitative finance reduces to one of two questions: "what input makes this formula match an observed number?" or "what input makes this formula as small (or as large) as possible?" Neither question usually has a clean algebraic answer — you can't always rearrange the formula by hand and isolate the variable the way the Optimization Basics lesson's simplest examples allow. Newton-Raphson and gradient descent are the two foundational tools that answer those questions numerically, iterating toward an answer a step at a time instead of solving for it in one closed-form move. Nearly everything built on top of this tier of the curriculum — convex optimization, quadratic programming, calibrating a model to market prices — is, underneath, a more careful, more constrained version of one of these two ideas.

💵

Bond yield-to-maturity

A bond's price is a known formula in the yield; there's no algebraic inverse. Newton-Raphson is the standard way a pricing engine solves for the yield that reproduces a quoted price.

📈

Option-implied volatility

Same shape of problem: Black-Scholes gives price from volatility, not the reverse. Solving "what σ reproduces this market price" is Newton-Raphson run on the option pricing formula.

🧠

Model calibration & machine learning

Fitting a model's parameters to minimize a loss function — whether it's a GARCH log-likelihood or a neural network's error — is gradient descent (or one of its faster cousins) at massive scale.

🧮

Every later lesson in this category

Convex optimization, KKT conditions, and quadratic programming all assume you already know what an iterative solver is doing and why it can fail — this lesson is the foundation underneath them.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Sliding down a tangent line, versus feeling your way downhill blindfolded

Imagine you're standing somewhere on a curve and you want to find exactly where it crosses zero. Newton-Raphson's trick: look at the curve's slope right where you're standing, draw the straight tangent line that matches that slope, and walk along the tangent (not the curve) until it hits zero. That's your next guess. Because a smooth curve looks almost exactly like its tangent line very close to a root, this move gets you dramatically closer with each step — as long as you're already reasonably close and the curve isn't doing anything strange (like going nearly flat) near where you're standing.

Gradient descent solves a different problem with the same flavor of idea, but blindfolded. Picture standing somewhere on a hilly landscape, unable to see the whole terrain, trying to reach the lowest point. You can feel which direction is steepest-downhill right under your feet — that's the gradient — so you take a step that direction. How big a step? That's the one knob you get to choose, the learning rate. Step too far and you might stride right over the valley floor and end up higher on the opposite slope than when you started; step too cautiously and you'll get there eventually, just very slowly. Newton-Raphson effectively lets you see the local curvature of the ground, not just its slope, and use that extra information to size the perfect step automatically — which is exactly what "Newton's method for optimization," covered below, adds to plain gradient descent.

Section 02

The math, step by step

Newton-Raphson — finding where a function crosses zero

The problem: given a function \(f\), find an \(x\) such that \(f(x) = 0\) (a root). Start from some guess \(x_n\). The tangent line to \(f\) at \(x_n\) has slope \(f'(x_n)\) and passes through the point \((x_n, f(x_n))\), so its equation is:

\[ y = f(x_n) + f'(x_n)(x - x_n) \]

Newton-Raphson's move is to find where that tangent line crosses zero, and use that as the next guess \(x_{n+1}\). Setting \(y=0\) and solving for \(x\):

\[ 0 = f(x_n) + f'(x_n)(x_{n+1} - x_n) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x_{n+1} = x_n - \frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)} \]

Repeat: compute \(f(x_{n+1})\) and \(f'(x_{n+1})\), take another step, and stop once \(|f(x_n)|\) (or the size of the step itself) drops below some small tolerance you choose in advance.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine you're trying to find exactly where a hilly path crosses ground level, but you can only see a little slice of the path at a time. Newton-Raphson's trick: look at how steep the path is right where you're standing, pretend it keeps going in a perfectly straight line at that same steepness, and walk along that imaginary straight line until it hits ground level. That's your next guess, and you repeat.

Why it converges so fast — quadratic convergence

Near an actual root \(x^\*\), a smooth function is very well approximated by its tangent line — the gap between the curve and the tangent shrinks proportionally to the square of the distance from \(x^\*\) (this falls straight out of a Taylor expansion of \(f\) around \(x^\*\); you don't need to carry out that expansion to use the result). That single fact is the entire reason Newton-Raphson is fast: if the current guess's error is \(\varepsilon_n = x_n - x^\*\), the next guess's error is approximately proportional to \(\varepsilon_n^2\), not \(\varepsilon_n\). Concretely — if a guess is off by \(0.1\), the next guess is off by roughly \(0.01\), then roughly \(0.0001\), then roughly \(0.00000001\): the number of correct decimal digits roughly doubles on every single iteration once you're close enough. This is called quadratic convergence, and it's dramatically faster than a method like bisection, which only ever halves the error each step (linear convergence) no matter how close you already are.

The catch: "once you're close enough" is doing a lot of work in that sentence. Quadratic convergence is a local guarantee — it describes what happens once \(x_n\) is already in a neighborhood of the true root where the tangent-line approximation is good. Far from the root, none of this speed guarantee applies.
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Once you're close to the real answer, Newton-Raphson gets dramatically more accurate with every single step — roughly doubling the number of correct digits each time, like going from being off by a dollar, to a penny, to a hundredth of a penny, in just three moves. The catch is that speed only kicks in once you're already reasonably close; far from the answer, none of that guarantee applies.

Where Newton-Raphson fails

The formula divides by \(f'(x_n)\), and every one of its practical failure modes traces back to that division or to the tangent-line approximation breaking down:

A flat derivative near the current guess

If \(f'(x_n)\) is close to zero, the correction term \(f(x_n)/f'(x_n)\) explodes — the tangent line is nearly horizontal, so its zero-crossing can be enormously far away, and the next guess can land somewhere wild, sometimes worse than where it started.

A bad starting point

Far from the root, the tangent line can be a poor stand-in for the actual curve — the method can overshoot, oscillate between two points forever, or converge to a completely different root than the one you wanted (if \(f\) has more than one).

In practice, production pricing engines rarely run pure Newton-Raphson unguarded — a common, robust fix is to start it from a sensible initial guess informed by the problem (e.g. the coupon rate for a bond near par, or an approximation formula for implied volatility), cap how large a single step is allowed to be, and fall back to a slower but guaranteed-to-converge method like bisection if Newton-Raphson's step ever moves outside a known bracket that must contain the root.

The same idea, applied: bond yield-to-maturity and implied volatility

Both of the following are, mechanically, the identical task: a pricing formula \(P(\theta)\) takes some unobservable input \(\theta\) and produces a price; the market gives you an observed price \(P_{mkt}\); you want the \(\theta\) that makes the formula agree with the market. Define \(f(\theta) = P(\theta) - P_{mkt}\) and Newton-Raphson finds its root:

\[ \theta_{n+1} = \theta_n - \frac{P(\theta_n) - P_{mkt}}{P'(\theta_n)} \]
θₙthe unobservable input — a bond's yield, or an option's volatility
P(θₙ)the pricing formula's output at the current guess
P'(θₙ)the pricing formula's sensitivity to θ — for a bond this is (up to sign and scaling) duration; for an option it's vega
P_mktthe one number you actually observed — the market price

For a bond, \(\theta\) is the yield \(y\) and \(P'(y)\) is closely related to the bond's (dollar) duration — the bond-pricing lesson elsewhere in this curriculum, Bond Pricing & Yield-to-Maturity, covers the pricing formula itself and its sensitivity in full; this lesson uses it only as the function Newton-Raphson is applied to. For an option, \(\theta\) is the implied volatility \(\sigma\) and \(P'(\sigma)\) is the option's vega — an option pricing formula (Black-Scholes, covered elsewhere in this curriculum) has no algebraic inverse for \(\sigma\), which is exactly why every implied-volatility solver in practice is some variant of Newton-Raphson (or a closely related root-finder) run against the pricing formula.

Gradient descent — finding where a function is smallest

A different, related problem: instead of "where does \(f\) cross zero," ask "for what \(x\) is \(f(x)\) as small as possible." Calculus says a smooth function's minimum occurs where its derivative is zero — so this even looks like a root-finding problem on \(f'\) rather than \(f\) — but for a function of many variables (as almost every real calibration or machine-learning problem is), computing and inverting the full second-derivative structure at every step is often too expensive. Gradient descent is the cheap alternative: at the current point, compute the gradient \(\nabla f(x_n)\) — the direction of steepest increase — and step a fixed distance in the opposite direction, since that's the direction of steepest decrease:

\[ x_{n+1} = x_n - \alpha \, \nabla f(x_n) \]
∇f(xₙ)the gradient — the direction (and steepness) of the fastest local increase
α(alpha) the learning rate — how far to step each time, chosen in advance
−α∇f(xₙ)a step of size α, taken downhill

In one dimension, \(\nabla f(x)\) is simply \(f'(x)\), so for the toy bowl function \(f(x) = (x-3)^2\), whose minimum is obviously at \(x=3\) by inspection, the update rule is:

\[ f(x) = (x-3)^2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad f'(x) = 2(x-3) \quad\Rightarrow\quad x_{n+1} = x_n - \alpha \cdot 2(x_n - 3) \]
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Gradient descent is like finding the bottom of a valley blindfolded, only able to feel which way is downhill right under your feet. You take a step that direction, feel again, take another step, and repeat. How big a step you take each time — the learning rate — is the one thing you get to choose in advance, and it makes all the difference.

The learning-rate tradeoff

\(\alpha\) is the single most important knob in gradient descent, and there is a genuine tradeoff, not a "bigger is always better" or "smaller is always safer" answer:

Too small an α

Every step moves reliably downhill, so convergence is guaranteed for any reasonable well-behaved function — but it can take a very large number of iterations to actually get close to the minimum, which is expensive at scale (imagine millions of parameters, each iteration a full pass over a dataset).

Too large an α

The step overshoots the minimum, landing on the opposite side — possibly farther from the minimum than the starting point. Repeated overshooting can oscillate with growing amplitude and diverge entirely, never settling anywhere.

For the one-dimensional bowl \(f(x)=(x-3)^2\), the update rule algebraically simplifies to \(x_{n+1} - 3 = (1-2\alpha)(x_n - 3)\) — each step multiplies the current error by the fixed factor \((1-2\alpha)\). That factor's size tells you exactly what will happen: if \(0 < \alpha < 0.5\), the factor is between 0 and 1 and the error shrinks every step, monotonically, toward zero. If \(\alpha = 0.5\) exactly, the factor is zero and the very next step lands exactly on the minimum — no overshoot at all (a special case that turns out to be exactly Newton's method for this function, below). If \(0.5 < \alpha < 1\), the factor is negative — the iterate flips sides of the minimum every step (oscillates) but the error still shrinks in magnitude, so it still converges, just less directly. If \(\alpha \ge 1\), the factor's magnitude is 1 or larger and the error no longer shrinks — the sequence oscillates with equal or growing amplitude and never converges. 🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Take steps too small and you'll shuffle toward the bottom of the valley forever, safe but painfully slow. Take steps too big and you can stride right over the valley floor and end up higher on the far side than where you started — and if you keep overshooting harder each time, you'll bounce away from the valley entirely instead of settling into it.

Newton's method for optimization — the bridge between the two ideas

Gradient descent only ever looks at the slope — first-derivative information — and has to guess a step size. Newton's method for optimization goes one derivative further: it also uses the curvature (the second derivative, or in many dimensions the Hessian matrix \(H\), the matrix of all second partial derivatives) to figure out automatically both which direction to move and how far:

\[ x_{n+1} = x_n - H^{-1} \nabla f(x_n) \]
∇f(xₙ)the gradient — same steepest-increase direction as before
Hthe Hessian — curvature information, second derivatives
H⁻¹inverse curvature — automatically rescales the step, replacing the hand-chosen α

In one dimension, \(H\) is just the scalar second derivative \(f''(x)\), so Newton's method for optimizing \(f(x)=(x-3)^2\) (where \(f''(x) = 2\) everywhere, a constant) is \(x_{n+1} = x_n - f'(x_n)/2\) — which is exactly the \(\alpha = 0.5\) special case identified above. That's not a coincidence: for a perfectly bowl-shaped (quadratic) function, Newton's method's automatically-chosen step size is precisely the one learning rate that reaches the minimum in a single step, because a quadratic function's curvature is constant and Newton's method reads that curvature directly instead of guessing it. For a general, non-quadratic function, Newton's optimization step still typically converges in far fewer iterations than gradient descent — the same quadratic-convergence property from root-finding carries over — but each step is more expensive to compute (forming and inverting \(H\), which for \(p\) parameters is a \(p \times p\) matrix — expensive when \(p\) is large, which is exactly why plain gradient descent, and its many refinements, remain the workhorse for large-scale machine learning where \(p\) can run into the millions).

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Plain gradient descent only feels the slope under its feet and has to guess how big a step to take. Newton's method for optimization also feels the curvature of the ground — is it a gentle slope or a sharp bowl — and uses that extra information to pick the perfect step size automatically, no guessing required. The catch is that "feeling the curvature" is expensive to compute when there are millions of directions to check at once.

Worked example

Two hand-worked iterations: a bond's yield, and a bowl's minimum

Part 1 — bond yield-to-maturity via Newton-Raphson. A 3-year bond, face value 100, annual coupon 5 (a 5% coupon rate), is quoted in the market at \(P_{mkt} = 97.50\) — trading below par, so the market's implied yield must be above the 5% coupon rate. The pricing formula is:

\[ P(y) = \frac{5}{(1+y)} + \frac{5}{(1+y)^2} + \frac{105}{(1+y)^3} \]

There's no algebraic way to invert this for \(y\) — Newton-Raphson it is. Define \(f(y) = P(y) - 97.50\), start from the natural guess \(y_0 = 0.05\) (the coupon rate), and iterate.

1

Iteration 0 → 1

At \(y_0 = 5.000\%\): a bond priced exactly at its coupon rate trades at par, so \(P(y_0) = 100.00\) exactly, and \(f(y_0) = 100.00 - 97.50 = 2.50\). The derivative (found by differentiating each cash-flow term) is \(P'(y_0) \approx -272.24\):

\[ y_1 = 0.05 - \frac{2.50}{-272.24} = 0.05 + 0.00918 = 0.05918 \ (5.918\%) \]
2

Iteration 1 → 2

At \(y_1 = 5.918\%\): \(P(y_1) \approx 97.5414\), so \(f(y_1) \approx 0.0414\) — the error has already shrunk from 2.50 to 0.0414, roughly 60-fold, in a single step. With \(P'(y_1) \approx -263.15\):

\[ y_2 = 0.05918 - \frac{0.0414}{-263.15} = 0.05918 + 0.000157 = 0.05934 \ (5.934\%) \]
3

Iteration 2 → 3 (converged)

At \(y_2 = 5.934\%\), \(P(y_2)\) is already within a fraction of a cent of \(97.50\); a third iteration moves \(y\) by less than \(0.0001\%\) — below any sensible tolerance. The solver reports yield-to-maturity ≈ 5.934%, found in three iterations from a naive starting guess.

Part 2 — gradient descent minimizing a bowl by hand. Minimize \(f(x) = (x-3)^2\) starting from \(x_0 = 0\), learning rate \(\alpha = 0.3\). The update rule is \(x_{n+1} = x_n - 0.3 \cdot 2(x_n-3) = x_n - 0.6(x_n - 3)\):

\[ \begin{aligned} x_1 &= 0 - 0.6(0-3) = 1.800, &&f(x_1) = 1.440 \\ x_2 &= 1.8 - 0.6(1.8-3) = 2.520, &&f(x_2) = 0.230 \\ x_3 &= 2.52 - 0.6(2.52-3) = 2.808, &&f(x_3) = 0.037 \\ x_4 &= 2.808 - 0.6(2.808-3) = 2.923, &&f(x_4) \approx 0.0059 \end{aligned} \]
0 → 1.8 → 2.52 → 2.808 → 2.923steadily closing in on the true minimum, x=3
9.0 → 1.44 → 0.23 → 0.037 → 0.0059f(xₙ) shrinking by a constant ratio (0.16) each step — linear, not quadratic, convergence

Notice the contrast with Part 1: the bond-yield solve using Newton-Raphson needed only 2-3 iterations because it's quadratically convergent; gradient descent here needs many more steps to close the same relative distance, because it only ever shrinks the error by a fixed ratio each step (here, \((1-2\times0.3)=0.4\) — matching the general formula from the derivation above) rather than squaring it. That's the real, quantitative version of "gradient descent is cheaper per step, Newton-type methods converge faster per step."

🎯
Real-world trading scenario

An implied-vol solver that quietly returns garbage on a deep out-of-the-money option

A vol-surface calibration process runs Newton-Raphson against the Black-Scholes formula for every strike on a listed options chain, solving for the implied volatility that reproduces each strike's last traded price. For most strikes, close to at-the-money, it converges in 2-3 iterations without incident — vega (the pricing formula's sensitivity to volatility, i.e. \(P'(\sigma)\) in the notation above) is comfortably large there, so the correction term stays well-behaved.

For a handful of very deep out-of-the-money strikes, though, vega is tiny — those options barely react to volatility at all, since they're almost certain to expire worthless (or almost certain to be exercised) regardless of small vol changes. Dividing by a near-zero vega produces an enormous correction term on the very first iteration, and the solver either returns a wildly implausible volatility (say, 400%) or fails to converge within its iteration cap entirely. If the calibration pipeline naively accepts whatever number the solver returns and feeds it straight into the day's volatility surface, that one bad strike can silently distort every model that reads off the surface near it — a smile/skew shape that looks like a genuine market signal but is actually a numerical-solver artifact. The standard practitioner fix is exactly the one described above: bound the solver's allowed step size, fall back to a bracketing method (bisection, or Brent's method) whenever Newton-Raphson's step leaves a sane range, and flag any strike whose solved volatility sits outside a plausible band for manual review rather than trusting the raw output.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

In practice almost nobody hand-codes a bare, unguarded Newton-Raphson loop for anything that touches live pricing. Production implied-volatility and yield solvers wrap the same core update rule in guardrails: a problem-informed starting guess (an approximation formula, or the previous day's solved value as a warm start), a maximum step size, an iteration cap, and a fallback bracketing method for the rare case where the pure Newton-Raphson step would leave a known-good bracket around the true root. Quant libraries almost universally expose this as a single "solve implied vol" or "solve yield" function precisely so individual users don't each re-derive these edge cases themselves.

On the optimization side, plain gradient descent with a fixed learning rate is rarely used as-is at scale either — it's the conceptual starting point for a whole family of refinements: momentum methods that smooth out oscillation, adaptive-learning-rate methods (AdaGrad, RMSProp, Adam) that effectively adjust \(\alpha\) per-parameter based on how the gradient has been behaving, and quasi-Newton methods (BFGS and its limited-memory variant, L-BFGS) that build up an approximation to the Hessian's useful information across iterations without ever forming or inverting the full matrix — capturing much of Newton's-method's speed advantage without its full cost. Any time a quant researcher says a model was "fit by gradient descent" or "calibrated with L-BFGS," they mean some member of this same family, built directly on the two update rules derived above.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where this goes wrong in practice

Trusting Newton-Raphson's output without checking it converged to a plausible root. A function with multiple roots, or a bad starting guess, can make the method converge — quickly and confidently — to the wrong root, or to a mathematically valid but economically nonsensical answer (a negative implied volatility, an implausible yield). Fast convergence is not the same claim as "converged to the answer you actually wanted."

Picking a single fixed learning rate and never revisiting it. A rate that works well for one calibration problem, or one region of parameter space, can be far too large or far too small for a different one — this is exactly why adaptive methods exist, and why "just run gradient descent" without any tuning is rarely the practitioner's actual workflow.

Mistaking convergence for correctness of the objective. Gradient descent (or Newton's method) reliably finds a point where the gradient is zero — but on a non-convex function, that point can be a local minimum that is nowhere near the true global minimum. The next lesson in this category, on convex optimization, is entirely about when "the gradient is zero" is enough to conclude "this is the best possible answer" versus when it isn't.

Ignoring the cost of the Hessian at scale. Newton's method for optimization looks strictly better than gradient descent on paper (fewer iterations), but forming and inverting a \(p\times p\) Hessian for a model with millions of parameters is computationally infeasible — which is exactly why large-scale machine learning still runs on gradient-descent variants rather than pure Newton's method, despite the latter's faster per-iteration convergence.

Section 04

Key Takeaways

✓Newton-Raphson follows the tangent line to its zero-crossing. \(x_{n+1}=x_n-f(x_n)/f'(x_n)\), and near a root it converges quadratically — correct digits roughly double each step.
✓Bond YTM and implied volatility are the same problem in disguise. Both solve "what input makes a pricing formula match an observed price" — the textbook use case for Newton-Raphson.
⚠Quadratic convergence is a local guarantee, not a global one. A bad starting point, a near-zero derivative, or multiple roots can make Newton-Raphson overshoot, oscillate, or converge to the wrong answer entirely.
✓Gradient descent steps a fixed distance downhill. \(x_{n+1}=x_n-\alpha\nabla f(x_n)\) — cheap per step, needing only first-derivative information, but the learning rate α must be tuned.
⚠The learning rate is a genuine tradeoff, not a free parameter. Too small converges reliably but slowly; too large overshoots, oscillates, or diverges entirely — there's no universally "safe" default.
✓Newton's method for optimization bridges both ideas. \(x_{n+1}=x_n-H^{-1}\nabla f(x_n)\) uses curvature (the Hessian) to pick the step size automatically — faster convergence, at the cost of computing and inverting \(H\).
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. Why does Newton-Raphson converge so much faster than a method like bisection, once it's close to the root?
Near a root, a smooth function is very closely approximated by its tangent line, and the gap between the curve and the tangent shrinks proportionally to the square of the distance from the root (from a Taylor expansion). That means each Newton-Raphson step's error is roughly proportional to the square of the previous step's error — the number of correct digits roughly doubles every iteration (quadratic convergence). Bisection, by contrast, only ever halves the size of the bracket containing the root each step, regardless of how close it already is — a fixed, linear rate that Newton-Raphson's local quadratic rate beats decisively once it's in range.
Q2. Why can Newton-Raphson produce a wildly wrong answer for a deep out-of-the-money option's implied volatility?
The update divides by the pricing formula's derivative with respect to the input being solved for — for implied volatility, that derivative is vega. Deep out-of-the-money options have very small vega (their price barely reacts to volatility, since they're overwhelmingly likely to expire worthless or be exercised regardless of small vol moves). Dividing the price gap by a near-zero vega produces a huge correction term, throwing the next guess far outside any plausible volatility — this is the "flat derivative" failure mode applied directly to a real pricing formula.
Q3. In gradient descent on f(x)=(x−3)², why does α=0.5 reach the minimum in exactly one step from any starting point, while α=0.3 takes many steps?
The update rule simplifies to \(x_{n+1}-3=(1-2\alpha)(x_n-3)\) — each step multiplies the current error by the constant factor \((1-2\alpha)\). At \(\alpha=0.5\), that factor is exactly zero, so the very next error is zero regardless of the starting error — one step reaches the minimum exactly. This isn't a coincidence: for this particular quadratic function, \(\alpha=0.5\) equals \(1/f''(x)\), which is precisely what Newton's method for optimization would compute automatically as the ideal step size. At \(\alpha=0.3\), the factor is \(1-0.6=0.4\) — the error still shrinks every step, but only by 60% each time, requiring many iterations to become negligibly small rather than one.
Q4. What specifically does Newton's method for optimization add on top of plain gradient descent, and what does that addition cost?
Gradient descent only uses first-derivative (gradient) information and needs a hand-chosen learning rate α. Newton's method for optimization additionally uses the Hessian (second-derivative / curvature information) and computes the step as \(x_{n+1}=x_n-H^{-1}\nabla f(x_n)\) — the inverse curvature automatically determines both the step's direction and its size, replacing the need to tune α by hand, and typically converging in far fewer iterations. The cost is forming and inverting the Hessian matrix, which for a function of \(p\) variables is a \(p\times p\) matrix — computationally expensive or infeasible once \(p\) is large, which is exactly why plain gradient descent (and its lighter-weight refinements) remains standard for large-scale problems like training a model with millions of parameters.
Q5. A researcher runs gradient descent and it converges — the gradient at the final point is essentially zero. Does that guarantee they've found the global minimum? Why or why not?
No. A zero gradient only guarantees a stationary point — which could be a local minimum, a local maximum, or a saddle point — and even if it is a genuine local minimum, that says nothing about whether it's the global minimum unless the function has a special property guaranteeing local minima are automatically global. That property is convexity, covered in the next lesson in this category: on a non-convex function, gradient descent (or Newton's method) can converge cleanly and confidently to a local minimum that is far worse than the true global minimum elsewhere in the search space.

Further reading
  • Isaac Newton, unpublished method described in De analysi per aequationes numero terminorum infinitas, c. 1669, later formalized and generalized by Joseph Raphson, Analysis Aequationum Universalis, 1690 — the historical origin of the root-finding method, developed independently by the two mathematicians roughly two decades apart.
  • Jorge Nocedal & Stephen J. Wright, Numerical Optimization, 2nd ed., Springer, 2006 — the standard graduate-level reference covering gradient descent, Newton's method, and the full family of quasi-Newton methods (BFGS, L-BFGS) in rigorous depth.
  • William H. Press, Saul A. Teukolsky, William T. Vetterling & Brian P. Flannery, Numerical Recipes: The Art of Scientific Computing, 3rd ed., Cambridge University Press, 2007 — a widely used, implementation-focused reference covering root-finding (including Newton-Raphson and Brent's method) with working code.
  • Sebastian Ruder, "An Overview of Gradient Descent Optimization Algorithms," arXiv:1609.04747, 2016 — a widely cited, accessible survey of the momentum and adaptive-learning-rate refinements (AdaGrad, RMSProp, Adam) built on top of plain gradient descent.
