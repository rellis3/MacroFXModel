Duration & Convexity

Duration is a bond's average cash-flow timing, in disguise — and, in a completely different disguise, the first term of a Taylor expansion of price around yield. Convexity is the second term, and it's the reason a duration-only estimate always underestimates a bond's true price after a yield move.

CONCEPT — not built or tested in this repo ~17 min read Prereqs: Bond Pricing & Yield to Maturity
TL;DR
  • Macaulay duration \(D_{Mac}\) is the weighted-average time to a bond's cash flows, where each cash flow's weight is its present value divided by the bond's total price — a "center of mass" for the bond's payment schedule, measured in years.
  • Modified duration \(D_{mod} = D_{Mac}/(1+y)\) converts that timing measure into a direct price-sensitivity number: \(\frac{\Delta P}{P} \approx -D_{mod}\,\Delta y\) — the first-order (linear) Taylor term of price as a function of yield.
  • Convexity \(C\) is the second-order Taylor term, giving the fuller approximation \(\frac{\Delta P}{P} \approx -D_{mod}\Delta y + \frac{1}{2}C(\Delta y)^2\) — and because \(C>0\) always for a plain bond, this term is always positive, meaning duration alone always underestimates the bond's true price after any yield move, up or down.
  • DV01 (dollar value of a basis point, also called PVBP) is duration expressed in dollars per 1bp of yield move — the practical, position-sizing version of the same sensitivity.
  • The worked example reuses Lesson 1's 5-year, 4% coupon, $1,000 face bond at 5% YTM (\(P=\$956.71\)): Macaulay duration ≈ 4.62 years, modified duration ≈ 4.40 years. For a 100bp yield rise, duration-only predicts \$914.61; duration+convexity predicts \$915.78; the exact re-priced value (at 6% YTM) is \$915.75 — convexity closes nearly the entire \$1.14 gap.
  • The interactive chart overlays the true price-yield curve with the duration-only tangent line and the duration+convexity quadratic, both recomputed live as you drag the current-yield slider.
Section 01

Why this matters

Lesson 1 established that bond prices move opposite to yields, and that the relationship curves rather than running straight. That lesson left two questions unanswered on purpose: how much does price move for a given yield change, and how good is a straight-line estimate of that move? Duration answers the first question to a first approximation; convexity answers exactly how wrong that first approximation is, and fixes most of the error. Together they are the single most-used risk measure in fixed income — every bond, bond fund, and bond futures position on a rates desk is sized and hedged primarily in duration terms, long before anyone reaches for a full re-pricing model.

This lesson also connects directly to this curriculum's calculus material: duration and convexity are not a bond-specific invention, they are literally the first two terms of the Taylor series expansion of the pricing function \(P(y)\) around the current yield — the exact same "linear approximation, then a curvature correction" idea covered generally elsewhere in this curriculum's foundation mathematics, applied here to one specific, very consequential function.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Duration is "how long, on average, do you have to wait to get your money back?"

Imagine two bonds, both maturing in 10 years. One is a zero-coupon bond — you get nothing until year 10, then one lump sum. The other pays a generous coupon every year along the way. Which one is more sensitive to a change in interest rates? The zero-coupon bond, clearly — every dollar of its value is tied up ten years out, fully exposed to whatever happens to rates between now and then. The coupon bond gets some of its money back earlier, so less of its value is exposed to that far-off uncertainty. Duration is the number that captures exactly this: it's the payment-weighted average time you wait to receive the bond's cash flows. A zero-coupon bond's duration equals its maturity exactly (all the weight sits at one single date); a coupon bond's duration is always shorter than its maturity, because some cash arrives earlier and pulls the average forward.

Convexity answers a subtler, related question: does that "average wait time" sensitivity estimate stay accurate for a big rate move, or only a tiny one? It doesn't stay accurate for a big move — and convexity is the number that says by how much, and in which direction, duration alone gets it wrong. It turns out bonds are always a little bit better than a simple duration estimate predicts, in both directions (yields up or down) — an asymmetry worth understanding precisely, not just memorizing.

Section 02

The math, step by step

Macaulay duration — a weighted-average payment date

Recall from Lesson 1 that a bond's price is the sum of the present values of its individual cash flows. Each cash flow \(CF_t\) (a coupon, or the final coupon-plus-face-value payment at maturity) contributes some share of the bond's total price — specifically, \(PV(CF_t)/P\). Macaulay duration weights each cash flow's timing \(t\) by that share, and sums:

\[ D_{Mac} = \sum_{t=1}^{n} t \cdot \frac{PV(CF_t)}{P} = \frac{1}{P}\sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{t \cdot CF_t}{(1+y)^t} \]
D_MacMacaulay duration — a weighted-average payment time, in years
PV(CF_t)/Pthe weight — what fraction of the bond's total value this cash flow represents
thow far in the future (in years) that cash flow arrives

Every weight \(PV(CF_t)/P\) is a genuine probability-like weight — all the weights are positive and, by construction, sum to exactly 1 (since they partition the bond's total price into shares). \(D_{Mac}\) is therefore a true weighted average, in exactly the same sense as the sample mean from this curriculum's statistics primer is a weighted average of data points — here the "data points" are payment dates, and the "weights" are each payment's share of present value instead of \(1/n\) each.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Macaulay duration is like finding the balance point of a seesaw where each of the bond's payments sits at its own future date, weighted by how big a chunk of the bond's value it represents. A bond that pays you a little bit every year balances closer to today than one that pays you nothing until the very last year — because more of its value shows up early.

Modified duration — turning timing into price sensitivity

Macaulay duration is measured in years — a statement about timing, not yet directly a statement about price sensitivity. Modified duration rescales it by one factor of \((1+y)\) to convert it into exactly that: a direct measure of how much the bond's price moves, in percentage terms, for a small change in yield.

\[ D_{mod} = \frac{D_{Mac}}{1+y} \]
1+yrescales a timing measure into a direct yield-sensitivity measure

This rescaling isn't arbitrary — it falls directly out of differentiating the price formula with respect to \(y\). Take the pricing formula from Lesson 1, \(P = \sum_t CF_t (1+y)^{-t}\), and differentiate term by term:

\[ \frac{dP}{dy} = \sum_{t=1}^{n} -t\, CF_t (1+y)^{-t-1} = -\frac{1}{1+y}\sum_{t=1}^{n} t \cdot \frac{CF_t}{(1+y)^t} = -\frac{1}{1+y}\left(D_{Mac}\cdot P\right) = -D_{mod}\cdot P \]
dP/dythe exact slope of the price-yield curve at the current yield
= −D_mod · Pduration is nothing but that slope, rescaled to be a percentage rather than a dollar figure

Rearranged, \(dP/P = -D_{mod}\,dy\), or, for a small but non-infinitesimal move \(\Delta y\):

\[ \frac{\Delta P}{P} \approx -D_{mod}\,\Delta y \]
−D_mod
The slope
How many percent the price moves per unit of yield change, near the current yield
Δy
The yield move
How far yield actually moves from where duration was measured
ΔP/P
The estimate
The predicted percentage price change — exact only in the limit of an infinitesimal move
This is a Taylor series, not a bond-specific trick. A first-order Taylor expansion of any smooth function \(f(y)\) around a point \(y_0\) is \(f(y_0+\Delta y) \approx f(y_0) + f'(y_0)\Delta y\). Apply that generic formula to the bond pricing function \(P(y)\), and \(f'(y_0) = dP/dy = -D_{mod}\cdot P\) is exactly the derivative computed above. Duration is the first-order Taylor term of the bond pricing function — nothing more exotic than that. This curriculum's Taylor series lesson (foundation mathematics tier) derives the general result; this page is one specific, high-stakes application of it.
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Modified duration answers one question: if yields nudge up or down just a little, roughly what percent does the bond's price move? It's exactly like using a hill's current steepness to guess how much you'll climb or drop after one small step forward — accurate for a small step, but only a guess, not the exact truth.

Why the linear approximation isn't enough — convexity as the second Taylor term

A first-order Taylor term is only exact in the limit of an infinitesimally small \(\Delta y\). For any real, finite yield move, the true function \(P(y)\) deviates from its tangent line by an amount that grows with the square of the distance moved — precisely the curvature Lesson 1's chart showed visually. The second-order Taylor term captures that curvature using the function's second derivative:

\[ \frac{d^2P}{dy^2} = \sum_{t=1}^{n} t(t+1)\, CF_t (1+y)^{-t-2} \]

Convexity is defined as this second derivative, rescaled (analogously to how modified duration rescaled the first derivative) so that it plugs cleanly into a percentage-price-change formula:

\[ C = \frac{1}{P}\frac{d^2P}{dy^2} = \frac{1}{P(1+y)^2}\sum_{t=1}^{n} t(t+1)\cdot\frac{CF_t}{(1+y)^t} \]
Cconvexity — the curvature of the price-yield relationship, rescaled to be usable directly
t(t+1)weights later cash flows even more heavily than duration's plain t does

A full second-order Taylor expansion of \(P(y)\) around the current yield \(y_0\) is \(P(y_0+\Delta y) \approx P(y_0) + P'(y_0)\Delta y + \frac{1}{2}P''(y_0)(\Delta y)^2\). Dividing through by \(P(y_0)\) and substituting \(P'/P=-D_{mod}\) and \(P''/P = C\) gives the fuller approximation this lesson is built around:

\[ \frac{\Delta P}{P} \approx -D_{mod}\,\Delta y + \frac{1}{2}\,C\,(\Delta y)^2 \]
−D_mod Δythe linear (first-order) term — same as before
½C(Δy)²the curvature (second-order) correction — always non-negative for a plain bond

Notice the correction term is always positive, because it's one-half times a positive number \(C\) times a squared quantity \((\Delta y)^2\), which can never be negative — and \(C\) itself is always positive for an ordinary fixed-coupon bond with no embedded options, because every term inside its defining sum is a positive cash flow, a positive discount factor, and a positive \(t(t+1)\) weight. This is the precise, mechanical reason duration alone always underestimates a plain bond's true price after any yield move, whether yields rise or fall: the true price-yield curve always lies on or above its own tangent line (this is exactly what "convex" means geometrically), so the straight-line duration estimate is always a lower bound on the truth, and the convexity term is precisely the missing piece that closes most of that gap.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

If duration is "guess how far you'll go using the hill's steepness right where you're standing," convexity is the correction for the fact that hills curve — the ground itself starts sloping differently the further you walk. Because a bond's price curve bends in a friendly direction, that correction is always a bonus: the bond ends up worth slightly more than the simple straight-line guess predicted, whether yields went up or down.

DV01 / PVBP — the practical dollar version

Trading desks rarely think in percentage sensitivity; they think in dollars per basis point, because that's what position sizing and hedging actually require. DV01 (dollar value of a basis point, also called PVBP, price value of a basis point) is exactly modified duration, restated as a dollar figure for a one basis-point (0.01%) yield move:

\[ DV01 \approx D_{mod}\cdot P \cdot 0.0001 \]
0.0001one basis point, expressed as a decimal (0.01%)

DV01 answers the question a trader actually asks — "if yields move 1bp, how many dollars does this position gain or lose?" — directly, without requiring anyone on the desk to re-derive the percentage math each time. It's the same information as modified duration, just denominated the way a risk book is actually managed.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

DV01 just translates duration into a language traders actually think in: real dollars. Instead of saying "this bond moves about 4.4% for a 1-percentage-point yield move," DV01 says "this bond gains or loses about this many dollars for every tiny 0.01% wiggle in yield" — the same fact, just priced out in cash instead of percent.

Worked example

Duration, convexity, and a 100bp shock — using Lesson 1's exact bond

Same bond as Lesson 1: 5-year, $1,000 face, 4% annual coupon (\(C=\$40\)), currently priced at \(P=\$956.71\) at a 5% YTM (\(y=0.05\)). Its five discounted cash flows (from Lesson 1) were \$38.10, \$36.28, \$34.56, \$32.91, and a final \$814.87 (the last coupon plus face value, \$1,040, discounted five years) — using more precise intermediate figures of \$38.10, \$36.28, \$34.55, \$32.91, \$31.34 (coupon-only) and \$783.53 (face-only) for the final year.

1

Weight each cash flow's timing by its share of price

Multiply each cash flow's present value by its own \(t\), then sum: \(1\times38.10 + 2\times36.28 + 3\times34.56 + 4\times32.91 + 5\times814.87\).

\[ 38.10 + 72.56 + 103.66 + 131.63 + 4074.34 = 4420.29 \]
2

Divide by the price to get Macaulay duration

\[ D_{Mac} = \frac{4420.29}{956.71} \approx 4.62\ \text{years} \]

Sanity check: 4.62 years is comfortably less than the bond's 5-year maturity, exactly as expected for any coupon-paying bond — some value arrives before the final year, which pulls the weighted average forward from 5.00.

3

Convert to modified duration

\[ D_{mod} = \frac{4.62}{1.05} \approx 4.40\ \text{years} \]
4

Compute convexity

Weight each cash flow's present value by \(t(t+1)\) this time — \(2\times38.10+6\times36.28+12\times34.56+20\times32.91+30\times814.87 = 25{,}812.69\) — then divide by \(P\cdot(1+y)^2\):

\[ C = \frac{25{,}812.69}{956.71 \times 1.05^2} = \frac{25{,}812.69}{1054.77} \approx 24.47 \]
5

Estimate the price after a 100bp (1 percentage point) yield rise — duration only

\[ \frac{\Delta P}{P} \approx -4.40 \times 0.01 = -4.40\% \quad\Rightarrow\quad P \approx 956.71 \times (1-0.0440) \approx \$914.61 \]
6

Now add the convexity correction

\[ \frac{\Delta P}{P} \approx -4.40\times0.01 + \tfrac{1}{2}\times24.47\times(0.01)^2 = -0.0440 + 0.00122 = -0.04278 \]
\[ P \approx 956.71\times(1-0.04278) \approx \$915.78 \]
7

Compare both estimates to the exact re-priced value

Plugging \(y=0.06\) directly into Lesson 1's exact pricing formula gives \(P(0.06) = \$915.75\) — the true value, no approximation.

Duration only: $914.61

Off by about \$1.14 (understates the true price) — the straight-line tangent estimate misses the curvature entirely.

Duration + convexity: $915.78

Off by only about \$0.03 — the second-order correction closes nearly the entire gap left by the linear estimate.

Real-world trading scenario

Hedging a bond book with futures, and getting the size wrong without convexity

A fixed-income desk holds a large position in a 30-year Treasury bond — a maturity where both duration and convexity are large — and wants to hedge against a surprise rate move using Treasury futures. The standard approach is a duration-neutral hedge: size the futures position so the portfolio's total DV01 nets to roughly zero, meaning a small parallel shift in yields produces close to no P&L either way.

That hedge works well for the kind of small, everyday yield wiggle it was sized for. It works far less well for a large, sudden shock — say, a surprise 75bp move in a single session, of the kind that can occur around an unexpected central bank decision. Because the desk's actual bond position has meaningfully more convexity than the futures contract used to hedge it (convexity itself varies by instrument and isn't automatically matched just because DV01 is), the portfolio isn't truly flat for a large move — it has a residual, positive convexity exposure that shows up as a P&L gain in either direction once the move is big enough for the second-order term to matter, an effect a duration-only (DV01) hedge is blind to by construction. This is exactly why credit and mortgage-derivatives desks in particular quote and trade convexity explicitly as its own risk factor, not merely as a footnote to duration — a large book that is duration-neutral but convexity-mismatched has real, quantifiable exposure to how big a rate move turns out to be, not just which direction it goes.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

Duration is the primary risk-sizing unit on every fixed-income desk. Positions, portfolios, and even entire fund mandates are routinely quoted and constrained in duration terms ("this fund runs roughly 6 years of duration," "we're currently 0.5 years short of benchmark duration") well before anyone re-prices a single bond off its full cash-flow schedule. DV01 is the trade-level version of the exact same idea, used to size hedges: a trader who is long \$50,000 of DV01 in cash bonds sells futures with a matching \$50,000 DV01 to flatten rate exposure.

Duration matching / immunization is a classic liability-driven-investing technique: a pension fund or insurer with known future cash obligations builds a bond portfolio whose duration matches the duration of those liabilities, so that a parallel shift in rates moves the value of assets and liabilities by roughly the same amount, protecting the fund's net funding position against exactly the first-order risk this lesson quantifies.

Convexity is bought and sold as its own factor, particularly in mortgage-backed securities (where prepayment behavior can make convexity negative — a crucial exception to the "always positive for a plain bond" result derived above, because an embedded prepayment option changes the cash-flow schedule itself as yields move) and in the options market, where a long options position is engineered to have large positive convexity ("long gamma," the options-pricing cousin of this same second-order idea). Desks that are structurally short convexity (many mortgage originators, for instance) actively hedge that exposure rather than simply reporting a DV01 and calling the risk fully described.

Barbell vs. bullet portfolio construction uses convexity explicitly: a "barbell" (combining very short- and very long-maturity bonds) can be built to match the same duration as a "bullet" (a single intermediate maturity) while carrying meaningfully higher convexity, because convexity grows faster with maturity than duration does — a standard relative-value lever fixed-income portfolio managers use even when two portfolios look identical on a duration-only basis.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where this goes wrong in practice

Using duration alone for a large or fast-moving yield shock. The whole point of this lesson's worked example is that the linear approximation's error grows with the square of the yield move — fine for a 5-10bp daily wiggle, increasingly wrong for a 100bp+ shock. Any stress test or scenario analysis covering a large hypothetical rate move needs the convexity term (or, better, full re-pricing) to be taken seriously.

Assuming convexity is always positive. It is, for a plain-vanilla, option-free bond — but mortgage-backed securities and any bond with an embedded call option can have negative convexity, because the issuer's option to call/prepay changes the bond's actual cash-flow schedule as yields move, breaking the fixed-CF assumption this lesson's formulas rely on. Applying this lesson's "always underestimates the price" conclusion to a callable or mortgage instrument without checking for negative convexity first is a real, common mistake.

Treating duration and convexity as accurate for a non-parallel yield curve move. Both measures, as derived here, assume every point on the yield curve shifts by the same \(\Delta y\) (a "parallel shift"). Real yield curve moves usually aren't parallel — short rates and long rates often move by different amounts, sometimes in different directions (a curve steepening or flattening). A portfolio can be perfectly duration-neutral to a parallel shift and still lose money on a steepening or flattening move; this is exactly why Lesson 3's bootstrapped curve, and key-rate ("partial") durations built on it, exist as a finer-grained tool.

Forgetting duration and convexity are themselves yield-dependent. Both are computed at a specific current yield and change as yield moves — the interactive chart above makes this visible directly: drag the tangent point and both \(D_{mod}\) and \(C\) recompute to different values. A duration figure quoted today is a local snapshot, not a fixed property of the bond.

Section 04

Key Takeaways

✓Macaulay duration is a payment-weighted average time. Each cash flow's weight is its share of the bond's present value — a genuine weighted average, in years.
✓Modified duration IS the first-order Taylor term of price w.r.t. yield. \(D_{mod}=D_{Mac}/(1+y)\) and \(\Delta P/P \approx -D_{mod}\Delta y\) — nothing more exotic than a tangent-line approximation.
✓Convexity is the second-order term, and it's always positive for a plain bond. The true price-yield curve always sits on or above its own tangent line — duration alone always underestimates price, in either direction of yield move.
✓Duration + convexity beats duration alone, by a lot, for a real-sized shock. The worked example's 100bp move: duration-only missed by \$1.14; adding convexity closed the gap to \$0.03.
✓DV01/PVBP is duration in dollars-per-basis-point. Same information, denominated the way trading desks actually size hedges.
⚠Both measures assume a parallel yield-curve shift. Real curve moves are rarely perfectly parallel — a duration-neutral book can still lose money on a steepening or flattening.
⚠Convexity can be negative for callable/mortgage instruments. An embedded prepayment or call option changes the bond's cash-flow schedule as yields move, which can flip the "always positive" result this lesson derives for a plain bond.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. Why is a coupon-paying bond's Macaulay duration always less than its time to maturity?
Because Macaulay duration is a weighted average of every cash flow's timing, weighted by each cash flow's share of present value, and a coupon-paying bond has cash flows arriving before maturity (the coupons themselves) in addition to the final maturity payment. Those earlier cash flows pull the weighted average forward from the final maturity date. Only a zero-coupon bond, with a single cash flow at maturity and 100% of the weight on that one date, has Macaulay duration exactly equal to its maturity.
Q2. In what precise sense is duration "the first-order Taylor term" of the bond pricing function?
A first-order Taylor expansion of any function \(f(y)\) around \(y_0\) is \(f(y_0+\Delta y)\approx f(y_0)+f'(y_0)\Delta y\). Differentiating the bond pricing function \(P(y) = \sum_t CF_t(1+y)^{-t}\) term by term gives \(dP/dy = -D_{mod}\cdot P\) exactly — so the linear price-sensitivity formula \(\Delta P/P \approx -D_{mod}\Delta y\) is literally the generic first-order Taylor formula applied to this one specific function, with \(-D_{mod}\cdot P\) playing the role of \(f'(y_0)\). There is no separate "duration mathematics" — it is calculus's linear approximation, applied to price-as-a-function-of-yield.
Q3. Why does duration alone always underestimate a plain bond's true price after a yield move, regardless of whether yields rose or fell?
Because the full approximation is \(\Delta P/P \approx -D_{mod}\Delta y + \frac{1}{2}C(\Delta y)^2\), and the correction term \(\frac12 C(\Delta y)^2\) is always non-negative — \(C\) is positive for a plain bond, and \((\Delta y)^2\) can never be negative regardless of which direction yield moved. Geometrically, this is exactly what it means for the price-yield curve to be convex: it always lies on or above its own tangent line at every point, so the tangent-line (duration-only) estimate is always a lower bound on the true price, whether the yield shock is up or down.
Q4. What does it mean for a mortgage-backed security to have negative convexity, and why does it break the "always positive" result derived in this lesson?
This lesson's convexity formula assumes the bond's cash flows \(CF_t\) are fixed, contractual amounts that don't change as yield moves — true for a plain bond, false for a mortgage security, where falling yields trigger more homeowner prepayment/refinancing, which shortens the actual expected cash-flow schedule right when the bond would otherwise be gaining the most value. That schedule-shortening effect works against the bondholder exactly when it matters most, which can make the security's actual price-yield curve bend the "wrong" way (concave rather than convex) over some yield range — negative convexity. The derivation in this lesson assumes fixed \(CF_t\) throughout, so it simply doesn't apply once the cash flows themselves are yield-dependent.
Q5. A portfolio is built to be exactly duration-neutral (net DV01 ≈ 0) to a parallel yield curve shift. Is it necessarily protected against all realistic yield curve moves? Why or why not?
No. Duration-neutrality, as derived in this lesson, specifically protects against a parallel shift — every maturity's yield moving by the same \(\Delta y\). Real yield curve moves are frequently non-parallel (a steepening, where long rates move more than short rates, or a flattening, the reverse), and a portfolio can be perfectly duration-neutral to a parallel shift while still having real, unhedged exposure to a steepening or flattening, since duration is a single number summarizing sensitivity to one uniform shift, not to the shape of the curve. Finer tools — key-rate or "partial" durations, built on the bootstrapped zero curve from Lesson 3 — are needed to hedge non-parallel curve risk.
