The Fed's Corridor: IORB, the SRF and the Discount Window

Every night trillions of dollars of cash are lent against Treasuries. The Fed doesn't set that price directly. It builds a floor under it and a ceiling over it, then lets the market find its level in between. This lesson covers the corridor: what each wall is, who leans on it, and what happened the two times it was tested.

CONCEPT — explained here; desk verdicts quoted where tested ~18 min read Prereqs: Collateral, Margin & Repo Markets
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide. It covers the same ground in short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • Overnight funding is cash lent until tomorrow, mostly through repo against Treasuries. SOFR is the market's measured price for it.
  • The floor: banks can earn IORB (interest on reserve balances) on cash parked at the Fed, so they won't lend much below it. Money market funds can't hold reserves; their floor is the ON RRP (overnight reverse repo facility), which sits at or near the bottom of the target range.
  • The ceiling: the Standing Repo Facility (SRF), made permanent in July 2021, lends cash against Treasuries at a fixed minimum rate. Nobody eligible should pay much more than that in the market. The discount window lends to banks at the same top-of-range rate, but borrowing there carries a stigma.
  • The mid-month chain: on tax dates, cash leaves money market funds, funds pull cash out of repo, cash gets scarcer, and SOFR rises. It's a calendar effect, not a crisis.
  • Two real tests: in September 2019 there was no standing ceiling, and SOFR spiked far above the target range until the Fed restarted repo operations. In March 2023, after SVB failed, discount-window borrowing hit a record and the Fed launched a new facility, the BTFP.
  • The desk's verdict: SOFR above the floor and SRF usage are not stress signals (funding-stress, NULL). A drawn SRF was followed by a calmer tape, −0.561 ATR. That was the opposite of the desk's own written prior, and it matches the "arbitrage, not collapse" reading.
Section 01

Why this matters

Collateral, Margin & Repo Markets covers how one repo works: a sale and a buy-back, with the price difference as the interest. Central Bank Policy covers why the Fed moves its target rate. This lesson connects the two. Once the Fed announces a target range for the federal funds rate, something has to make overnight money actually trade inside that range. The Fed doesn't do it by dealing in the market all day. It sets administered rates, offers it will always honour, and arbitrage pushes market rates between them.

It matters for reading a macro board because SOFR, the facility take-up numbers and the discount-window balance all get printed as if they were stress gauges. Most of the time they aren't. They move with the calendar: month-end balance-sheet dressing, mid-month tax dates, Treasury settlement days. Knowing which wall a rate is pressing against, and why, separates "the plumbing is doing its job" from "something is breaking." This desk tested that distinction on its own data, and the result went against its own prior.

💡
Plain-English intuition

A price kept in a lane by two standing offers

Picture a market for overnight cash. The Fed stands at the bottom and says: "Anyone with spare cash can leave it with me and earn this rate." Nobody sensible will then lend to anyone else for less, which makes that rate the floor. The Fed also stands at the top and says: "Anyone who needs cash and has good collateral can borrow from me at this rate." Nobody sensible will then pay more to anyone else, which makes that rate the ceiling.

Between the two, the market sets the price. When cash is plentiful it sits near the floor. When cash is scarce it climbs toward the ceiling, and when it gets there borrowers stop paying more and go to the Fed instead. The Fed's offers do the steering, and the market does the trading.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine a lemonade stand where a grown-up promises: "I'll always buy lemonade for 40 cents, and I'll always sell it for 50 cents." Now no kid on the street will ever sell lemonade for 30 cents, because the grown-up pays more. And no kid will ever pay 70 cents, because the grown-up sells for less. So the price on the street always stays between 40 and 50 cents. The Fed is that grown-up, and the lemonade is overnight cash.

Section 02

Overnight funding, and who is in the room

Overnight funding means borrowing cash today and paying it back tomorrow. Most of it is done as repo: the borrower hands over Treasuries as collateral, gets cash, and buys the Treasuries back the next day for slightly more. A bank, a securities dealer or a fund manages its daily cash this way. Holding a large bond inventory, meeting a margin call or parking spare cash safely overnight all run through repo.

SOFR, the Secured Overnight Financing Rate, is published each morning by the New York Fed. It is computed from the previous day's actual overnight Treasury repo transactions, so it is a measured price rather than a survey. When people say "funding got tight," they usually mean SOFR rose relative to the Fed's administered rates.

🏦

Banks

Hold reserves, cash in an account at the Fed, and earn IORB on them. A bank lends into repo only when the market pays more than IORB after balance-sheet costs. Banks are the only ones who can use the discount window.

📑

Primary dealers

The securities firms that trade directly with the New York Fed and bid at Treasury auctions. They hold large bond inventories and fund them overnight in repo, so they are the big cash borrowers and middlemen between cash lenders and leveraged investors. They have access to the SRF.

💵

Money market funds

The big cash lenders. They hold investors' cash and must keep it safe and liquid, so they lend it overnight in repo. They can't hold reserves and can't use the discount window, but they can use the Fed's ON RRP.

🏛️

The Treasury's account

The US Treasury keeps its cash in the Treasury General Account (TGA) at the Fed. When taxes are paid or new debt settles, money moves out of private hands into the TGA. Reserves and market cash shrink by that amount. The TGA isn't a trader, but its swings drain and refill the system.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Think of a big overnight swap meet. The money funds show up with piles of cash and want a safe place to leave it until morning. The dealers show up with piles of government bonds and need cash to keep holding them. They swap for one night. Banks can join in when it's worth their while. And the government's own piggy bank sometimes swallows a big pile of cash on tax day, which leaves less cash on the tables for everyone else.

The corridor in one line

Put the administered rates in order and you have the corridor. With every rate quoted annualised, on an ordinary day:

\[ r_{\text{ON RRP}} \;\le\; \text{SOFR} \;\lesssim\; r_{\text{SRF}} \]
r_ON RRPthe floor for non-banks: what a money fund earns by lending to the Fed instead
SOFRthe market's measured overnight repo rate, set by supply and demand for cash
r_SRFthe ceiling: the minimum bid rate at which the Fed lends cash against Treasuries

IORB sits inside the band, usually a little below the top. The \(\lesssim\) is deliberate. The ceiling is soft: not every borrower has access to the SRF, and some would rather pay a few basis points over the rate than be seen using a Fed facility. SOFR can print a little above the SRF rate. Without any standing ceiling, as in 2019, it can print far above.

Each wall comes from an arbitrage. A bank compares two uses for a dollar of reserves:

\[ \text{Lend in repo} \iff \text{SOFR} - c_{\text{bs}} > \text{IORB} \]
SOFRwhat the bank earns lending that dollar overnight in repo
c_bsthe balance-sheet cost: capital and leverage rules make every dollar of assets cost something
IORBwhat the bank earns by doing nothing and leaving the dollar at the Fed

A dealer that needs cash makes the mirror-image comparison at the top:

\[ \text{Borrow from the SRF} \iff \text{market repo rate} > r_{\text{SRF}} \]
market repo ratewhat private cash lenders are charging tonight
r_SRFwhat the Fed charges, against the same Treasury collateral
SOFRSecured Overnight Financing Rate. Built from actual overnight Treasury repo trades and published each business day by the New York Fed.
IORBInterest on reserve balances. The rate the Fed pays banks on cash held in their Fed accounts.
\(r_{\text{ON RRP}}\)The overnight reverse repo rate. Eligible non-banks, mainly money market funds, can lend cash to the Fed at this rate against Treasuries. It is set at or near the bottom of the target range (at the bottom since late 2024).
\(r_{\text{SRF}}\)The Standing Repo Facility's minimum bid rate. The Fed lends cash against Treasuries and agency securities at this rate. It sits at the top of the target range.
\(c_{\text{bs}}\)Balance-sheet cost. The implicit charge a bank pays for growing its balance sheet under capital and leverage rules. It explains why repo can trade above IORB without banks instantly arbitraging it away.
bpBasis point: one hundredth of a percentage point. 0.05% = 5bp.
TGATreasury General Account. The government's cash account at the Fed. When it rises, reserves fall by the same amount.
Section 03

The floor: IORB for banks, ON RRP for everyone else

Since 2008 the Fed has run with ample reserves: banks hold far more cash at the Fed than they need for payments. In that system the main tool is the rate the Fed pays on those reserves. If the Fed pays banks IORB risk-free, a bank has little reason to lend overnight for less. Raise IORB and every overnight rate moves up with it. That is how a rate decision reaches the market the same afternoon.

But many cash lenders aren't banks. Money market funds, some government-sponsored enterprises and others hold huge cash balances and cannot earn IORB, because they have no reserve account. Without help they could end up lending below IORB, which would leak the floor. The ON RRP facility plugs that gap: eligible non-banks can lend cash to the Fed overnight, against Treasury collateral, at a fixed rate set at or near the bottom of the target range (exactly at the bottom since late 2024). A money fund then has no reason to lend to a dealer for less than the Fed pays.

IORB: the floor for banks

Paid on reserves. Only banks hold reserves. It is usually set inside the target range, a little below the top, so it pulls the fed funds rate into the range.

ON RRP: the floor for non-banks

Open to money market funds and other eligible counterparties. It is set at or near the bottom of the range. When cash is abundant, heavy ON RRP usage means funds have more cash than private borrowers want.

In practice ON RRP usage is a good gauge of excess cash. When cash is plentiful, funds park large amounts there. When cash gets scarcer, private repo starts paying more than the ON RRP rate, funds move their cash into the market, and ON RRP balances shrink. A falling ON RRP balance usually means the system is moving from abundant cash toward something tighter.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Banks have a special piggy bank at the Fed that pays them interest. So why would they lend their money to anyone else for less? Money funds don't get that special piggy bank, so the Fed gives them a slightly smaller one, the ON RRP. Now nobody has a reason to lend their cash out for peanuts.

Section 04

The ceiling: the Standing Repo Facility, and the discount window

A floor alone doesn't stop rates going up. If, on one particular day, cash lenders are short of cash and borrowers badly need it, the overnight rate can climb well above the target range. For a decade after 2008 the Fed had no standing tool to cap that. It could step in with ad hoc repo operations, but they weren't guaranteed in advance.

That changed in July 2021, when the FOMC set up the Standing Repo Facility as a permanent part of its framework. Through the SRF the Fed stands ready every day to lend cash overnight against Treasuries and agency securities at a minimum bid rate set at the top of the target range. Primary dealers have access, and eligible banks can sign up. If market repo would cost more than the SRF rate, a dealer can borrow from the Fed instead, and the market rate gets capped near that level.

The discount window is much older. It lets banks (only banks) borrow directly from their regional Reserve Bank against a wide range of collateral, including loans and securities a dealer couldn't easily repo. Its main programme, primary credit, has in recent years been priced at the top of the target range, the same level as the SRF. So why does it matter which one is used?

Standing Repo Facility

Repo form, against Treasuries and agency debt. Built to be used routinely and to cap repo rates day to day. Using it is meant to signal nothing more than "this was the cheapest cash tonight."

Discount window

A loan to a bank, against broad collateral. A last-resort source of liquidity. Borrowing there has long carried a stigma: banks fear that if it becomes known, markets will read it as "this bank can't raise cash anywhere else."

Stigma is the reason the distinction matters. A backstop that everyone is afraid to touch doesn't work as a ceiling, because borrowers will pay well over its rate in the market to avoid being seen there. The SRF was designed to be the ordinary, unembarrassing ceiling, while the discount window stays the emergency door. That gives the clearest practical reading of the two:

Arbitrage, not collapse. A dealer that uses the SRF on a day when market repo costs more than the SRF rate is doing the obvious thing: borrowing where it's cheaper. Rising SRF usage on its own is evidence that the ceiling is being used as designed. Heavy, sustained discount-window borrowing, especially by banks under pressure, is a different and more serious signal.
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

There are two doors to borrow money from the Fed. One is the normal front door (the SRF), and using it just means you found the cheapest deal in town. The other is the emergency door (the discount window). It works fine, but everyone worries that people who use it must be in trouble, so they avoid it even when they need it.

The market curve is a stylised supply-and-demand shape, not a fitted model: almost flat while cash is plentiful, then increasingly steep as the last spare cash is used up. With the SRF on, the curve is capped a few basis points above the ceiling, because not every borrower can or will use the facility. With it off, nothing stops the climb. That is the shape of September 2019.

The mid-month chain: why SOFR lifts around the 15th

Here is how a perfectly ordinary calendar event becomes a rate move, with each actor doing the one thing it is forced to do:

Mid-month tax dateCorporate and individual tax payments fall due; Treasury coupon settlement often lands nearby
→
InvestorsPull cash out of money market funds to pay the bill
→
Money market fundsMust meet redemptions, so lend less in repo that night
→
Treasury's accountThe payments land in the TGA at the Fed, draining reserves and market cash
→
DealersStill need to fund the same bond inventory, so they bid up for scarcer cash
→
OutcomeSOFR lifts toward the ceiling for a day or two, then settles

Nothing in that chain is a failure. Every link is someone meeting a known obligation on a known date. The rate moves because cash is briefly in the wrong place: in the Treasury's account instead of the repo market. The month-end version has a different cause, banks shrinking their balance sheets for reporting dates, but the same character: predictable, temporary, and calendar-driven.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

On tax day, lots of people take money out of their savings to pay the government. The savings funds then have less to lend that night, while the bond dealers still need the same amount. Fewer lenders and the same number of borrowers means the price of borrowing goes up for a day. Then the money flows back and everything calms down.

🧮
Worked example

Both arbitrages, in dollars, on the illustrative corridor

Using the chart's illustrative settings (ON RRP 4.25%, IORB 4.40%, SRF 4.50%) and the actual/360 convention used in US repo:

1

The dealer at the ceiling

On a tight night, private repo is quoted at 4.62%. A dealer needs $1 billion overnight. Borrowing from the SRF at 4.50% instead saves 12bp:

\[ 1{,}000{,}000{,}000 \times 0.0012 \times \tfrac{1}{360} = \$3{,}333.33 \text{ saved, for one night} \]

That is the whole motive. Nothing about the dealer's health changed; the Fed was simply the cheaper lender that night.

2

The bank inside the corridor

SOFR is 4.45%, 5bp over IORB. A bank with $500 million of spare reserves could lend them in repo instead of leaving them at the Fed:

\[ 500{,}000{,}000 \times 0.0005 \times \tfrac{1}{360} = \$694.44 \text{ extra per night, before balance-sheet cost} \]

If the bank's balance-sheet cost \(c_{\text{bs}}\) is worth more than 5bp to it, it stays put. This is why SOFR can sit a little above IORB without banks instantly closing the gap.

3

The money fund at the floor

On an abundant-cash night a dealer offers a money fund 4.22% for overnight cash. The ON RRP pays 4.25%. The fund lends to the Fed instead, so the dealer must offer at least the ON RRP rate to get the cash. That is the floor at work.

🎯
Real-world trading scenario

SOFR prints 8bp above IORB on the 16th of the month

A macro trader sees the morning SOFR print come in well above IORB, and the SRF shows some take-up. The headline writes itself: "funding stress." Before reacting, the trader checks the calendar. It is the 16th, a corporate tax date, and a Treasury coupon settlement landed the day before. That is the mid-month chain above, exactly. The trader also notes that the facility did its job: the rate was capped near the SRF rate rather than running away.

The honest read is "calendar tightness, ceiling working," not a risk-off signal. What would change the read: the rate staying elevated for days after the tax date; SOFR blowing well past the SRF rate; or large and rising discount-window borrowing outside a quarter-end. Even then, this desk's own tests (Section 06) say to be careful about acting on it.

Section 05

It actually happened: two tests of the walls

It actually happened

September 2019: the repo spike, with no ceiling in place

In mid-September 2019, two drains on cash landed together: quarterly corporate tax payments fell due on the 16th, and a large settlement of newly issued Treasury securities went through the same day. Both moved cash from private hands into the Treasury's account at the Fed. Reserves had already been shrinking for some time as the Fed ran down its balance sheet.

Cash lenders pulled back, and overnight repo rates jumped. On September 17, SOFR printed 5.25% against a target range of 2.00–2.25%, and the effective fed funds rate briefly moved above the top of its range. There was no standing facility to cap the move. The New York Fed restarted overnight repo operations on September 17 for the first time in about a decade, and kept offering them in the weeks that followed. In October the Fed also began buying Treasury bills to rebuild reserves.

The lesson the Fed drew is visible in the toggle on the chart above. A ceiling you have to improvise after the spike is worse than one that is always there. The Standing Repo Facility, made permanent in July 2021, is that standing ceiling.

It actually happened

March 2023: SVB, the discount window, and the BTFP

Silicon Valley Bank failed on March 10, 2023, after a run by uninsured depositors. Like many banks, it held large amounts of Treasuries and agency securities bought when rates were low. Once rates rose, those bonds were worth well below face value (the inverse price-yield relationship from Bond Pricing & Yield to Maturity). Selling them to meet withdrawals locked in the loss.

On March 12 the Fed announced the Bank Term Funding Program (BTFP). It offered loans of up to one year to banks against Treasuries and agency securities valued at par, not at their lower market value. That removed the need to sell at a loss to raise cash. At the same time banks turned to the discount window in size: the Fed's weekly figure for primary credit reached about $152.9 billion in the week of March 15, a record, above the previous peak of about $110.7 billion in October 2008. The BTFP stopped making new loans in March 2024.

This was the emergency door being used for its real purpose: a bank-specific run, not a calendar date. The contrast with an ordinary SRF day is the whole point of this lesson. One is a liquidity backstop catching real distress; the other is a price cap doing routine work.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

In 2019 there was no "always-open" borrowing door, so when cash ran short on tax day, the price of borrowing shot up until the Fed rushed to open one. In 2023 a bank got into real trouble because people pulled their money out all at once, and the Fed opened a special door so banks wouldn't have to sell their bonds at a loss. The first was a traffic jam; the second was a real accident.

Section 06

What this desk found when it tested the claim

The folk claim is simple: SOFR above the Fed's floor, or the Standing Repo Facility being drawn, is a funding-stress warning, so expect wider, rougher markets next. This desk pre-registered that test and ran it on its own data (2,123 SOFR sessions, 2018-04 to 2026-09, next-five-session range against an ATR-quintile-matched control).

Desk test: nothing found funding-stress: SOFR above the floor is the calendar, not stress. With month-end excluded, headline SOFR ≥ 3bp over the floor left just 15 episodes, below the pre-registered floor of 20, so untestable. 12 of those 15 fall on the 14th–18th of a month, the mid-month tax and settlement dates in the chain above. Removing month-end didn't remove the calendar; it revealed a different one. Direction was null on all three instruments.
Desk test: nothing found funding-stress: a drawn SRF was followed by a CALMER tape, not a wider one. 21 episodes. SPX500 next-5-session range −0.561 ATR [−0.984, −0.181] versus control, negative in both halves (−0.928 / −0.228). EUR/USD −0.483 [−1.150, +0.119] and USD/JPY +0.417 [−0.066, +0.963] are both null. And the SRF is drawn on 667 of 2,123 sessions (31%), which makes "the backstop was used" routine plumbing rather than a stress marker.
Desk data: base rate repo-stress-range: the 99th-percentile SOFR spike is month-end plumbing. 26 episodes 2018–2026. Next-five-session range vs matched control: SPX500 +0.14 ATR [−0.54, +0.98], EUR/USD +0.13 [−0.37, +0.65], USD/JPY +0.08 [−0.76, +0.93], and the dollar moved less than usual (0.45% vs 0.59%). The setup fires on a quarter of all sessions since 2024. Read it as month-end tightness unless the backstops are in use.
The framing beat the desk's own prior. The pre-registered expectation was that a drawn SRF precedes wider ranges: the instinctive "the backstop was used, so something is wrong" reading. The data went the other way. The "arbitrage, not collapse" reading in Section 04 predicts exactly what came back: SRF days are mostly ordinary days on which the Fed was the cheapest lender. Next time a plumbing claim gets a prior assigned by instinct, ask first which wall is being leaned on, and why.

Note the step that the result does not license. The calmer tape after SRF use is reported as measured, not as evidence that the backstop works. With usage this routine, SRF days are mostly ordinary days, and the sample starts in 2018 and contains no crisis. The question still open is whether funding stress matters when the facility is not there to cap it, which is September 2019's world, not today's. And these are range findings: they say nothing about which way markets move.

Section 07

How practitioners actually use this

📍

Read SOFR relative to the walls

The level of SOFR means little alone. Its spread to IORB, to the ON RRP rate and to the SRF rate says which wall is binding and how much room is left.

🗓️

Check the calendar first

Month-ends, quarter-ends, mid-month tax dates and Treasury settlement days explain most SOFR jumps. A spike on one of those days is the null hypothesis, not a finding.

📉

Watch the ON RRP balance drain

A shrinking ON RRP balance means spare cash is moving into private markets. Once it is near empty, further drains such as a TGA rebuild or balance-sheet runoff come out of bank reserves directly.

🚪

Treat the two doors differently

SRF take-up on a tight day is a price cap working. Large, rising discount-window borrowing away from a quarter-end is the signal worth a second look, and even then a level, not a "was it used" flag.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where the corridor gets misread

  • Reading SRF usage as an alarm. It is drawn on about a third of sessions. On most of those days it's simply the cheapest cash. The desk's test found calmer, not rougher, markets afterwards.
  • Forgetting the calendar. A SOFR jump on the 15th or the last business day of a quarter is almost always tax, settlement or balance-sheet dressing.
  • Treating the ceiling as hard. SOFR can print a few basis points above the SRF rate because not every borrower has access, and some avoid any Fed facility. "Capped near" is more accurate than "capped at."
  • Lumping the two doors together. The SRF and the discount window may charge the same rate, but they serve different users, take different collateral and carry very different signalling weight.
  • Over-reading the null. "Calmer after SRF use" does not prove the backstop works in a crisis. The sample has no crisis in it.
Section 08

Key Takeaways

✓The Fed steers overnight rates with standing offers, not daily trading. A floor (IORB for banks, ON RRP for money funds) and a ceiling (the SRF, permanent since July 2021) keep SOFR in a lane.
✓Each wall is an arbitrage. No one lends below what the Fed pays them; no one with access borrows above what the Fed charges.
✓The mid-month chain is calendar, not crisis. Tax date → money fund withdrawals → funds pull cash from repo → cash scarcer → SOFR lifts.
✓Two doors, two meanings. The SRF is the routine price cap; the discount window is the stigmatised emergency door, used in size in March 2023 alongside the new BTFP.
✗SOFR above the floor is not a stress signal on this desk's data (funding-stress, NULL). 12 of 15 non-month-end episodes land on the 14th–18th of a month.
✗A drawn SRF did not precede a wider tape; the desk's prior lost. SPX500 −0.561 ATR [−0.984, −0.181] (calmer), with the SRF drawn on 667 of 2,123 sessions (31%). Reported as measured, not as proof the backstop works.
⚠The ceiling is soft, and the sample has no crisis. September 2019's spike happened without a standing ceiling. Whether stress matters when no facility caps it is still untested here.
Section 09

Self-test

Q1. Why do money market funds need the ON RRP when banks already have IORB?
Money market funds can't hold reserves at the Fed, so they can't earn IORB. Without another option they might lend cash below IORB, and overnight rates could slip under the Fed's intended floor. The ON RRP lets them lend to the Fed at a fixed rate at or near the bottom of the target range, so no fund needs to lend privately for less.
Q2. The SRF and the discount window charge the same top-of-range rate. Why does it matter which one a borrower uses?
They serve different users and signal different things. The SRF is a repo facility against Treasuries and agency debt, open to primary dealers and eligible banks, and built for routine use as a price cap. The discount window lends only to banks, against broader collateral, and has long carried a stigma: using it can be read as "this bank can't raise cash anywhere else." A ceiling that people are afraid to touch doesn't cap rates, which is why a routine, unembarrassing facility was built.
Q3. Walk the mid-month chain from a tax date to a higher SOFR, naming what each actor is forced to do.
Taxes fall due → investors withdraw from money market funds to pay them → the funds must meet those redemptions, so they lend less cash in repo that night → the payments land in the Treasury's account at the Fed, draining market cash → dealers still need to fund the same bond inventory, so they bid more for scarcer cash → SOFR rises for a day or two, then settles once the cash flows back.
Q4. What was different about September 2019 compared with a tight day today?
In 2019 there was no standing ceiling. When tax payments and Treasury settlement drained cash on the same day, nothing capped the overnight rate, and SOFR printed 5.25% against a 2.00–2.25% target range until the New York Fed restarted repo operations. Today the Standing Repo Facility is always available at the top of the range, so on a similar day dealers would borrow there instead and the market rate would be capped near the SRF rate.
Q5. The desk found SPX500 ranges were narrower (−0.561 ATR) after SRF draws. Why can't you conclude "the backstop works"?
Because the SRF is drawn on 31% of sessions, so SRF days are mostly ordinary days, and the 2018–2026 sample contains no crisis. The comparison can't show what would have happened without the facility. The result does refute the original prior (that SRF use warns of wider markets), and it fits the "arbitrage, not collapse" reading. But it is a range finding with no counterfactual, not proof the backstop prevents stress.
