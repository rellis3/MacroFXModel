Four Numbers, One Whole Yield Curve

Bootstrapping strips an exact zero rate out of every bond you feed it — and knows nothing at all about the maturities in between. Nelson-Siegel and Svensson throw that exactness away on purpose: pick a smooth mathematical shape flexible enough to describe how real yield curves actually look, and fit a handful of parameters to it instead. Four numbers (or six) replace a jagged connect-the-dots line with one curve that a central bank can publish, extrapolate, and forecast — every single trading day.

Section 1

Exact isn't the same as well-behaved

Bootstrapping reprices every input bond perfectly. That's exactly the problem in between them.

Same bonds, two ways of connecting them
The black dots are the same five observed bonds in both curves. The red path connects them exactly, one segment at a time — and overshoots, dips, and reverses direction where two input bonds happen to be far apart or one quote is noisy. The green path never can — it's one continuous function, not a sequence of straight-line hops.

This is a direct continuation of the prior lesson's bootstrapping method: strip a zero rate out of each bond, one maturity at a time, and connect the results. It's exact everywhere it has data, and it knows nothing beyond that data — the shape between two input maturities is only as sensible as whatever interpolation rule glues them together, and the shape past the last input bond is close to a guess.

Why bootstrapping still gets taught first

Bootstrapping is the right tool when you need an exact price for a specific bond right now — it reprices its own inputs by construction, which a parametric fit does not. Parametric curves solve a different problem: a stable, storable, forecastable description of the whole curve's shape, at the cost of a small, expected residual against every individual bond.

Section 2

One smooth shape, four knobs

Nelson-Siegel (1987): describe an entire curve's level, slope and hump with four parameters instead of one rate per maturity.

📏

β₀ — Level

The long-run yield the curve settles toward as maturity → ∞. Should be an economically sensible rate on its own.

📉

β₁ — Slope

Short end minus the long-run level. Its sign alone flips the curve between upward-sloping and inverted.

🐫

β₂ — Curvature

The size and direction of one hump (or dip) in the belly of the curve — zero at both ends, not in between.

⏳

λ — Decay

Sets where along the maturity axis that hump sits, and how fast the short end fades into the long-run level.

Nearly every real yield curve, on nearly every day, is one of a small number of shapes: flat, tilted up, tilted down, or one of those with a bump or dip somewhere in the belly. Four dials is enough to draw all of them — and turning those dials can never accidentally produce a jagged kink, because the underlying function is smooth everywhere by construction.
Section 3

Inside the formula — three pieces stacked together

Let x = τ/λ. The whole curve is a baseline, plus a short-end piece that fades out, plus a hump piece that's zero at both ends.

y(τ) = β₀ + β₁·L(τ) + β₂·(L(τ) − e⁻ˣ)
β₀
+
β₁ · L(τ)
+
β₂ · (L(τ) − e⁻ˣ)
Baseline — never moves with τ. Slope term — starts at 1 (τ=0), decays smoothly to 0 as τ grows. Curvature term — exactly 0 at both τ=0 and τ→∞, but bulges away from zero in between. Where L(τ) = (1 − e⁻ˣ) / x.

Nothing here is piecewise — every one of these three pieces is a single smooth exponential expression evaluated at whatever τ you ask for. That's the entire mechanism behind "cannot kink": there is no seam anywhere along the maturity axis for a kink to live at.

Section 4

Read β₀ and β₁ straight off the two extremes

Evaluate y(τ) at τ→0 and τ→∞ — the loadings collapse to plain numbers.

τ → 0 — the instantaneous short rate

As x→0, L(τ)→1 and the curvature loading L(τ)−e⁻ˣ→1−1=0. The formula collapses to y(0) = β₀ + β₁. This is exactly why β₁ is called the slope: a negative β₁ pulls the short end below β₀ (normal, upward-sloping); a positive β₁ pushes it above (inverted).

τ → ∞ — the long-run level

As x→∞, both L(τ)→0 and the curvature loading →0−0=0. Every term but the baseline vanishes: y(∞) = β₀. β₀ is exactly the level the curve is converging toward — and it should look like a plausible long-run rate on its own, or the fit has gone wrong somewhere.

Where this needs qualifying

Real curves don't extend to infinite maturity — "τ→∞" is a mathematical limit, not a tradable point. In practice β₀ is best read as "the rate the curve is heading toward well beyond the longest bonds actually observed," which is exactly why a plausible β₀ matters: nothing in the data directly pins it down past the last input maturity.

Section 5

Turn one knob at a time

Every other parameter held fixed — what does changing just this one do to the curve's shape?

Try it
Raising β₀ lifts the whole far end of the curve — the entire curve rides up or down toward wherever β₀ says it should eventually settle. It barely touches the very short end, which is governed by β₀+β₁, not β₀ alone.
Both curves share the exact same β₀ and end up at the exact same long-run level — only the sign of β₁ decides whether the short end starts below it (green, the historically normal case) or above it (red, an inversion, often read as a recession signal).
With β₂ at zero, the curve is just the β₀/β₁ blend between the two ends — no bulge. A nonzero β₂ bows the belly away from that straight blend: positive lifts it into a hump, negative pulls it into a dip. The two endpoints, y(0) and y(∞), don't move at all — the curvature loading is exactly zero there.
λ doesn't change the hump's size (β₂ does that) — it slides the hump's peak along the maturity axis. A small λ pulls the hump toward short maturities; a large λ pushes it out toward long ones. The same λ also sets how fast the β₁ slope term fades away.

Section 9

When one hump isn't enough

Plain Nelson-Siegel's curvature term can only ever produce exactly one hump — or one dip. No amount of tuning β₂ or λ changes that.

Svensson (1994) fixes this with one more term, structurally identical to Nelson-Siegel's curvature term but built on its own separate decay parameter λ₂:

β₀ + β₁L₁(τ) + β₂(L₁(τ) − e⁻ˣ¹)
+
β₃(L₂(τ) − e⁻ˣ²)
β₃

A second, independent hump

Size and direction of a curvature term separate from β₂ — its own bump, its own maturity.

λ₂

Where the second hump sits

A second decay parameter, independent of λ₁ — the two humps can sit at genuinely different maturities.

Six parameters (β₀, β₁, β₂, β₃, λ₁, λ₂) instead of four — best read as "Nelson-Siegel, twice, sharing one level and one slope." This is the extra flexibility central banks like the ECB and the U.S. Federal Reserve actually use in the curves they publish daily.

Section 10

Fitting is optimization, not algebra

λ sits inside an exponential and a division — that rules out ordinary linear regression.

Fixed λ — linear

For a known, fixed λ, y(τ) is just a weighted sum of fixed loading functions in β₀, β₁, β₂ (and β₃) — ordinary linear regression solves that instantly, with a closed-form answer.

λ also unknown — nonlinear

λ has to be estimated from the data too, and it sits inside e⁻ᵗ/λ and τ/λ — nonlinear in λ, no closed form. Solved iteratively: minimize Σ(y_obs − y(τ; β,λ))² until it stops improving.

The genuinely hard part

Because the objective is nonlinear in λ (doubly so for Svensson's λ₁,λ₂ pair), the optimizer can converge to different local optima depending on where it starts. When λ₁ and λ₂ end up close together, the two curvature terms become nearly collinear — the fit can no longer tell the two humps apart reliably. Practitioners bound or separate the two decay parameters, or grid-search several starting values and keep the best-converged fit — a single optimizer run from one arbitrary guess isn't enough to trust.

A second trap sits right next to this one: bolting on more curvature terms to chase every last wiggle defeats the entire point of a parametric fit. A six-parameter Svensson fit to eight bonds is barely different from an unconstrained interpolation — it loses exactly the stability that was the whole reason to fit a smooth shape in the first place.

Section 11

How practitioners actually use this

🏛

Daily curve publication

Central banks and Treasury departments refit fresh each trading day from that day's observed bond prices — the ECB and the Fed both publish a Svensson-family curve, not a raw bootstrap.

📐

Pricing an odd maturity

A desk pricing a bond at 17 years — between the usual 15Y and 20Y quotes — reads the rate straight off the fitted curve, which behaves sensibly everywhere, not just at the input bonds' own maturities.

🎛

A compact risk decomposition

β₀, β₁, β₂ map directly onto level, slope and curvature risk — a compact summary of how the whole curve moved, used for hedging without tracking every point separately.

📈

Forecasting the curve itself

Diebold & Li (2006): treat fitted β₀(t), β₁(t), β₂(t) as three time series, forecast each one, then reassemble a forecast curve for a future date — forecasting a whole shape, not one point.

Where this needs qualifying

A fitted Svensson curve generally does not reprice its own input bonds exactly. A small residual between the curve and any one bond's actual yield is normal and expected — treating it as a mispriced bond to trade against is trading fitting noise, not a real market inefficiency, unless confirmed by something independent of the fit itself.

Section 12

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

A fitted curve has β₀ = 4% and β₁ = −1.5%. Is the curve upward-sloping or inverted?

y(0) = β₀ + β₁ = 4% − 1.5% = 2.5%, below β₀ = 4%. A negative β₁ always means the short end sits below the long-run level — a normal, upward-sloping curve.
Question 2

What does the curvature loading L(τ) − e⁻ˣ equal at τ = 0 and at τ → ∞?

At τ=0, L→1 and e⁻ˣ→1, so the loading is 1−1=0. As τ→∞, both L and e⁻ˣ→0, so it's 0−0=0. β₂'s hump lives strictly in between, never at the endpoints.
Question 3

Plain Nelson-Siegel (four parameters) is fit to a curve with two genuine, separate humps. What happens?

The curvature loading has exactly one interior extremum. A genuinely two-humped curve needs Svensson's second, independent curvature term (β₃, λ₂) to represent both bumps at once.
Question 4

Why can't Nelson-Siegel be fit with ordinary linear regression?

For a fixed λ, the model is linear in the betas. But λ is unknown too, and it appears inside e⁻ᵗ/λ — nonlinear least squares, solved iteratively, is required once λ is also being estimated.
Question 5

A Svensson fit leaves a small residual against one input bond's actual yield. Is that a mispricing to trade against?

Unlike a bootstrap, a parametric fit was never built to reprice every input bond exactly — that's the whole trade-off it makes for smoothness and stability. Trading the residual is trading fitting noise unless confirmed by something independent of the fit.
Section 13

What to carry forward

01

Bootstrapping is exact at its input bonds and unconstrained everywhere else — sparse or noisy inputs can kink the curve between them, or make extrapolation past the last bond close to a guess.

02

Nelson-Siegel replaces that with one smooth function of four parameters: β₀ (long-run level), β₁ (slope), β₂ (curvature), λ (where the hump sits) — smooth everywhere by construction.

03

The two limits pin down the endpoints directly: y(0) = β₀+β₁ is the short rate, y(∞) = β₀ is the long-run level. Everything in between is the curvature term's hump.

04

The curvature loading is zero at both ends and nonzero in the middle — β₂ sets the hump's size and direction, λ slides where along the maturity axis it sits.

05

Svensson adds one more term (β₃, λ₂) for a second, independent hump — six parameters instead of four, and the form central banks like the ECB and the Fed actually publish.

06

Fitting is nonlinear least squares, not algebra — λ has no closed-form solution and the optimizer can land on different local optima depending on where it starts.

What this now lets you do

Read a published central-bank curve's β₀/β₁/β₂/λ values and know exactly what shape they imply, without plotting a single point. Price a bond or swap at an odd maturity straight off four or six numbers instead of inventing an ad-hoc interpolation rule. Use level, slope and curvature themselves as a compact, three-number summary of how an entire curve moved — for hedging and scenario analysis, not just one maturity at a time.
