Entropy, KL Divergence & Mutual Information

Correlation only sees straight-line relationships. This lesson builds the machinery — entropy, KL divergence, mutual information — that measures how much two variables actually tell each other, in bits, whether or not the relationship is a straight line at all.

CONCEPT — background math, not built or tested anywhere in this repo ~16 min read Prereqs: Probability Primer, Correlation & Regression primer
TL;DR
  • Shannon entropy \(H(X) = -\sum_x p(x)\log p(x)\) is the expected "surprise" of a distribution — a fair coin (\(p=0.5\)) is 1 bit of pure uncertainty; a coin that lands heads 99% of the time is far less surprising, and its entropy is correspondingly close to 0.
  • KL divergence \(D_{\text{KL}}(P\|Q) = \sum_x p(x)\log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\) measures the extra bits wasted when you use a model \(Q\) to describe data that really comes from \(P\). It is always \(\ge 0\) (Gibbs' inequality), zero only when \(P=Q\), and — critically — not symmetric: \(D_{\text{KL}}(P\|Q) \ne D_{\text{KL}}(Q\|P)\) in general.
  • The worked example below computes both directions on a concrete biased-coin-vs-model pair: 0.919 bits one way, 2.329 bits the other — a 2.5× gap from swapping which distribution is "truth" and which is "model."
  • Mutual information \(I(X;Y) = D_{\text{KL}}\big(P(X,Y)\,\|\,P(X)P(Y)\big) = H(X) - H(X|Y)\) measures how much knowing \(Y\) shrinks your uncertainty about \(X\) — a nonlinear generalization of correlation that catches relationships correlation is structurally blind to.
  • The \(Y=X^2\) worked example makes this concrete and exact: linear correlation between \(X\) and \(Y\) is precisely zero, while mutual information comes out to 1.522 bits — clear, computable evidence that "uncorrelated" and "independent" are not the same claim.
Section 01

Why this matters

Correlation and its regression cousins are the workhorse tools for "does knowing one variable tell you anything about another" — and they are structurally limited to one specific kind of relationship: a straight line. Two variables can be locked together by an unmistakable, completely predictable rule and still show a Pearson correlation of exactly zero, simply because the rule that connects them isn't linear. A regression-only view of the world will report "no relationship" in exactly the cases where the relationship is real but curved, cyclical, or threshold-based — precisely the kind of structure that shows up in volatility responding to the size of a move rather than its sign, or in a level acting as a magnet from either side.

Information theory asks a more general question: not "is there a linear relationship" but "how much does knowing one variable reduce your uncertainty about the other, in any form at all." Entropy is the tool for measuring uncertainty itself; KL divergence is the tool for measuring how costly it is to describe reality with the wrong model; and mutual information — built directly from both — is the general-purpose answer to "how much do X and Y actually share," with correlation as a special case it strictly generalizes. This lesson builds all three from nothing, because the next two lessons (transfer entropy, and the description-length view of model selection) are both built directly on top of them.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Surprise, wasted bits, and shared information

Entropy is how surprised you'd be, on average, learning the outcome of a random event. A fair coin flip is maximally surprising — you genuinely have no idea which way it'll land, so every flip carries a full "unit" of surprise. A coin that lands heads 99 times out of 100 is barely surprising at all — you already knew, almost for certain, what was coming. Entropy puts an exact number on that: how many yes/no questions, on average, you'd need to ask to pin down the outcome.

KL divergence is the cost of being wrong about which situation you're in. If you built a mental model assuming a fair coin, but the coin is actually 99%-biased, every observation surprises your (wrong) model more than it should — you're carrying around a belief that keeps needing correcting. That extra, avoidable surprise, averaged over time, is what KL divergence measures. Critically, the cost of assuming "fair" when it's really "biased" is not the same cost as assuming "biased" when it's really "fair" — being blindsided by a rare event you'd written off as basically impossible is far more expensive than being pleasantly unsurprised more often than you expected.

Mutual information asks: if I tell you \(Y\), how much less surprised are you now about \(X\)? If knowing \(Y\) tells you nothing about \(X\), mutual information is zero. If knowing \(Y\) pins \(X\) down completely, mutual information equals all of \(X\)'s own entropy. Anywhere in between, it's a graded, always-nonnegative number — and unlike correlation, it doesn't care whether the relationship is a straight line, a curve, a threshold, or something with no simple shape at all.

Section 02

The math, step by step

Shannon entropy — the expected surprise

Start with a single outcome \(x\) of a random variable \(X\), occurring with probability \(p(x)\). Define its information content (or "surprise") as \(-\log p(x)\) — a rare outcome (\(p(x)\) small) has a large, positive surprise; a near-certain outcome (\(p(x)\) close to 1) has surprise close to zero; an outcome that's literally certain (\(p(x)=1\)) carries zero surprise, correctly. Using \(\log_2\) makes the resulting unit a bit — one bit is the surprise of a fair coin flip. (Using the natural log gives units of nats instead; this lesson uses bits throughout for concreteness, but the underlying math is identical up to a constant scale factor of \(\ln 2\).)

Entropy is simply the expected surprise, averaged over every possible outcome, weighted by how likely each one is:

\[ H(X) = -\sum_{x} p(x) \log_2 p(x) \]
−log₂ p(x)the surprise of one specific outcome x
p(x)how likely that outcome is — the averaging weight
H(X)expected surprise across all outcomes — measured in bits
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Entropy is a number for "how surprised will I be, on average, before I find out what happens." A fair coin keeps you guessing every single time, so it scores high. A coin that almost always lands heads barely surprises you, so it scores low — and a coin that always lands the same way surprises you never, scoring exactly zero.

Entropy is maximized when every outcome is equally likely (maximum genuine uncertainty) and minimized (at exactly zero) when one outcome is certain (\(p=1\)) and all others impossible. For a single coin flip with heads-probability \(p\), the entropy — the binary entropy function \(H(p) = -p\log_2 p - (1-p)\log_2(1-p)\) — peaks at exactly 1 bit when \(p=0.5\) and falls toward 0 bits as \(p\) approaches either 0 or 1, in both directions symmetrically. The chart below plots this function exactly.

The binary entropy function H(p) — uncertainty peaks at p = 0.5
\(H(p) = -p\log_2 p - (1-p)\log_2(1-p)\), plotted exactly across \(p \in [0,1]\). A fair coin (\(p=0.5\)) carries the maximum possible 1 bit of genuine uncertainty per flip — you truly cannot do better than a coin-flip guess. A heavily biased coin (\(p=0.1\) or \(p=0.9\)) carries only about 0.469 bits — you can predict the outcome most of the time, so there's less left to be surprised by.

Entropy as defined above is for a discrete variable — a finite or countable set of outcomes. For a continuous variable with density \(f(x)\), the natural analogue replaces the sum with an integral, giving differential entropy:

\[ h(X) = -\int f(x) \log_2 f(x)\, dx \]
The caveat: differential entropy is not a direct generalization of the discrete case in every respect that matters. Unlike \(H(X)\), which is always \(\ge 0\), \(h(X)\) can be negative (a very tightly concentrated density can have negative differential entropy), and — unlike discrete entropy — it is not invariant to a simple change of units (rescale \(X\) and \(h(X)\) shifts by a constant, since probability density itself has units of "per unit of X"). Treat \(h(X)\) as useful for comparing distributions of the same variable under the same units, not as an absolute, unit-free measure of uncertainty the way \(H(X)\) is for a discrete variable.
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Discrete entropy counts surprise across a list of distinct possibilities — heads or tails, 1 through 6. A continuous variable, like tomorrow's exact temperature, has infinitely many possible values packed together, so the same bit-counting trick doesn't translate perfectly — it can even come out negative, which just means "this distribution is bunched up so tightly that the usual accounting breaks down," not that uncertainty itself went negative.

KL divergence — the cost of the wrong model

Now suppose you have two distributions over the same outcomes: \(P\), the one data actually comes from, and \(Q\), a model or approximation you're using instead. The Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence from \(Q\) to \(P\) — read "the divergence of \(P\) from \(Q\)," and written \(D_{\text{KL}}(P\|Q)\) — measures the average number of extra bits wasted per observation when you encode data drawn from \(P\) using a scheme built for \(Q\) instead of \(P\)'s own, optimal scheme:

\[ D_{\text{KL}}(P\|Q) = \sum_x p(x) \log_2\frac{p(x)}{q(x)} \]
p(x)/q(x)

log₂(·)

A ratio of 2 becomes "1 extra bit wasted here"
Σ p(x) · (·)

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

KL divergence is the price you pay for believing the wrong story. If you packed for sunny weather (your mental model, Q) but it's actually been raining all week (the true situation, P), every day you're caught off guard costs you something. KL divergence adds up exactly how many "extra surprises," in bits, believing the wrong thing costs you on average.

Two properties matter enough to prove, not just state. First, \(D_{\text{KL}}(P\|Q) \ge 0\) always, with equality only when \(P=Q\) — this is Gibbs' inequality. The proof is a short application of Jensen's inequality (concavity of \(\log\)):

\[ -D_{\text{KL}}(P\|Q) = \sum_x p(x)\log_2\frac{q(x)}{p(x)} \;\le\; \log_2\left(\sum_x p(x)\cdot\frac{q(x)}{p(x)}\right) = \log_2\left(\sum_x q(x)\right) = \log_2(1) = 0 \]
Σ p(x)log(q/p)negative of D_KL, written as an expectation under P
Jensenlog is concave, so E[log(·)] ≤ log(E[·]) — the inequality step
Σq(x)=1Q is a valid probability distribution, so this sums to exactly 1

Since \(-D_{\text{KL}}(P\|Q) \le 0\), it follows immediately that \(D_{\text{KL}}(P\|Q) \ge 0\). Jensen's inequality is an equality only when the quantity being averaged (\(q(x)/p(x)\)) is constant across every \(x\) with \(p(x)>0\) — which, combined with both distributions summing to 1, forces \(q(x)=p(x)\) everywhere. That is the "\(=0\) iff \(P=Q\)" half of the claim.

Second, and just as important: KL divergence is not symmetric. \(D_{\text{KL}}(P\|Q) \ne D_{\text{KL}}(Q\|P)\) in general — swapping which distribution plays "truth" and which plays "model" changes the answer, often substantially. It is therefore not a true mathematical distance (a real distance metric would need to be symmetric and satisfy a triangle inequality; KL divergence satisfies neither), even though it behaves like one in the loose sense of "how far apart are these two distributions." The worked example below makes the size of that asymmetry concrete with real numbers.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Why does the direction matter? Being slightly wrong about something ordinary is cheap — but being caught totally unprepared for something you'd written off as basically impossible is expensive. That's why "assuming the coin is fair when it's really biased" and "assuming the coin is biased when it's really fair" cost different amounts: getting blindsided by an outcome you'd dismissed hurts far more than mildly under-preparing for something common.

🧮
Worked example

The same two coins, both directions

Let \(P = (0.99, 0.01)\) — a coin that lands heads 99% of the time, the true process — and \(Q = (0.5, 0.5)\) — a naive model that (wrongly) assumes the coin is fair. Compute \(D_{\text{KL}}(P\|Q)\), the cost of describing the real, biased coin using the fair-coin model:

\[ D_{\text{KL}}(P\|Q) = 0.99\log_2\frac{0.99}{0.5} + 0.01\log_2\frac{0.01}{0.5} \approx 0.99(0.9855) + 0.01(-5.6439) \approx 0.919 \text{ bits} \]

Now compute the other direction, \(D_{\text{KL}}(Q\|P)\) — the cost of describing a truly fair coin using a model that (wrongly) assumes the 99%-biased coin:

\[ D_{\text{KL}}(Q\|P) = 0.5\log_2\frac{0.5}{0.99} + 0.5\log_2\frac{0.5}{0.01} \approx 0.5(-0.9855) + 0.5(5.6439) \approx 2.329 \text{ bits} \]

\(0.919 \ne 2.329\) — a factor of roughly 2.5×, from swapping which distribution is treated as "truth" and which as "model," with the exact same pair of coins. The reason the second number is so much larger is exactly the intuition-box scenario: a code built assuming a 99%-biased coin spends almost no bits preparing for the "wrong" outcome, because it has written that outcome off as nearly impossible. When reality actually is a fair coin and that "nearly impossible" outcome shows up half the time, the mismatch is expensive. Overconfidently assigning near-zero probability to something that turns out to be common is punished far more harshly, in bits, than the reverse — assigning only moderate probability to something that turns out to be rare.

The trap this generalizes to: a risk model that assigns near-zero probability to a large adverse move — because historical data made it look nearly impossible — pays an outsized KL cost exactly the moment that move actually happens. The asymmetry above is not just a coin-flip curiosity; it is the same reason underestimating tail risk is structurally more costly than overestimating it.

Mutual information — how much X and Y actually share

Mutual information asks the two-variable version of the KL question: how different is the actual joint distribution of \(X\) and \(Y\) from what it would look like if they were completely independent? If \(X\) and \(Y\) were independent, their joint distribution would factor exactly as \(p(x)p(y)\) — knowing one would tell you nothing about the other. Mutual information is defined as the KL divergence between the real joint distribution and that hypothetical independent one:

\[ I(X;Y) = D_{\text{KL}}\big(P(X,Y) \,\|\, P(X)P(Y)\big) = \sum_{x,y} p(x,y)\log_2\frac{p(x,y)}{p(x)p(y)} \]

Because it's a KL divergence, \(I(X;Y) \ge 0\) always, with equality exactly when \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent (\(p(x,y)=p(x)p(y)\) everywhere) — a direct consequence of Gibbs' inequality proved above. Unlike KL divergence itself, mutual information is symmetric: \(I(X;Y) = I(Y;X)\), because swapping \(X\) and \(Y\) leaves \(D_{\text{KL}}(P(X,Y)\|P(X)P(Y))\) unchanged — the joint distribution and the independence benchmark are both symmetric in \(X\) and \(Y\) to begin with.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Mutual information answers one plain question: if I tell you what Y turned out to be, how much less do you need to guess about X? If knowing Y is totally useless for guessing X, mutual information is zero. If knowing Y lets you nail X exactly, mutual information is as large as it can possibly get. Unlike correlation, it doesn't care whether the connection is a straight line, a curve, or something weirder — it just measures whether knowing one genuinely helps predict the other, in any shape at all.

Mutual information also has an equivalent, often more intuitive form in terms of conditional entropy \(H(X|Y)\) — the expected remaining uncertainty about \(X\) once you already know \(Y\):

\[ I(X;Y) = H(X) - H(X|Y) = H(Y) - H(Y|X) = H(X) + H(Y) - H(X,Y) \]
H(X)total uncertainty about X, before seeing Y
H(X|Y)remaining uncertainty about X, after seeing Y
I(X;Y)the reduction — exactly how much Y told you about X

Read it plainly: \(I(X;Y)\) is "how much your uncertainty about \(X\) shrank once you learned \(Y\)." If \(Y\) is completely uninformative about \(X\), \(H(X|Y) = H(X)\) and \(I(X;Y)=0\). If \(Y\) determines \(X\) completely, \(H(X|Y) = 0\) and \(I(X;Y) = H(X)\) — knowing \(Y\) resolves all of \(X\)'s uncertainty. That three-way equality above (via \(X\), via \(Y\), via the joint) is not three different quantities that happen to agree — it's the same number, because mutual information is fundamentally symmetric in what it measures: the overlap between what \(X\) and \(Y\) each carry.

\(p(x)\)the probability of outcome \(x\) under distribution \(P\)
\(H(X)\)Shannon entropy of \(X\) — expected surprise, in bits (using \(\log_2\))
\(h(X)\)differential entropy — the continuous-variable analogue; can be negative, not unit-invariant
\(f(x)\)a probability density function, for a continuous variable

Real numbers from the worked example directly below, not a schematic. \(Y\)'s circle sits entirely inside \(X\)'s circle because \(H(Y|X)=0\) exactly — \(Y=X^2\) is a deterministic function of \(X\), so there is no "\(Y\)-only" region in a correct picture. Worked example

Y = X², zero correlation, clearly positive mutual information

Let \(X\) take the five values \(\{-2,-1,0,1,2\}\), each with probability \(\tfrac{1}{5}\), and let \(Y = X^2\) (deterministic here, to keep every number exact and checkable by hand; adding a small amount of noise to \(Y\) softens the exact figures below but does not change the qualitative point). \(Y\) then takes the values \(\{0,1,4\}\) with probabilities \(P(Y=0)=\tfrac15\) (only \(X=0\) maps here), \(P(Y=1)=\tfrac25\) (\(X=\pm1\) both map here), and \(P(Y=4)=\tfrac25\) (\(X=\pm2\)).

Step 1 — linear correlation is exactly zero. By symmetry, \(E[X]=0\), and:

\[ \text{Cov}(X,Y) = E[XY] - E[X]E[Y] = E[X^3] - 0 = \tfrac15\big((-2)^3+(-1)^3+0^3+1^3+2^3\big) = \tfrac15(0) = 0 \]

Every odd power of \(X\) cancels by the symmetry of the distribution around zero, so \(\text{Cov}(X,Y)=0\) and the Pearson correlation is exactly zero — not "small," exactly zero, even though \(Y\) is a deterministic, perfectly predictable function of \(X\).

Step 2 — entropies. \(X\) is uniform over 5 equally likely values:

\[ H(X) = \log_2 5 \approx 2.322 \text{ bits} \]

\(Y\)'s distribution \((\tfrac15,\tfrac25,\tfrac25)\) gives:

\[ H(Y) = -\left[\tfrac15\log_2\tfrac15 + \tfrac25\log_2\tfrac25 + \tfrac25\log_2\tfrac25\right] \approx 1.522 \text{ bits} \]

Step 3 — conditional entropy \(H(X|Y)\). Given \(Y=0\), \(X=0\) with certainty (0 bits left). Given \(Y=1\), \(X=+1\) or \(X=-1\), equally likely (1 bit left — the sign is a coin flip). Given \(Y=4\), same story (1 bit left). Averaging:

\[ H(X|Y) = \tfrac15(0) + \tfrac25(1) + \tfrac25(1) = 0.8 \text{ bits} \]

Step 4 — mutual information, computed two ways as a cross-check:

\[ I(X;Y) = H(X) - H(X|Y) = 2.322 - 0.8 = 1.522 \text{ bits} \]
\[ I(X;Y) = H(Y) - H(Y|X) = 1.522 - 0 = 1.522 \text{ bits} \]

Both routes agree exactly (\(H(Y|X)=0\) because \(Y\) is a deterministic function of \(X\) — no uncertainty left once \(X\) is known). Correlation: exactly 0. Mutual information: 1.522 bits, clearly and provably positive. This is not a subtle statistical artifact — it is the defining example of why "uncorrelated" must never be read as "unrelated," and mutual information is the tool that tells the two apart.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

Entropy, KL divergence and mutual information show up under several names depending on the field, which can obscure how directly they connect. In machine learning, training a classifier by minimizing cross-entropy loss is, up to a constant that doesn't depend on the model's parameters, exactly minimizing \(D_{\text{KL}}(P_{\text{data}}\|Q_{\text{model}})\) — the KL divergence from the model's predicted distribution to the true label distribution. In feature selection, mutual information is used directly as a model-free relevance score: rank candidate predictors by \(I(X_i; Y)\) rather than by correlation, precisely to avoid discarding a genuinely predictive but nonlinear feature the way a correlation-only filter would. In signal processing and information theory proper, entropy sets the absolute floor on lossless compression (Shannon's source coding theorem: you cannot compress a source below its entropy, on average, without losing information) — the direct ancestor of the description-length ideas built out fully in this category's third lesson.

Real-world trading scenario

A volatility feature correlation would miss entirely

Suppose a researcher is screening candidate features for predicting next-session realized volatility. One candidate is yesterday's signed return; another is yesterday's absolute return. If volatility clusters symmetrically around large moves regardless of direction — a large up-day and a large down-day both tend to precede an elevated-volatility tomorrow, in roughly equal measure — then the signed-return feature can show a Pearson correlation with next-day volatility close to zero, for exactly the same structural reason the \(Y=X^2\) example above does: the relationship is real but symmetric around zero, which a linear correlation coefficient is mathematically incapable of detecting. Computing mutual information instead — \(I(\text{signed return}_{t}\,;\,\text{volatility}_{t+1})\) — would register a clearly positive number, exposing exactly the kind of magnitude-driven, sign-agnostic relationship this lesson's worked example makes concrete. A researcher relying only on a correlation matrix to screen candidate features would silently discard this one as "no relationship" and never look further — the entire point of building mutual information into a research toolkit.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where this goes wrong in practice

Zero probability, undefined KL divergence. If \(q(x)=0\) for some \(x\) where \(p(x)>0\), \(D_{\text{KL}}(P\|Q)\) is technically infinite (you cannot encode an outcome your model believes is flatly impossible). In practice this means any model with a hard zero somewhere in its support needs smoothing (a small floor probability) before KL divergence against real data is computable at all — a silent \(\infty\) or `NaN` is usually this, not a bug elsewhere.

Reporting KL divergence without saying which direction. Because \(D_{\text{KL}}(P\|Q)\) and \(D_{\text{KL}}(Q\|P)\) can differ by a large factor (2.5× in the worked example above, and unboundedly more in general), quoting "the KL divergence between P and Q" without specifying the direction is ambiguous and can misrepresent the comparison.

Mutual information needs a lot of data to estimate well, especially for continuous variables. The clean examples in this lesson use small, exactly-known discrete distributions. Estimating \(I(X;Y)\) from a finite, noisy, continuous sample requires binning or kernel-density estimation, both of which introduce their own bias — a naive estimator can report a spuriously large mutual information purely from overfitting the joint density to a small sample, the same overfitting risk this repo's validation discipline warns about everywhere else.

Mutual information detects any dependence, not a useful one. A provably nonzero \(I(X;Y)\) says the two variables are not independent — it does not say the relationship is strong enough, stable enough, or simple enough to trade on. Detecting a dependency and having an edge are different claims; explaining the concept is not itself a claim that it produces edge in this repo.

Key Takeaways

✓Entropy is expected surprise. \(H(X)=-\sum p(x)\log_2 p(x)\) peaks at 1 bit for a fair coin and falls toward 0 as an outcome becomes more predictable — computed exactly in the binary entropy chart above.
✓KL divergence is always \(\ge 0\), and it is not symmetric. Gibbs' inequality (via Jensen) proves \(D_{\text{KL}}(P\|Q)\ge0\) with equality iff \(P=Q\); the worked coin example shows the two directions differ by a factor of roughly 2.5×.
⚠Underestimating a rare event is far costlier, in bits, than overestimating one. The direction of the KL asymmetry that hurts most is exactly the direction of an overconfident risk model getting blindsided by a "nearly impossible" tail event.
✓Mutual information is a symmetric, nonlinear generalization of correlation. \(I(X;Y)=H(X)-H(X|Y)=I(Y;X)\ge0\), zero exactly when independent — a strictly more general question than "is there a straight-line relationship."
✓The \(Y=X^2\) example proves the gap concretely. Correlation exactly 0; mutual information exactly 1.522 bits, cross-checked two independent ways. Uncorrelated does not mean unrelated.
⚠Detecting dependence is not the same as finding a tradeable edge. A nonzero mutual information says two variables aren't independent — nothing more. This lesson explains a measurement tool, not a signal.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. Why is entropy maximized when all outcomes are equally likely, and minimized (at zero) when one outcome is certain?
Entropy is the expected surprise \(-\log_2 p(x)\), averaged under \(p(x)\). When every outcome is equally likely, you genuinely have no basis for predicting which one will occur — every outcome carries real surprise, and the average is as large as it can get. When one outcome has probability 1 (and every other has probability 0), that outcome carries zero surprise (you already knew what would happen) and the impossible outcomes contribute nothing to the sum (their probability weight is zero, and by convention \(0\log_2 0=0\)) — so the expected surprise collapses to exactly zero. There is genuinely nothing left to be surprised by.
Q2. Derive why \(D_{\text{KL}}(P\|Q)\ge 0\) always, without just citing "Gibbs' inequality" as a name.
Write \(-D_{\text{KL}}(P\|Q) = \sum_x p(x)\log_2\frac{q(x)}{p(x)}\), an expectation under \(P\) of \(\log_2(q(x)/p(x))\). Because \(\log_2\) is concave, Jensen's inequality says the expectation of a concave function is at most the concave function of the expectation: \(\sum_x p(x)\log_2\frac{q(x)}{p(x)} \le \log_2\left(\sum_x p(x)\cdot\frac{q(x)}{p(x)}\right) = \log_2\left(\sum_x q(x)\right)\). Since \(Q\) is a valid probability distribution, \(\sum_x q(x)=1\), so the right-hand side is \(\log_2(1)=0\). That gives \(-D_{\text{KL}}(P\|Q)\le 0\), i.e. \(D_{\text{KL}}(P\|Q)\ge0\).
Q3. In the worked coin example, why is \(D_{\text{KL}}(Q\|P)\) (2.329 bits) so much larger than \(D_{\text{KL}}(P\|Q)\) (0.919 bits), when it's the same two coins?
\(D_{\text{KL}}(Q\|P)\) is the cost of describing a truly fair coin using a code built assuming a 99%-biased coin. That code, built around \(P=(0.99,0.01)\), spends very few bits preparing for the "tails" outcome, since \(P\) treats it as nearly impossible. When the real process is actually fair (\(Q=(0.5,0.5)\)) and that "nearly impossible" outcome shows up half the time, the mismatch is expensive every single time it occurs. The reverse direction, \(D_{\text{KL}}(P\|Q)\), only has to pay the (much smaller) cost of a fair-coin code slightly under-preparing for an outcome that, under the true biased process, is actually common — a cheaper kind of mistake. Same two distributions; the direction of "which one is truth" changes which mistake you're paying for.
Q4. Why is mutual information symmetric (\(I(X;Y)=I(Y;X)\)) while KL divergence is not?
Mutual information is defined as \(D_{\text{KL}}\big(P(X,Y)\|P(X)P(Y)\big)\) — a KL divergence between one specific pair of distributions: the actual joint, and the hypothetical independent joint. Both of those distributions are symmetric in \(X\) and \(Y\) — swapping \(X\) and \(Y\) doesn't change the joint distribution \(P(X,Y)\), nor the product \(P(X)P(Y)\), it just relabels the axes. So the KL divergence between them is unchanged by the swap, meaning \(I(X;Y)=I(Y;X)\). This is not a general property of KL divergence — it's specific to the fact that mutual information always compares the joint to the *same* joint's independence benchmark, a symmetric pair, even though KL divergence between two genuinely different distributions \(P\) and \(Q\) is not symmetric in general.
Q5. In the \(Y=X^2\) worked example, why is \(H(Y|X)\) exactly zero, and why does that make \(I(X;Y)\) equal to all of \(H(Y)\)?
\(H(Y|X)=0\) because \(Y\) is a deterministic function of \(X\): once you know \(X\), \(Y=X^2\) is fully determined with no remaining randomness at all — there's nothing left to be uncertain about. Using the identity \(I(X;Y)=H(Y)-H(Y|X)\), if \(H(Y|X)=0\) then \(I(X;Y)=H(Y)-0=H(Y)\) exactly — knowing \(X\) resolves *all* of \(Y\)'s uncertainty, so the "shared information" between them equals the entirety of \(Y\)'s own entropy. This is also why the Venn-style chart draws \(Y\)'s circle entirely nested inside \(X\)'s: there is no "\(Y\)-only" region left over, because nothing about \(Y\) is unexplained by \(X\).
Q6. A colleague says "the correlation between our feature and the target is 0.02, so there's no relationship — let's drop it." What's the gap in that reasoning, and what would you check instead?
A near-zero Pearson correlation only rules out a *linear* relationship — it says nothing about a nonlinear one. The \(Y=X^2\) worked example proves this concretely: a perfectly deterministic, completely predictable relationship can still show exactly zero correlation, simply because the relationship is symmetric around a point rather than a straight line. Before concluding "no relationship," compute the mutual information \(I(\text{feature};\text{target})\) — it will register any dependence, linear or not — and, if it comes back clearly positive despite the near-zero correlation, investigate the shape of the relationship (a scatter plot, a binned conditional mean) rather than discarding the feature on the correlation number alone.
