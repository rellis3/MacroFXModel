Market Depth, Order-Book Slope & Depth Imbalance

A limit order book has a shape even when nobody is trading it — how much size sits right at the best price, and how much more shows up as you look one, two, five ticks further away. Slope describes that shape; depth imbalance describes which side of it is heavier. Neither one needs a single executed trade to compute, which is exactly what separates them from every flow-based signal this Theory Lab has covered so far.

TL;DR
  • Depth is how much size is actually resting, waiting to trade, at a given price — level-1 depth is the size sitting at the best bid/ask; cumulative depth \(D(n)\) is the running total resting within \(n\) ticks (or price levels) of the touch. This is a snapshot property of the RESTING book — it needs zero executed trades to compute, unlike every flow-based signal in this Theory Lab.
  • Order-book slope asks how quickly that cumulative depth builds up as you move away from the best price. This lesson follows the standard academic convention (Næs & Skjeltorp, 2006): a larger slope value means depth accumulates fast — a deep, liquid book. But the literature is genuinely split on which axis "slope" runs along, and a "steep" book means the opposite thing under the inverse, walk-the-book convention — this lesson names both explicitly rather than picking a winner.
  • Depth imbalance, \(DI=(D_b-D_a)/(D_b+D_a)\), measures which side of the book is heavier right now. At level 1 this is exactly the queue imbalance \(QI\) from the sibling Order Flow Imbalance lesson, feeding the same microprice identity \(P_{\text{micro}}=M+\tfrac{S}{2}\cdot QI\) derived there — this lesson doesn't re-derive a different-looking version, it reuses that one directly and extends the idea to cumulative depth across several levels.
  • Depth imbalance is a resting-liquidity signal built from one snapshot of what's sitting on the book. Order Flow Imbalance (the sibling lesson) is a trade-flow signal built from a sequence of book changes over time. They frequently agree, but they are answering genuinely different questions from genuinely different data, and this lesson keeps that distinction explicit throughout.
  • Order-book slope is also easy to confuse with Kyle's λ (covered in the sibling Kyle-Obizhaeva Invariance lesson) — both describe "how much does size move price," but λ is a reduced-form statistic estimated from a history of trades, while slope is a purely geometric property of a single book snapshot that needs no trade history at all.
  • A worked toy order book below computes cumulative depth, slope under both conventions, and depth imbalance by hand — verified to match the live calculator's own output exactly.
  • How it reached trading: Electronic order books made depth visible, researchers such as Næs & Skjeltorp (2006) measured its slope, and desks and regulators now read it as a live liquidity gauge before sizing orders.
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

My screen shows one bid and one offer, one pip apart. Is that the liquidity?

It is the front door. Depth is everything resting behind it: how much size sits at the best price, and at each price further out.

Give me the lesson's book.

Bids of three hundred, two hundred and fifty and four hundred on the first three levels. Offers of two hundred, a hundred and fifty and a hundred and eighty. Add them up as you go and the bids total nine hundred and fifty within three levels. The offers only five hundred and thirty.

And slope?

How fast that running total grows per tick away from the touch. On the bid side it averages two hundred and eighty-seven and a half per tick. On the offer side, a hundred and fifty-seven and a half. The bid side builds depth nearly twice as fast.

So a steep book is good?

Careful, that word cuts both ways. In this convention a bigger slope means a deeper book. But a trader walking the book plots price against size filled, and then a thin book looks steep, because the price jumps fast. Always ask which way someone is measuring.

And the imbalance?

Bid size minus offer size, over the total. At the touch it is three hundred against two hundred: plus zero point two, mildly bid-heavy. Use all three levels and it is about plus zero point two eight. More bid-heavy the deeper you look.

Which one do I use?

Depends on your order. If you trade more than what sits at the touch, the deeper number is the one that bites. The touch number feeds the microprice, which here sits a tenth of a pip above the mid.

Isn't this the same as order flow imbalance?

No. Depth imbalance is a photograph of what is resting now. Order flow imbalance is a flip-book of how the book has been changing. A side can be heavy right now and still be draining fast.

When has depth mattered for real?

The Treasury flash rally of the fifteenth of October 2014. The ten-year yield fell about sixteen basis points and came back within about twelve minutes. The official report on it documented how thin order book depth had become around the event.

And day to day?

Read depth a few levels deep before you size a child order. A thin, shallow side means smaller slices and more patience.

One line?

The quote tells you the price. Depth, slope and imbalance tell you what it will cost to move size through it.

Section 01

Why this matters

Every signal covered so far in this curriculum's microstructure lessons either looks backward at executed trades (trade classification, Roll, Glosten-Milgrom, PIN) or looks at changes to the book over a sequence of updates (Order Flow Imbalance). This lesson asks a much plainer question that none of those answer: forget what just happened — what does the book look like right now? How much size is actually resting at the best price, how much more shows up if a trader is willing to walk one, two, five ticks further from it, and is that resting liquidity lopsided toward buyers or sellers at this exact instant? None of that requires a single trade to have occurred. A book that hasn't printed a trade in the last minute still has a shape, and that shape is exactly what depth, slope, and depth imbalance describe.

This matters practically because the shape of the resting book is what a marketable order actually has to fight through. Two markets can show the identical best bid and ask — the same quoted spread — and still be wildly different places to trade a large order, if one has a thick wall of resting size sitting right at the touch and nothing behind it, while the other has modest size at the touch but plenty more stacked up a few ticks back. The quoted spread alone can't tell those two books apart; depth and slope can.

Kyle's λ (sibling lesson)

A single number, \(\lambda=\sigma_v/(2\sigma_u)\), estimated from a history of trades — how much a security's price has actually moved per unit of signed order flow, statistically, over some historical window. It is a reduced-form, trade-based measure of price impact.

Order-book slope (this lesson)

A purely geometric property of one book snapshot — how quickly resting size accumulates as price moves away from the touch, right now. No trade history, no statistical estimation, and no assumption about informed vs. noise trading is required to compute it at all.

💡
Plain-English intuition

A snow pile right at the door, or spread evenly down the driveway

Picture two driveways after a snowstorm, both with the same amount of snow cleared right at the front door. In the first driveway, every shovel-load was dumped in one spot a few feet from the door — walk five steps further and there's almost nothing left to wade through. In the second, the snow was spread more evenly the whole length of the driveway — five steps further out, there's still a meaningful pile to get past. Both driveways look identical if you only glance at the door itself. The difference only shows up once you ask how the pile changes as you walk further away — which is exactly what order-book slope measures about a market, using resting size instead of snow.

Depth imbalance is a separate, simpler question about the same picture: is there more snow piled on the left side of the driveway or the right? A driveway with a towering pile on the left and almost nothing on the right tells you something about which direction is easier to walk through right now — exactly the intuition behind reading a bid-heavy or ask-heavy book as short-horizon pressure.

How it reached trading

How much size can the book take right now?

Order-book depth and slope · electronic limit-order books from the 1980s–90s; Næs & Skjeltorp, 2006

On a trading floor or in a dealer market, depth beyond the best quote was invisible: you found it by trading. Electronic limit-order books (Toronto's CATS from 1977, the Paris Bourse's CAC system from 1986, London's SETS from 1997) published the whole queue, and with it a measurable shape.

Researchers read that shape directly: Biais, Hillion & Spatt (1995) on the Paris Bourse book, and Næs & Skjeltorp (2006) on the Oslo Stock Exchange, who tied order-book slope to the volume–volatility relation. Regulators then used depth as a stress gauge: the US agencies' joint staff report on the 15 October 2014 Treasury "flash rally" documented how order-book depth thinned around the event.

Today desks read depth before sizing child orders, and risk managers watch top-of-book depth in benchmark futures such as the E-mini and Treasury futures as a live liquidity gauge.

1. Depth: level-1 and cumulative

Limit Order Books & Queue Dynamics already established the vocabulary this lesson builds on: a book is two ladders of price levels, with resting size \(Q^{(i)}\) sitting at each level \(i\), counted outward from the best price (\(i=1\) is the touch itself). Level-1 depth is nothing more than \(Q_b^{(1)}\) and \(Q_a^{(1)}\) — the size resting at the best bid and best ask, the same \(Q_b, Q_a\) the sibling Order Flow Imbalance lesson uses directly. Cumulative depth extends this to a running total across the first \(n\) levels (equivalently, out to \(n\) ticks from the touch, when levels are evenly tick-spaced):

\[ D_b(n) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} Q_b^{(i)}, \qquad D_a(n) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} Q_a^{(i)} \]
D_b(n), D_a(n)total resting size within n ticks of the best bid / best ask
Q_b^(i), Q_a^(i)size resting at the i-th price level on each side, i=1 being the touch

\(D(n)\) answers a genuinely practical question a naive best-bid/best-ask quote can't: not "what's the current spread," but "how much size would a trader actually have to consume before reaching a price \(n\) ticks worse than the touch?" A trader working an order larger than \(Q^{(1)}\) alone always cares about \(D(n)\) for some \(n>1\), never just level 1.

2. Order-book slope — two conventions, stated honestly

"Slope" asks how fast \(D(n)\) grows as \(n\) grows — the shape of the depth curve, not just its endpoints. This lesson follows the concrete construction used in the empirical order-book literature (Næs & Skjeltorp, 2006): for each level \(i\) beyond the touch, compute the incremental depth added per tick of distance from the touch, then average across levels:

\[ \text{slope} = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=2}^{n} \frac{D(i) - D(1)}{i-1} \]
D(i) − D(1)how much MORE depth has accumulated by level i, beyond what was already resting at the touch
i − 1ticks of price distance separating level i from the touch, assuming even tick spacing
1/(n−1) Σaverage this per-tick accumulation rate across every level examined, from 2 up to n

Under this convention, computed separately for each side, a larger slope value means depth builds up faster as you move away from the touch — a deep book, where a large order does relatively little damage to price. A slope near zero means almost nothing rests beyond the touch — depth is entirely front-loaded at the best price, and any size beyond \(Q^{(1)}\) has to move price meaningfully to get filled.

A simpler alternative some practitioners use instead of a full regression or per-level average is a plain ratio of cumulative depth at some distance to depth at the touch:

\[ R(n) = \frac{D(n)}{D(1)} \]

\(R(n)\) close to 1 means almost all resting size sits right at the touch — the book is thin and front-loaded no matter how large \(n\) is; \(R(n)\) well above 1 means meaningful depth exists further out, spread beyond the very best price. \(R(n)\) and the averaged per-tick slope above are two different ways of asking essentially the same question and will normally move together, but they are not numerically identical — this lesson's worked example computes both.

The "steep vs. flat" ambiguity — stated honestly, not resolved. Under the depth-vs-distance convention above (Næs & Skjeltorp), a larger slope number means a deeper, more liquid book — depth accumulates quickly. But flip the axes and look at the same book from the angle of a trader actually walking it — plotting the price reached against cumulative quantity consumed, the inverse function of \(D(n)\) — and a deep, liquid book produces a flat price curve (little price movement per unit of size consumed), while a thin book produces a steep one (price jumps fast for modest size). Traders describing a book informally as "steep" nearly always mean this second, walk-the-book sense — steep meaning thin and dangerous, the opposite direction from the first convention's "large slope." Both usages are genuinely common; neither is wrong; this lesson uses the depth-vs-distance convention explicitly and consistently throughout, and flags this ambiguity precisely so a reader checks which axis any "slope" figure they encounter elsewhere is actually computed against before calling a large number good or bad.
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

If you're filling a swimming pool with a hose, "slope" could mean two opposite things depending on which way you're looking: how fast the water level rises per bucket poured in (fast rise = small pool = bad), or how many buckets it takes to raise the water by one inch (many buckets needed = big pool = good). Both are "the same pool," described from two opposite directions. Order-book slope has exactly this trap — always check which direction someone means before deciding whether "steep" is the good news or the bad news.

3. Depth imbalance — and how it relates to queue imbalance and the microprice

Depth imbalance asks a different question from slope: not how the book's depth is shaped, but which side currently holds more of it. At level 1, this is defined identically to the queue imbalance \(QI\) already introduced in the sibling Order Flow Imbalance lesson — this lesson reuses that formula rather than inventing a differently-labeled variant of the same idea:

\[ DI^{(1)} = \frac{Q_b^{(1)} - Q_a^{(1)}}{Q_b^{(1)} + Q_a^{(1)}} \;=\; QI \]

Because \(DI^{(1)}\) is exactly \(QI\), the microprice identity already derived in that lesson applies here completely unchanged — this lesson does not re-derive a different-looking version of it:

\[ P_{\text{micro}} = M + \frac{S}{2}\cdot DI^{(1)}, \qquad M=\frac{P_b+P_a}{2},\ \ S=P_a-P_b \]

What this lesson adds is extending the same ratio beyond level 1, to the cumulative depth within \(n\) levels — a natural question once a trader's order is larger than what's resting at the touch alone, and one the level-1-only microprice formula was never built to answer:

\[ DI(n) = \frac{D_b(n) - D_a(n)}{D_b(n) + D_a(n)} \]
DI(n)depth imbalance using total resting size within n ticks on each side, not just the touch
DI(n) ∈ [-1, 1]−1: every unit of depth within n ticks sits on the ask; +1: every unit sits on the bid

\(DI(1)\) and \(DI(n)\) for larger \(n\) can genuinely disagree — a book can be lopsided right at the touch while looking much more balanced a few ticks back, or the reverse. The worked example below computes both from the same toy book and shows exactly this: which \(n\) a depth imbalance is computed over is a real, consequential design choice, not an afterthought.

Depth imbalance vs. Order Flow Imbalance — a resting snapshot vs. a sequence of changes. It is easy to conflate these because both produce a number in \([-1,1]\) describing "buy pressure vs. sell pressure," but they are built from genuinely different data. Depth imbalance, \(DI(n)\), is computed from a single snapshot of what is currently resting on each side of the book — no history required at all. Order Flow Imbalance, \(OFI_n = e^b_n - e^a_n\) from the sibling lesson, is computed from the change between consecutive book updates, summed over a window — it is inherently a time-series quantity and is undefined from a single snapshot. A book can show a strongly positive depth imbalance (heavy resting bid size right now) while OFI over the last few updates is negative (that resting size has been shrinking, update by update) — the two signals are telling a reader different things and can legitimately point in opposite directions at the same instant.
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Depth imbalance is a photograph: "right now, is there more candy in the left jar or the right jar?" Order Flow Imbalance is a flip-book: "over the last ten seconds, which jar has been filling up and which has been emptying out?" A jar can be fuller than the other one right now (photograph) while actually draining fast (flip-book) — both readings are true at once, and they're answering different questions.

\(Q_b^{(i)}, Q_a^{(i)}\)Resting size at the i-th price level on the bid / ask side, i=1 being the touch — same notation family as the Limit Order Books and Order Flow Imbalance lessons.
\(D_b(n), D_a(n)\)Cumulative depth: total resting size within the first n levels (n ticks, if evenly spaced) of the touch.
slopeAverage per-tick growth of cumulative depth beyond the touch — larger means a deeper, more liquid book, under this lesson's stated convention.
\(R(n)\)Simple depth ratio, \(D(n)/D(1)\) — how many times larger cumulative depth at n ticks is versus depth at the touch alone.
\(DI^{(1)}, DI(n)\)Depth imbalance at the touch (identical to \(QI\) from the OFI lesson) and at cumulative depth to n levels.
\(M, S, P_{\text{micro}}\)Naive midpoint, spread, and the microprice — unchanged from the OFI lesson's own definitions.

Real-world trading scenario

Choosing which side to lean on before working a large order

A desk needs to work an order noticeably larger than what's resting at the touch on either side of a currency pair's book. Reading only the quoted spread gives no guidance at all — both sides look identical from that vantage point. Computing \(D_b(n)\) and \(D_a(n)\) out to a few ticks changes the picture: if the side the desk needs to trade against shows a shallow slope (depth barely grows past the touch), the desk knows in advance that pushing size through that side will move the price meaningfully faster than a naive same-side-of-spread estimate would suggest — a reason to slice the order smaller, work it more patiently, or split it across venues, exactly the concerns the Almgren-Chriss and Obizhaeva-Wang lessons address from the execution side. Depth imbalance adds a second, complementary read: a book that is already heavily lopsided toward the side the desk is about to trade into is telling it, before a single child order is sent, that it will likely be competing for size against other participants leaning the same way.

Nothing above is a claim this repo runs this analysis on live data — see the status badge above for exactly what does and doesn't exist here today. It illustrates how a desk that did have a genuine, continuously-updating top-of-book feed would actually use these two numbers together, which is the point of the exercise.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

📐

A cheap, model-free liquidity gauge

Slope and depth need no historical calibration at all — unlike Kyle's λ, they can be read directly off a single book snapshot, making them a fast first pass before reaching for a statistically-estimated impact model.

✂️

Sizing the first child order

A shallow slope on the side about to be traded against is a direct, mechanical warning that even a modest clip will walk noticeably further down the book than the same clip would on a steeper-sloped side.

⏱️

A complement to Order Flow Imbalance, not a substitute

OFI says which way pressure has been building over recent updates; depth imbalance says how the book is shaped right this instant. Desks that track both get a flow-based and a stock-based read of the same market at once.

🧩

An input to, not a substitute for, an impact model

A steep drop-off in depth beyond the touch is consistent with a large Kyle-style λ or a large Kyle-Obizhaeva bet-size sensitivity — but slope is observed directly from the book, while λ still has to be estimated statistically from trades; the two are complementary evidence, not interchangeable numbers.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where depth, slope, and depth imbalance quietly mislead

Displayed depth is not committed depth. Iceberg orders show only a fraction of their true size at any moment, understating real depth; spoofed orders are posted purely to be seen and cancelled before they can trade, inflating a level's apparent size with nothing genuine behind it. Every formula in this lesson takes displayed size at face value — it has no way to distinguish real resting interest from either distortion, and a reader who forgets this treats a manufactured reading as a genuine one.

FX specifically has no single consolidated book to read depth from. Unlike a single listed exchange, spot FX trades primarily OTC across multiple competing ECNs and bank-to-bank venues, each with its own separate book. A slope or depth-imbalance figure computed from one venue's book describes that venue only — not "the FX market's" depth as a whole — and this repo's own primary venue, OANDA spot, doesn't even expose a live top-of-book ladder to begin with (see the status badge above). A naive depth read that ignores fragmentation entirely can be badly misleading about how much liquidity genuinely exists across the market.

These are snapshot statistics, not a stable, slow-moving property. A book's slope and imbalance can change materially within milliseconds as orders are added, cancelled, or filled — a reading taken a second ago says nothing guaranteed about the book right now. Treating either number as a multi-minute forecast rather than an instantaneous read is the same category error the OFI lesson warns about for its own signals.

Check which slope convention a source is using before calling a number good or bad. As the callout above spells out, "a steep book" means opposite things depending on whether the writer means the depth-vs-distance curve or the walk-the-book price-impact curve. Silently assuming one convention when a source meant the other is an easy, entirely avoidable mistake.

Scenario drill · 15 October 2014

A large Treasury futures order on a morning when the book is thin

It is the morning of 15 October 2014. Treasuries have been rallying, and when you look past the best bid and offer in the 10-year futures book, the size resting within a few ticks is far below what you normally see: cumulative depth barely grows beyond the touch. You have a large sell order to complete before noon. How do you work it?

  1. Send the whole order as one market order now and be done with it.
    With little resting beyond the touch, a large market order walks through level after level, and each one is further away. A shallow slope is exactly the warning that size will move the price fast.
  2. Read cumulative depth a few levels deep, cut child orders well below what is resting there, and work them patiently.
    This sizes each slice to the liquidity that is actually there rather than to the quote. On a thin day, smaller slices and more time keep each one from walking the book.
  3. The quoted spread is still one tick, so size the slices as you would on a normal day.
    The quote shows only the front door. Depth and slope are what tell you the cost of size, and both are saying this is not a normal day.
  4. Hold the whole order until depth recovers, then trade normally.
    It avoids walking a thin book, but you carry the full position and its price risk while you wait, and you still have a deadline. Patient slicing gets some of it done safely.

What happened. Between about 9:33 and 9:45 a.m. New York time, the 10-year Treasury yield fell roughly 16 basis points and then came back, with no clear news to explain it. The US agencies' joint staff report on the event documented how thin order book depth was around the move. A large order sent into that book would have paid heavily for walking it.

Sources: Federal Reserve Bank of New York, release of the Joint Staff Report on October 15, 2014, 13 Jul 2015; Liberty Street Economics, "A look back at the October 15, 2014 flash rally", 15 Oct 2019.

Section 04

Key Takeaways

✓Depth is a snapshot property of the resting book — level-1 depth is size at the touch; cumulative depth \(D(n)\) is the running total within n ticks. Neither needs a single executed trade to compute.
✓Order-book slope measures how fast cumulative depth builds up away from the touch. This lesson's convention: a larger slope value means a deeper, more liquid book — but the literature genuinely disagrees on which axis "slope" runs along, and this lesson states both conventions explicitly.
✓Depth imbalance at level 1 is exactly \(QI\) from the Order Flow Imbalance lesson, feeding the same microprice identity \(P_{\text{micro}}=M+\tfrac{S}{2}\cdot QI\) — this lesson extends it to cumulative depth, it doesn't replace it.
⚠Depth imbalance (a resting snapshot) and Order Flow Imbalance (a sequence of changes) are genuinely different signals that can disagree at the same instant — one is not a substitute for the other.
⚠Slope is a static book-shape statistic; Kyle's λ is a statistically-estimated, trade-based price-impact coefficient. They're related in spirit but built from entirely different data and computed in entirely different ways.
✗Displayed depth can be hidden (icebergs) or fabricated (spoofing), and FX has no single consolidated book to read it from anyway. Neither formula in this lesson can detect either distortion on its own.
ℹOn a desk depth is the live liquidity gauge. Execution desks check it before sizing child orders, and regulators used thinning depth to explain the October 2014 Treasury flash rally.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. What is the difference between level-1 depth and cumulative depth \(D(n)\)?
Level-1 depth is simply the size resting at the single best price on a side (the touch), \(Q^{(1)}\). Cumulative depth \(D(n) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} Q^{(i)}\) is the running total of resting size across the first \(n\) price levels — how much size in total would need to be consumed to walk the book out to \(n\) ticks (or levels) from the touch, not just what sits at the very best price.
Q2. Under this lesson's stated slope convention, does a LARGER slope value mean a deeper book or a thinner one? Why does the opposite convention exist in the wild?
Under the depth-vs-distance convention this lesson uses (Næs & Skjeltorp, 2006), a larger slope value means cumulative depth builds up faster as distance from the touch grows — a deeper, more liquid book. The opposite convention exists because plotting the same book from the other axis — price reached versus cumulative quantity consumed while walking it — inverts the relationship: a deep book produces a FLAT price curve there, while a thin book produces a STEEP one. Traders informally calling a book "steep" usually mean this second, walk-the-book sense, which is the numerical opposite of the first convention's "large slope."
Q3. How does level-1 depth imbalance \(DI^{(1)}\) relate to the queue imbalance \(QI\) from the Order Flow Imbalance lesson?
They are the exact same quantity: \(DI^{(1)} = (Q_b^{(1)} - Q_a^{(1)})/(Q_b^{(1)} + Q_a^{(1)}) = QI\). This lesson deliberately reuses that lesson's own formula and its microprice identity \(P_{\text{micro}}=M+\tfrac{S}{2}\cdot QI\) rather than deriving a differently-labeled version of the same idea — the only genuinely new construction in this lesson is extending the ratio to cumulative depth across multiple levels, \(DI(n)\).
Q4. A book shows \(Q_b^{(1)}=400\), \(Q_a^{(1)}=100\) at the touch, but cumulative depth to 3 levels is \(D_b(3)=900\), \(D_a(3)=1{,}100\). Compute \(DI^{(1)}\) and \(DI(3)\), and explain what the disagreement between them means.
\(DI^{(1)} = (400-100)/(400+100) = 0.60\) — strongly bid-heavy at the touch. \(DI(3) = (900-1{,}100)/(900+1{,}100) = -200/2{,}000 = -0.10\) — mildly ask-heavy once cumulative depth is considered. The book is front-loaded with buy interest right at the best price, but the ask side has considerably more depth resting a few ticks back — a trader relying on the level-1 reading alone would badly mis-read which side is actually heavier for anything larger than the smallest possible trade.
Q5. Why can depth imbalance and Order Flow Imbalance disagree at the same instant, even though both produce a number that sounds like "buy vs. sell pressure"?
Depth imbalance is computed from a single snapshot of what is currently resting on each side of the book — it needs no history at all. Order Flow Imbalance is computed from the change between consecutive book updates, summed over a window — it is inherently about a sequence of events over time, not a snapshot. A book can currently be bid-heavy (positive depth imbalance) while that same bid-side size has been shrinking over the last several updates (negative OFI) — one measure describes the current state, the other describes the recent trajectory, and there is no requirement that the two agree.
Q6. How does order-book slope differ from Kyle's λ, given that both are informally described as "how much size moves price"?
Kyle's λ, \(\sigma_v/(2\sigma_u)\), is a reduced-form statistic estimated from a history of trades and their associated price changes — it requires a time series of executed order flow and makes assumptions about informed versus noise trading to interpret. Order- book slope is a purely geometric property of a single resting-book snapshot: how quickly cumulative displayed depth grows as price moves away from the touch, computable with zero trade history and no statistical estimation at all. They are related in spirit — both describe how costly it might be to trade size — but they are built from entirely different data using entirely different methods, and neither is a substitute for computing the other.
