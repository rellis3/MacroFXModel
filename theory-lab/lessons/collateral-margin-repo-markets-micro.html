Collateral, Margin & Repo Markets, the Plumbing Underneath Leverage

Every leveraged position and every derivatives trade rests on machinery most people never see: collateral posted as a buffer against default, discounted for its own riskiness with a "haircut," and funded overnight through the repo market — a market so central that SOFR, the reference rate from the earlier Swap Curves & OIS Discounting lesson, is built directly out of it. This deck opens a brand-new category — Collateral, Funding & Rehypothecation — and this is its first lesson.

Section 1

Why any leveraged position needs a buffer

Leverage means one side is exposed to the other for more than either has actually handed over. Something has to sit in reserve against the chance that exposure isn't honored.

⚠

Leverage creates counterparty risk

A leveraged position or a derivatives trade means one party could owe the other a meaningful sum if the market moves. If that party can't pay, the other side is exposed — this is true whether the counterparty is a bank, a fund, or a broker.

🛡

Collateral is the buffer

Posting collateral — cash, government bonds, other liquid securities — gives the counterparty something to seize and liquidate if the other side defaults, instead of simply absorbing the loss.

↔

It moves both ways

Collateral obligations aren't fixed at trade inception — they're recalculated and re-exchanged as the position's risk and value change, which is exactly why margin has two distinct flavors, covered next.

None of this is exotic — it's the ordinary plumbing sitting underneath market-making, leverage, and derivatives clearing everywhere in the world. It's usually invisible precisely because it's working correctly.
Scope check

This is institutional market-structure background — how collateral, margin, and repo work across banks, dealers, and clearinghouses generally. It is not a claim about anything built, tested, or traded inside this repo's own bots and broker code, which run simple, unleveraged-margin-free paper/live P&L tracking with no SPAN/SIMM/haircut/repo modeling of any kind — verified directly against the current source before writing this deck.

Section 2

Two margins, two different questions

Initial margin asks "what could go wrong?" Variation margin asks "what already did?" Step through a position's first three days.

Try it
Initial margin
posted once
buffer, upfront
IM is posted before anything has moved. It's sized to cover the worst plausible loss that could build up in the short window it would take to close out the position if the counterparty defaulted right now — not a payment for anything that happened.
Position gains $50k
→
VM: $50k paid to the winner
VM moves with the day's mark-to-market change. The losing side wires $50k to the winning side today, zeroing out yesterday's unrealized gain into a realized cash settlement. IM from Day 0 hasn't moved at all.
Position loses $80k
→
VM: $80k paid the other way
VM can flip direction daily, sometimes intraday. Yesterday's winner is today's payer. This is precisely why VM keeps current exposure near zero at every exchange — gains and losses get settled as they occur instead of piling up unrealized.
IM sits still unless the position's risk profile itself changes materially. VM moves every settlement cycle, in whichever direction the market just moved.
Section 3

Initial margin: a forward-looking buffer

IM is not guessed independently by each firm — it's sized using standardized, industry-recognized methodologies. This deck names them without deriving their internal mechanics, exactly as the full lesson does.

📊

SPAN

Standard Portfolio Analysis of Risk, developed by CME. Used for exchange-traded and centrally cleared derivatives — a scenario-based approach evaluating a portfolio's worst plausible loss across a grid of price and volatility shifts.

🧮

ISDA SIMM

The Standard Initial Margin Model, from ISDA. Used for non-centrally-cleared, bilateral OTC derivatives, mandated for large participants under post-2008 uncleared-margin rules — sensitivity-based, computed from a portfolio's risk-factor exposures.

Where this needs qualifying

SPAN and SIMM are built for structurally different market segments — cleared vs. bilateral — and aren't interchangeable. The window IM protects against is called the "margin period of risk": the time it would actually take to close out or replace a defaulting counterparty's position.

Section 4

Variation margin: settling what already happened

VM transfers cash equal to a position's realized mark-to-market change since the last exchange — commonly daily, intraday for volatile positions. It is not a cushion against the future; it's a receipt for the past.

What VM is

A daily (or more frequent) true-up. The side that gained gets paid; the side that lost pays. Current exposure between the two parties is pulled back toward zero at every exchange.

What VM is not

Not a buffer against tomorrow's move — that's IM's job. Not returned at trade close the way IM is (net of losses) — VM has already settled and isn't "owed back."

Think of a security deposit versus a monthly rent payment. The deposit (IM) sits aside for something that might break later. Rent (VM) settles for a month you've already lived through.
Section 5

A haircut: why $100 of collateral often isn't worth $100

Collateral is rarely counted at full face value. Two risks justify the discount: the collateral's own price could fall, and selling it quickly — especially in a stressed default scenario — can itself move the price against the seller.

Worked example
$100 bond
× (1 − 5%)
$95 eligible
Eligible collateral value = market value × (1 − haircut %). A 5% haircut on $100 of market value leaves $95 counted toward whatever requirement it's posted against — the other $5 is the receiving party's built-in buffer.
🟢

~0–2%

Cash & short-dated G7 government bonds — highly liquid, low volatility.

🟡

~5–10%

Investment-grade corporate bonds — more price and liquidity risk.

🔴

~15%+

Listed equities — materially higher volatility.

These ranges are illustrative, order-of-magnitude figures — real haircut schedules vary by counterparty, tenor, credit rating, and regulatory regime.

Section 7

A repurchase agreement: a loan wearing two sales as a costume

One party sells a security today, agreeing to buy the exact same security back later at a slightly higher price. Nothing about the security changes — only cash and the security swap hands, twice, in opposite directions each leg.

Legally this is two sales. Economically it's a fully collateralized cash loan — the party selling the security is the cash borrower, the party buying it is the cash lender holding the security as collateral. From the lender's side, the identical trade is called a reverse repo — same trade, mirror-image name.

Section 9

Overnight, rolled daily — and where SOFR comes from

🔁

Overnight is the norm

A large share of repo activity is overnight, rolled fresh day after day — letting a dealer fund a securities inventory flexibly rather than locking into fixed-term borrowing.

🏷

General collateral vs. "specials"

Most repo references a broad basket of eligible securities (GC) at a standard rate. A specific security in high demand can trade "special" — a lower repo rate paid purely to get hold of that exact security.

The direct tie to Swap Curves & OIS Discounting

SOFR — the USD reference rate that replaced LIBOR and anchors OIS discounting for collateralized derivatives, covered in Swap Curves & OIS Discounting — is not a survey or an estimate. It is computed directly from actual, large-volume overnight Treasury repo transaction data, published daily by the New York Fed. The repo mechanism on the previous slide is, literally, the market SOFR is measured from.

That earlier lesson discounts collateralized derivatives at OIS/SOFR specifically because those trades are backed by daily-exchanged collateral under a Credit Support Annex (CSA) — the same variation-margin idea from Section 2 of this deck. The two lessons describe one connected system, not two unrelated topics.

Section 10

Getting this wrong, in practice

Common mistakes
  • Confusing IM (forward-looking buffer) with VM (backward-looking settlement)
  • Treating a repo's legal form (two sales) as if it changed its economic substance (a collateralized loan)
  • Applying one haircut to every asset, regardless of its own volatility and liquidity
  • Assuming SPAN and SIMM are interchangeable — they serve structurally different market segments
How it's actually used
  • Dealers fund a securities inventory overnight via repo instead of tying up balance-sheet cash
  • Money market funds use reverse repo as a safe, short-term, fully-collateralized place to park cash
  • A portfolio's IM is recalculated whenever its risk profile changes materially — SPAN if cleared, SIMM if bilateral OTC
  • VM calls settle daily mark-to-market moves whether or not anyone is anywhere near default
Section 11

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

What is the core difference between initial margin and variation margin?

IM protects against what might happen next; VM is a receipt for what already occurred. Getting the direction of that distinction backwards is one of the most common real mistakes in this area.
Question 2

$100 of bond market value, 5% haircut — how much counts as eligible collateral?

Eligible value = market value × (1 − haircut %) = $100 × 0.95 = $95.
Question 3

Economically, what is a repurchase agreement equivalent to?

Legally it's two sales; economically the security acts as collateral for a cash loan, with the price difference between the two legs standing in for interest.
Question 4

What is SOFR actually computed from?

Unlike LIBOR, SOFR isn't a survey or an estimate — it's built directly from real repo trades of exactly the kind covered in this deck, published daily by the New York Fed.
Question 5

A "reverse repo" is the exact same trade as a repo, viewed from which side?

Repo and reverse repo describe the identical transaction from opposite sides — cash borrower calls it a repo, cash lender calls the same trade a reverse repo.
Section 12

What to carry forward

01

Any leveraged position needs a buffer against counterparty default — that buffer is collateral, and it moves according to two distinct rules.

02

Initial margin is forward-looking — sized (via SPAN or ISDA SIMM) to cover a worst-case loss over the close-out window, posted upfront and recalculated as risk changes.

03

Variation margin is backward-looking — exchanged daily (or more often) to settle the realized mark-to-market move, keeping current exposure near zero.

04

A haircut discounts collateral below face value to price in the collateral's own price risk and how long it might take to liquidate — $100 at a 5% haircut counts as $95.

05

A repo is legally two sales but economically a fully collateralized loan — the price gap between the two legs is the interest, the repo rate.

06

SOFR is computed directly from real overnight Treasury repo data — a concrete, factual bridge to the OIS-discounting machinery in Swap Curves & OIS Discounting.

What this now lets you do

Read a margin call and correctly identify whether it's a forward-looking IM top-up or a backward-looking VM settlement. Understand why $100 of posted collateral rarely counts as $100 toward a requirement. Recognize a repo for what it economically is — a secured loan — no matter how its legal paperwork is dressed. And see precisely why SOFR, the rate anchoring modern derivatives discounting, is not an abstraction but a direct measurement of the repo market covered here.
