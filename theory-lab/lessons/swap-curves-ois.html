Swap Curves & OIS Discounting

Government bond yields carry the idiosyncratic scars of whoever happens to be issuing and repo-ing that specific bond. A swap is a pure, unbundled bet on interest rates — which is exactly why swap curves, and increasingly OIS curves, replaced government bond curves as the market's working definition of "risk-free," and why the 2008 crisis forced the industry to split one curve into two.

TL;DR
  • An interest rate swap exchanges a fixed rate for a floating rate on a notional principal that is never itself exchanged — only the net cashflow difference changes hands each period.
  • The swap rate is the par rate: the fixed rate \(K\) that makes the swap worth exactly zero at inception, given today's discount curve.
  • Swap curves — and increasingly OIS curves — replaced government bond curves as the market's standard "risk-free" discounting curve because government bond yields carry idiosyncratic supply, issuance and repo-specialness effects that have nothing to do with the pure time value of money, while a swap is two parties expressing a pure interest-rate view with no bond to borrow or own.
  • Post-2008, the industry moved from LIBOR discounting to OIS discounting for collateralized derivatives because LIBOR (an unsecured interbank lending rate) embeds bank credit risk, while OIS/SOFR (secured, overnight, nearly risk-free) essentially doesn't — the gap between the two blew out from a few basis points to well over 100bp during the crisis.
  • The multi-curve framework formalizes the split: one curve (OIS) discounts cashflows to present value, a separate curve (a SOFR-derived forward curve) forecasts the floating leg's future resets — pre-2008 these were, in practice, the same curve; post-2008 they structurally are not.
  • The worked example prices a 2-year annual-pay swap off a flat 3% OIS curve: the par rate comes out to ≈3.00%, and marking an off-market swap actually trading at a fixed rate of 3.2% gives a value to the fixed-rate payer of about −$38,269 on a $10,000,000 notional — close to zero at the fair rate, meaningfully negative when locked into paying above it.
Section 01

Why this matters

For decades, "the risk-free rate" meant the government bond curve — Treasuries in the U.S., Gilts in the U.K., Bunds in Germany. That made intuitive sense: a government (in its own currency) is about as close to a riskless borrower as exists. But government bond yields are never purely about the time value of money. A specific bond's yield is also a function of how much of it is outstanding, whether it's the current on-the-run benchmark or an older off-the-run issue, how expensive or cheap it is to borrow in the repo market, and temporary supply/demand imbalances around auctions and index rebalancing — idiosyncratic, bond-specific noise layered on top of the genuine interest-rate signal.

A swap has none of that baggage. Two counterparties agree to exchange a fixed rate for a floating rate on a notional amount, with nothing to issue, own, borrow, or repo. The swap rate is, in a real sense, a cleaner, more direct expression of "what does the market think interest rates will average out to over this horizon" than any single bond's yield — which is exactly why trading desks gradually adopted the swap curve, rather than the government bond curve, as their working definition of the risk-free discounting curve, and why — after the 2008 financial crisis exposed a real credit-risk component hiding inside the specific rate (LIBOR) swap curves had been built on — the industry pushed one step further, to OIS.

💡
Plain-English intuition

A swap is a bet on interest rates with nothing else attached

Imagine two companies: one has borrowed at a floating rate and would rather have the certainty of a fixed payment; the other has borrowed fixed and thinks rates are going to fall, so it would rather pay floating. Instead of either company refinancing its actual loan (with all the paperwork, fees, and balance-sheet consequences that involves), they can simply agree with each other: "you pay me a fixed rate on $10 million for two years, I'll pay you whatever the floating rate turns out to be on that same $10 million, and we'll never actually hand each other the $10 million itself — just the net difference each year." That's a swap. Nobody's actual loan changes hands. Nobody has to buy or sell a bond. It's a pure side bet on the path of interest rates, stripped of every other complication a real bond carries.

Because it's stripped of those complications, the rate at which that side bet is agreed to be fair — the swap rate — is a cleaner read on "where the market thinks rates are" than a specific government bond's yield, which is also quietly being pushed around by how scarce that particular bond happens to be this month.

Section 02

The math, step by step

Consider the simplest case: a plain vanilla, fixed-for-floating interest rate swap with notional \(N\), maturity \(T_n\) (in years), and annual payments at times \(t_1,t_2,\dots,t_n\) (so \(t_i=i\) years and \(t_n=T_n\)). One party pays a fixed rate \(K\) on \(N\) each year; the other pays whatever the floating reference rate resets to for that year, also on \(N\). Only the net difference is actually exchanged each period, and \(N\) itself is never paid or received by either side.

Valuing the fixed leg

The fixed leg is the easy half: it's just a known, certain stream of payments \(K\!\cdot\!N\) at each \(t_i\), discounted back to today using the discount factor \(DF(t_i)\) implied by today's discount curve (for now, take that curve as given — the OIS-vs-LIBOR question of which curve to use is exactly the subject of this lesson):

\[ PV_{\text{fixed}} = K\cdot N \sum_{i=1}^{n} DF(t_i) \]
K·Nthe known fixed cashflow paid each period
DF(tᵢ)today's discount factor for the payment at time tᵢ
Σ DF(tᵢ)often called the swap's "annuity factor" or "PV01 basis" — the sum of all discount factors
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

A swap is like two people betting on interest rates without ever touching the underlying pot of money. One agrees to pay a fixed amount each year, the other pays whatever the floating rate happens to be that year, and they only ever hand each other the difference. Valuing the fixed side is easy — it's just adding up a bunch of known future payments, each one shrunk down to today's value, exactly like pricing a bond's coupons.

Valuing the floating leg — a telescoping argument

The floating leg looks harder — its future cashflows aren't known yet, they depend on where the floating reference rate actually resets each period. But in the simplest case, where the same curve is used both to discount cashflows and to project the floating rate's future resets, a classical bond-math argument collapses the entire floating leg to a strikingly simple closed form.

1

A floating-rate note prices at par on its own curve

Consider a floating-rate note (FRN) that pays the reference rate observed at the start of each period on notional \(N\), and repays \(N\) itself at maturity. A standard result in bond math is that such a note is worth exactly its par value \(N\) on every reset date, provided it's discounted using the same curve the floating rate resets off of — each period's coupon is precisely the fair compensation for lending \(N\) forward one more period on that curve, so the whole note telescopes back to being worth \(N\) today.

2

The swap's floating leg = that FRN, minus the principal repayment

The swap's floating leg is economically identical to that FRN, except the notional \(N\) is never actually repaid at maturity (swaps don't exchange notional). So its value is the FRN's value minus the present value of receiving \(N\) back at \(T_n\):

\[ PV_{\text{float}} = \underbrace{N}_{\text{FRN value}} - \underbrace{N\cdot DF(T_n)}_{\text{principal repayment, removed}} = N\big(1-DF(T_n)\big) \]
3

Only the final discount factor survives

Notice what disappeared: every individual period's forward rate cancelled out of the sum, leaving only \(DF(T_n)\), the discount factor to the swap's final maturity. That's the telescoping result — and it depends entirely on the assumption in the box above, that one curve is doing double duty as both the discounting curve and the rate the floating leg resets against. That single assumption is exactly what stopped holding after 2008, which is where the multi-curve framework below picks up.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

The floating side of a swap turns out to have a surprisingly simple value once you do the math: it behaves just like lending someone your money and getting it back at the end, except you never actually get the principal back since a swap never exchanges the underlying amount. That leaves just one clean number — a stripped-down way of saying "the value of not getting your principal back."

The par swap rate

The swap rate quoted in the market is the par rate — the value of \(K\) that makes the swap worth exactly zero to both sides at inception, i.e. \(PV_{\text{fixed}} = PV_{\text{float}}\):

\[ K_{\text{par}} = \frac{1-DF(T_n)}{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n} DF(t_i)} \]

Once a swap has been dealt at some rate \(K\) — whether that's today's par rate or a rate agreed further in the past, now off-market as rates have moved — its mark-to-market value to the party paying fixed is simply the floating leg minus the fixed leg:

\[ V_{\text{payer}} = PV_{\text{float}} - PV_{\text{fixed}} = N\big(1-DF(T_n)\big) - K\cdot N\sum_{i=1}^{n} DF(t_i) \]
K > K_parfixed payer is locked into paying above the fair rate — negative value to the payer
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

The par swap rate is the "fair price" of the bet — the fixed rate where neither side has an edge on day one. If you're stuck paying a fixed rate higher than what's currently fair, your side of the deal is worth less than zero, because you agreed to pay more than the going rate for something the other side would now happily give you cheaper.

Why OIS, not LIBOR: the discounting revolution

None of the derivation above cared which curve \(DF(\cdot)\) comes from — only that one curve is used consistently. For decades, that curve was built from LIBOR (the London Interbank Offered Rate) — the rate at which large banks reported they could borrow unsecured from each other. LIBOR is not risk-free: it embeds the credit risk of lending to a bank without collateral, which is normally small and stable, but is exactly the kind of risk that stops being small and stable in a banking crisis.

LIBOR — unsecured, embeds bank credit risk

An interbank unsecured lending rate. A lender to another bank at LIBOR is taking on that bank's credit risk for the loan's term — normally a few basis points of spread over a genuinely risk-free rate, but that spread is exactly what widens sharply when banks start doubting each other's solvency.

OIS / SOFR — secured, overnight, near risk-free

An Overnight Indexed Swap references an overnight rate (in the U.S., increasingly SOFR — the Secured Overnight Financing Rate, collateralized by Treasury repo) compounded over the period. Overnight and (for SOFR) secured by collateral, it carries essentially none of LIBOR's embedded bank credit risk.

Through 2007–2008, the gap between LIBOR and OIS (the "LIBOR-OIS spread") — normally a handful of basis points — widened dramatically as banks grew reluctant to lend to each other unsecured at any price they trusted, making the spread itself a widely watched real-time gauge of banking-system stress (see the chart below). That episode made unmistakably concrete what had previously been a modeling footnote: LIBOR-discounted derivative valuations were quietly baking a bank credit-risk premium into supposedly "risk-free" discounting, for trades that were fully collateralized (cash posted daily against mark-to-market moves) and therefore carried essentially none of that credit risk in reality. The industry response — what's often called the OIS discounting revolution — was to discount collateralized derivatives at the rate actually earned on posted cash collateral, which for most major currencies is an OIS-family rate, not LIBOR.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

LIBOR is like asking your neighbor for an unsecured personal loan — there's a small chance they never pay it back, so the rate quietly includes some "trust tax." OIS/SOFR is like a loan backed by collateral you could seize if they didn't pay — much safer, so the rate is cleaner and closer to the true, riskless cost of money. After 2008 revealed how much that "trust tax" could suddenly balloon, the industry switched to pricing safe, collateral-backed trades off the cleaner rate.

The multi-curve framework

Pre-2008, the LIBOR curve did double duty exactly as the single-curve derivation above assumed: it discounted cashflows and it forecast the floating leg's future resets, because LIBOR was both the reference index paid on the floating leg and (for an uncollateralized or loosely collateralized world) a reasonable stand-in for the discount rate. Once collateralized trades started being discounted at OIS instead, that convenient coincidence broke: the curve used to discount (OIS) and the curve used to forecast the floating leg's resets (still LIBOR historically; now, post-transition, a SOFR-derived forward curve) are genuinely different objects, built from different instruments, and they no longer move in lockstep.

\[ PV_{\text{float}}^{\text{multi-curve}} = N\sum_{i=1}^{n} DF_{\text{OIS}}(t_i)\cdot \tau_i \cdot F_i^{\text{forecast}} \]
DF_OIS(tᵢ)discount factor from the OIS curve — used only to bring cashflows to present value
F_i^forecastthe forward rate for period i, read off a separate forecast curve (e.g. SOFR-derived)
τᵢthe accrual fraction (year fraction) for period i, from its day-count convention
The one-sentence version: pre-2008, one curve discounted cashflows and forecast floating resets. Post-2008, those are two separate curves — OIS discounts, a forward curve forecasts — and the neat single-curve telescoping shortcut above (\(PV_{\text{float}}=N(1-DF(T_n))\)) stops being exactly correct once the two curves genuinely diverge; each period's forward rate has to be estimated from the forecast curve explicitly, then discounted at the OIS rate.
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Before 2008, one single ruler measured both "how much is this future payment worth today" and "what rate will the floating payment actually pay." After 2008 those turned out to need two separate rulers — one for discounting money back to today, a different one for guessing what the floating rate will actually reset to — because the two jobs are no longer reliably answered by the same number.

Worked example

Pricing a 2-year annual-pay swap off a flat 3% OIS curve

Take a flat OIS discount curve at \(r=3\%\) per annum (annual compounding, matching the swap's annual payment frequency), notional \(N=\$10{,}000{,}000\), and a 2-year annual-pay swap. This is a simplification — a real curve isn't flat and a real desk discounts each collateral currency off its own OIS curve — but it isolates the mechanics cleanly.

1

Discount factors

\[ DF(1) = \frac{1}{1.03} = 0.970874 \qquad DF(2) = \frac{1}{1.03^2} = 0.942596 \]

Annuity factor: \(\sum DF(t_i) = 0.970874+0.942596 = 1.913470\).

2

PV of the floating leg

\[ PV_{\text{float}} = N\big(1-DF(2)\big) = 10{,}000{,}000\times(1-0.942596) = \$574{,}041 \]
3

The par swap rate

\[ K_{\text{par}} = \frac{1-DF(2)}{\sum DF(t_i)} = \frac{0.057404}{1.913470} \approx 3.0005\% \]

Notice this comes out almost exactly equal to the flat curve's own 3% rate — expected, since on a flat curve the par swap rate and the curve's own level should nearly coincide.

4

PV of the fixed leg at the actual traded rate, K = 3.2%

\[ PV_{\text{fixed}} = 0.032\times 10{,}000{,}000\times 1.913470 = \$612{,}310 \]
5

Mark-to-market value to the fixed-rate payer

\[ V_{\text{payer}} = PV_{\text{float}} - PV_{\text{fixed}} = 574{,}041 - 612{,}310 = -\$38{,}269 \]

At the par rate (≈3.0005%), \(PV_{\text{fixed}}\) would equal \(PV_{\text{float}}\) almost exactly, and the swap's value would be close to $0 (the small residual is only rounding) — confirming the par rate is indeed the fair rate. Locked into paying 3.2% instead — 20bp above par — the fixed-rate payer is stuck paying more than the market currently requires, and the swap is worth about −$38,269 to them, or roughly −0.38% of notional.

Real-world trading scenario

The LIBOR-to-SOFR transition

LIBOR's fall from being the default reference rate underlying trillions of dollars of swaps, loans, and mortgages is one of the most concrete, well-documented events in modern rates markets. Two separate problems converged on it. First, the 2008 crisis exposed the discounting issue covered above: LIBOR embeds bank credit risk, which is exactly the wrong thing to be baked into the discount rate for a fully collateralized derivative. Second, and independently, a 2012 scandal revealed that panel banks had been manipulating their LIBOR submissions — LIBOR was set from a small panel of banks self-reporting an estimate of their own borrowing cost, with too little actual transaction volume behind it to police reliably, and regulators concluded the whole benchmark needed replacing rather than patching.

Regulators and industry bodies (in the U.S., the Alternative Reference Rates Committee, convened by the Federal Reserve) settled on SOFR — the Secured Overnight Financing Rate, derived from actual, large-volume overnight Treasury repo transactions, first published by the New York Fed in April 2018 — as LIBOR's replacement. The transition took years: new swaps and loans gradually moved to referencing SOFR through the early 2020s, ISDA published a standardized fallback protocol so that existing LIBOR-referencing contracts would convert cleanly once LIBOR stopped, and most LIBOR panel-rate publication ceased at end-2021, with a handful of USD tenors kept alive in a "synthetic," non-panel-based form only for legacy contracts until final cessation on 30 June 2023.

For a rates desk, the transition meant maintaining two curve worlds in parallel for years — legacy LIBOR-referencing trades needing the old curve until they matured or converted, new SOFR-referencing trades needing the new one — on top of the OIS-vs-forecast-curve split this lesson already covers, and repricing every existing LIBOR-linked contract's fallback terms once LIBOR actually stopped publishing.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

CSA discounting. A collateralized derivative trade is governed by a Credit Support Annex (CSA) specifying what collateral gets posted against mark-to-market moves and in what currency. Standard practice is to discount that trade's cashflows at the rate actually earned on the posted collateral — for cash collateral, that's an overnight rate (SOFR in USD, €STR in the euro area, SONIA in the U.K.) — which is precisely the OIS-discounting principle above, applied trade by trade according to each CSA's specific collateral terms.

Building the forecast curve separately from the discount curve. A rates desk builds its OIS/discount curve from OIS-family instruments, and builds a separate forecast curve (for USD, a SOFR-derived forward curve, typically bootstrapped from SOFR futures and SOFR-referencing swaps) purely to project future floating-rate resets — two distinct curve-construction exercises feeding into the same trade.

Swap spreads as a market signal. The gap between a swap rate and the yield on a government bond of the same maturity (the "swap spread") is itself a widely watched indicator — it moves with balance sheet costs, repo market conditions, and relative supply/demand for government paper versus swaps, separate from the pure interest-rate view either curve is meant to express.

Pricing credit off swaps, not government bonds. Corporate bonds, loans, and mortgages are very often quoted and priced at a spread over the swap curve rather than over government bonds, particularly outside the U.S. — precisely because the swap curve is viewed as the cleaner, more purely interest-rate-driven benchmark to spread a credit instrument against.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where swap valuation goes wrong in practice

Discounting a collateralized trade at LIBOR (or any credit-risky rate) after 2008. Doing so systematically mis-marks the trade relative to how it's actually collateralized — the quantified size of this exact error, once the industry recognized it, is what drove the OIS-discounting migration in the first place.

Using one curve to both discount and forecast, once they've genuinely diverged. The clean telescoping shortcut \(PV_{\text{float}}=N(1-DF(T_n))\) only holds when the discounting and forecasting curves are the same curve. Applying it in a genuine multi-curve setting (OIS discounting, SOFR-forward forecasting) without separating the two curves produces a subtly wrong valuation, not merely an imprecise one.

Treating "swap curve" as literally risk-free. A swap curve — and even an OIS curve — is a practical approximation to risk-free, not a literal one. It still carries its own basis effects: different collateral currencies imply different effective discount rates for the same USD cashflow (cross-currency basis), and even OIS rates in different jurisdictions embed that market's own repo and collateral dynamics.

Day-count and compounding mismatches. Mixing annual vs. semi-annual payment frequencies, or actual/360 vs. 30/360 vs. actual/365 day-count conventions, when comparing swap rates quoted in different markets is one of the most common real errors — a rate that looks like a clean arbitrage across two markets often just reflects an uncorrected convention mismatch, not a genuine mispricing.

✓Swap curves replaced government bond curves as the standard "risk-free" curve because bond yields carry idiosyncratic supply/repo effects a pure interest-rate swap doesn't.

Section 05

Self-test

Q1. Why is no notional exchanged in an interest rate swap, and what does that mean for counterparty risk relative to holding a bond of the same notional?
A swap is purely an exchange of interest-rate cashflows — each side already effectively "has" the notional (it's a reference amount used to size the fixed and floating payments, not principal being lent). Since the principal itself never changes hands, there's no principal at risk if a counterparty defaults — only the net difference of the remaining cashflows is exposed, which is a much smaller amount than the full notional a bondholder would have at risk if the bond's issuer defaulted. This is a large part of why swaps could grow to such enormous notional volumes without being nearly as risky, position for position, as holding bonds of the same face amount.
Q2. What makes a swap rate the "par rate," and how do you solve for it?
The par rate is the fixed rate \(K\) at which the swap's value is exactly zero to both parties at inception — neither side pays anything up front to enter the trade. Algebraically, set the fixed leg's PV equal to the floating leg's PV, \(K\cdot N\sum DF(t_i) = N(1-DF(T_n))\), and solve for \(K\): \(K_{\text{par}} = (1-DF(T_n))/\sum DF(t_i)\), the floating leg's per-unit-notional value divided by the annuity factor.
Q3. Why did the industry move from LIBOR discounting to OIS discounting for collateralized derivatives after 2008?
LIBOR is an unsecured interbank lending rate, so it embeds the credit risk of lending to a bank without collateral — a risk that widened sharply and visibly during the 2008 crisis (the LIBOR-OIS spread spike). A fully collateralized derivative trade, by contrast, carries close to no counterparty credit risk, because collateral is posted daily against mark-to-market moves. Discounting that trade's cashflows at LIBOR was therefore quietly baking in a credit-risk premium that didn't belong in the valuation of a trade that was, in practice, secured. OIS/SOFR — secured, overnight, essentially free of bank credit risk — matches the actual risk (or lack of it) in a collateralized trade far more accurately, which is why the industry moved the discounting convention to it.
Q4. What is the multi-curve framework, and why weren't the discount curve and the forecast curve always different?
The multi-curve framework separates the curve used to discount cashflows to present value (OIS) from the curve used to forecast the floating leg's future rate resets (a SOFR-derived, or historically a LIBOR-derived, forward curve). Pre-2008, LIBOR effectively did both jobs at once — it was both the reference index paid on the floating leg and, in a world without widespread OIS-based collateral discounting, a reasonable proxy for the discount rate too — so the single-curve simplification (one curve discounts and forecasts) held well enough in practice. Once collateralized trades started being discounted at OIS specifically, that coincidence broke: OIS and the floating-rate-forecast curve are built from different instruments and no longer move together, so they have to be treated, and constructed, as two separate curves.
Q5. In the worked example, why is the swap's value close to zero at the par rate but meaningfully negative to the fixed-rate payer at 3.2%?
The floating leg's present value, \(N(1-DF(T_n))\), doesn't depend on the fixed rate \(K\) at all — it's fixed once the discount curve is fixed. The fixed leg's present value, \(K\cdot N\sum DF(t_i)\), scales directly with \(K\). At the par rate (≈3.0005% here), the fixed leg's PV is constructed, by definition, to exactly equal the floating leg's PV — that's what "par" means. At 3.2%, a higher fixed rate than par, the fixed leg's PV is larger than the floating leg's PV, so the party paying that fixed rate is paying more, in present-value terms, than they're receiving on the floating side — a swap worth negative to them, by exactly that gap (about −$38,269 in the worked example).
