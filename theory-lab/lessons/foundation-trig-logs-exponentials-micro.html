Lesson 02 · Foundation Mathematics

Trigonometry, Logs & Exponentials, Cycles and Continuous Growth

Two unrelated-looking topics share this lesson because they show up everywhere later for the same reason: both describe things that behave with mathematical precision — one repeats, one compounds. Sine and cosine describe anything that cycles, from seasons to intraday rhythms. e^x and ln x describe anything that grows continuously, and nearly every pricing model and returns calculation later in this curriculum is built on exactly those two functions. Get comfortable with both here, in plain form, before the notation gets dressed up in Greek letters and finance jargon.

Section 1

A point walking around a circle

Draw a circle of radius 1 around the origin. Pick an angle θ, swept counterclockwise from 3 o'clock. Sine is the height of the point you land on; cosine is how far sideways it is.

Try it — pick an angle
θ = 0 radians. Point (1, 0). cos θ = 1, sin θ = 0 — the starting position, before any walking has happened.
θ = π/4 radians ≈ 0.785. Point (0.707, 0.707). cos θ = sin θ = √2/2 ≈ 0.7071 — an ordinary angle, not lined up with either axis.
θ = π/2 radians ≈ 1.571. Point (0, 1). cos θ = 0, sin θ = 1 — a quarter of the way around, straight up.
θ = π radians ≈ 3.142. Point (−1, 0). cos θ = −1, sin θ = 0 — halfway around, directly opposite where it started.
θ = 3π/2 radians ≈ 4.712. Point (0, −1). cos θ = 0, sin θ = −1 — three-quarters of the way around.
tan θ = sin θ / cos θ — the slope of the line from the center to the point. It's undefined wherever cos θ = 0, exactly at 90° and 270°, where that line goes straight vertical.

This is the whole definition — sine and cosine aren't ratios memorized from a right triangle, they're just the coordinates of a point walking around a circle of radius 1. Every trig identity used later in this curriculum ultimately traces back to this picture.

Section 2

Radians measure the walk, not the wedge

Degrees split a circle into 360 arbitrary pieces — a leftover convention from ancient Babylonian astronomy. Radians measure the actual arc length walked.

2π radians
=
360°

One radian sweeps out an arc exactly 1 unit long on a unit-radius circle. Since the full circumference of that circle is 2π (from C = 2πr with r = 1), one full lap is exactly 2π radians. To convert: radians = degrees × π/180.

°

Degrees

360 equal slices of a circle. Familiar, but arbitrary — nothing in the geometry demands exactly 360.

↻

Radians

Distance actually walked around a unit circle. One full lap is always 2π radians, whatever the circle's real-world size.

∫

Why calculus insists on radians

Every calculus-based trig formula — derivatives of sin and cos, the identity behind the next lesson's complex numbers — is only clean and correct when the angle is in radians.

Where this breaks in practice

Most programming languages' built-in sin/cos functions assume radians. Feed in a raw degree value — "90" meaning 90 degrees — and it silently computes sin(90 radians) instead, a completely different, essentially arbitrary number. No error is raised. Convert first, every time.

Section 3

Walk one full lap, and the wave repeats exactly

Because θ + 2π lands on the same point as θ, sine and cosine repeat their values identically every 2π radians — that's periodicity.

One full period, θ from 0 to 2π
sin(θ + 2π) = sin θ and cos(θ + 2π) = cos θ, always — the wave doesn't approximately repeat, it repeats exactly.
This exact repeating property is the whole reason cyclical data — a seasonal effect, an intraday rhythm, a mean-reverting oscillation — is modeled using sine and cosine waves of different frequencies, added together. Decomposing a messy series into a sum of such waves, across many frequencies at once, is what a Fourier transform does — covered properly in a later Signal Processing lesson, built directly on this vocabulary.
Section 4

e — the number growth settles on

Compound 100% growth over more and more sub-periods within one unit of time, and the result doesn't run away to infinity — it settles on a specific number.

lim (1 + 1/n)ⁿ, as n → ∞
=
e ≈ 2.71828
The natural exponential, e^x
Never touches zero, never turns downward, and gets steeper the further right it goes — the slope keeps increasing.
+

Always positive

e raised to any real power — however negative — never reaches zero or goes below it.

↗

Always increasing

There's no flat spot, no dip. Bigger x always means a bigger e^x.

=

Its own rate of change

e^x's slope at any point equals its own value at that point — a calculus fact, not needed to use the function, but the reason it keeps appearing in growth models rather than an arbitrary base.

Section 5

ln x runs growth backward

The natural logarithm is defined as the exact inverse of e^x — "what power must e be raised to, to get x?"

ln(e^x)
=
x

And running the other way: e^(ln x) = x, defined only for x > 0 — e raised to any power is never zero or negative, so there's no power that produces one.

×→+

Product rule

ln(ab) = ln a + ln b — multiplying two numbers becomes adding their logs.

÷→−

Quotient rule

ln(a/b) = ln a − ln b — dividing becomes subtracting.

ⁿ→×

Power rule

ln(aⁿ) = n·ln a — an exponent inside the log becomes a plain multiplier outside it.

These aren't arbitrary rules to memorize — they fall straight out of the exponent rule e^a·e^b = e^(a+b). Let a = ln x and b = ln y, so x = e^a and y = e^b: then xy = e^a·e^b = e^(a+b), and taking ln of both sides gives ln(xy) = a + b = ln x + ln y. That's the whole derivation of the product rule.

Section 7

Log-returns add up. Simple returns don't.

A simple return is R = (P₁ − P₀)/P₀. A log-return is instead r = ln(P₁/P₀). Chain two periods together and the difference shows up immediately.

Simple returns

Two periods compound as (1+R₁)(1+R₂) − 1. That's not R₁ + R₂ — adding simple returns is only a rough approximation, and a worse one the more volatile the period.

Log-returns

ln(P₂/P₀) = ln(P₂/P₁ · P₁/P₀) = ln(P₂/P₁) + ln(P₁/P₀) = r₂ + r₁ — the product rule turns the price ratio's multiplication straight into addition, exactly.

That's the whole reason log-returns are additive across time: total price ratio P₂/P₀ is the product of the two step ratios — that's just algebra — and the log product rule converts that product directly into a sum of log-returns. No approximation, no compounding correction needed.

Try it — check the identity yourself

Enter any three prices. ln(P₂/P₁) + ln(P₁/P₀) always exactly equals ln(P₂/P₀) — it's an identity, not a coincidence of these particular numbers.

Section 8

Two colleagues, one month, two different numbers

A trader wants a month's performance from 21 daily returns. Two colleagues compute it two different ways.

Colleague A
  • Sums the 21 simple daily percentage returns directly
  • Reports that total as "the month's return"
  • Wrong — and increasingly wrong the more volatile the month was
Colleague B
  • Converts each day to a log-return, sums those 21 numbers
  • Converts the sum back to a percentage with e^(sum) − 1
  • Exact — because log-returns really do sum across time

Colleague A's mistake is silently ignoring that gains and losses compound multiplicatively, not additively — the same "+50% then −50% is a real loss, not a wash" trap from the returns primer elsewhere in this curriculum. Colleague B's extra step, converting back at the very end with e^(·) − 1, is just the inverse of how log-returns were defined in the first place.

Section 9

Why desks actually work this way

%

Log-returns are the default for statistics

Volatility, correlation, regression on a return series — quant desks default to log-returns specifically because they add across time with no compounding correction.

±

They're symmetric, too

A log-return of +x and −x are exact opposites in a way that +x% and −x% simple returns aren't, once you check what price each actually implies.

e

Continuous compounding is the pricing default

A bond's discount factor, an option's risk-free growth term — most "grows at rate r" assumptions in a continuous-time model use e^(rt), because continuous-time calculus is far cleaner than a formula tied to a specific compounding frequency.

〰️

Sine/cosine build every cycle model

A seasonal effect, an intraday pattern — fit a wave of the matching period (or several, of different periods) and check how much variation it explains. Doing that systematically across many frequencies at once is the Fourier transform.

Section 10

The mistakes worth knowing before you make them

Same underlying math, two very different outcomes depending on whether the habit is in place.

Skips the habit
  • Feeds degrees straight into a formula or function expecting radians
  • Sums simple returns across multiple periods
  • Takes ln of whatever value a feed hands it, price or not
  • Quotes a continuous rate and a simple rate as if interchangeable
Builds the habit
  • Converts to radians first — always, before anything calculus-based
  • Sums log-returns, converts back to a percentage only at the end
  • Checks a price is strictly positive before taking its log
  • Converts explicitly: 5% simple ≈ 4.879% continuous, not the same number
Where this needs qualifying

None of these mistakes throw an error — that's what makes them dangerous. A degree fed where a radian was expected, or a summed simple return, produces a number that looks perfectly plausible and is simply wrong. The habit is the only real guard.

Section 11

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Convert 90° to radians.

90 × π/180 = π/2 ≈ 1.571 radians — the point at that angle is (0, 1), straight up from the center.
Question 2

Which log rule turns an exponent inside a log into a plain multiplier outside it?

The power rule is the one that pulls an exponent out of the log entirely, turning it into a multiplier — the other two handle multiplication and division of the log's argument.
Question 3

$500 compounded continuously at 6% for 2 years — ending value?

FV = 500 × e^(0.06×2) = 500 × e^0.12 ≈ 500 × 1.1275 ≈ $563.75.
Question 4

A price moves 50 → 55, then 55 → 55 (no change). Do the two daily log-returns sum to the direct 2-period log-return?

ln(55/50) + ln(55/55) = 0.09531 + 0 = 0.09531, matching ln(55/50) directly — additivity holds exactly, even with a zero-return period.
Question 5

Why is passing a degree value straight into a programming language's sin() function dangerous?

Most built-in sin/cos functions assume radians. "90" meaning 90° gets read as 90 radians — many full laps past 90° — producing an arbitrary, plausible-looking wrong answer with no warning.
Section 12

What to carry forward

01

Sine and cosine are coordinates on a unit circle — (cos θ, sin θ) is the point at angle θ, periodic every 2π radians, which is exactly why cyclical data gets modeled with them.

02

Radians, not degrees, are what calculus-based formulas expect — convert with radians = degrees × π/180 before plugging an angle into anything beyond basic geometry.

03

e^x and ln x are exact inverses — the log rules (product → sum, quotient → difference, power → multiply) turn multiplicative problems into additive ones.

04

e^(rt) is compounding pushed to infinite frequency — it falls straight out of the limit that defines e itself, not a separate formula to memorize.

05

Log-returns add exactly across time; simple returns only approximate that, worse the more volatile the series.

06

A continuously-compounded rate is always a touch smaller than the equivalent simple rate — 5% simple ≈ 4.879% continuous over one year, not interchangeable numbers.

What this now lets you do

Convert confidently between degrees and radians before any calculus-based formula. Recognize e^(rt) on sight as continuous growth, not an arbitrary formula to memorize. Explain why a desk sums log-returns instead of simple returns, and convert correctly between a simple rate and its continuous equivalent.
