Primer 4 · Math Primer

Returns, Log-Returns & the Cost of Volatility

There are two mathematically distinct ways to measure "how much did the price move" — the simple percentage return everyone computes first, and the log return every stochastic model in this series is secretly built on. They agree for small moves and diverge for large ones, and that gap is the reason a volatile strategy compounds slower than its own average return suggests, the reason option pricing assumes prices are lognormal, and the reason this repo scales its volatility bands by the square root of the horizon rather than the horizon itself.

Section 1

The return everyone computes first

The simple return is the plain percentage change — the number on a brokerage statement.

The formula
P₍t₋₁₎ = $100
→
Pₜ = $110
⇒
Rₜ = +10%
Rₜ = (Pₜ − P₍t₋₁₎) / P₍t₋₁₎ = (110 − 100) / 100 = 0.10 = 10%.
Worth keeping: 1 + Rₜ = Pₜ / P₍t₋₁₎ — the "wealth factor" you actually multiply a starting balance by each period. Every compounding calculation later in this primer runs through that one identity.

Neither definition of "return" in this primer is more correct in the abstract — a 10% simple gain is a real, literal 10% more money, which is exactly what a P&L statement should show. But the moment you start chaining periods together, simple returns and log returns behave completely differently, and that difference is the whole reason quantitative models default to log returns almost everywhere.

Section 2

Chain two periods, and a cross term shows up

Up 10% one day, up 10% the next — is that 20% total? No.

Try the arithmetic
1.10 × 1.10
=
1.21
That's a 21% two-day gain, not 20%. The extra percentage point is a cross term: 0.10 × 0.10 = 0.01.

Starting wealth W₀ becomes W₀(1+R₁) after period 1, then W₀(1+R₁)(1+R₂) after period 2. Expand that and the two-period simple return is (1+R₁)(1+R₂) − 1 = R₁ + R₂ + R₁R₂ — the sum of the two returns plus a cross term that only vanishes when one of the returns is exactly zero. Simple returns compound multiplicatively, not additively.

Why this matters

This cross term is exactly what made the classic "+50% then −50% averages to 0%" example misleading if you tried to add the raw percentages. It's the same mechanism, just with a smaller cross term when the moves are 10% instead of 50%.

Section 3

Log returns: built so the cross term disappears

rₜ = ln(Pₜ / P₍t₋₁₎) — the natural log of the same price ratio.

Why it adds exactly
ln(P₁/P₀)
+
ln(P₂/P₁)
=
ln(P₂/P₀)
No cross term, no approximation — an exact identity for any size of move, for any number of periods chained together.

This falls straight out of one logarithm rule: ln(ab) = ln a + ln b. Since P₂/P₀ = (P₁/P₀)·(P₂/P₁), taking the log of both sides gives r₀→₂ = r₀→₁ + r₁→₂. Extend the same telescoping argument to any number of periods and the log return from time 0 to time T is just r₁ + r₂ + ⋯ + rₜ — the plain sum of the one-period log returns in between.

This is the single most useful property in this primer. It's why models that need to reason about returns over different horizons — a day, a week, a month, an option's time to expiry — almost always work in log-return space: addition is a far simpler operation to build statistics on top of than the multiplicative compounding simple returns require.

Section 5

Two different "averages" of a return series

"Average return" is a trap if you don't say which average.

Arithmetic mean R̄

R̄ = (1/T)·Σ Rₜ — the plain average. Answers: "if I picked one period at random, what return would I expect?"

Geometric mean g

Solves (1+g)ᵀ = ∏(1+Rₜ). Answers: "what single, constant per-period return would have turned my actual starting wealth into my actual ending wealth?"

The geometric mean is the number that actually describes compounded performance; the arithmetic mean describes the typical single period. They coincide only when every Rₜ is identical — zero variance. The moment there's any spread at all, g falls below R̄, and it falls further the more variance there is.

The classic case

+50% then −50%: arithmetic mean = (0.50 + (−0.50))/2 = 0% — "nothing happened." Compound it for real: $100 × 1.50 = $150, then $150 × 0.50 = $75. A 25% loss on a 0% average return. Next slide generalizes exactly this.

Section 6

A symmetric round trip always leaves you short

Drag the slider. Whatever the swing size, the ending balance lands below where it started — unless the swing is exactly zero.

Try it
Swing magnitude (±x%)x = 50%
Ending value = 100 × (1 − x²) = $75.00. That's a real loss of 25.00%, despite a 0% arithmetic average return every time.

The −x% is always taken off a bigger base ($150 at x=50%) than the +x% was added to ($100), so the two moves are never true opposites once you're compounding. This is the +50%/−50% worked example, generalized to any swing size — and it's an exact identity, not an approximation.

Section 7

The general version: g ≈ R̄ − σ²/2

The two-period round trip is exact. Across many periods, the same effect shows up as an approximation called volatility drag.

Where it comes from

For small x, ln(1+x) ≈ x − x²/2 (a standard Taylor approximation). Apply it to each period's simple return and average over many periods: the average of Rₜ is R̄, and the average of Rₜ² is approximately σ² (variance, from the statistics primer). Because log returns add across time, the average log return over many periods is the compounded growth rate g, which gives:

R̄
−
σ²/2
=
g
R̄ is the naive average return. σ²/2 is volatility drag — the cost of compounding through variance. g is what your money actually experiences.
Drag grows quadratically

Doubling volatility while holding the arithmetic mean fixed doesn't double the drag — it quadruples it, because the term is σ², not σ. This is exactly why two strategies with identical average returns can compound to very different real outcomes, and it's the same relationship the Kelly Criterion lesson later builds a sizing argument around.

Section 8

Why volatility scales with √time, not time

A direct algebraic consequence of two facts already derived: log returns add, and variances of independent periods add.

The derivation

If one-period log returns are approximately independent, the T-period log return is their sum, r₀→ₜ = Σrₜ. Variance of a sum of independent variables is the sum of their variances (not true of standard deviations — only variances), so if every period shares variance σ²: Var(r₀→ₜ) = Tσ². Standard deviation is the square root of variance, so σₜ = √(Tσ²) = σ√T.

If daily volatility is σ, 4-day volatility is 2σ (not 4σ), and 16-day volatility is 4σ. The time horizon has to increase sixteenfold just to double the volatility.
Where this needs qualifying

This rests on periods being approximately independent. Real markets violate that routinely — calm periods followed by calm periods, turbulent followed by turbulent (volatility clustering). Under positive serial correlation in volatility, naive √T scaling understates true multi-period risk. It's a useful default, not a universal law.

Section 10

How practitioners actually use this

🧮

Quant modeling

Defaults to log returns for volatility, GARCH, regression, and price-path simulation — additivity across time and unbounded range make normality far more defensible.

📑

Performance reporting

Almost always uses simple returns and the geometric mean — a fund's "annualized return" is CAGR, because that's what reconciles with the dollars in the account.

📏

Risk desks & VaR scaling

Scale a 1-day VaR to a 10-day VaR by multiplying by √10 rather than re-estimating — a convenience that collapses if the independence assumption breaks.

Seeing arithmetic mean return and CAGR side by side on a tearsheet is a legitimate, quick volatility-drag check: the bigger the gap between them, the more variance is eating into real compounded performance — independent of whatever the win rate looks like in isolation.
Common pitfall

Any headline "average return" that doesn't specify arithmetic or geometric should be treated as ambiguous — and, whether intentional or not, usually the more flattering of the two numbers.

Section 11

Quick check

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Why can two return series share the same arithmetic mean but compound to very different ending values?

The geometric mean that governs compounded wealth depends on variance as well as the mean. Two series can share R̄ while having very different σ² — the higher-variance one suffers more drag and ends lower, even though its "typical period" looks identical.
Question 2

Why are log returns additive across time while simple returns are not?

Chaining simple returns multiplies wealth factors: (1+R₁)(1+R₂) = 1 + R₁ + R₂ + R₁R₂, a nonzero cross term. Chaining log returns adds the logs of those same factors directly — an exact identity with no cross term, for any size of move.
Question 3

Daily volatility is 1%. Roughly what's the volatility over 16 trading days?

σₜ = σ√T, because variances of independent periods add and standard deviation is the square root of variance. √16 = 4, so 16-day volatility is 4× the daily figure, not 16×.
Question 4

A strategy is +50% one period, then −50% the next. What actually happens to $100?

$100 × 1.50 = $150, then $150 × 0.50 = $75. The −50% is taken off the larger $150 base, not the original $100 — the entire primer in one trade.
Question 5

Why can naive √T volatility scaling break down in real markets?

The √T derivation rests entirely on independence between periods. Real markets show volatility clustering — calm periods follow calm periods, turbulent follow turbulent — and under that positive serial correlation, naive √T scaling understates true multi-period risk.
Section 12

What to carry forward

01

Simple return Rₜ = (Pₜ−P₍t₋₁₎)/P₍t₋₁₎; log return rₜ = ln(Pₜ/P₍t₋₁₎). They're linked by 1+Rₜ = e^rₜ, so rₜ = ln(1+Rₜ) and Rₜ = e^rₜ − 1.

02

Log returns add exactly across time; simple returns pick up a nonzero cross term the moment you chain more than one period.

03

Arithmetic mean describes a typical single period; geometric mean describes what actually happened to your money — they coincide only at zero variance.

04

Volatility drag: g ≈ R̄ − σ²/2, growing quadratically with σ — a 0% average return can still be a real loss, as the +50%/−50% case shows exactly.

05

Volatility scales with √T, not T, because variances — not standard deviations — of independent periods add. This is the exact reason this repo scales daily σ by ×1 / ×√5 / ×√20 for daily, weekly, and 20-day horizons.

06

Both properties assume independent periods — volatility clustering in real markets breaks that assumption, and naive √T scaling then understates true risk.

Convert cleanly between simple and log returns. Spot an ambiguous "average return" claim on sight and know which average would answer the question actually being asked.
