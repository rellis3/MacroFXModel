Purchasing Power Parity

The Law of One Price, applied to whole economies — a real and well-documented anchor for where a currency "should" sit over years, and a famously bad clock for when it gets there.

CONCEPT — a long-run anchor, not a timing signal ~15 min read Prereqs: Interest Rate Parity
TL;DR
  • PPP is the Law of One Price scaled up from a single good to a whole economy's basket: an identical basket should cost the same in every currency, or the exchange rate should move until it does.
  • Absolute PPP (\(S = P_{\text{domestic}}/P_{\text{foreign}}\)) is the strict, rarely-true version; relative PPP (\(\%\Delta S \approx \pi_{\text{domestic}} - \pi_{\text{foreign}}\)) — the currency should depreciate by roughly the inflation gap — is the weaker, more empirically useful version.
  • The real exchange rate (\(S \times P_{\text{foreign}}/P_{\text{domestic}}\)) is the practical "cheap or expensive" gauge: if PPP held exactly it would be constant, but in practice it wanders for years above or below its own average.
  • PPP deviations are real but close so slowly — Rogoff's "PPP puzzle" finds half-lives of roughly three to five years — that it is a hopeless short-run timing signal; a "20% undervalued" reading can widen for years before it narrows.
  • This repo has no PPP-based signal or CPI/price-level data pipeline today — it's a stated gap, not a built brick.

Why this matters

The previous lesson, Interest Rate Parity, showed that the forward FX rate is pinned down almost mechanically by two interest rates and a no-arbitrage condition — a relationship that holds to the penny, continuously, because it's enforced by riskless covered arbitrage. Purchasing Power Parity (PPP) is the other half of classical international finance, and it could not be more different in character. PPP is also a "prices should equalize across currencies" idea, but instead of anchoring a forward rate to interest rates, it anchors the spot rate to relative price levels — the cost of a common basket of goods in each country. And unlike covered interest parity, nothing forces PPP to hold quickly. There is no riskless arbitrage that closes a PPP gap in seconds the way there is for covered interest parity; closing it requires real goods to physically flow, real wages and prices to adjust, and real capital to reallocate, all of which take years. PPP is one of the oldest and best-documented ideas in international economics, and it is also one of the clearest cases in this entire Theory Lab where "the theory is basically right over the long run" and "the theory is nearly useless for timing a trade" are both true at once. Holding those two facts together, without collapsing one into the other, is the actual point of this lesson.

Plain-English intuition

If a coffee costs $3 in the US and £3 in the UK, something has to give

Imagine, ridiculously, that an identical cup of coffee — same beans, same size, same everything — costs exactly $3 in New York and exactly £3 in London, and the GBP/USD exchange rate is 1.50 (one pound buys $1.50). Converting the London coffee's price into dollars, it costs $4.50 — 50% more than the New York coffee, for a genuinely identical good. That's the Law of One Price: once you strip away transport costs, tariffs, and taxes, an identical, freely tradeable good should cost the same everywhere when priced in a common currency, because if it didn't, someone could buy it where it's cheap and sell it where it's dear until the price gap closed. Purchasing Power Parity is just the Law of One Price scaled up from a single good to an entire economy's basket of goods — instead of one coffee, compare the general price level (something like a CPI basket) in each country. If a whole economy's basket is systematically cheaper once converted to a common currency, PPP says either the price levels have to converge, or the exchange rate has to move to make the comparison fair again — the same "buy low, sell high" logic as the single-coffee case, just applied to millions of goods and services and taking a lot longer to actually play out, because you can't physically arbitrage a haircut or a hospital visit across a border the way you can a tradeable commodity.

The math, step by step

PPP comes in two versions — a strict, "in theory" version and a looser, "in practice" version — and the gap between them is exactly where the theory's real-world usefulness lives.

Absolute PPP

The strict version says the nominal exchange rate should exactly equal the ratio of the two countries' price levels:

\[ S = \frac{P_{\text{domestic}}}{P_{\text{foreign}}} \]
\(S\)The nominal spot exchange rate, expressed as units of foreign currency per unit of domestic currency (or however the pair is quoted — the convention just has to stay consistent through the derivation).
\(P_{\text{domestic}}\)The domestic price level — the cost, in domestic currency, of a representative basket of goods and services (conceptually, a CPI index level).
\(P_{\text{foreign}}\)The foreign price level — the cost, in foreign currency, of the same representative basket.

Read it plainly: the exchange rate should be whatever number makes the same basket cost the same amount in either currency. This is the "one price for one basket" idea taken completely literally. It is also, empirically, close to never exactly true, for reasons that are well understood and not particularly mysterious: national consumption baskets differ (a Japanese basket and an Argentine basket don't contain the same goods in the same proportions), a large share of any real economy is non-tradeable (haircuts, rent, a restaurant meal, most healthcare and construction — services that simply cannot be shipped across a border to arbitrage a price gap), and tariffs, transport costs, and taxes drive a further wedge between "priced the same" and "priced the same once you account for friction." Absolute PPP is the clean textbook statement, but it's best treated as a limiting case rather than something you'd expect to hold to the decimal in real data.

Relative PPP — the version that's actually useful

Because absolute PPP requires measuring identical baskets in identical proportions across countries — something real CPI baskets don't do — economists lean on a weaker, more empirically tractable version instead. Take absolute PPP and convert it into a statement about rates of change rather than levels. Starting from \(S = P_{\text{domestic}}/P_{\text{foreign}}\), take logs of both sides and then differentiate (or, more simply, difference) with respect to time. The ratio becomes a difference, and the level becomes a growth rate — inflation is, after all, just the growth rate of the price level:

\[ \%\Delta S \;\approx\; \pi_{\text{domestic}} - \pi_{\text{foreign}} \]
\(\%\Delta S\)The percentage rate of change of the nominal exchange rate over some period (e.g. year-over-year).
\(\pi_{\text{domestic}}\)The domestic inflation rate over the same period (read the Greek letter as "pi" — the growth rate of \(P_{\text{domestic}}\)).
\(\pi_{\text{foreign}}\)The foreign inflation rate over the same period.

This is relative PPP, and it's the version that shows up in almost every practical discussion of PPP because it sidesteps the "identical basket in identical proportions" problem — it never requires the level of prices to match, only that the currency of the higher-inflation country should depreciate by roughly the size of the inflation gap. The intuition: if domestic prices are rising 8% a year and foreign prices are rising 2% a year, and nothing happens to the exchange rate, the domestic economy's goods become steadily, mechanically more expensive relative to the foreign economy's goods every year — a widening Law-of-One-Price violation. Relative PPP says the currency should depreciate by roughly the 6-point gap to keep the relative competitiveness position unchanged, even if the absolute price levels were never comparable to begin with. This is weaker than absolute PPP (it says nothing about whether \(S\) currently equals \(P_{\text{domestic}}/P_{\text{foreign}}\) in levels, only about the direction and rough size of future changes), which is exactly why it survives contact with real data better — a claim about a growth-rate differential is testable and roughly sensible in a way a claim about matching absolute price levels across two different consumption baskets simply isn't.

The real exchange rate

PPP's most useful practical construct is the real exchange rate — the nominal rate adjusted for the two countries' price levels, which turns "is this currency cheap or expensive" into a number you can actually track over time:

\[ \text{Real FX} = S \times \frac{P_{\text{foreign}}}{P_{\text{domestic}}} \]

If PPP held exactly at all times, the real exchange rate would be a constant — nominal moves would always exactly offset the inflation differential, leaving the "real" cost comparison unchanged. In practice, the real exchange rate wanders, sometimes for years, away from any constant, and that wandering is precisely what "a currency is overvalued or undervalued in PPP terms" means: not that the currency has broken some law of physics, but that the real exchange rate currently sits far from its own historical average, on the view that it should eventually revert toward that average as goods markets, wages, and relative competitiveness re-equilibrate. "Overvalued" means the real rate is unusually high relative to its history (domestic goods currently look expensive to foreigners on a PPP basis); "undervalued" means the opposite. The Big Mac Index

The most famous informal illustration of absolute PPP is The Economist's Big Mac Index, published since 1986. The idea is disarmingly literal: a McDonald's Big Mac is a near-identical, standardized product assembled from broadly similar inputs (beef, bread, cheese, labor, rent) in dozens of countries, so comparing its price converted to a common currency gives a rough, easy-to- communicate absolute-PPP check — if a Big Mac costs $5.50 in the US and the equivalent of $3.20 in another country at the current exchange rate, that currency is "cheap" by roughly 42% on a Big Mac basis. It is genuinely useful as popular education and as a rough, transparent cross-check, precisely because everyone understands what a Big Mac is. It is explicitly not a rigorous economic instrument: it's one good (not a representative basket), it embeds a lot of non-tradeable local input costs (rent, labor) that have nothing to do with currency valuation, and it says nothing about timing. Treat it the way its creators intend it — a memorable illustration of the Law of One Price, not a model to trade off.

The PPP puzzle — why it holds so slowly

The honest empirical finding, well established in the international-finance literature, is that PPP deviations are large, persistent, and take a very long time to close. Kenneth Rogoff's widely cited 1996 review coined this the "PPP puzzle": real exchange rates do appear to mean- revert over long enough samples, but the estimated half-life of a PPP deviation is typically found to be somewhere in the range of three to five years — remarkably slow compared to how fast standard macro models suggest prices and nominal exchange rates ought to adjust, and slow enough that it's genuinely difficult to reconcile with plausible degrees of price stickiness alone. The puzzle isn't "does PPP hold" (the reversion shows up reasonably robustly given a long enough sample) — it's "why does it take so absurdly long," given that nothing in standard theory obviously predicts a multi-year half-life. That slowness is the central empirical fact this lesson is built around: PPP is a real, long-run regularity and a hopeless short-run clock, simultaneously.

Real-world trading scenario

"This currency looks 20% undervalued on PPP" — what does that actually license you to do?

Suppose a macro analyst publishes a note: based on the Big Mac Index (or a more careful relative- PPP calculation using FRED-style CPI data), Currency X looks roughly 20% undervalued against the dollar. What does that actually tell a trader, concretely?

On a normal trading horizon — days to a few months — the honest answer is: almost nothing. PPP deviations of 20%, 30%, even more, have historically persisted for years, and have been known to widen further before eventually narrowing — there is no mechanism that stops an undervalued currency from becoming more undervalued next month, next quarter, or even next year. "Cheap on PPP" is not a timing signal, and a short-term trader who shorts the expensive side (or goes long the cheap side) purely because of a PPP reading, with no other entry logic, can be wrong — and lose money — for years before the reversion Rogoff documented ever shows up. Being "right" on a three-to-five-year half-life is cold comfort to a position sized and risk-managed for a multi-week trading horizon; the mark-to-market drawdown along the way can be larger than most trading books are built to absorb, long before the thesis pays off, if it ever does inside the trader's remaining career or capital.

Where the same 20% reading is a genuinely useful, real input: a multi-year strategic FX view, or a hedging decision with a matching time horizon. A corporate treasurer deciding whether to hedge a five-year foreign-currency liability is exactly the kind of decision-maker for whom a slow, multi-year reversion tendency is actually relevant information — they're not trying to time a three-week entry, they're trying to form a view about where a currency is more or less likely to sit, on average, over a horizon long enough for PPP's glacial clock to plausibly matter. The same is true for a pension fund or sovereign wealth fund setting a strategic (not tactical) currency hedge ratio, or a long-horizon asset allocator deciding how much currency risk to run passively. The lesson isn't "PPP is useless" — it's "PPP's usefulness is horizon-conditional," and confusing a multi-year anchor for a trading signal is a well-known way to lose money being directionally correct.

Where this connects to MacroFXModel

This repo has no PPP-based signal or price-level data pipeline today

To be direct about it: nothing in this codebase currently ingests CPI or price-level data, computes a real exchange rate, or builds a PPP-based fair-value estimate for any pair. This is a genuine gap, not a built brick with caveats — there is no pppCore.js or equivalent to point to, and this lesson is not describing something already wired into the forecaster or any backtest engine.

If a PPP-based fair-value view were ever built here, the natural data feed already exists as a named environment variable in CLAUDE.md's data-source table: FRED_KEY, which is listed for "macro / quotes / news data feeds" and is the standard source for national CPI series. Building it properly would mean pulling CPI for each side of a pair from FRED, resampling to a common release frequency (per this repo's data-modeling discipline — never hold a monthly CPI print "live" against daily bars), constructing the real exchange rate above, and — per Lego Principle 5 and this project's honest-teammate contract — proving any resulting signal on a true out-of-sample split with a non-trivial trade count before calling it anything more than a candidate. Given the multi-year half-lives documented above, an honest OOS test of a PPP-based signal would also need a matching multi-year evaluation horizon and a will to accept that most trading-frequency backtests simply cannot validate a signal this slow — a scope/data limitation to state plainly rather than paper over with a lookalike fast-horizon test.

Common pitfalls

Ways PPP gets misused or misunderstood

  • Treating "undervalued on PPP" as a buy signal on a trading horizon. This is the single most common misuse, and the scenario box above walks through exactly why it's wrong: a real, multi-year-average regularity says nothing about the next weeks or months, during which the deviation can easily widen rather than close.
  • Confusing absolute PPP with relative PPP. Absolute PPP (exchange rate equals the ratio of price levels) is a much stronger and less realistic claim than relative PPP (the exchange rate's change tracks the inflation differential). Citing a Big Mac "20% undervalued" reading as if it were a precise, levels-based absolute-PPP fact overstates what a single-good comparison with non-tradeable local input costs can actually establish.
  • Ignoring non-tradeable goods and productivity differences (the Balassa-Samuelson effect). A meaningful share of any CPI basket — rent, haircuts, healthcare, most services — can't be arbitraged across borders at all, and richer, more productive countries tend to have structurally higher price levels for these goods even in long-run equilibrium (higher wages in the productive tradeable sector pull up wages, and therefore prices, in the non-tradeable sector too). A currency that looks "PPP-rich" may partly just reflect this structural effect rather than a temporary mispricing that will revert.
  • Forgetting that PPP deviations can widen, not just narrow. Mean reversion over a multi-year sample is a statement about the eventual, average tendency in the data — it doesn't mean every current deviation is at or near its turning point. Nothing in the PPP literature claims a deviation can't grow larger before it starts to close.
  • Using PPP as a fair-value anchor without checking the horizon of the decision it's informing. The theory doesn't become wrong when applied to a hedging or strategic- allocation decision — it becomes appropriate, because the decision horizon finally matches PPP's own multi-year clock. The error is using the same reading to inform decisions at the wrong horizon, not the reading itself.

Self-test

Q1. Why is relative PPP more useful in practice than absolute PPP?
Absolute PPP requires the exchange rate to equal the ratio of price levels, which in turn requires comparable, identically-weighted consumption baskets across countries — something real CPI baskets don't provide, since countries consume different goods in different proportions and a large share of any basket (services, rent, healthcare) is non-tradeable and can't be arbitraged across borders anyway. Relative PPP sidesteps this by only claiming something about rates of change — that the exchange rate's percentage change should track the inflation differential between the two countries — which doesn't require the absolute levels to ever have matched, or the baskets to be identical, only that the growth rates move together. That weaker, differenced claim survives contact with real, imperfectly comparable data far better than the levels-based version.
Q2. Why can a currency stay "PPP-cheap" for a decade or more?
Because nothing forces the correction quickly. Unlike covered interest parity, which is enforced by near-riskless arbitrage that closes gaps in seconds, PPP is only enforced by slow real-economy channels: relative prices and wages adjusting, trade flows and competitiveness shifting, capital reallocating, and non-tradeable-sector prices catching up. None of that happens fast, and empirical work (Rogoff's "PPP puzzle") finds real exchange rate deviations have half-lives typically estimated around three to five years — meaning it can easily take a decade or more for a substantial deviation to fully close, and there is no mechanism guaranteeing it won't widen further first.
Q3. What does it mean for a currency to be "overvalued" or "undervalued" in PPP terms?
It means the real exchange rate — the nominal rate adjusted for the ratio of the two countries' price levels — currently sits far from its own historical average. "Overvalued" means the real rate is unusually high (domestic goods look expensive to foreigners on a PPP basis); "undervalued" means unusually low. It is a statement about distance from a historical norm with an expectation of eventual reversion, not a statement about a precise, objectively "correct" level, and it carries no information on its own about when that reversion will happen.
Q4. Is the Big Mac Index a rigorous test of PPP? What is it actually good for?
No — it's a famous, informal illustration of absolute PPP, not a rigorous economic instrument. It compares the price of a single standardized good rather than a representative basket, and that good's price embeds a lot of non-tradeable local input costs (rent, labor) that have little to do with currency valuation, which the Balassa-Samuelson effect explains structurally. What it's genuinely good for is popular education and a quick, transparent, easy-to-communicate cross-check on the Law of One Price idea — not for precisely quantifying misvaluation or informing a trade.
Q5. A trader shorts a currency purely because it looks "PPP-rich." What's the realistic risk in that trade, and why might it still be a defensible input for someone else?
The realistic risk is that "PPP-rich" says nothing about timing: the deviation can persist or even widen for years, so the trader can be losing money on a directionally "correct" thesis for far longer than most trading books, risk limits, or careers can tolerate — being eventually right on a three-to-five-year half-life is not the same as being profitably positioned on a normal trading horizon. The same PPP reading is a far more defensible input for a decision-maker whose horizon actually matches PPP's slow clock — e.g. a corporate treasurer setting a multi-year hedge ratio on a long-dated foreign-currency liability, or a long-horizon strategic asset allocator — where the slow reversion tendency is exactly the kind of information that decision needs.
Q6. Does MacroFXModel currently have a working PPP-based fair-value signal? What would it take to build one honestly?
No — there is no PPP module, real-exchange-rate computation, or CPI data pipeline anywhere in this repo today; this is a stated gap, not an existing brick. Building one honestly would mean sourcing national CPI series (FRED is the natural feed, via the already-listed FRED_KEY env var), resampling to the CPI release's true cadence rather than holding a monthly print "live" against daily bars, computing the real exchange rate, and then validating any resulting signal on a true out-of-sample split with a non-trivial trade count — and, given PPP's multi-year half-lives, accepting that the evaluation horizon itself would need to be multi-year to be a fair test, which is a real scope constraint most fast-cadence backtests in this repo aren't built to accommodate.

Further reading
  • Kenneth Rogoff, "The Purchasing Power Parity Puzzle," Journal of Economic Literature, 1996 — the classic, widely-cited assessment of PPP's real-world half-life problem: real exchange rates do appear to mean-revert, but far more slowly (three-to-five-year half-lives) than standard price-stickiness arguments can easily explain.
  • The Economist, "The Big Mac Index" — the long-running, publicly updated informal illustration of absolute PPP using a single standardized good; useful as popular education, explicitly not a rigorous valuation model.
  • Bela Balassa (1964) and Paul Samuelson (1964), independently — the structural explanation (productivity differentials driving non-tradeable-sector prices) for why richer countries have persistently higher price levels even in long-run equilibrium, a key reason absolute PPP doesn't hold even asymptotically.
  • Alan M. Taylor and Mark P. Taylor, "The Purchasing Power Parity Debate," Journal of Economic Perspectives, 2004 — a readable survey of the empirical PPP literature after Rogoff, covering the econometric debates (unit roots, panel methods) around estimating just how slow the reversion really is.
