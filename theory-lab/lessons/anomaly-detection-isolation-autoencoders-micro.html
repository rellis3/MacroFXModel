Anomaly Detection, Without Writing a Threshold by Hand

The earlier lesson on data cleaning caught bad ticks with a rolling median/MAD threshold — a fast, honest, deliberately univariate rule that watches one series at a time. This lesson generalizes that idea: Isolation Forest and autoencoders let a model learn "this doesn't look like the rest" across many variables at once, catching the anomalies that only show up in a combination of features, none of which looks unusual on its own.

The whole idea
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

My data filter already throws out any tick that sits too far from its recent median. What does anomaly detection add?

Your filter looks at one number at a time. Some bad ticks are normal on every single number. Spread normal, volume normal, quote rate normal. It is the combination that has never happened before.

And a one-at-a-time filter can't see a combination.

Right. The lesson has two tools that can. The first is Isolation Forest, from 2008. Its idea is that anomalies are few and different.

How does it use that?

It asks random questions. Pick a feature at random, cut it at a random value, and repeat until the point you care about is alone. A point far from the crowd gets walled off in a couple of cuts. A point buried in the crowd takes many more.

So the number of cuts is the signal.

Averaged over many random trees, yes. Then it is turned into a score between zero and one. Take two hundred and fifty-six points. An ordinary point needs about ten cuts. One that needs only three point two on average scores about point eight. Near one means likely anomaly. Around a half means ordinary.

And the second tool?

An autoencoder. A neural network that squeezes each tick through a narrow bottleneck and then tries to rebuild it. Trained on mostly normal ticks, it gets good at rebuilding normal patterns and stays bad at strange ones.

So the miss is the score.

Exactly. How far the rebuild lands from the original. The quantity it trained on and the anomaly score are the same thing.

Great. So a high score means a bad tick I should delete, or a move I should trade?

Neither, and that is the most important part. The score says unusual. Not wrong. Not a trading opportunity. A real news move can score exactly like a bad print.

What else bites?

Regimes. Train it on a calm year and a volatile but perfectly real market looks like one long anomaly. It has simply never seen anything like it.

So where does it sit on a desk?

At the front of the pipeline, flagging ticks for a person to check before they reach a model or a risk number. It hands you a ranked list, not a verdict.

One line?

These models catch strange combinations that one-number filters miss, and a human still decides what the strangeness means.

Section 1 · How it reached trading

A bad tick, a fat finger, or something worse?

Outlier detection · tick-data cleaning (e.g. Brownlees & Gallo, 2006) and surveillance; Isolation Forest (2008) and autoencoders in the 2010s

How it reached trading

Every feed has bad prints, and every venue sees fat fingers and possible manipulation. Early filters checked one reading at a time. Brownlees & Gallo (2006), for example, drop a tick far from a trimmed mean of its neighbours. That misses a tick whose spread, volume and quote rate are each normal but whose combination is not.

Isolation Forest (2008) and autoencoders brought multivariate checks to data quality and trade surveillance. On a desk they flag ticks to clean and conditions unlike the training data.

Section 2

Where a per-variable threshold stops working

A rolling median/MAD check, from Data Cleaning: Outliers, Bad Ticks & Missing Data, looks at one series at a time.

Spread and volume ordinarily move together — the blue diagonal band is what "normal" actually looks like. The red point's spread is unremarkable on its own, and its volume is unremarkable on its own, so a MAD filter run separately on each series flags neither. But that spread paired with that volume has never co-occurred before — it sits nowhere near the diagonal. Catching that requires reasoning about the variables jointly, which is exactly what a univariate rule structurally cannot do.

Carried forward from the earlier lesson

The MAD-threshold approach isn't wrong — it's deliberately simple, and that simplicity is the right tradeoff for fat-finger prints and stale quotes, one series at a time. Isolation Forest and autoencoders don't replace it; they extend the same "how unusual is this" question into many dimensions at once.

The anomaly (red, left) sits apart from the dense cluster, so a random split is disproportionately likely to fall between it and everyone else — two cuts and it's alone. Section 4

Turning "how many splits" into a score

Average the path length across a whole forest of random trees, then normalize it into a 0–1 score.

h

Path length — h(x)

How many random splits it took to wall this one point off alone in its own leaf, in a single tree.

E

Average path length — E[h(x)]

One tree's path length is noisy — one lucky or unlucky sequence of random cuts. Averaging across the whole ensemble smooths that out.

c

Baseline — c(n)

The expected path length for a typical, unremarkable point among n points — the yardstick every point's average path length gets compared against.

s

Score — s(x,n) = 2^(−E[h(x)]/c(n))

Shrinks E[h(x)] relative to c(n) and the score climbs toward 1. A path length close to c(n) settles the score around 0.5.

Nothing exotic is happening in that formula — it's just a rescaling. A path length far shorter than the typical c(n) pushes the score toward 1 (likely anomaly); a path length close to c(n), the ordinary case, lands the score around 0.5. Scores meaningfully below 0.5 mean this point took longer than usual to isolate — squarely unremarkable.

Section 8

Two mechanisms, one question

Both ask "how hard is this point to explain," just in structurally different ways.

Compare
Random splits
→
Isolated fast (short path)
Few splits to isolate → high anomaly score. No distance or density math, no gradient descent — just recursive random partitioning, averaged over many trees. Fast to train, handles many features at once, needs little tuning.
Compress → rebuild
→
Poor reconstruction
Large reconstruction gap → high anomaly score. A trained neural network, smoothly differentiable — better suited to sequences, images, or high-dimensional embeddings, and its per-feature error is itself a diagnostic.

Isolation Forest is the default workhorse for tabular, many-column data — fast and low-maintenance. Autoencoders earn their extra complexity when the data has structure a tree ensemble handles less naturally, or when inspecting which dimension drove the error matters.

Section 9

A combination that never happened before

One FX tick, three variables: spread sits at the 60th percentile of its own history, volume at the 55th, quote-update frequency at the 65th. Every single variable, on its own, is completely unremarkable — a rolling MAD filter run on each series separately would flag none of them. But wide spreads and heavy quote activity ordinarily co-occur with clearly high or clearly low volume, not a bland middle value — and that specific combination has essentially never occurred in this pair's history. An Isolation Forest would isolate it in a handful of splits; an autoencoder would reconstruct it poorly. Either way, what's flagged is the joint structure — not any one number.
Mandatory honest framing

An anomaly score answers "how statistically unusual is this" — never "this is wrong," and never "this is a trading opportunity." A model trained on one calm regime cannot tell "genuinely wrong" apart from "a different, entirely legitimate regime it has simply never seen before" — both look identical to it: unfamiliar. What counts as unusual-enough-to-act-on is always a human decision made after the score comes back.

Section 10

Where each one actually gets used

Reach for Isolation Forest when…
  • The data is tabular — many columns, one row per observation
  • Training speed and low tuning overhead matter
  • You need a data-quality layer watching dozens of fields on every incoming record
Reach for an autoencoder when…
  • The data is sequential, image-like, or a high-dimensional embedding
  • A smoothly differentiable, inspectable score is worth the extra training cost
  • Knowing which specific input dimension drove the error matters
Where this needs qualifying

Neither method is built, backtested, or validated anywhere in this repo as of this writing — this remains a conceptual, educational treatment, exactly like the full lesson it accompanies. In practice, both methods are fit on a window assumed to be mostly normal and scored as one input into human review, never as an automated accept/reject gate on anything consequential.

Section 11

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Why can a rolling MAD filter, run separately on each variable, miss a real anomaly entirely?

A univariate check only ever asks "is this one number too far from its own recent neighbors." It has no way to notice that a combination of otherwise-ordinary values has never occurred together before.
Question 2

In Isolation Forest, why does an anomaly typically get isolated in fewer random splits than a normal point?

Anomalies are "few and different" — being separated from the crowd, almost any random cut has a good chance of walling them off, while a point buried in a dense cluster needs the whole surrounding crowd carved away first.
Question 3

What forces an autoencoder to learn something other than just copying its input straight through?

Because z is lower-dimensional than x, the network is forced to choose which patterns are common enough to compress efficiently — which is exactly why it stays bad at rebuilding patterns it rarely saw.
Question 4

A model trained only on data from a calm market regime is then run on a volatile-but-entirely-legitimate period. What happens?

Both methods learn "normal" entirely from what they were trained on. A different-but-legitimate regime is unfamiliar in exactly the same way a real error would be — which is why a high score means "unusual," never "wrong."
Question 5

An anomaly score comes back high. What is the one thing that score, by itself, tells you?

A high score is a measure of rarity relative to training data, full stop — deciding whether that rarity means "error," "opportunity," or "legitimate but new" requires human judgment about the specific context.
Your call
Scenario drill · 15 January 2015

Every EUR/CHF tick is flagged at once

You run an Isolation Forest over every incoming FX tick, trained on months of recent data. For years EUR/CHF has traded just above the 1.20 floor the Swiss National Bank has defended. This morning, within minutes, almost every EUR/CHF tick scores near 1: prices far below 1.20, spreads blown out, quote rates unlike anything in the training data. Your pipeline is set to drop flagged ticks automatically. What do you do?

  1. Let the pipeline drop them. The detector says they are anomalies, so they are bad data.
    A score measures rarity, not correctness. A genuine, sharp news-driven move scores exactly like a data error. Dropping these ticks would erase a real move from your prices, your models and your risk numbers.
  2. Stop the automatic drop, have a person check the flagged ticks against the news and other venues, and keep them if the move is real.
    This is the human-judgment step the lesson insists on. The model surfaces a ranked list of unusual ticks; a person decides whether unusual means wrong. Here a quick check shows a policy announcement, not a broken feed.
  3. Buy the move. A score this high means the market is mispriced.
    Nothing about an anomaly score implies the pattern is profitable or repeatable. It describes rarity relative to history. It is not a forecast of edge.
  4. Retrain the detector on today's data so it stops flagging everything.
    Sensible later, once the new regime is understood. A model trained on the calm, floor-defended regime will keep flagging the new one. But retraining in the middle of the event does not answer today's question: are these ticks real?

What happened. On 15 January 2015 the Swiss National Bank announced it was abandoning the 1.20 minimum exchange rate for EUR/CHF. The franc jumped and EUR/CHF traded well below 1.00 within minutes. The ticks were unusual and entirely real. A detector trained on the floor years was right that nothing like it had been seen, and a pipeline that equated "anomalous" with "wrong" would have deleted some of the most important prints of that year.

Section 12

What to carry forward

01

Per-variable thresholds miss multivariate anomalies — a point can be unremarkable on every single feature while its combination has never occurred before.

02

Isolation Forest's core insight: anomalies are "few and different," so random splits isolate them in fewer cuts than a point buried in a dense normal cluster.

03

A shorter average path length pushes the score s(x,n) toward 1 — the anomaly end of the scale; a typical path length settles it around 0.5.

04

Autoencoders compress input through a bottleneck and try to rebuild it — trained mostly on normal data, they get specifically good at reconstructing normal patterns.

05

Reconstruction error ‖x − x̂‖² is the score directly — the same quantity minimized in training, just evaluated on new data.

06

An anomaly score means "statistically unusual," never "wrong" and never "an opportunity" — a model trained on one regime flags any different regime identically, and only a human can tell them apart.

07

How it reached trading: one-variable bad-tick filters first, then Isolation Forest and autoencoders for multivariate data-quality checks and surveillance. A check, not a signal.

What this now lets you do

Recognize the exact moment a per-series MAD filter stops being enough — when the anomaly lives in the combination of variables, not any one of them. Reach for Isolation Forest as the fast, low-tuning default on tabular, many-column data, and for an autoencoder when the data is sequential, high-dimensional, or when inspecting which feature drove the error matters. And read any anomaly score for exactly what it is: a measure of statistical rarity that still needs a human decision layered on top, never a verdict on its own.
