Factor-Based Portfolio Construction, Chosen on Purpose

Alpha vs. Beta Separation split a return stream against one named factor — the market. PCA & Factor Models found how many statistically independent risks a basket really contains, with no economic story attached to what any of them represent. This deck combines both ideas and pushes one step further: name a small handful of economically meaningful factors — a broad market factor, a carry factor, maybe momentum — express every asset's return as a mix of them, and then build a portfolio deliberately around the exposures you actually want, instead of picking assets one at a time and hoping the aggregate works out.

Section 1

Every return, as a few shared exposures

Instead of treating each asset as its own independent unknown, write its return as a weighted mix of shared factors plus whatever's left over.

r_i — asset i's return

The thing every model is ultimately trying to explain, one asset at a time.

β

β_ik — asset i's loading on factor k

How many units of factor k's move show up in asset i's return. This is the number a portfolio gets built around.

f

f_k — factor k's own return that period

Shared across every asset that loads on it — the market's move, the carry factor's move, and so on.

ε

ε_i — idiosyncratic residual

Whatever the named factors don't explain about this one asset. Assumed uncorrelated across assets, once the shared factors are removed.

Setting K=1 and letting f₁ be the market's excess return recovers exactly the Alpha vs. Beta Separation lesson's single-index regression — that lesson is this one's one-factor special case. And where PCA & Factor Models found its factors statistically, purely from correlation structure with no story attached, this lesson names them on purpose — carry, momentum, a broad market factor — so the exposures mean something you can deliberately target.
Section 2

Why bother — the parameter count

A 30-instrument universe, compared directly vs. through 2 shared factors.

Estimate every pairwise covariance
  • N(N−1)/2 pairwise covariances — 435 numbers for N=30
  • Every one of those 435 is its own noisy sample estimate
  • Gets worse, quadratically, as the universe grows
  • Tells you nothing about why two assets move together
Route it through K shared factors
  • N×K loadings + a K×K factor covariance + N idiosyncratic variances
  • 126 numbers for N=30, K=3 — versus 435 direct
  • Grows linearly in N for fixed K, not quadratically
  • Each loading pools information across the same factor series, so it's typically more stable than a lone pairwise estimate

In matrix form: Σ = B F Bᵀ + D, where B is the N×K matrix of loadings, F is how the factors themselves co-move, and D collects the leftover idiosyncratic variances. The win isn't just fewer numbers — it's numbers that are cheaper and more reliable to estimate in the first place.

Section 5

Knowing your exposures vs. choosing them

📊

Attribution answers "what happened"

Alpha vs. Beta Separation and PCA & Factor Models both measure exposure after the fact — how much of a return stream's history is explained by the market, or by an unnamed statistical factor.

🎯

Construction answers "what to build"

Once every candidate asset's loadings are known, you can flip the question: given the loadings, what weights deliver the factor profile I actually want?

🎲

The naive alternative

Pick individual assets that "feel" right and hope the resulting basket's aggregate factor exposure lands somewhere sensible. It usually doesn't, and there's no way to know until after the fact.

→

The target-driven alternative

Specify the exposure profile first — "long carry, zero market beta" — then solve for the weights that deliver exactly that, by construction rather than by luck.

This is the actual payoff of a factor model for construction, not just attribution: it turns "which assets feel right" into "which weights hit this specific, stated target" — a solvable problem instead of a guess.
Section 6

The construction recipe, in three moves

Portfolio-level exposure is just a weighted sum of asset-level loadings — B_p = wᵀB. Construction runs that equation backwards.

1. Target profile q
→
2. Solve Bᵀw = q
→
3. Weights w

With exactly as many assets as factors (N=K), Step 2 is a square linear system with one exact solution — the reference deck's worked example is precisely this case. With more assets than factors (N>K, the realistic case), infinitely many weight vectors hit the same target equally well, so a second objective usually gets layered on top of Step 2 — minimize idiosyncratic risk, cap any single position, or hold the smallest total notional that still hits the target. Which tiebreaker gets used is itself a real modeling choice, not a neutral technicality.

Where this needs qualifying

Every β_ik feeding Step 2 is a regression estimate with its own standard error, not a known constant. A target-hitting weight solve only delivers the target as accurately as the loadings it was built on — noisy inputs, noisy output.

Section 9

The honest trade-off

What a factor model buys you
  • Far fewer parameters, and typically more stable ones, than direct covariance estimation
  • A construction process built on stated, deliberate exposures instead of asset-by-asset guessing
  • A shared, reusable vocabulary — "long carry, neutral momentum" — across an entire desk
What it can quietly miss
  • Idiosyncratic risk — ε_i is assumed uncorrelated across assets, which isn't always true in a real crisis
  • Factor-timing risk — a factor's historical premium can compress once it's crowded, with no warning built into the loadings themselves
  • A factor model is only as good as the factors it includes — which factors make the list is itself a modeling choice, not a neutral default
Fact-check against this repo's actual code

The full lesson states flatly: "no factor loadings are estimated, and no factor-tilted portfolio has been backtested, here." That's now partly stale. js/mve/factorModel.js does fit real loadings — OLS-regressing each instrument's return against a small set of macro factors (real-rate, DXY, risk-appetite, inflation-expectations, liquidity) — and bot/modules/portfolio_beta.py aggregates a live book's exposure to those same factors, weighted by each open position's size and direction. But neither one builds a portfolio: factorModel.js is documented in its own header as "read-only / diagnostic by design — it does not size trades on its own," and portfolio_beta.py only totals up whatever exposure the existing positions already happen to carry. The actual construction step this lesson teaches — solving Bᵀw = q for the weights that deliver a target exposure — is not implemented anywhere in the repo. So: loadings are estimated and portfolio-level exposure is measured, but deliberate factor-based construction remains unbuilt.

Section 10

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

In r_i = Σ β_ik f_k + ε_i, what does β_ik represent?

f_k is the factor's own return; ε_i is the idiosyncratic leftover. β_ik is specifically how many units of factor k's move show up in asset i's return — the number a target-exposure solve is built around.
Question 2

How does this factor model relate to Alpha vs. Beta Separation's single-index regression?

Setting K=1 and f₁ = r_m - r_f recovers exactly r_i - r_f = α_i + β_i(r_m - r_f) + ε_i. The market model is this lesson's one-factor special case, not a separate idea.
Question 3

With exactly 2 assets and 2 factors (N=K), and a non-singular loadings matrix, how many weight vectors exactly hit a stated target exposure?

Bᵀw = q is a square system when N=K — as many equations as unknowns — so a non-singular Bᵀ pins down exactly one solution, the same way a 2×2 linear system pins down exactly one (x, y).
Question 4

A desk wants long carry exposure but zero market-beta exposure. Buying one asset with a high carry loading that also carries a positive market-beta loading...

Any nonzero market-beta loading, scaled by whatever weight is needed to hit the carry target, brings along unwanted market exposure. A second position with an offsetting loading (like the slider's USDJPY leg) is what cancels it out.
Section 11

What to carry forward

01

A factor model writes return as shared exposures plus a residual — it generalizes Alpha vs. Beta Separation's one-factor market model and gives economic names to the kind of statistically-discovered dimensions PCA & Factor Models finds.

02

It's a real parameter-count win: N×K loadings instead of N(N-1)/2 pairwise covariances, and those loadings are typically more stable since every one pools information from the same factor series.

03

Portfolio-level exposure is just a weighted sum of asset-level loadings, B_p = wᵀB — nothing more exotic than a dot product.

04

Construction flips that equation around: state the exposure profile you want, then solve (or optimize) for the weights that deliver it — instead of picking assets and hoping the aggregate works out.

05

N=K gives one exact solution; the realistic N>K case is underdetermined, so a secondary objective (minimize idiosyncratic risk, cap positions) picks one portfolio out of an entire family that all hit the target equally well.

06

It's a simplification, not a free lunch — idiosyncratic risk and factor-timing risk sit outside the exposures themselves, and which factors make the list is a real modeling choice, not a neutral default.

What this now lets you do

Given a set of assets' factor loadings, specify the exposure profile you actually want — "long carry, market-neutral," say — and solve for the weights that deliver it, rather than picking individual assets and hoping the aggregate lands somewhere sensible. Treat carry, momentum, and value not just as descriptive after-the-fact labels but as dials a portfolio can be deliberately built around.
