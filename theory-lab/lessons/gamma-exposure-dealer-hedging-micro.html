Gamma Exposure & Dealer Hedging, Told As Cause and Effect

Every option a dealer sells to the public leaves the dealer needing to rehedge continuously as price moves — and gamma is exactly the rate at which that rehedging has to happen. Sum it across an entire strike ladder and it turns into dealer gamma exposure (GEX): a real, formula-derived read on how much forced buying or selling a given move will trigger, which direction that flow pushes the market, and the price level — the gamma-flip — where the pressure can flip from calming things down to speeding them up.

Section 1

Why sellers end up short gamma, buyers long

Gamma's sign follows directly from which side of the trade you're on — nothing more exotic than that.

+Γ

Long an option → positive gamma

Buying a call or a put, either one, always carries positive gamma. That's true regardless of strike, moneyness, or which type of option it is.

−Γ

Short an option → negative gamma

Writing (selling) an option flips the sign of every one of its Greeks, gamma included. The dealer who wrote it now holds negative gamma on that contract.

🙋

Customers are typically net buyers

The standard assumption: retail and institutional flow buys calls for upside speculation and buys puts for downside protection or speculation — net long options, in aggregate.

🏦

Dealers absorb that demand

To fill customer buy orders, dealers take the other side — writing options — which leaves dealers net short gamma across most of the book.

Same contract, opposite gamma. The customer's long position is exactly mirrored by the dealer's short one — that mirroring is where "dealers are net short gamma" comes from.
This is a standard, usually-reasonable assumption about who's on which side of the trade — not something read directly off a strike's open interest. Slide 4 comes back to exactly how far that assumption can be trusted.
Section 2

A call and a put share the same gamma

Put-call parity — C − P = S − Ke⁻ʳᵀ — forces this, exactly, not approximately.

Delta — differs

The right-hand side of parity, S − Ke⁻ʳᵀ, has a first derivative of exactly 1 with respect to S. So Δcall − Δput = 1 — delta shifts by a constant, and a long call and a long put end up with opposite-signed delta.

Gamma — identical

Differentiate parity twice. S − Ke⁻ʳᵀ is linear in S, so its second derivative is exactly zero: Γcall − Γput = 0. Same strike, same expiry, same gamma — always.

Γcall
=
Γput
Why this matters for GEX

Because gamma is identical for a call and a put at a strike, it's never "calls behave differently from puts" that decides whether hedging flow amplifies or dampens a move — it's purely the dealer's own long/short sign at that strike. Get that sign wrong and the whole downstream read flips.

Section 3

The dealer's sign is an assumption, not an observation

State this plainly

Open interest tells you how many contracts are outstanding at a strike. It does not tell you who holds the long side and who holds the short side. "Dealers are net short this strike" is a standard, usually-reasonable, but genuinely unverifiable assumption layered on top of the raw OI number — not a fact read directly off the data. Different providers assuming differently will disagree on a strike's sign even while agreeing on its raw OI.

Section 4

Building the GEX formula, one step at a time

Γ × OI × 100 × S² × 0.01 looks arbitrary until you build it up piece by piece. Step through it.

Try it
Γ × OI × 100
How many shares the dealer's hedge changes by, per $1 move in the underlying — a share count, not a dollar figure yet. The ×100 is the standard contract multiplier (one contract covers 100 shares/units).
Γ × OI × 100 × S
Multiply by S, the price of one share, and it becomes "how many dollars the hedge position's value changes by, per $1 move" — sometimes called dollar gamma on its own.
Γ × OI × 100 × S × (S × 0.01)
A $1 move means something different for a $20 stock than a $2,000 one, so GEX is quoted per 1% move instead: S × 0.01 is what a 1% move is worth in dollars. Multiplying by that instead of by 1 rescales dollar gamma from "per $1" to "per 1%" — giving Γ × OI × 100 × S² × 0.01.
Nothing in this formula is a new idea — it's unit conversion, applied three times in a row, to a quantity (Γ) the Black-Scholes lesson already derived.
Section 5

Aggregate it, and a flip level appears

Sum signed GEX across every strike in the chain and the result is a genuine function of price — not a fixed number. Below is an illustrative strike ladder, S = 100, using the repo's own (callOI − putOI) × Γ sign convention.

Put-heavy strikes below spot dominate on the left (negative — dealers net short gamma there); call-heavy strikes above spot dominate on the right (positive — net long). The crossing between them is the gamma-flip. Move to the next slide for what living on either side of it actually means.

Section 6

Long gamma dampens, short gamma amplifies

Same underlying move, opposite dealer response, depending purely on which side of the trade the dealer is on.

Hedging flow has the same sign as the move — buying into strength, selling into weakness. Market effect: amplifying / destabilizing. Moves tend to accelerate once they start.

Dealer sells
Price falls

Dealer buys
Hedging flow has the opposite sign to the move — selling into strength, buying into weakness. Market effect: dampening / stabilizing. Moves tend to get faded back toward where they started.

This isn't a preference or a strategy — it's the mechanical consequence of the hedge-flow-rate formula, d(hedge shares)/dS = Γ × OI × 100, applied with the dealer's own signed Γ. A short option's negative gamma flips the sign of the required hedge trade relative to the move itself; a long option's positive gamma doesn't.

Section 7

The gamma-flip level — an estimate, not a fixture

Above the flip

Aggregate dealer gamma is net positive. Stabilizing hedging flow dominates — dealers, on net, sell into rallies and buy into dips, which tends to compress realized volatility.

Below the flip

Aggregate dealer gamma is net negative. Destabilizing hedging flow dominates — dealers, on net, chase the move in either direction, which tends to expand realized volatility.

Two honest caveats

First, the flip is estimated from the current OI distribution and the assumed dealer signs — it shifts every time OI at any strike changes or the front-month expiry rolls, not a fixed constant painted on a chart. Second, it inherits the same unverifiable-sign problem as any single strike's GEX: if the true mix of who's long and who's short differs from the standard assumption, the true flip differs from the estimated one too.

A "wall" isn't sellers lying in wait — it's the point where the hedging demand that had been fuelling a move runs out because the hedge is already complete. When a wall breaks instead, delta jumps faster than the existing hedge accounted for, and that same mechanism reverses into a squeeze.

Drag the slider. The curve is Γ(K) ≈ φ(d₁)/(Sσ√T) — the same Black-Scholes gamma the Ito's Lemma / Black-Scholes lessons derive — plotted across a strike ladder around a fixed S = 100.

This is why the final trading day before expiry behaves so differently from the two weeks before it: Γ ∝ 1/√T, so shrinking T matters disproportionately more once T is already small. It's also why a large near-dated ATM strike can move a book's total GEX more than a much larger but far-dated or far-OTM strike — gamma, not size alone, decides how much a strike's OI matters.

Section 10

How this actually gets used — and where it's over-trusted

📊

A daily positioning read

Data services estimate aggregate dealer gamma from public OI using exactly the sign conventions above, publishing it alongside price as context, not a trade signal on its own.

🧭

A regime filter

Net-short vs. net-long dealer gamma is used to set expectations for how a given order-flow size should behave — amplified or dampened — layered on an existing setup, not traded standalone.

📐

The flip as a structural pivot

Some desks track the estimated flip price precisely because price action either side of it is theorized to differ in character — while still treating the level as an estimate that moves, not a fixed line.

Common pitfalls

Open interest is lagged — public OI typically reflects the previous session's close, so any GEX estimate built on it describes yesterday's book. Dealer positioning is assumed, not observed — every GEX number and flip estimate inherits the sign-convention uncertainty from Section 3.

Genuinely unsettled

Whether apparent "pinning" toward high-OI strikes is an active-trading pattern or a pure hedging byproduct is a real, open question in academic and practitioner research — not resolved in either direction. Treat any confident claim that it's "definitely" one or the other with real skepticism.

Section 11

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Why do a call and a put at the same strike and expiry have exactly the same gamma?

C − P = S − Ke⁻ʳᵀ is linear in S, so differentiating both sides twice gives exactly zero on the right — Γ_call = Γ_put, always, not approximately. Delta differs by exactly 1 instead, since the first derivative of a linear function is a nonzero constant.
Question 2

Does open interest, on its own, tell you whether dealers are net long or net short a strike?

"Dealers are net short this strike" is a standard, usually-reasonable assumption layered on top of raw OI — it isn't something the OI number itself reveals.
Question 3

In the typical case (dealer short gamma), what happens to hedging flow as price rises?

Short gamma flips the sign of the required hedge trade relative to the move itself: buying into strength, selling into weakness — adding fuel to whichever direction is already happening.
Question 4

Why does ATM gamma accelerate — not just grow steadily — as expiry approaches?

Because gamma depends on 1/√T, the same number of remaining days matters far more when there are few of them left — the reason the final trading day carries disproportionately more hedging intensity than an equivalent one-day shift three weeks out.
Question 5

What actually happens at the gamma-flip level?

It's the price at which the aggregate signed GEX across the whole chain sums to zero — an estimate that moves every time OI changes at any strike or an expiry rolls, not a fixed line and not a guarantee of anything about price itself.
Section 12

What to carry forward

01

Any option that's bought carries positive gamma; any option that's written carries negative gamma — dealers absorbing customer buying end up net short gamma across most of the book, an assumption about who's on which side, not a directly observable fact.

02

Calls and puts at the same strike and expiry have identical gamma, by put-call parity — it's the dealer's long/short sign, never the option type, that decides whether hedging flow amplifies or dampens a move.

03

GEX rescales gamma into dollars-per-1%-move — Γ × OI × 100 × S² × 0.01 — the unit that lets strikes, underlyings, and expiries actually be compared to each other.

04

Short dealer gamma pushes hedging flow the same direction as price, amplifying moves; long dealer gamma pushes it the opposite direction, dampening them — buy the dip vs. sell the rip, mechanically, not by choice.

05

The gamma-flip level is where aggregate signed dealer GEX crosses zero — an estimate that shifts with every OI change and every expiry roll, not a fixed line on a chart.

06

Gamma isn't linear in time to expiry — it scales as 1/√T, so the last few days before expiry carry disproportionately more hedging intensity than the weeks before them.

What this now lets you do

Read a GEX-by-strike chart and know what the bars actually represent — signed dealer exposure, not "support and resistance" in the technical-analysis sense. Explain why a level can hold on the way in and then accelerate straight through once broken, without needing a story about sellers changing their minds. Treat any specific GEX figure or flip level as a same-day estimate built on yesterday's open interest and an unverifiable sign assumption — useful context, not a certainty.
