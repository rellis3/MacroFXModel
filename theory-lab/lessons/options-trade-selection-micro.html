Lesson 4 · Trading Options

From View to Trade, or Why Being Right Isn't Enough

An option pays only if you are right about direction, size and timing, and by more than the market has already charged you for. This guide shows how to read that charge and choose an expiry and strike against it.

Risk

Education, not advice. All numbers are illustrative. A bought option can lose its whole premium and a sold option can lose far more. Size every trade as if the worst case will happen.

The whole idea
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

I think this stock rises about four percent over the next four weeks. So I buy a call and wait. Done?

Not yet. A call is not a bet on direction alone. It is a bet on direction, size and timing, and the market has already priced its own guess at all three. What does the market think the stock will do in four weeks?

Go up or down, I suppose.

Wrong question. The right one is how far, in either direction. Take a hundred-dollar stock with twenty-two percent implied volatility. Over twenty-eight days a typical move, one standard deviation, is about six dollars. And the at-the-money straddle, a call plus a put, costs about four dollars eighty-six. That is the market's price for the size of the move.

So my four percent is smaller than the market's typical move?

Yes. The market already expects more wobble than your target. So direction alone is not an edge. Your edge is that you think the centre of the distribution sits higher than the market does, and that gap has to pay for the premium.

And the call itself?

About two fifty at the money, twenty-eight days. Break even is a hundred and two fifty-one at expiry. A handy rule: an at-the-money option costs roughly four tenths of a typical move, whatever the expiry.

Which expiry, then? My view is four weeks.

Not four weeks. If you buy exactly four weeks and the move turns up on day thirty, the option is dead. Let the expiry outlast your window with room to spare. Eight weeks costs about three fifty-nine. It loses a bit under one percent of its value a day to time decay, against nearly two percent a day for the four-week option.

So longer is just better?

Longer is more robust. It costs more cash and has more sensitivity to volatility. If everything happens on schedule the two do about the same. The longer one wins when you are late.

And the strike?

Further out of the money is cheaper but needs a bigger move. Five percent out of the money at eight weeks costs about a dollar sixty-six and needs the stock to rise nearly seven percent by expiry just to break even. Cheap is not the same as good value.

What about volatility itself?

You are paying twenty-two percent. If you think the stock will really move at eighteen, you are paying about a fifth more than the call is worth to you. And watch events. Before earnings, implied volatility is high, then it collapses. In our example a call gains only fourteen percent on a five percent rally, and loses forty percent if the stock just sits there.

Anything else?

Costs. Five cents of spread on a three-fifty option is nothing. Five cents on a twenty-two cent option is nearly a quarter of what you pay. And remember that averages are not ceilings. In April twenty twenty-two Netflix reported its first fall in subscribers in over a decade. Commentary at the time put the options-implied move near ten percent. The shares fell about thirty-five percent in a day.

So the one-line version?

Before you buy an option, write down direction, size, timing and what you are paying for each. Trade it only if your numbers beat the market's by more than the costs.

Section 1

A view has four parts, and the price knows two of them

"I'm bullish" is not enough to choose a contract. Write the view in four parts.

🧭

Direction

Up or down. The easy part, and the only part most beginners write down.

📏

Size

How far. The market prices its own answer through implied volatility.

⏳

Window

By when. The market prices this too, through the time left to expiry.

🎚

Confidence

How sure you are, and what would prove you wrong. This sets how much you risk.

The premium is a bookmaker's quote. You do not win by agreeing with it. You win only if your own numbers beat it by more than its margin.

Read the market's quote before you pick a contract

A call plus a put at the same strike pays the size of the move. The average size, ignoring sign, is √(2/π) ≈ 0.8 standard deviations. Black-Scholes agrees: $4.86. This is the market's price for "how far, either way".
R = (K + C − S) / M₁

≈ 0.4 at the money
R is the breakeven distance measured in implied moves. An at-the-money option costs about 0.4 of a typical move at every expiry, so R is about 0.4. Strikes further out need a larger R: cheap means "needs a bigger move".

Your +4% target is smaller than the market's typical move of 6.1%. Direction alone is not an edge: your edge is that the centre of the distribution sits higher than the market thinks.

A 28-day option is worth zero if the stock is still at the strike on the day your window ends. A longer option costs more cash but loses a smaller share a day and survives a late move. You pay for the days you hold, not the days you buy.

A far strike has a big percentage return if it works and a small chance of working. It also has the widest spread relative to its price. A strike set at your target pays nothing if you are exactly right.

Section 5

The volatility you pay, and the event that crushes it

Illustrative: a $100 stock the day before earnings. 30-day at-the-money call at 50% implied volatility costs $5.79.

If volatility stayed at 50%
  • Stock +5%: call worth $8.70
  • Gain: $2.90
After the event, 30%
  • Stock +5%: call worth $6.63, a gain of only $0.84 (14%)
  • Stock unchanged: call worth $3.45, a loss of −40%

Right direction, small gain: the earnings move was priced in advance, and its volatility vanishes the moment the event passes. Compare the straddle with your own estimate of the move. In the main example, paying 22% when you expect 18% means paying 21% more for the same call.

Where this needs qualifying

Real strikes have their own implied volatility (the smile and skew), real moves have fat tails, and volatility moves after you buy. The numbers show the shape of the trade-offs, not prices to trade at.

Section 8 · It actually happened

Netflix, 20 April 2022: not a ceiling

It actually happened

Netflix reported after the US close on 19 April 2022 that it had lost about 200,000 subscribers, its first fall in over a decade, having guided to adding about 2.5 million. Market commentary put the options-implied move near ±10% (a single figure from commentary, so approximate). The shares closed down about 35% on 20 April, at $226.19, taking roughly $54 billion off its market value, per Bloomberg that day. A put struck 15% below the pre-report price, outside the "expected move", ended deep in the money.

Section 9

Where choosing a contract quietly goes wrong

🎯

Strike at the target

Exactly right pays nothing. Put the breakeven inside the move you expect.

🎟

Cheap far strikes

Cheap because unlikely. A 15% out-of-the-money call needs R of 1.77, and a $0.05 spread is 22.6% of its price.

⏱

Expiry equals window

A late move turns a correct view into a total loss.

📣

Paying peak volatility

An event inside the option's life: its volatility vanishes when it passes.

Δ

Delta as probability

A 0.30-delta call is not a 30% chance of profit. Breakeven sets that.

🧮

Sizing by contracts

Size by the maximum loss you chose in advance, not by the number of contracts.

None of these is a reason to avoid options. Each is a reason to put a number on the trade before you click.
Section 10

The pre-trade options checklist

01

Write the view in four parts: direction, size, window, confidence, and what proves you wrong.

02

Compute M₁ and the straddle for your expiry. Is your move bigger, or is the centre higher?

03

Expiry beyond the window. Note theta as a share of premium and the cost of holding flat.

04

Breakeven and R inside your expected move. Never the strike at the target.

05

Implied against realised and against your forecast. Any event inside the life?

06

Spread as a share of premium, open interest, volume. Work a limit order.

07

Size by the worst case you chose, not by margin or contract count.

08

Write the profit target, the thesis stop and the time stop before you trade.

Section 11

Quick check

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

The at-the-money straddle on a $100 stock costs $6. Roughly what is the market's one-standard-deviation move?

The straddle is about 0.8 of a standard deviation, so M₁ ≈ 6 / 0.8 = 7.5.
Question 2

You expect a stock to reach exactly $110 and you buy a 110-strike call. What do you get if you are exactly right at expiry?

The call is worth spot minus strike at expiry, which is zero at 110. The premium is lost. The breakeven is the strike plus the premium.
Question 3

Your window is four weeks. Which expiry best protects you against a late move?

An expiry beyond the window survives a delay, and it loses a smaller share of its value each day. It costs more cash, which is the price of that protection.
Question 4

A stock rises 5% after earnings, but your call gains only 14%. The most likely reason is:

The event's volatility was in the price before the report and vanished after it, cancelling most of the gain from the move.
Question 5

Why is a far out-of-the-money call usually poor value even though it is cheap?

The low price reflects a low chance, measured by R. A fixed spread is also a larger percentage of a small premium.
Your call
Scenario drill · an illustrative day before earnings

You expect good results and the weekly straddle costs $8

This is a made-up situation with invented numbers, to practise the arithmetic. A stock is at $100 and reports after tomorrow's close. You expect good results and a rise of about 4%. The seven-day at-the-money straddle costs $8.00, so the market is pricing an average move of about 8% either way (about 72% annualised volatility). The seven-day call costs $3.99. Your past studies of this stock's earnings moves suggest they average 4 to 5%. What do you do?

  1. Buy the seven-day at-the-money call. Direction is all that matters.
    Breakeven is about $104, exactly where you expect the stock to go, so there is no edge before costs. And if the stock does rise 4% while volatility falls to 30% after the report, the call is worth only about $4.34, a gain of $0.35 on $3.99 before the spread. You can be right on direction and make almost nothing.
  2. Compare your expected move with the straddle first. The market is charging $8 for a move you expect to be about half that, so pass, or use a smaller defined-risk structure.
    The straddle is the average absolute move the market is pricing. You expect roughly half of it. Unless you can show the move will exceed the implied one, or that the direction is unusually predictable, you are overpaying for the volatility. "No trade" is a position.
  3. Buy a three-month call instead, to avoid the volatility crush.
    Longer options carry less event premium, so there is less to crush, and a late reaction is survivable. But you still pay for the same direction view, with more vega and more cash at risk, and a one-day event is a poor reason to own three months of time.
  4. Sell the straddle: your history says the stock moves 4 to 5% and the market prices 8%.
    Averages favour the seller here, but the loss when it goes wrong is a multiple of the premium collected and on the call side has no limit. One gap of 20 or 30% costs many quiet events' profits. The short-premium lesson later in this series covers exactly this trade, and sizing for it.

What this shows. This is an illustrative scenario with invented numbers, not a real episode, so there is no outcome to report and no source to cite. The arithmetic is real: a $100 stock with a $8.00 seven-day straddle implies about 72% annualised volatility. After a report, with volatility at 30% and six days left, the same call would be worth about $4.34 after a 4% rise and about $8.07 after an 8% rise (the size the straddle was pricing). Only a move at least as big as the quoted one pays well, which is why the comparison comes before the trade.

What this now lets you do

Take any view and turn it into a contract on purpose: read the market's expected move, score each strike by its breakeven, choose an expiry that survives your own timing error, and know the volatility and costs you are paying before you click. If the numbers do not beat the market's by more than the costs, you can say "no trade" and mean it.
