Regime-Switching State Space Models

Fusing the Hidden Markov Model's discrete hidden regime with the Kalman filter's continuous hidden state — and the approximation, Kim's filter, that makes tracking both at once computationally possible at all.

CONCEPT — not built or tested anywhere in this repo ~18 min read Prereqs: Hidden Markov Models & Regime Detection, Kalman Filter & Adaptive Relationships
TL;DR
  • A Markov-switching state-space model lets the Kalman filter's own parameters — how fast the continuous state drifts (\(Q\)), how noisy the observation is (\(R\)), even the relationship itself (\(A, H\)) — depend on a hidden discrete regime \(S_t\) that follows its own Markov chain, exactly as in the Hidden Markov Model lesson.
  • Filtering this exactly is computationally intractable: because the regime is hidden, every step must in principle branch over every possible regime the system could have been in, and those branches multiply — after \(T\) steps there are \(N^T\) regime paths to track, an exponential blowup.
  • Kim's filter (Kim, 1994) makes this tractable by collapsing the \(N^2\) regime-pair-conditional estimates produced at each step back down to just \(N\), via a moment-matching approximation — combining several Gaussians that disagree into one Gaussian whose mean and variance match the mixture's, at a real, quantifiable cost whenever the underlying branches truly disagreed.
  • The worked example collapses two regime-conditional estimates that disagree on the mean by 0.20, and shows the collapsed variance (0.0130) exceeding either input branch's own variance (0.004 and 0.010) — the concrete cost of forcing disagreeing branches into a single Gaussian.
  • This lesson builds nothing new: every idea here is the Hidden Markov Model lesson's discrete regime and the Kalman filter lesson's continuous state, fused — read those two lessons first, not as a suggestion but as the actual prerequisite content this one assumes.
Section 01

Why this matters

Two lessons in this curriculum each modeled a hidden quantity, but in very different ways. The Hidden Markov Model lesson tracked a fully discrete hidden regime — trending or choppy — with observations drawn directly from a per-regime Gaussian, no continuous drift involved. The Kalman filter lesson tracked a fully continuous hidden state — a drifting hedge ratio — but assumed one single, fixed set of dynamics (one \(Q\), one \(R\), one linear relationship) held for the entire series.

Real relationships often want both properties at once. A hedge ratio might genuinely drift slowly and predictably during a calm, range-bound period — small \(Q\), tight observation noise \(R\) — and then become erratic and fast-moving during a risk-off or crisis period, where \(Q\) should be much larger and the relationship itself may even weaken or shift sign. Neither tool alone captures this: a pure HMM has no notion of a continuously drifting quantity within a regime, and a pure Kalman filter has no notion of a discrete, switching set of dynamics governing how it drifts. A regime-switching (Markov-switching) state-space model is exactly the fusion: the continuous state still evolves by a Kalman-style recursion, but which set of Kalman parameters is "active" at any moment is itself governed by a hidden discrete regime, evolving by the same kind of Markov chain the HMM lesson built. This is precisely Hamilton's Markov-switching idea (cited at the end of the HMM lesson) extended from a simple regression's mean to a full continuous state-space model.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Two guessing games, nested inside each other

Picture running the Hidden Markov Model's "which weather regime is it" guessing game and the Kalman filter's "where exactly is the boat" tracking game simultaneously, with the two guesses feeding each other. Your current best guess of the boat's exact position depends on which weather regime you currently believe you're in — in calm weather you trust the boat to drift slowly and predictably, in a storm you expect it to move erratically and fast. But your belief about which weather regime you're in also depends on how well each regime's implied drift pattern actually matches the radar pings you've been receiving — if the boat has been moving in a way that looks calm and predictable, that's itself evidence you're probably in the calm regime, not the storm. Each guess sharpens the other, round after round: better regime beliefs sharpen the position tracking, and better position tracking sharpens the regime beliefs. That circularity is exactly what makes this model powerful and exactly what makes it hard to compute exactly.

Section 02

The math, step by step

Reuse both prior lessons' notation directly. The hidden discrete regime \(S_t \in \{1, \ldots, N\}\) follows a Markov chain with the same transition matrix \(A\) from the Hidden Markov Model lesson (\(A_{ij} = P(S_t = j \mid S_{t-1} = i)\), every row summing to 1). Conditional on which regime is active, the continuous hidden state \(x_t\) follows a Kalman-style state-space model — but with regime-specific parameters:

\[ x_t = A_{S_t}\, x_{t-1} + w_t, \qquad w_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, Q_{S_t}) \qquad\qquad y_t = H_{S_t}\, x_t + v_t, \qquad v_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, R_{S_t}) \]
A_{S_t}, H_{S_t}the state/observation coefficients — now indexed by which regime is currently active
Q_{S_t}, R_{S_t}the process/observation variances — also regime-specific: a calm regime might pair small Q with small R, a crisis regime large Q

Compare this line-by-line with the Kalman filter lesson's \(x_t = x_{t-1} + w_t\), \(y_t = H_t x_t + v_t\): the recursion's shape is unchanged, only every coefficient now carries a regime subscript, and which regime is "active" is itself the HMM lesson's hidden \(S_t\), evolving by \(A\). There are \(N\) complete sets of Kalman parameters — one full \((A_k, Q_k, H_k, R_k)\) per regime \(k\) — and the model switches between which set governs the continuous state, according to the discrete Markov chain.

Why exact filtering blows up exponentially

If \(S_t\) were actually observed at every step, this would be trivial: at each time step, just look up which regime is active and run the ordinary Kalman filter using that regime's parameters, exactly as in the Kalman filter lesson. The entire difficulty is that \(S_t\) is hidden — the filter never gets to see which regime was actually active, only the resulting continuous observation \(y_t\), which was itself shaped by whichever regime happened to be true.

Because of this, a filter that wants to be exactly correct must, in principle, keep a separate Kalman estimate for every possible regime path that could have led to the current step — because the correct estimate of \(x_t\) genuinely differs depending on which sequence of regimes actually occurred. At \(t=1\), there are \(N\) possible values for \(S_1\), so \(N\) regime-conditional Kalman estimates must be tracked. At \(t=2\), each of those \(N\) branches must further split over the \(N\) possible values of \(S_2\), giving \(N \times N = N^2\) branches. By time \(T\):

\[ \text{Number of regime paths to track} = N^T \]
N^Texponential in the number of time steps — computationally infeasible beyond a handful of steps for any N ≥ 2

Even for a small, 2-regime model (\(N=2\)), a mere 30 time steps already implies over a billion regime paths — hopelessly infeasible to track exactly, and the problem only gets worse from there. This is the exact same "exponentially many hidden paths" issue the Hidden Markov Model lesson's forward algorithm and Viterbi decoding were built to sidestep for a purely discrete hidden state — but here the continuous state \(x_t\) riding along inside each regime path makes the equivalent trick harder, because there's no way to simply reuse a single running sum the way the forward algorithm does; each regime path carries its own distinct Kalman mean and variance that would need to be tracked separately, forever, without an exact way to merge them.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine trying to track a friend's route through a city where, every single block, they might turn left or right, and you honestly don't know which — so to be exact, you'd have to keep a separate, complete map for every possible sequence of turns they could have made so far. After a couple of blocks that's a handful of maps. After thirty blocks, it's over a billion maps. Nobody can actually carry that many maps around, which is exactly the wall exact regime-switching filtering hits.

Kim's filter: collapse the branches, don't track them all

Kim's filter (Kim, 1994, cited below) keeps the computation linear in \(T\) instead of exponential, by approximating rather than tracking every path exactly. At each time step, after the \(N\) branches from the previous step are each propagated forward through all \(N\) possible current regimes (producing \(N \times N = N^2\) regime-pair-conditional Kalman estimates, one per "came from regime \(i\), now in regime \(j\)" combination), Kim's filter collapses the \(N\) branches that share the same current regime \(j\) back down into a single approximate Gaussian, before moving to the next step. This keeps the running total at exactly \(N\) branches at every step, no matter how large \(T\) grows — the same "collapse the exponential tree back down to something manageable" idea that motivated the forward algorithm's running sums and Viterbi's dynamic programming in the Hidden Markov Model lesson, here applied to a continuous state instead of a discrete label.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Instead of carrying every possible map of your friend's route forever, Kim's filter does something more practical: at the end of every block, it looks at all the maps that currently agree your friend is standing on, say, the same corner, and merges them into one best-guess map for that corner — throwing away the "how did we get here" detail and keeping only "where are we now, and how sure are we." Do that merge after every single block, and you never end up holding more maps than there are corners, no matter how long the walk gets.

The collapse itself is a moment-matching approximation: given \(N\) different Gaussian estimates that all currently claim to describe "the state, given we're now in regime \(j\)" — one estimate per possible previous regime \(i\) — replace all \(N\) of them with a single Gaussian whose mean and variance match the overall mixture's mean and variance, weighted by each branch's own posterior probability:

\[ \hat{x}_t^{(j)} = \sum_i \pi_{i\mid j}\, \hat{x}_t^{(i,j)} \qquad\qquad P_t^{(j)} = \sum_i \pi_{i\mid j} \left[ P_t^{(i,j)} + \left(\hat{x}_t^{(j)} - \hat{x}_t^{(i,j)}\right)^2 \right] \]
x̂_t^(i,j), P_t^(i,j)the mean/variance of the branch that came from regime i and is now in regime j — one ordinary Kalman estimate per (i,j) pair
π_{i|j}the posterior probability of having come from regime i, given the system is now in regime j — the collapse weights, summing to 1 over i
x̂_t^(j), P_t^(j)the single collapsed Gaussian that replaces all N branches feeding into regime j

Read the collapsed variance formula as two separate pieces added together — and recognize the pattern: it is exactly the law of total variance (total variance = the weighted average of each branch's own variance, plus the variance of the branches' means around the collapsed mean). The first term, \(\sum_i \pi_{i\mid j} P_t^{(i,j)}\), is simply the weighted average of how uncertain each individual branch already was. The second term, \(\sum_i \pi_{i\mid j}(\hat{x}_t^{(j)} - \hat{x}_t^{(i,j)})^2\), is new uncertainty manufactured by the collapse itself — it only appears because the branches disagreed with each other on the mean, and forcing several different opinions into one number necessarily loses information about how much they actually disagreed. If all \(N\) branches happen to agree closely, this second term is close to zero and the collapse costs almost nothing; if they disagree substantially, the collapse inflates the reported uncertainty to (partially) reflect that lost distinction.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Suppose two witnesses both say your friend is "near the corner" — one says exactly at the corner, the other says a block away. If you have to boil their two statements down into a single best guess, the honest thing to do isn't just to average their locations and call it a day; you also have to admit you're now less sure than either witness was individually, because they disagreed. That extra dose of "we're less sure because our sources disagreed" is exactly the second term in the collapsed-variance formula — merging opinions that disagree is never free.

Worked example

Collapsing two disagreeing branches into one Gaussian

Suppose \(N=2\) regimes, "calm" and "turbulent." At some time step \(t\), before collapsing, two regime-conditional Kalman estimates both ended up in the same current regime \(j=\text{calm}\), having arrived there from two different previous regimes:

Branch from \(i=\text{calm}\) (stayed calm): \(\hat{x}_t^{(1,j)} = 1.30\), \(P_t^{(1,j)} = 0.004\), posterior weight \(\pi_{1\mid j} = 0.75\).
Branch from \(i=\text{turbulent}\) (switched to calm): \(\hat{x}_t^{(2,j)} = 1.10\), \(P_t^{(2,j)} = 0.010\), posterior weight \(\pi_{2\mid j} = 0.25\).

Collapse them into a single Gaussian for regime "calm":

\[ \hat{x}_t^{(j)} = 0.75(1.30) + 0.25(1.10) = 0.975 + 0.275 = 1.250 \]

Now the collapsed variance — the weighted average of the branches' own variances, plus the weighted variance of their means around the new collapsed mean:

\[ \underbrace{0.75(0.004) + 0.25(0.010)}_{\text{weighted average of } P^{(i,j)}} = 0.0055 \qquad\qquad \underbrace{0.75(1.250-1.30)^2 + 0.25(1.250-1.10)^2}_{\text{weighted variance of the means}} = 0.0075 \]
\[ P_t^{(j)} = 0.0055 + 0.0075 = 0.0130 \]

Notice the collapsed variance, 0.0130, is larger than either individual branch's own variance (0.004 and 0.010) — even though it's a weighted average of two numbers that are both smaller than it. That's not an arithmetic mistake; it's the entire point of moment matching. The two branches disagreed meaningfully about the mean (1.30 vs. 1.10, a 0.20 gap), and once they're forced into a single Gaussian, that disagreement itself becomes extra uncertainty in the collapsed estimate — a genuine, quantifiable cost of the approximation. Had the two branches agreed closely (say both near 1.28), the second term would have been tiny and the collapse would have cost almost nothing.

A named scenario

A carry-trade hedge ratio that behaves differently in calm carry regimes vs. risk-off unwinds

Consider a hedge ratio between a carry-trade currency pair and a related rate-sensitive instrument, where the relationship is plausibly stable and slow-drifting during ordinary, low-volatility carry regimes — small \(Q\), tight \(R\), much like the Kalman filter lesson's original worked example — but becomes fast-moving and noisier during a risk-off unwind, when carry trades get closed in a rush and the relationship itself can temporarily weaken. A regime-switching state-space model would let the hidden discrete regime (inferred, not hand-set by a volatility threshold) select which \((Q, R)\) pair governs the continuous tracking at any moment, rather than forcing one fixed \(Q\) to serve both calm and crisis conditions equally badly. This is presented purely as an illustration of what the fused model is for — it is explicitly not a claim that this specific setup has been built, backtested, or shown to outperform either a plain Kalman filter or a hand-set regime rule in this repo. It is also worth flagging plainly that this model multiplies the Kalman filter lesson's own \(Q\)/\(R\)-tuning caution across every one of its \(N\) regimes at once, rather than resolving it.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

Regime-switching state-space models are a standard tool in macro-econometrics, most thoroughly developed in Kim & Nelson's textbook (1999, cited below) — used there for problems like modeling GDP growth with a hidden expansion/recession regime (directly extending Hamilton's original Markov-switching idea, cited in the Hidden Markov Model lesson) where the growth rate's own dynamics, not just its mean, differ by regime. In finance, the same fused idea shows up wherever a factor loading, a hedge ratio, or a volatility-like state plausibly has genuinely different dynamics across a small number of distinct market conditions, rather than one dynamics profile stretched to cover all of them.

Kim's filter is specifically the standard workhorse for making the likelihood of such a model computable at all — since fitting the model's parameters (the transition matrix \(A\) and every regime's Kalman parameters) by maximum likelihood requires being able to evaluate that likelihood efficiently, and Kim's collapsing approximation is what makes that tractable instead of exponential. A genuine alternative worth naming, closing the loop back to the first lesson in this category: a particle filter can also approximate this same fused model, representing the joint regime-and-continuous-state uncertainty with a swarm of particles instead of collapsing branches via moment matching — trading Kim's filter's collapse-approximation bias for the particle filter's Monte Carlo sampling variance instead. Neither is free of approximation; which one is preferable depends on the specific problem.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where the fusion gets over-trusted

Treating Kim's filter's output as exact. It is a genuine approximation — the moment-matching collapse loses real information whenever the regime-conditional branches actually disagree, exactly as the worked example showed a collapsed variance (0.0130) exceeding both individual branch variances. A model built on this filter can meaningfully understate the true uncertainty in periods where the regime is genuinely ambiguous.

An explosion of free parameters. Each of the \(N\) regimes needs its own \(Q\), \(R\), and possibly its own \(A\) and \(H\), on top of the \(N \times N\) transition matrix itself — a large parameter count relative to most sample sizes. This compounds the Hidden Markov Model lesson's own warning that adding states always raises the fitted likelihood without that alone being evidence a richer structure is real; here the overfitting risk is worse, because every added regime brings an entire extra set of continuous-state dynamics with it, not just a mean and variance.

The same lookahead trap as the smoother and the HMM. A regime-switching model produces both smoothed regime probabilities and smoothed continuous-state estimates when run over a full historical dataset — and, exactly as in the Kalman smoother and Hidden Markov Model lessons, those smoothed quantities use future data and must never be substituted for what a live, forward-only filtered estimate would have produced at the time.

Reaching for this model because it's more general, not because the data demands it. A model that can represent more possibilities is not automatically a better one — it is automatically a more overfittable one. Per this project's working discipline, added complexity has to earn its place with real out-of-sample evidence beating a simpler incumbent (a plain Kalman filter, or a hand-set regime rule), not merely with theoretical elegance.

Worth remembering: this model is the most parameter-hungry of the three covered in this category. Before reaching for it, be honest about whether the data actually supports \(N\) separate regimes' worth of continuous-state dynamics, or whether a single-regime Kalman filter — or a hand-set regime classifier feeding a single Kalman filter — already captures what matters with far fewer moving parts.
Section 04

Key Takeaways

✓A Markov-switching state-space model fuses the HMM's discrete regime with the Kalman filter's continuous state — the continuous dynamics' own parameters (\(Q\), \(R\), even \(A\)/\(H\)) depend on which hidden regime is currently active.
✓Exact filtering is intractable — the number of regime-conditional Kalman branches needed multiplies by \(N\) every step, reaching \(N^T\) after \(T\) steps.
✓Kim's filter (1994) collapses \(N^2\) branches back to \(N\) every step via moment matching — a weighted mean plus a law-of-total-variance-style weighted variance-of-means term — keeping the computation linear in \(T\) instead of exponential.
✓The worked example's collapsed variance (0.0130) exceeded both input branches' own variances (0.004, 0.010) — the concrete, quantifiable cost of forcing two disagreeing Gaussians into one.
⚠Kim's filter is an approximation, not an exact answer. It loses real information whenever regime-conditional branches genuinely disagree — the worse the disagreement, the more it costs.
⚠Concept lesson only, and the most parameter-heavy of the three in this category. Nothing here is built, tested, or validated in this repo. Explaining the fusion is not a claim it produces edge — and its parameter count compounds the overfitting risk already flagged separately in the Hidden Markov Model and Kalman filter lessons.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. What, specifically, is allowed to depend on the hidden regime \(S_t\) in a Markov-switching state-space model?
The continuous state's own dynamics — the process variance \(Q\), the observation variance \(R\), and potentially the state/observation coefficients \(A\) and \(H\) themselves — are each indexed by the currently active regime, \(Q_{S_t}\), \(R_{S_t}\), etc. The underlying recursion (predict, then update) is unchanged from the plain Kalman filter; only which specific set of parameters governs it at each moment is now regime-dependent.
Q2. Why can't the filter simply look up which regime is active and run the ordinary Kalman filter with that regime's parameters?
Because the regime \(S_t\) is hidden, not observed — the filter never actually knows which regime was truly active at any step, only the resulting continuous observation \(y_t\). If \(S_t\) were observed, this would reduce to exactly that simple lookup; the entire difficulty of this model comes from having to infer the regime and the continuous state simultaneously, each from data shaped by the other.
Q3. Explain concretely why exact filtering requires N^T regime paths after T steps.
At each time step, every branch currently being tracked must itself split into \(N\) new branches — one for each possible value the next regime could take — because the correct continuous-state estimate genuinely differs depending on which regime path actually occurred. Starting from 1 branch at \(t=0\), this gives \(N\) branches after step 1, \(N^2\) after step 2, and by induction \(N^T\) after \(T\) steps — the branching multiplies the count by \(N\) at every single step rather than adding to it.
Q4. In the worked example, why is the collapsed variance (0.0130) larger than both of the two branches' individual variances (0.004 and 0.010)?
The collapsed variance is the sum of two terms: the weighted average of the branches' own variances (0.0055) plus a second term (0.0075) reflecting how much the branches' means actually disagreed (1.30 vs. 1.10). That second term only appears because moment matching must account for the spread between the branches' means, not just their individual spreads — collapsing two Gaussians that genuinely disagree on location manufactures new uncertainty to (partially) represent the information that's being discarded by forcing them into one Gaussian.
Q5. What would happen to the collapsed-variance formula's second term if the two branches being merged agreed almost exactly on the mean?
It would shrink toward zero, since that term is the weighted variance of the branches' means around the collapsed mean — if the means are nearly identical, there's almost no disagreement to account for, and the collapsed variance would reduce to close to just the weighted average of the branches' own individual variances. This confirms the collapse only "costs" something when the branches genuinely disagreed in the first place.
Q6. Name two separate honest reasons to hesitate before reaching for a regime-switching state-space model over a plain Kalman filter or a hand-set regime classifier.
First, it is the most parameter-heavy of the three tools in this category — every added regime brings an entire extra set of continuous-state dynamics (its own \(Q\), \(R\), possibly \(A\)/\(H\)), on top of the transition matrix, a real overfitting risk relative to typical sample sizes. Second, even the standard estimation method (Kim's filter) is only an approximation, not an exact likelihood — so both the model's added complexity and its estimation method carry costs that a simpler incumbent doesn't, and per this project's working discipline, that complexity needs genuine out-of-sample evidence before it earns a place over something simpler.
