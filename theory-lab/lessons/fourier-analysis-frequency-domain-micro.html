Fourier Analysis, One Frequency at a Time

Every time series so far in this curriculum has been read one way: value after value, in calendar order. Fourier analysis asks a completely different question of the exact same numbers — forget when each value happened, and ask instead which repeating cycles, added together at different speeds and strengths, would reconstruct this series. That shift, from the time domain to the frequency domain, is the whole idea. The periodogram is the practical tool built on top of it, and it comes with one honest, easy-to-miss caveat that this deck spends real time on.

The whole idea
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

People talk about weekly cycles and monthly cycles in markets. How would I know if one is really there?

Think of a piano chord. A microphone records one messy wave, but your ear hears the separate notes. Fourier analysis is that ear for a time series. It rewrites the series as a sum of pure waves at different speeds, and tells you how strong each one is.

How does it find them?

For each speed, it multiplies the series by a test wave of that speed and adds up the results. If the series really contains that speed, the products line up and the total is big. If it doesn't, they point every which way and cancel to nothing.

Give me an example.

Ten days, two trading weeks, with a pattern that repeats every five days. Test the wave that does two cycles in ten days, and the total comes out at five. Test any other speed, and it comes out at exactly zero.

And the chart people show?

That's the periodogram. Square the total and divide by the number of days: twenty-five over ten, two and a half. One tall bar, at two cycles in ten days, which is a cycle every five days. It found the period without being told.

So I run it on my returns and look for the tall bars.

Two warnings. First, a single bar is very noisy, and more data does not fix that. More data gives you more, finer bars, each just as noisy. A periodogram from ten thousand points is as jumpy, bar for bar, as one from a hundred.

So how do you trust a spike?

Average. Cut the data into segments, compute a periodogram for each, and average them. A real cycle survives the averaging. A noise spike melts away.

And the second warning?

On raw returns there's usually nothing to find. Clive Granger and Oskar Morgenstern looked at stock prices in 1963 and found spectra close to white noise: flat. The tool earns its keep on volume, volatility and seasonal series, like an intraday volume cycle around the session opens.

Anything else it can't do?

It tells you which cycles are in the whole sample, never when they happened. That is what wavelets fix. The one-line version: Fourier tells you which rhythms are there and how loud, but a single spike is a hint, not proof.

Section 1 · How it reached trading

Is there really a cycle in this series?

How it reached trading

Markets were full of claimed cycles but had no test for them. In the 1960s Clive Granger brought the periodogram to economics: stock-price spectra looked close to white noise (with Morgenstern, 1963), and economic series shared a "typical spectral shape" with power at low frequencies (1966).

The FFT later became an option-pricing engine: Carr & Madan (1999) priced a whole strike grid from the characteristic function in one pass, now standard for calibrating Heston-type models.

Section 2

Two lenses on the same series

No information is lost moving from one view to the other — it's a rewrite, not a summary.

x_t — time domain
Fourier transform ⇄
X(f) — frequency domain
Time domain

The series as you're used to seeing it: one value per timestamp, plotted left to right against the calendar. A rolling average and an autocorrelation function both live here — they answer "how does today relate to a few days ago."

Frequency domain

The exact same series, rewritten as a list of cycles: for every candidate speed of oscillation, how strongly (and with what timing offset) is that cycle present. A periodogram lives here — it answers "which repeating rhythm, if any, dominates."

Any reasonably well-behaved series — a stretch of daily returns, an hour of trading volume, a chord played on a piano — can be decomposed the same way: as a sum of pure sine waves, each at its own frequency, each with its own strength and timing offset. A line chart that looks like unstructured noise can still have one or two frequencies quietly doing most of the work underneath.
Section 3

A chord is several notes at once

The discrete Fourier transform is the mathematical version of an ear that separates them back out.

Play a chord and a microphone records one messy-looking waveform — no obvious structure to the eye. But your ear (and a spectrum analyzer) can tell you exactly which individual notes are sounding together, because each note is a pure tone at its own frequency and the chord is nothing more than those tones added up. X(f) = Σ x_t · e^(−i2πft) does this for any series: multiply by a candidate frequency's wave and sum. A frequency genuinely present reinforces itself into a large X(f); one that isn't there cancels toward zero.
〜

Every candidate frequency gets checked

For N observations, the transform is evaluated at N discrete frequencies f_k = k/N — every "speed" the sample could possibly resolve.

✓

Present → constructive

If the series truly wiggles at frequency f, multiplying by that frequency's wave lines the terms up in the same direction and they add to something large.

✗

Absent → cancellation

If the series has nothing at frequency f, the terms point in essentially random directions around the complex plane and mostly cancel to near zero.

½

Only half the frequencies are new

Because the input is real-valued, X(f_k) and X(f_{N−k}) mirror each other — only frequencies up to the Nyquist bin, k = N/2, carry independent information.

Section 7

The periodogram, precisely

X

X(f) — the transform

A complex number per candidate frequency: how strongly, and with what timing offset, that frequency is present in the series.

²

|X(f)|² — power spectral density

Drop the timing offset, keep only the strength: the squared magnitude is how much of the series' total variance sits at frequency f.

I

I(f_k) = (1/N)|X(f_k)|² — the periodogram

The one-sample estimate you can actually compute: normalize the power by sample length N, so series of different lengths are comparable.

A 10-point synthetic series, x_t = cos(2π·2t/10) — a clean period-5 cycle disguised as a list of numbers.

Every drop of the series' variance lands in a single spike at k=2 — a cycle repeating every 10/2 = 5 steps, exactly the period built into the synthetic data, recovered without ever being told the answer in advance. A real series would add noise on top and the spike would be less than perfectly clean — which is exactly the honest caveat two slides from now.

Section 8

What a periodogram reveals in practice

Dominant cycles show up as peaks, standing above a flat noise floor.

📊

A tall spike = a dominant cycle

Trading volume with a real weekly rhythm, or a macro series with real seasonality, shows up as an outsized bar at the matching frequency — far easier to spot there than by eyeballing a line chart.

🕐

An intraday liquidity cycle

Hourly FX volume with an Asia-session lull and a London/New York pickup repeats on a strict 24-hour clock — exactly the kind of calendar-driven structure a periodogram is built to surface.

📉

Flat ≠ broken

A close-to-flat periodogram across most frequencies isn't a failed analysis — for raw FX or equity returns, it's the expected, near-random-walk result, echoing what the ACF/PACF and Hurst/variance-ratio lessons already find from other angles.

🎯

Better suited to volume than to returns

This tool earns its keep on volume, realized volatility, and seasonality series far more reliably than on raw price returns, where there's usually very little periodic structure to find in the first place.

Section 10

The honest caveat: it isn't a consistent estimator

This is the single most important thing to know before trusting a periodogram plot.

The caveat to remember

Away from f=0 and the Nyquist frequency, each periodogram ordinate I(f_k) is approximately (true PSD/2) times a chi-squared random variable with 2 degrees of freedom — and that spread does not shrink as N grows. Adding more data buys more, finer-spaced frequencies to look at (the previous slide's Δf); it does not make any single bin's estimate more precise. A periodogram from 10,000 points is exactly as noisy, bin for bin, as one from 100.

One raw, unsmoothed spike
  • Statistically noisy regardless of sample size
  • A tall bar is a hint, not a confirmed cycle
  • Easy to overstate — "I found a 17-day cycle" from one short sample carries very little weight alone
Smoothed or averaged
  • Bartlett's or Welch's method: average periodograms from several segments
  • Or smooth neighboring frequency bins together
  • Trades away some frequency resolution for real statistical precision

This trade is exactly analogous to how a rolling average trades timing precision for less noise. A single sharp spike surviving after averaging across several independent segments is real evidence; the same spike appearing only once, unsmoothed, is much weaker evidence of a genuine cycle.

Section 11

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

What does moving from the time domain to the frequency domain actually change?

The Fourier transform is a rewrite, not a summary — X(f) encodes exactly as much information as x_t, just organized by frequency instead of by time.
Question 2

A periodogram shows one tall spike at the frequency corresponding to a 7-day cycle. What does that spike mean?

A spike is evidence of concentrated energy at that frequency — strong evidence once smoothed or replicated, but on its own just a hint, per the honest-caveat slide.
Question 3

Why is the raw periodogram called "not a consistent estimator" of the true power spectrum?

A consistent estimator's variance shrinks toward zero as N grows. A periodogram ordinate is roughly chi-squared-with-2-df distributed regardless of N — more data buys finer bins, not a sharper answer at any one of them.
Question 4

Sampling once per day (f_s = 1/day) for N = 200 days, what's the periodogram's frequency resolution Δf?

Δf = f_s / N = 1 / 200 = 0.005 cycles per day — the spacing between adjacent frequency bins the periodogram can distinguish.
Question 5

A colleague computes one unsmoothed periodogram on 90 days of raw FX returns and reports "a strong 17-day cycle." How much should that be trusted on its own?

Two independent reasons for skepticism stack here: raw FX/equity returns typically produce a close-to-flat spectrum, and any single unsmoothed ordinate carries a wide sampling distribution no matter the sample size.
Your call
Scenario drill · an illustrative afternoon

A monthly cycle in EUR/USD returns?

A colleague computes one raw periodogram of two years of EUR/USD daily returns. Most bars are low and jagged, but one bar, at a period of about a month, stands clearly above the rest. They want to build a trade around the monthly cycle. What do you suggest?

  1. Trade it. It is the tallest bar in the spectrum.
    Each raw periodogram bar is roughly chi-squared with 2 degrees of freedom: very noisy on its own. Among dozens of bars, one will always stand tallest, cycle or not. The tallest bar is not yet evidence.
  2. Split the data into segments, average their periodograms, and see whether the spike survives, keeping in mind that raw return spectra are usually close to flat.
    Averaging across segments (Bartlett or Welch style) is the standard way to trade resolution for precision. A real cycle survives it; a noise spike melts. And the prior is against a cycle in raw returns: the classic finding is a near-flat spectrum.
  3. Get two more years of data and recompute the raw periodogram to sharpen the spike.
    More data gives you more, finer-spaced bars, not a less noisy bar. The raw periodogram is not a consistent estimator: each ordinate stays just as noisy however long the sample.
  4. Drop it, and drop spectral analysis for FX altogether.
    Right about this spike being weak evidence, but the tool is still useful on other FX series. Volume and volatility have real calendar cycles, such as the intraday pattern around the session opens, and a periodogram is well suited to those.

What the lesson says. This is an illustrative case, not a dated episode. The lesson's honest finding is that raw FX and equity return periodograms tend to be close to flat, going back to Granger and Morgenstern's spectral studies of stock prices in 1963, and that a single unsmoothed spike is a hint, not a confirmed cycle. Smooth or average first; then, even if a cycle survives, it still has to become a tested rule before it is a signal.

Section 12

What to carry forward

01

Any series can be rewritten as a sum of sine waves — the frequency domain is a lossless rewrite of the time domain, not a summary.

02

The periodogram I(f_k) = (1/N)|X(f_k)|² is the practical, computable estimate of how much energy sits at each frequency.

03

A tall spike above a flat baseline is how a dominant cycle — a weekly pattern, a seasonal rhythm, an intraday session cycle — actually shows up.

04

Frequency and period are reciprocals (f = 1/T), and frequency resolution is Δf = sampling rate ÷ N — more data sharpens which cycles you can tell apart.

05

The raw periodogram is not a consistent estimator — its variance doesn't shrink with N, so a single unsmoothed spike is weak evidence on its own.

06

Smoothing or averaging across segments (Bartlett's, Welch's) trades frequency resolution for the statistical precision the raw version lacks.

07

Spectral analysis reached markets through Granger in the 1960s, whose first finding was mostly noise; the FFT's main desk job today is option pricing (Carr & Madan, 1999).

What this now lets you do

Read a periodogram plot correctly: spot a genuine dominant cycle, translate its frequency back into an intuitive period, and know exactly how much to trust — or distrust — a single spike before treating it as a real finding.
