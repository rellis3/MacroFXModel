Girsanov's Theorem, or How to Legally Ignore μ

The Black-Scholes lesson priced an option using the risk-free rate r instead of the underlying's real expected return μ, and called the result a "risk-neutral probability" without fully explaining what that meant. This is the lesson that explains it: what a probability measure actually is, how Girsanov's 1960 theorem lets you legally re-weight one into another, and why that specific re-weighting — not a convenient shortcut — is what makes option pricing possible without ever having to forecast the market.

The whole idea
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

The option pricing formula never asks me where I think the stock is going. That has always bothered me. Isn't the expected return the most important number there is?

For a forecast, yes. For a price, no. And there is a theorem that says exactly why. Igor Girsanov proved it in 1960.

Go on.

Take a stock you think grows eight percent a year on average, with twenty percent volatility. Cash pays four percent. That is the real world: your actual odds.

Fine.

Girsanov says you can keep the same stock, the same volatility and the same set of possible paths, and just reweight how likely each path is. Make the high paths a little less likely and the low paths a little more likely, until the average growth is four percent, the same as cash.

That sounds like cheating. You have just thrown my view away.

It is not a different stock. Nothing that could happen becomes impossible, and nothing impossible becomes possible. Only the odds sheet changes. Mathematicians call the two odds sheets equivalent.

How far do you have to shift?

By the extra return per unit of risk. Eight minus four is four points. Divide by twenty percent volatility and you get zero point two. That is the market price of risk, the stock's Sharpe ratio, and it is exactly the size of the shift.

And why land on four percent? Why the cash rate and not somewhere else?

Because it is the only choice that makes the price, measured in today's money, a fair game, with no predictable drift left at all. And no arbitrage forces every traded price to be a fair game under that same odds sheet. Harrison, Kreps and Pliska proved that link between 1979 and 1981.

So the option price is what, exactly?

The average payoff under the reweighted odds, discounted at the cash rate. Your eight percent never appears. You need a volatility, never a forecast.

What about the Black-Scholes chance of finishing in the money? Is that a fake number?

It is a real probability, worked out under the reweighted odds. If you truly expect eight percent, your own chance of finishing in the money is usually higher. The number is real. The odds sheet is the device.

So if I am bullish euro dollar, I should not price the call higher?

No. Price and hedge it at the interest-rate differential, like everyone else. Put your view in the price and you quote too high, your hedge ratio is wrong, and counterparties who hedge properly pick you off. Express the view as a separate trade: buy the spot, or hold a call without hedging it.

One line?

The risk-neutral world prices; it does not forecast. Keep your view in a separate trade.

Section 1 · How it reached trading

One pricing rule for every derivative

Harrison & Kreps · 1979 · Harrison & Pliska · 1981

How it reached trading

Black-Scholes priced one product with one hedge and one PDE. Harrison, Kreps and Pliska (1979–81) showed that no arbitrage means a measure exists under which every price is a discounted expectation; Girsanov's 1960 theorem builds it by swapping μ for r.

That turned pricing into simulation, and change of numeraire (1995) carried it into rates and FX. A risk-neutral distribution prices; it does not forecast.

Section 2

Same coin, a different odds sheet

A probability measure is nothing mystical — it's just a specific weighting of how likely each possible future outcome is.

ℙ

The real-world measure

The weighting that reflects what investors actually expect. A genuinely risky stock earns a drift μ above the risk-free rate r, because investors demand compensation — a risk premium — for bearing uncertainty they can't diversify away.

ℚ

The risk-neutral measure

The same stock, same volatility, same set of possible future paths — described under a different weighting, engineered so its expected return comes out to exactly r instead of μ.

≡

"Equivalent," precisely

ℙ and ℚ agree on which outcomes are possible at all — nothing ℙ rules out becomes possible under ℚ, and nothing ℚ rules out was possible under ℙ. They only disagree on how likely the possible outcomes are.

🎲

Not a different stock

ℚ isn't a fantasy or an approximation of the real stock. It's the same randomness, relabeled — like a bookmaker's odds sheet redrawn on the same race, same horses, so the "average return" comes out to exactly r.

Nobody thinks ℚ is what will actually happen. It's a deliberately constructed accounting device — and the rest of this deck is about exactly why that specific device, and no other, is the one a no-arbitrage price needs.
Section 3

One stock, written two ways

Same σ, same source of randomness, same possible paths — only the drift term changes between measures.

Try it
dS = μS·dt + σS·dWℙ
Drift = μ. This is the measure under which μ is a real, arguable-over forecast about the economy — and the one nobody knows for certain.
dS = rS·dt + σS·dWℚ
Drift = r. Same σ, same shape, same width, same set of possible paths — only the center of the probability mass shifted left, toward the risk-free forward price.
Notice what never moves between panels: σ, and the curve's shape. Girsanov's theorem is precisely the license to relabel one curve's probabilities as the other's, without touching either.
Section 4

Drag the real-world drift

r and σ stay fixed. As μ moves, watch the gap between the two measures' means — that gap, in units of σ, is θ.

Try it
Real-world drift μμ = 9.0%
Fixed: r = 5.0%, σ = 20.0%
Market price of risk: θ = (μ − r) / σ = 0.20

θ is the exact size of the shift Girsanov's theorem applies to the Brownian motion to move from ℙ to ℚ: Wℚ = Wℙ + θt. Drag μ down to r and the gap — and θ — collapses to zero: if investors truly were risk-neutral, ℙ and ℚ would already be the same measure, and there'd be nothing to shift.

Section 5

Girsanov's theorem — the license to reweight

Shifting a Brownian motion by a deterministic drift doesn't just look like a new random process — it provably still is one, once you're willing to reweight the underlying probabilities.

The theorem, in one shift
Wℙt
+ θt →
Wℚt
Girsanov (1960): the shifted process Wℚ is itself a standard Brownian motion — mean-zero increments, variance t, independent increments, all preserved — just under the new measure ℚ rather than ℙ.
dℚ/dℙ = exp(−θ·WℙT − ½θ²T)
The Radon-Nikodym derivative — the precise multiplicative factor that converts a ℙ-probability of any given path into its ℚ-probability. This is what actually does the reweighting; Girsanov's theorem is the guarantee that it exists and behaves (never inventing a possibility, never destroying one).
1

Any well-behaved θ works

The theorem itself doesn't say which θ to pick for pricing — only that shifting by any reasonably well-behaved θ produces some valid equivalent measure.

2

Only the drift moves

σ — the diffusion term — is untouched by the change of measure. The model's view of how much the price jiggles around is not up for renegotiation, only where it's centered.

3

It's infrastructure

No desk derives the Radon-Nikodym derivative before pricing a swap. It's the theorem that makes "price as if drift were r" legitimate, not a daily calculation.

Section 7

Why this shift, specifically

Girsanov's theorem says a shift produces some valid measure. Only one choice of θ makes the discounted price a martingale — and that's the one pricing needs.

Discount the price

Define S̃t = e−rtSt — today's dollars, stripping out the risk-free growth every asset earns for free. Differentiating the discount factor alone contributes a drift term of −r·S̃t·dt.

Add the ℚ-dynamics

Under ℚ, St itself drifts at exactly r: dSt = rSt·dt + σSt·dWℚ. Plugging that in contributes a second drift term of +r·S̃t·dt.

The two drift terms cancel exactly. What's left is dS̃t = σS̃t·dWℚ — pure noise, zero expected drift. A process with zero drift is, by definition, a martingale: 𝔼ℚ[S̃T | ℱt] = S̃t.
This is the exact martingale property from the Efficient Market Hypothesis lesson — 𝔼[Pt|ℱt−1] = Pt−1, a "fair game." There, it was an empirical, testable claim about real markets under ℙ. Here, under ℚ, it holds by construction — the entire reason ℚ was built the way it was.

Section 9

N(d₂) is real. The measure is the fiction.

Under ℚ, ln ST is normal with mean ln S₀ + (r − σ²/2)T — same lognormal shape as under ℙ, centered on r instead of μ.

What N(d₂) actually is

N(d₂) is literally ℚ(ST > K) — the genuine, well-defined probability, under the genuine, well-defined measure ℚ, that the option finishes in the money. There is nothing fictitious about the number. What's a deliberate device is the measure it's computed under: since μ ≥ r for a typical risky asset, the real-world probability of finishing in the money is usually higher than N(d₂) — the two coincide only when μ = r, i.e. θ = 0.

V₀ = e−rT · 𝔼ℚ[VT]
Nowhere does μ appear. Not because it's approximated away, but because r is precisely the drift that makes the discounted price a martingale — the one property a no-arbitrage price actually needs.
A bullish view is a separate bet, never a pricing input. A trader convinced EUR/USD will outrun the rate differential still prices and hedges the option under ℚ. Their view gets expressed by buying spot, or by an intentionally un-hedged option — not by quietly swapping μ in for r inside the pricing formula. Doing that produces a price too high and a hedge ratio that's systematically wrong.
Section 10

How a derivatives desk actually uses this

💰

Risk-neutral valuation is the default

Every derivatives pricing model — plain Black-Scholes through local-vol and stochastic-vol — computes a discounted expectation under some ℚ, never under ℙ.

🎲

Monte Carlo engines simulate under ℚ

Pricing a path-dependent exotic by averaging a discounted payoff over simulated paths uses drift r (or the FX rate differential) in every path — never ℙ's μ.

🔄

Calibration works backwards through ℚ

Backing out implied volatility from a traded price infers a parameter of ℚ, not ℙ — why implied and realized volatility are related but distinct.

📉

Risk management switches back to ℙ

VaR, stress tests, and real-world scenario probabilities are computed under the actual estimated measure — "how bad could this get" is a ℙ-question.

Verified in this codebase

This repo's own dealer-positioning greeks (js/gammaGreeks.js) compute Black-Scholes d₁/d₂, vanna, and charm using the risk-free rate r as the drift term in the formula — never an estimated real-world μ. It's a small, concrete instance of the exact same ℚ-pricing convention this lesson derives, already sitting in production code rather than only in the theory.

Section 11

Where "risk-neutral" gets misread

Common misreading
  • "Risk-neutral" means real investors don't care about risk
  • A rising N(d₂) means "the market thinks this is more likely"
  • Any drift can be shifted away for free, no conditions attached
  • A house view can justify a different price for the derivative itself
What's actually true
  • "Risk-neutral" describes the measure's math, not investor psychology — real investors are risk-averse, which is exactly why μ > r under ℙ
  • N(d₂) is a ℚ-probability, a pricing device — directionally suggestive, not the same claim as a ℙ-forecast
  • Girsanov requires a technical integrability condition (Novikov's condition) on θt — automatic for constant θ, a real constraint in exotic models
  • A view belongs in a separate position — spot, forwards, an un-hedged option — never inside the pricing model
Why the name is confusing

"Risk-neutral" sounds like a claim about people. It isn't — it's a label for a measure under which every asset happens to drift at r. If investors genuinely were risk-neutral, μ would already equal r, θ would be zero, and ℙ and ℚ would be the same measure — there'd be no shift to name in the first place.

Section 12

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

What replaces μ in an option's no-arbitrage price?

Under the risk-neutral measure ℚ, every asset's drift is engineered to be exactly r — that's the whole point of the measure change.
Question 2

Girsanov's theorem says shifting a Brownian motion's drift by θ produces what?

The shifted process W^ℚ = W^ℙ + θt keeps every defining property of Brownian motion — just weighted under ℚ instead of ℙ.
Question 3

θ = (μ − r) / σ is called the...

θ is the excess return per unit of volatility — the exact size of the ℙ→ℚ shift. The Radon-Nikodym derivative is the reweighting factor built from θ, a different object.
Question 4

The discounted price S̃_t = e⁻ʳᵗS_t is a martingale under ℚ...

θ = (μ−r)/σ is chosen specifically because it's the unique shift that cancels the drift after discounting — no testing required, it's true by design.
Question 5

Is N(d₂) the real-world probability the option finishes in the money?

N(d₂) is a genuine probability, just computed under ℚ (drift r), not ℙ (drift μ). The two coincide only when μ = r.
Your call
Scenario drill · an illustrative morning

You are bullish EUR/USD and a client wants a call

Your own macro read says EUR/USD will rise over the next six months by more than the interest-rate differential implies. A client asks you to quote a six-month EUR/USD call, and you will hedge whatever you sell. How do you price it?

  1. Price it with your own expected drift. You are confident in the view, so the call is worth more.
    That swaps the risk-free drift for your real-world drift inside the price. You quote too high against anyone who prices under the risk-neutral measure, and the delta you hedge with is wrong, because it no longer matches what a replicating portfolio needs.
  2. Shade it a little: blend part of your view into the drift, not all of it.
    Still a mispriced number, only smaller. A desk whose house view leaks into its pricing misprices consistently in the direction of that view, and counterparties who hedge correctly pick it off.
  3. Price and hedge the call at the rate differential, like everyone else, and put the view on as a separate trade: buy EUR/USD spot or forwards.
    This is the split the theorem asks for. The no-arbitrage price comes from the risk-neutral measure and needs only a volatility. Your view is a real bet about the real-world measure, so it belongs in its own explicit position, sized on its own merits.
  4. Decline to quote until you are less bullish.
    It avoids the mistake, but it gives up a fair trade for no reason. The price and the view are separate questions, so your view is no reason to stop quoting.

What happens. This is an illustrative situation, not a dated episode. The lesson's point is general: an option priced with a real-world drift is systematically off in the direction of the view, and the hedge built from it is off too. Price under the risk-neutral measure, and keep the directional bet as a separate, visible position.

Section 13

What to carry forward

01

A probability measure is just a weighting of possible future paths — ℙ is the real-world weighting (includes a risk premium), ℚ is an equivalent but different weighting built for pricing.

02

Girsanov's theorem licenses shifting a Brownian motion's drift by θ and still having a valid Brownian motion — just under a new, equivalent measure.

03

The specific shift θ = (μ−r)/σ lands the underlying's ℚ-drift exactly on r — and that's what makes the discounted price e⁻ʳᵗS_t a martingale under ℚ.

04

Because that martingale property is what no-arbitrage pricing requires, V₀ = e⁻ʳᵀ·𝔼^ℚ[V_T] never needs μ — only r and σ.

05

N(d₂) is a genuine probability — ℚ(S_T > K) — computed correctly. What's a deliberate device is only the measure, never the number.

06

A real-world view is a separate directional bet — spot, forwards, an un-hedged option — never a silent adjustment to the drift inside a pricing model.

07

How it reached trading: Harrison, Kreps & Pliska (1979–81) made price = discounted expectation under Q, and Girsanov's theorem builds Q.

What this now lets you do

Price and hedge a derivative without ever forecasting the underlying's real expected return — replace an unknowable μ with the observable risk-free rate r, confident the substitution is licensed by a theorem rather than a convenient shortcut. Read N(d₂)-style outputs as real probabilities under a deliberately built measure, not market forecasts. Know exactly where a directional view belongs — a separate position — and exactly where it never belongs — inside the pricing model.
