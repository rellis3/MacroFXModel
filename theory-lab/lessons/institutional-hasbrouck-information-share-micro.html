Hasbrouck Information Share, Which Price Actually Hears the News First?

The VECM lesson compared how hard each of two prices gets pulled back once they've already drifted apart. This lesson asks the sharper question: when brand-new information hits, whose price actually moves first — and how much of the total variance can genuinely be credited to each venue? The answer is rigorous, useful, and depends uncomfortably on one arbitrary modeling choice.

Section 1

Adjustment speed vs. information leadership

Two different questions that a VECM's own numbers can't fully separate on their own.

🐕

VECM's α_Y vs α_X

How hard each price gets pulled back once a spread has opened up. A large |α_Y| means Y does most of the eventual correcting.

⚡

Hasbrouck's Information Share

Of the variance in the shock that moves the shared "true" price, how much is attributable to venue Y vs venue X, the instant news arrives?

🔀

Related, not identical

A slow self-corrector can still be the venue where news shows up first. The two questions often agree, but they're not the same claim.

📐

Same VECM, deeper analysis

Nothing about the underlying model changes — this deck starts from the exact same two-equation system the VECM deck already built.

A price can be a lazy, slow self-corrector and still be the one that always reacts to news first. Being quick to react and being quick to self-correct afterward are not automatically the same trait — Information Share is built to measure the first one.
Section 2

Four steps from VECM to a number

Only step 3 needs the VECM's own α; step 4 is Hasbrouck's actual contribution.

The pipeline
1. Fit the VECM
→
2. Wold/VMA form
→
3. Common-factor loadings ψ
→
4. Cholesky-orthogonalize
Step 3 turns the VECM's own α_Y, α_X into a shared weighting vector ψ. Step 4 is where Hasbrouck attributes variance — and where the ordering problem, next section, comes from.

Every stationary system has a Wold representation: express Δp_t purely as a weighted sum of current and past shocks, no lagged levels needed. The long-run sum of those weights, Ψ(1), is what step 3 uses.

Section 3

One shared row: the common-factor loadings ψ

Because both venues track one true price, Ψ(1)'s two rows come out identical.

Derived directly from α
ψ_Y
=
α_X / (α_X − α_Y)
ψ_X
=
−α_Y / (α_X − α_Y)
ψ_Y + ψ_X = 1 always. Sanity check: if α_X = 0 (X never adjusts — the VECM lesson's "weak exogeneity" case), then ψ_Y = 0 and ψ_X = 1 — X, the pure anchor, carries the entire common factor.

The less a venue reacts to disequilibrium, the more it looks like the fixed reference the other venue is chasing — and the more weight ψ hands it. This is the Gonzalo-Granger (1995) permanent-transitory decomposition, worked out for the two-venue case.

Section 4

Hasbrouck's trick: orthogonalize, then attribute

The problem it solves

The two venues' shocks are usually correlated (σ_YX ≠ 0) — they share contemporaneous information. That leaves a jointly-owned cross term in the common factor's total variance, σ_w² = ψ_Y²σ_Y² + 2ψ_Yψ_Xσ_YX + ψ_X²σ_X², with no natural rule for splitting it between the two venues.

Cholesky-decompose Ω = FF′ to manufacture orthogonal shocks with no cross term left — but this requires picking which venue goes first. That venue claims the whole shared, correlated portion of the variance; the second-ordered venue gets only what's left over.

Whichever venue is listed first in the Cholesky decomposition wins the tie-break on jointly-owned variance. Nothing in the economics says which venue that should be.

Section 5

The ordering problem — the standard critique

Same VECM, same Ω, different answer

Swap which venue is ordered first in the Cholesky decomposition, and the Information Share split can change — sometimes dramatically — even though nothing about the underlying economics changed at all. There is no principled rule for which venue "should" go first. This is the single most-cited limitation of Hasbrouck's Information Share.

Next slide shows exactly how large that swing can get, using one fitted example: α_Y = −0.40, α_X = 0.10, and a residual covariance matrix with a 0.5 correlation between the two venues' shocks.

Section 9

Two ways the literature responds

Hasbrouck's own fix
  • Compute IS under both orderings
  • Report the midpoint, or the full range
  • Doesn't remove the ambiguity — makes it visible and bounded
Alternative measures
  • Gonzalo-Granger (1995) Component Share — same ψ, raw (non-orthogonalized) variances, no ordering step
  • Putniņš (2013) Information Leadership Share — reconciles cases where IS and Component Share disagree
  • Described conceptually — see the original papers for exact algebra

Neither response is "the" correct fix — a wide gap between the two Cholesky orderings is itself a meaningful diagnostic, not a nuisance to average away and forget.

Section 10

Where this quietly goes wrong

🔀

Reporting only one ordering

Always compute both, or explicitly justify the ordering choice — usually neither venue's ordering is more defensible than the other.

🚫

No genuine cointegration

The whole common-factor framework needs a real, tested price-parity relationship — not just any two correlated series.

⏱️

Sampling-frequency sensitivity

Tick-level vs. one-minute vs. daily data can flip which venue appears to lead — microstructure noise and averaging both distort the answer differently.

💧

IS ≠ liquidity

A high information share means news shows up there first — not that the venue is cheaper or more liquid to trade in. See this repo's Glosten-Milgrom, PIN, and Roll's Spread Estimator lessons for that separate question.

Section 11

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

How does Information Share differ from comparing α_Y and α_X directly?

A venue can adjust slowly (small |α|) and still be where news shows up first — related questions, not the same claim.
Question 2

If α_X = 0 exactly, what does the common-factor loading vector become?

A venue that never responds to disequilibrium behaves like the fixed anchor — the math correctly hands it the whole permanent component.
Question 3

What mechanically causes the Cholesky-ordering sensitivity?

Whenever σ_YX ≠ 0, the venue ordered first claims the shared, correlated variance; the second-ordered venue only gets what's left over.
Question 4

If σ_YX = 0 exactly, does the Cholesky ordering matter?

The ordering problem only exists because of the cross term 2ψ_Yψ_Xσ_YX — set σ_YX to zero and that term vanishes entirely.
Question 5

Name one of the two standard literature responses to the ordering problem.

Hasbrouck's own suggested fix is the midpoint/range; Gonzalo-Granger (1995) and Putniņš (2013) offer alternative measures without the same ordering dependence.
Question 6

A venue has a much higher Information Share than another for the same asset. Does that mean it's more liquid?

Liquidity is a related but distinct question, covered by this repo's Glosten-Milgrom, PIN, and Roll's Spread Estimator lessons — not by Information Share.
Section 12

What to carry forward

01

Hasbrouck's Information Share decomposes the variance of the common efficient price's innovation, attributing a share to each venue — a sharper question than the VECM lesson's α_Y-vs-α_X adjustment-speed comparison.

02

The common-factor loading vector ψ derives directly from the VECM's own α: ψ_Y = α_X/(α_X−α_Y), ψ_X = −α_Y/(α_X−α_Y) — the less a venue adjusts, the more common-factor weight it carries.

03

Cholesky-orthogonalizing the residual covariance matrix requires an arbitrary ordering choice, and the IS split can swing hard depending on it — 57.1% vs. 10.7% in this deck's own worked example, same fitted VECM throughout.

04

Two standard responses exist: Hasbrouck's own midpoint/range fix, and alternative measures — Gonzalo-Granger's (1995) Component Share and Putniņš' (2013) Information Leadership Share — that don't depend on the same arbitrary ordering.

05

Information Share needs genuine cointegration, is sensitive to sampling frequency, and answers a variance-attribution question — not a liquidity question, which belongs to this repo's Glosten-Milgrom, PIN, and Roll's Spread Estimator lessons instead.

What this now lets you do

Read a fitted VECM's α_Y, α_X, and residual covariance matrix all the way through to a genuine, defensible variance-attribution number for each venue — and know to report a range across Cholesky orderings rather than a single, arbitrarily-ordered figure.
