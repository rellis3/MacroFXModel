Extreme Value Theory & Tail Risk

Your worst historical day is not your worst possible day — EVT is the theory of how to say something honest about the losses you haven't seen yet, without pretending a handful of data points can tell you the whole story.

CONCEPT — extends the empirical VaR/CVaR statistics used elsewhere in this project ~16 min read Prereqs: Merton Jump-Diffusion Model, Kelly Criterion & Position Sizing
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • Historical VaR/CVaR read the answer straight off your sample, so far out in the tail (99.5%+) they're really just reporting the identity of your worst day or two, not a stable estimate.
  • Peaks-over-threshold (POT) fixes this by discarding everything below a high threshold \(u\) and fitting only the exceedances beyond it — using more of the tail data than a block-maxima approach, while still isolating tail-specific behavior.
  • The Generalized Pareto Distribution (GPD) is the shape those exceedances converge to (Balkema-de Haan-Pickands theorem); its shape parameter \(\xi\) tells you whether the tail is heavy/Pareto-type (\(\xi>0\)), exponential (\(\xi=0\)), or bounded (\(\xi<0\)).
  • A fitted GPD lets you extrapolate VaR/CVaR past what the raw sample supports — but that's a modeling assumption, not a measurement, and it's unverifiable at the exact quantile you're extrapolating to.
  • This project's own empirical risk statistics ship the honest half (historical VaR/CVaR, skew/kurtosis), and a peaks-over-threshold GPD fit (probability-weighted moments, in js/evtTail.js and js/extremesCore.js) also runs on strategies' and instruments' daily-return series. It is fit to daily P&L, not trade logs, which are far too small to fit \(\xi\) reliably; even a few hundred days gives only dozens of exceedances, so its \(\xi\) carries wide error bars.
  • Real-world episode: on 19 October 1987 the S&P 500 fell about 20% in one session, on the order of twenty standard deviations for a market whose daily moves were then around 1%, and the Dow's 22.6% fall nearly doubled the worst days of 1929. A heavy tail (\(\xi>0\)) makes such a day rare but possible; a normal makes it impossible. Whether a GPD tail estimate is breached at its advertised rate out of sample is untested here, and "something follows a rare move" is tested null (mv-extreme-forward).
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

My backtest has two years of daily data, and my worst day is in there. Is that my worst case?

It is your worst day so far. Not your worst possible day. Two years is about five hundred days. That tells you a lot about an ordinary day and almost nothing about a one-in-two-thousand-day event.

Why almost nothing?

Do the arithmetic. A one-in-two-thousand-day loss turns up about once every eight years. In five hundred days you probably have not seen one. Even the one-in-two-hundred-day level gets read off between your second and third worst days.

So the historical number is really just naming my worst day or two.

Exactly. Shift the window a little and it can swing a lot. Extreme value theory is the honest way to say something about losses you have not seen yet.

How does it do that?

Pick a high bar, say the ninetieth percentile of your losses. With five hundred days, about fifty days clear it. Throw everything else away and study only how far past the bar those fifty went.

Why does throwing data away help?

Because of a theorem. Whatever the original data looked like, the overshoots past a high enough bar settle into one family of shapes, the generalized Pareto. It is the tail version of averages turning into a bell curve.

And the shape tells me what?

One number, the shape parameter, tells you the kind of tail. Positive means a heavy tail, where huge losses fade away slowly. Zero is a plain, medium tail. Negative means a hard ceiling. Financial losses usually come out positive.

Then I just read the one-in-two-thousand loss off the fitted curve.

You can, and the formula will give you a number. But past your data, that number is the fitted shape talking. Report it next to the historical number, and treat the gap between them as how much you are leaning on the model.

Has a normal curve ever been badly wrong out there?

The nineteenth of October 1987. The S and P 500 fell about twenty percent in one session. Daily moves had been running around one percent, so that was roughly twenty standard deviations. A normal model calls that impossible. A heavy tail calls it rare.

So the heavy tail got it right.

Closer, not perfect. The extreme days came in a cluster that week, and the crash had its own mechanism: forced selling into market plumbing that could not keep up. Both strain the theory's own assumptions.

So the one-line version?

Your worst day so far is not your worst day possible. Fit the tail honestly, and remember that past your data it is an assumption, not a measurement.

Section 01

Why this matters

Every risk number this repo already computes — historical VaR, historical CVaR, max drawdown, the skew/kurtosis pair — is built from whatever trades or returns actually happened to occur in the sample you have. That is the right way to start (per this project's own working agreement: report the honest number off the real path, don't approximate it), but it has a specific, structural blind spot. A sample of, say, 500 daily returns can tell you a lot about the middle of the distribution — the everyday chop, the typical win/loss — because you have hundreds of observations to average over. It can tell you almost nothing reliable about the 1-in-2,000-day event, because by definition your 500-day sample probably doesn't contain one, and even if it does, it contains at most one or two, which is not enough to estimate anything with statistical stability. Yet the 1-in-2,000-day event — the flash crash, the surprise central-bank decision, the liquidity air-pocket — is exactly the one that determines whether a leveraged systematic strategy survives or blows up. Extreme Value Theory (EVT) is the branch of statistics built specifically for this mismatch: it doesn't try to model the whole distribution of returns well (that's what the rest of statistics is for); it asks a narrower, sharper question — "given that we're already looking at an extreme observation, what shape does the tail beyond it take, and can I use that shape to say something honest about outcomes worse than anything I've actually observed?" This lesson explains the theory behind that question, is explicit about what it can and can't buy you, and is honest about where this repo stands: the empirical, sample-bound numbers are the default, and the GPD tail fit built alongside them is an extrapolation to be read with its small-sample caveats, claiming only what the data actually supports.

💡
Plain-English intuition

You don't need to see a 9.5-magnitude earthquake to model one

Seismologists studying earthquake risk face the identical problem traders face with crashes: the events that matter most for planning purposes (an 8.5+ magnitude earthquake somewhere on a given fault) are, almost by definition, rare enough that no single region's recorded history contains many of them — sometimes none at all. And yet insurers and engineers still need a defensible number for "what's the chance of a catastrophic quake in the next 50 years," not just a shrug. The intellectual move that makes this tractable is this: instead of trying to model the entire distribution of every tremor ever recorded — most of which are tiny, uninteresting, and behave completely differently from a catastrophic one — you throw away everything below some high threshold and look only at what happens among the events that already cleared it. It turns out that the pattern of "how much do things overshoot a very high bar, given that they clear it at all" settles into one of a small, well-understood family of shapes, almost regardless of what the full underlying distribution looked like — much like how averages of almost anything tend toward a bell curve (the Central Limit Theorem), extremes of almost anything tend toward one of a small family of extreme-value shapes. That's the whole idea of EVT in one sentence: don't model everything; model only the shape of "how bad does it get, given that it's already bad," using the handful of worst observations you do have, and let that fitted shape extrapolate a little further out than your raw data alone could honestly reach. It is not magic — you are still, unavoidably, extrapolating past what you've observed — but it is a principled way to extrapolate rather than an arbitrary one.

Section 02

The math, step by step

Watch the formula build
From a fitted tail to a VaR you can extrapolate, one term at a time
\[ P(Y > y \mid X > u) = \left(1 + \color{#fbbf24}{\xi} \frac{y}{\color{#fbbf24}{\beta}}\right)^{-1/\color{#fbbf24}{\xi}} \]

Start with the tail shape. Past a high threshold \(u\), the overshoot \(Y = X - u\) follows the Generalized Pareto Distribution. The shape \(\xi\) says what kind of tail it is; the scale \(\beta\) says how big the overshoots run.

\[ P(X > u + y) = \color{#fbbf24}{\frac{n_u}{n}} \left(1 + \xi \frac{y}{\beta}\right)^{-1/\xi} \]

To be beyond \(u + y\) you first have to clear the bar at all. The observed exceedance rate \(n_u/n\) estimates that chance: with \(n = 500\) days and \(u\) at the 90th percentile, it is \(50/500\).

\[ \frac{n_u}{n} \left(1 + \xi \frac{y}{\beta}\right)^{-1/\xi} = \color{#fbbf24}{1 - p} \]

VaR at confidence \(p\) is the loss exceeded with probability \(1 - p\). Set the tail probability equal to it. For a 1-in-2,000-day loss, \(1 - p = 0.0005\).

\[ \color{#10b981}{\widehat{\text{VaR}}_p = u + \frac{\beta}{\xi}\left[ \left(\frac{n}{n_u}(1-p)\right)^{-\xi} - 1 \right]} \]

Solve for \(y\) and add back \(u\). Everything here is observed (\(u, n, n_u\)) or fitted (\(\xi, \beta\)), and because it is smooth in \(p\) it can be evaluated past the edge of your data. That is the extrapolation, and the assumption.

We'll build this in the order a practitioner actually uses it: first define VaR and CVaR precisely (the two numbers you're ultimately trying to estimate), then show exactly why the plain historical method for estimating them breaks down far out in the tail, then introduce the peaks-over-threshold method and the Generalized Pareto Distribution that EVT uses to patch that gap.

VaR and CVaR, defined precisely

Let \(L\) be a random variable representing a loss (positive \(L\) = you lost money; this lesson follows the convention of working in loss units so "bigger is worse" reads naturally — flip the sign if you'd rather work directly with returns, where losses are negative). Fix a confidence level \(p\), typically 0.95 or 0.99.

\[ \text{VaR}_p(L) = \inf\{ \ell : P(L > \ell) \le 1-p \} \]
pthe confidence level — e.g. 0.99
ℓthe smallest loss threshold satisfying the probability condition
1−pthe tail probability you're willing to tolerate exceeding

Read this as: "the smallest loss threshold \(\ell\) such that the probability of losing more than \(\ell\) is at most \(1-p\)." In plainer words, \(\text{VaR}_{0.99}\) is the loss level you expect to exceed only 1% of the time — equivalently, it's the 99th percentile of the loss distribution. VaR answers exactly one question: where is the bad-outcome cutoff. It says nothing at all about how bad things get once you're past that cutoff — a distribution where losses beyond the 99th percentile are all just barely past \(\text{VaR}_{0.99}\), and one where they occasionally run ten times larger, can have the identical \(\text{VaR}_{0.99}\). That blindness is VaR's best-known and most consequential weakness, which is exactly what CVaR (also called Expected Shortfall, ES) is built to fix:

\[ \text{CVaR}_p(L) = E[\, L \mid L > \text{VaR}_p(L) \,] \]
E[·]the average, not just a single cutoff
L > VaR_p(L)conditioning on already being in the bad tail

Read this as: "the average loss, given that you're already in the bad tail beyond VaR." Where VaR only marks the threshold, CVaR asks "and once I'm past it, how bad does it typically get?" This is why CVaR is called a coherent risk measure and VaR is not, in the technical sense economists and risk theorists use the word: a coherent risk measure has to satisfy a short list of sane properties, and the one VaR specifically fails is subadditivity — the requirement that the risk of a combined portfolio can never be worse than the sum of the risks of its parts taken separately (diversification should never look like it makes things worse). VaR can, in constructed examples, actually go up when you combine two positions that individually looked fine, because it only cares about a single quantile cutoff and ignores everything about the shape beyond it; CVaR, being an average over the whole tail rather than a single point on it, doesn't have this failure mode. This is not a purely academic distinction — it's the reason banking regulation (the Basel Committee's market-risk framework) shifted its standard tail-risk measure from VaR toward Expected Shortfall after the 2008 crisis specifically exposed VaR's tail-blindness in practice.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

VaR just answers "how bad does a really bad day have to be before I call it one of my worst 1%?" — it draws a line and stops there. CVaR asks the more useful follow-up: "okay, and once I'm past that line, how bad does it typically get?" Only knowing where the line is can hide a lot — two situations can have the exact same line, but one barely crosses it on its worst days while the other blows way past it, and VaR alone can't tell them apart.

Why the plain historical (empirical) method breaks down far into the tail

The most direct way to estimate VaR or CVaR from data — and exactly what this repo's own historical VaR/CVaR implementation does — is to sort your observed sample and read the answer straight off it: empirical VaR at confidence \(p\) is just the \(p\) sample quantile of losses (the value such that a fraction \(1-p\) of your observed losses lies beyond it — equivalently the \((1-p)\) quantile of returns), and empirical CVaR is the average of every observation at or beyond that point. This is honest, assumption-free, and exactly right — as long as the quantile you're asking for is comfortably inside the range your sample actually explores. The problem is entirely about what happens as \(p\) moves toward the edge of, or past, what your sample size can support. With \(n\) observations, the empirical \((1-p)\)-quantile is built from roughly \(n(1-p)\) data points sitting at or beyond that level. Set \(n=500\) (roughly two years of daily data) and \(p=0.99\): you're estimating a threshold from \(500 \times 0.01 = 5\) observations. Push to \(p=0.995\) — a "1-in-200-day" event — and you're down to \(2.5\) observations, meaning the 99.5th percentile is being read off between your second-worst and third-worst historical observation, interpolated. Push further to the "1-in-2,000-day" event this lesson's title alludes to, and you would need roughly \(2{,}000/252 \approx 8\) years of daily data just to see one such event on average — and one occurrence tells you almost nothing about the shape of the distribution beyond it, any more than one coin flip tells you a coin's bias. Empirical VaR/CVaR at that quantile isn't really "estimating" anything at that point; it's reporting the identity of your single worst historical day and calling it a statistic. That's the precise problem EVT exists to address.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

If you ask "what's a typical Tuesday like," a couple hundred Tuesdays gives you a solid answer. But if you ask "what's the worst thing that could ever happen on a Tuesday," a couple hundred Tuesdays probably hasn't even shown you one. Reading that answer straight off your data, this far out, isn't really measuring anything — it's just naming whatever your single worst day happened to be, which could easily have been different by luck alone.

The Fisher-Tippett-Gnedenko theorem, stated conceptually

EVT's foundational result concerns the distribution of the maximum of many observations — informally: if you take a large number of independent draws from some distribution and record only the biggest one, then take another batch and record its biggest, and so on, what does the distribution of "the biggest of a batch" converge to as the batch size grows? The Fisher-Tippett-Gnedenko theorem (Ronald Fisher and Leonard Tippett, 1928; put on rigorous footing by Boris Gnedenko, 1943) says that — under fairly general conditions on the underlying distribution — that limiting distribution, suitably rescaled, can only ever take one of three shapes, unified into a single family called the Generalized Extreme Value (GEV) distribution. This is the direct tail-behavior analogue of the Central Limit Theorem: the CLT says sums/averages of almost anything converge to a Gaussian regardless of the underlying distribution; Fisher-Tippett-Gnedenko says maxima of almost anything converge to one of the GEV shapes, regardless of the underlying distribution. This lesson won't derive that theorem, but it's the reason EVT gets to make a strong, distribution-agnostic claim at all: you don't need to know or assume the full shape of daily FX returns to say something principled about their extremes — the extremes themselves are constrained to a small family of possible shapes by a general theorem, not by an assumption you bolted on.

Peaks-over-threshold and the Generalized Pareto Distribution

The GEV theorem is about block maxima (the single worst day out of each month, say), which is a legitimate approach but wastes data — it throws away every observation in a block except the single worst one. The more data-efficient and more commonly used approach in modern risk work is peaks-over-threshold (POT), built on a companion theorem due to A. A. Balkema and Laurens de Haan, and independently James Pickands (both 1974/1975): fix a sufficiently high threshold \(u\), and look at every observation that exceeds it. For each such observation \(X\), define the exceedance as \(Y = X - u\) — how far past the threshold it landed. The Balkema-de Haan-Pickands theorem says that, as the threshold \(u\) is pushed high enough, the distribution of these exceedances converges to the Generalized Pareto Distribution (GPD), essentially regardless of what distribution the original data came from — this is the POT-specific counterpart to Fisher-Tippett-Gnedenko, and it's the theorem that actually underwrites the practical fitting procedure below.

The shape parameter \(\xi\) is the payload of the whole method — it tells you the qualitative kind of tail you're dealing with, not just its scale:

📉

ξ > 0 — heavy, Pareto-type tail

The probability of exceeding \(y\) decays only polynomially as \(y\) grows, not exponentially — the "fat tail" regime of financial returns and insurance losses. No finite variance once \(\xi \ge 0.5\), no finite mean once \(\xi \ge 1\) — ordinary summary statistics stop being well-defined.

➖

ξ = 0 — exponential (boundary)

The GPD formula reduces to a simple exponential distribution — exceedances decay at a constant proportional rate, a "medium," textbook tail with no special heaviness.

🧱

ξ < 0 — bounded tail

A hard finite upper limit to how large an exceedance can get, no matter how much data you collect. Shows up for physically bounded quantities, but is the least common case for financial losses.

Fitting a GPD means estimating \(\xi\) and \(\beta\) from the observed exceedances \(Y_1, \ldots, Y_{n_u}\) — typically via maximum likelihood, choosing the \((\xi, \beta)\) pair that makes the observed exceedances most probable under the GPD formula. This lesson won't derive the maximum- likelihood equations, but conceptually it's the same fitting philosophy as anywhere else in statistics: pick the parameters that make what you actually saw as unsurprising as possible under the model.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Peaks-over-threshold means picking a high bar and only paying attention to what happens once things clear it — throw away all the ordinary, everyday data and study just the overshoot. It turns out that overshoot pattern, no matter what the original data looked like, always settles into one of three basic shapes: a genuinely scary heavy tail, a plain medium tail, or a tail with a hard ceiling. That one shape-defining number (ξ) is the single most important thing this whole method produces.

From a fitted GPD back to VaR and CVaR

Once \((\xi, \beta)\) are fitted, there's a closed-form way to convert them into a VaR estimate at any confidence level \(p\) — including levels beyond what your raw sample could support directly, which is the entire point:

\[ \widehat{\text{VaR}}_p = u + \frac{\beta}{\xi}\left[ \left(\frac{n}{n_u}(1-p)\right)^{-\xi} - 1 \right] \]
uthe threshold you already crossed
ξ, βthe fitted tail shape, doing the extrapolating
n_u/nthe observed exceedance rate

Don't worry about memorizing this formula — the important structural point is what it's built from: the threshold \(u\) you already crossed, plus a correction term built entirely from the fitted tail shape \((\xi,\beta)\) and how far past \(p\) sits relative to the exceedance rate \(n_u/n\). Because that correction term is a smooth function of \(p\), it can be evaluated at a \(p\) more extreme than anything in your sample — that's the "extrapolation" this lesson keeps returning to, made mechanically concrete. And once you have \(\widehat{\text{VaR}}_p\) from the GPD, there's a similarly closed-form CVaR estimate built on top of it (valid for \(\xi < 1\), where the tail has a finite mean):

\[ \widehat{\text{CVaR}}_p = \frac{\widehat{\text{VaR}}_p}{1-\xi} + \frac{\beta - \xi u}{1-\xi} \]
1 − ξvalid only for ξ < 1, where the tail has a finite mean

The key honesty check, stated plainly and repeated because it's the whole crux of this lesson: every quantity in both formulas above is either something you observed (\(u\), \(n\), \(n_u\)) or something you fitted (\(\xi\), \(\beta\)) from a necessarily small number of tail observations. The further \(p\) sits beyond your data, the more the answer is determined by the fitted shape parameters rather than by anything directly observed — which is exactly the tradeoff explored in the worked example below.

The honesty check: everything in these formulas is either observed (\(u\), \(n\), \(n_u\)) or fitted (\(\xi\), \(\beta\)) from a small number of tail points. The further \(p\) sits beyond your data, the more the answer is the fitted shape talking, not anything you actually saw.
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Once you've fitted the tail's shape, you can plug in any "how rare" level you like — even one rarer than anything in your data — and the formula will happily hand you a number. That's genuinely useful, but it's worth remembering it's a guess built by stretching a pattern further than you've actually watched it go, not a measurement of something you observed.

🧮
Worked example

Estimating a 1-in-2,000-day loss from two years of daily data

Suppose you have \(n=500\) daily P&L observations (roughly two trading years) and you want a 99.5% VaR — the loss level exceeded only about 1 in 200 days, i.e. the "1-in-200-day" quantile — and you're curious what a 1-in-2,000-day loss might look like, ten times rarer still.

The plain historical method. \(1-p = 0.005\), so the empirical VaR is read off around the \(500 \times 0.005 = 2.5\)th-worst observation — interpolated between your second-worst and third-worst historical day. That number is not a stable statistical estimate; it is, almost literally, "whatever your worst two or three days happened to be." Re-run the same backtest with a slightly different two-year window and that number can swing enormously, because it's driven by one or two data points rather than by an underlying pattern estimated across many. And the 1-in-2,000-day quantile is entirely out of reach by this method — with only 500 observations, you would need on average four separate two-year samples just to expect to see one such event at all; empirical VaR at that level can do no better than return your single worst day (the quantile formula is asking for data past the edge of what you have).

The EVT/POT method. Instead of trying to nail the exact 99.5th or 99.95th percentile directly, set a more moderate threshold \(u\) — say the 90th percentile of losses, which with \(n=500\) gives you roughly \(n_u = 50\) exceedances to work with, a far more workable sample size than "2.5 observations." Fit a GPD to those 50 exceedances, obtaining estimates of \(\xi\) and \(\beta\) (in practice these are checked with a diagnostic plot — a "mean excess plot" — and by testing sensitivity to the threshold choice, not accepted blindly from one fit). Plug the fitted \((\xi, \beta)\) into the VaR formula above at \(p=0.995\), or even \(p=0.9995\) (the 1-in-2,000-day level) — the formula produces an answer either way, because it's extrapolating the fitted smooth curve rather than reading off a specific data point.

The honest tradeoff. The EVT estimate is smoother and more stable than the single-worst-observation empirical number — it's built from the pattern across 50 tail observations rather than the identity of one or two, so it doesn't swing wildly if your single worst day happens to fall out of the sample. But it is not more "true." It is a model-based extrapolation, conditional entirely on the assumption that the GPD shape fitted from the 90th–99th percentile region continues to describe the distribution's behavior all the way out to the 1-in-2,000-day level — a region you have, by construction, never actually observed. If the real world has a structural break out there (a circuit breaker, a central bank intervention, a fundamentally different mechanism that only kicks in at true crisis scale), the fitted GPD has no way of knowing that, because nothing in the 90th–99th percentile data could tell it. The honest summary: EVT trades "wildly noisy but assumption-free" for "smooth but conditional on an extrapolation assumption you cannot directly verify at the level you're extrapolating to." Both numbers are worth having, and they should be reported together, with the gap between them itself treated as information about how much you're really relying on the model.

Section 03

It actually happened: 19 October 1987, the day a normal curve forbids

It actually happened

One session that nearly doubled the worst days of 1929

On Monday 19 October 1987 the S&P 500 fell about 20% in a single session and the Dow Jones Industrial Average fell 22.6%. Before that day, the Dow's worst sessions of the modern era were the October 1929 crash days, falls of about 12–13%. So 1987 was not a slightly worse version of the worst day anyone remembered. It nearly doubled it.

Now scale it the way a risk model would. US index daily moves in the years before had a standard deviation of roughly 1%, so a 20% fall was on the order of twenty standard deviations. Under a normal distribution the probability of that is so small that it should not happen once in any span of history you care to name. A normal model calibrated on the years before would not have called it unlikely. It would have called it impossible, which is the worst kind of wrong for anyone sizing leverage.

Nor did it arrive alone. The week before had already been heavy: the Dow fell about 4.6% on Friday 16 October. The sessions after swung violently in both directions, and the Dow regained 288 points, 57% of the Monday fall, within the next two sessions. The extremes came as a cluster around one event, not as independent draws spread through time. The mechanism behind the day (portfolio insurers forced by their own hedges to sell futures into the fall, and exchange order handling that could not keep up) is told in the Black-Scholes-Merton lesson.

Where the textbook version breaks

The normal model breaks first, because its tails are far too thin. EVT does better, since a heavy tail gives such a day a small but non-zero probability. But 1987 also strains two of EVT's own assumptions. Exceedances are not independent: they came in a cluster around one event. And the shape fitted near the threshold need not hold further out, because at crisis scale a different mechanism (forced, price-insensitive selling into broken market plumbing) switched on. That is the "structural break" the worked example warns no 90th–99th percentile data can reveal.

It says "big ones are rare, but they happen." It still can't tell you the day one will come, and big waves tend to come in groups, not one at a time.

Section 04

How practitioners actually use this

EVT shows up in a fairly specific, disciplined set of roles in real risk management — it is a specialist tool for a specific job, not a general-purpose replacement for empirical statistics:

  • Regulatory and internal tail-capital calculations. Insurers (where EVT has some of its deepest roots — modeling catastrophic claims) and banks use POT/GPD fits to set capital reserves against extreme, rarely-observed loss scenarios, precisely because regulators require an answer at confidence levels (99.5% for insurance solvency under Solvency II, 99.9% for bank credit capital) that raw historical data cannot support directly.
  • Threshold selection is itself a live judgment call, not a solved problem. Set \(u\) too low and you violate the theorem's premise (the GPD approximation is only asymptotically valid for a sufficiently high threshold, and low-threshold exceedances still carry "bulk of the distribution" behavior that biases the fit); set it too high and you're back to having too few exceedances to fit reliably. Practitioners typically fit across a range of candidate thresholds and look for a stretch where the fitted \(\xi\) and \(\beta\) stay roughly stable — a "plateau" in parameter estimates as a function of threshold — as the practical sign that the threshold is high enough for the theorem's approximation to have kicked in, but not so high that too little data remains.
  • Stress testing and scenario design, alongside — not instead of — historical scenarios. EVT-fitted tails are commonly used to generate "what would a 1-in-N-year loss plausibly look like" scenarios that complement, rather than replace, actual historical stress events (2008, 2020, a specific flash crash) — the fitted number and the realized worst historical case are triangulated against each other, not treated as substitutes.
  • Never treated as a single point estimate with false precision. Serious practitioners report EVT-based extreme quantiles with confidence intervals around \(\xi\) and the resulting VaR/ CVaR (these can be wide, given how few data points anchor the fit), and routinely check sensitivity to threshold choice, estimation window, and even which parametric family was assumed — treating the headline number as one point in a range of plausible extrapolations, not a fact.
⚠️
Common pitfalls

Ways EVT gets over-trusted or misapplied

  • Forgetting the extrapolation is a modeling assumption, not a measurement. A fitted GPD tail is a statement of the form "if the exceedance pattern observed near the threshold continues in the same shape further out, then the extrapolated quantile is X." That "if" is doing real work and is fundamentally unverifiable at the exact quantile you're extrapolating to — by definition, you have no data there to check against. Presenting an EVT-derived 1-in-10,000-day number with the same confidence as an empirical 1-in-20-day number (which you can actually check against dozens of realized observations) conflates two very different epistemic situations.
  • Fitting a GPD on too small a tail sample and trusting \(\xi\) anyway. A handful of exceedances produces a fitted shape parameter with wide, often-unreported uncertainty — as the repo box above makes concrete. A small change in which observations happen to sit above the threshold can swing \(\xi\) from "moderately heavy" to "extremely heavy" tail behavior. Always ask how many exceedances the fit is actually built from before trusting the headline number.
  • Threshold instability treated as a footnote instead of a diagnostic. If the fitted \(\xi\) and \(\beta\) change substantially as you nudge the threshold \(u\) up or down, that instability is itself the most important piece of information the exercise produced — it's telling you the "sufficiently high threshold" condition the underlying theorem needs may not yet hold, or that the tail genuinely isn't well-described by a single GPD across that range. Reporting only the number from one threshold choice, without the stability check, hides the exact uncertainty EVT is supposed to be honest about.
  • Assuming i.i.d. when returns cluster. The classical POT/GPD machinery assumes exceedances behave like independent draws. Financial returns violate this routinely — volatility clusters (the GARCH lesson's core point), so extreme days tend to arrive in clumps around the same macro event rather than being scattered independently through time. Extensions exist (declustering the exceedances, or explicitly modeling time-varying tail behavior), but a naive i.i.d. GPD fit on raw daily data quietly assumes away exactly the clustering this project's own volatility-regime work says is real.
  • Using EVT to launder an in-sample-only tail number into something that sounds validated. Per the standard validation discipline, a fitted model number is not evidence of anything until it's checked against what actually happens next — an EVT tail estimate is no exception. A GPD fit that hasn't been checked against subsequent realized extremes (to whatever extent that's even possible, given how rare they are) is a hypothesis about the tail's shape, not a validated fact about it — the same in-sample-versus-OOS discipline this repo applies everywhere else applies here too, even though "OOS-testing a tail estimate" is inherently slow and data-hungry by nature.
Scenario drill · Friday 16 October 1987

A heavy week, and your risk model says relax

You run a leveraged long book in US stocks. It has been a week of heavy falls, and today the Dow fell about 4.6%. Your risk model is a normal distribution fitted to recent years, when daily index moves had a standard deviation of roughly 1%. On that model, a fall several times larger than today's is so unlikely it is not worth a thought. What do you do with the leverage over the weekend?

  1. Keep the leverage. The model says another big fall is vanishingly unlikely.
    A normal curve has far too little mass in the tail, so it calls large losses impossible rather than rare. And extreme days cluster: a heavy week is exactly when the next extreme is most likely, not least.
  2. Add to the book. After a fall this large, a bounce is due.
    A rare move is a measurement, not a forecast. It tells you the tail is live, which is a reason to hold less, not more.
  3. Cut leverage until a loss far beyond anything in your sample is survivable, sizing off a heavy-tailed or stress loss rather than the normal VaR.
    This is what EVT is for: a heavy tail makes a much worse day rare but possible, and sizing has to survive it. Accept that the extrapolated number is an assumption and leave a margin for it.
  4. Cut leverage a little, using the worst day in your own sample as the stress loss.
    Better than trusting the normal, but historical VaR this far out just names your worst observed day. The next extreme need not respect it.

What happened. On Monday 19 October 1987 the S&P 500 fell about 20% and the Dow 22.6%, nearly doubling the Dow's falls of about 13% and 12% on 28 and 29 October 1929. Against daily moves of about 1%, that was on the order of twenty standard deviations: impossible under a normal model. The sessions after swung violently both ways, and the Dow regained 288 points, 57% of the Monday fall, within the next two sessions. The extremes came as a cluster around one event.

Sources: Federal Reserve History, "Stock Market Crash of 1987" (Dow −4.6% on 16 Oct, −22.6% on 19 Oct, 288 points regained over the next two sessions); Report of the Presidential Task Force on Market Mechanisms (Brady Report), January 1988.

Key Takeaways

✓Historical VaR/CVaR break down far in the tail. Beyond roughly the 99.5th percentile they're really just reporting the identity of your worst day or two, not a stable estimate.
✓Peaks-over-threshold (POT) fixes this efficiently. It discards everything below a high threshold u and fits only the exceedances beyond it — more data-efficient than block maxima, while still isolating tail behavior.
✓The GPD shape parameter ξ is the payload. Heavy/Pareto-type (ξ>0), exponential (ξ=0), or bounded (ξ<0) — it tells you the qualitative kind of tail, not just its scale.
⚠A fitted GPD extrapolates — it doesn't measure. Past your sample, the answer is the fitted shape talking, not anything directly observed, and it's unverifiable at the exact quantile you're extrapolating to.
✓The empirical half is already a real, tested piece of this project's own risk tooling. This project's own empirical risk statistics already ship the honest, assumption-free version of this lesson's core question today.
⚠A GPD/POT layer is built, on daily returns rather than trades. js/evtTail.js (threshold at the 90th loss percentile, needs 30+ days and 8+ exceedances) feeds the portfolio pages' EVT VaR/CVaR; js/extremesCore.js (95th percentile, 40+ observations, 10+ exceedances) feeds the analytics desk and forecast-coverage tail reads. Trade logs (often under 1,000 trades, some under 100) stay far too small to fit ξ, and even daily series give only dozens of exceedances, so read ξ with its error bars.
✓Report both numbers together, not one alone. The gap between the empirical and EVT-extrapolated estimate is itself information about how much you're relying on the model.
⚠A "20-sigma day" is a statement about the model, not the market. 19 October 1987 (S&P about −20%) was impossible under a normal and merely rare under a heavy tail. It also arrived in a cluster and came from a crisis-only mechanism, which strains EVT's own assumptions. GPD tail calibration out of sample is untested here; "something follows a rare move" is null (mv-extreme-forward).
Section 06

Self-test

Q1. Why can't you reliably estimate a 1-in-2,000-day VaR from two years (about 500 days) of daily data using the plain historical (empirical quantile) method?
A 1-in-2,000-day event happens, on average, about once every 8 years. In a 500-day (roughly 2-year) sample you'd expect to see well under one such event on average — the empirical quantile formula is effectively asking for a data point past the edge of what the sample contains. Even the much milder 1-in-200-day (99.5%) quantile is only supported by about 2.5 observations in a 500-day sample, meaning the "estimate" is really just an interpolation between your second-worst and third-worst historical day — a number driven by two or three data points, not a stable statistical pattern.
Q2. What does a positive Generalized Pareto shape parameter (\(\xi>0\)) tell you about the tail, and how is that different from \(\xi<0\)?
\(\xi>0\) means a genuinely heavy, Pareto-type tail: the probability of exceeding a given loss level decays only polynomially as that level grows, not exponentially — extreme outcomes stay meaningfully possible far out, and once \(\xi \ge 0.5\) the distribution doesn't even have a finite variance. \(\xi<0\) means the opposite qualitative behavior: a bounded tail with a hard finite upper limit on how large an exceedance can get, no matter how much data you collect — a shape financial losses have no strong structural reason to exhibit, unlike some physically capped quantities.
Q3. Why is CVaR (Expected Shortfall) considered a "coherent" risk measure in a way plain VaR is not?
VaR only marks a threshold — the loss level exceeded with probability \(1-p\) — and says nothing about how bad things get once you're past it; two distributions with identical VaR can have very different behavior beyond that point (one barely exceeds it, another runs far past it). CVaR is the average loss given you're already beyond VaR, so it captures the shape of the tail, not just its starting point. Technically, VaR can fail subadditivity (combining two positions can, in constructed examples, make VaR look worse even when each position was individually fine), which violates one of the properties a coherent risk measure must satisfy; CVaR, as an average over the whole tail, doesn't have this failure mode — which is part of why post-2008 banking regulation moved its standard tail-risk measure toward Expected Shortfall.
Q4. What is the peaks-over-threshold (POT) method doing, conceptually, and what theorem justifies fitting a GPD to the result?
POT picks a high threshold \(u\), discards everything below it, and looks only at how far the remaining observations exceed \(u\) (the exceedances \(Y=X-u\)). The Balkema-de Haan-Pickands theorem (1974/1975) says that, as the threshold is pushed high enough, the distribution of these exceedances converges to a Generalized Pareto Distribution essentially regardless of what distribution the original data came from — the POT-specific counterpart to the Fisher-Tippett- Gnedenko theorem for block maxima. That general theorem is what licenses fitting a single GPD family to the tail rather than needing to know or assume the shape of the whole underlying distribution.
Q5. In the worked example, why is the EVT-based estimate described as "smoother but not more true" than the empirical estimate?
The EVT estimate is smoother because it's built from a pattern fitted across many tail exceedances (e.g. 50) rather than read off one or two extreme data points, so it's less sensitive to exactly which observations happened to land in the sample. But it is not more "true" because it is a model- based extrapolation: it assumes the GPD shape fitted from the observed exceedances (say, the 90th– 99th percentile region) continues to hold all the way out to a quantile you've never actually observed (e.g. 1-in-2,000-day). If reality has a structural break out there that the observed region couldn't reveal, the fitted tail has no way of knowing — the smoothness comes from a genuine assumption, not from additional evidence.
Q6. Why would fitting a GPD to most of this repo's actual strategy trade logs (often well under 1,000 trades, sometimes well under 100) be a low-power, easily-overfit exercise right now?
A GPD fit needs a reasonable number of exceedances above the chosen threshold to be even weakly stable — the worked example used 50, already on the low end of typical practice. A strategy with a few hundred trades might offer only a handful of exceedances above a sensible threshold, nowhere near enough to trust the fitted shape parameter \(\xi\), let alone extrapolate it to a rarer quantile than the sample can support. Fitting EVT machinery on that little data doesn't solve the small-sample problem the empirical method already has — it just hides it behind more sophisticated-looking math, which is why this repo fits its GPD layer to daily-return series rather than trade logs, and keeps the empirical historical VaR/CVaR as the default number beside it.
