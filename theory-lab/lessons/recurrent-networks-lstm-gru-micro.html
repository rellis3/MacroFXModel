Recurrent Networks, Carrying Memory Forward

A plain recurrent network reads a sequence one step at a time, carrying a hidden state forward as a running summary of everything relevant it has seen — in principle, arbitrarily far back. In practice, training that network multiplies the same gradient signal by itself at every single step, and it vanishes long before "arbitrarily far back" becomes real. LSTM and GRU cells exist to fix exactly that, with an explicit, gated pathway that lets information — and gradient — survive the trip.

Section 1

A hidden state carries the past forward

→

One step at a time

A recurrent network reads a sequence in order — day 1, then day 2, then day 3 — rather than seeing a whole window as one flat, order-blind snapshot the way a feedforward network or a gradient-boosted tree does.

🗒

The hidden state is a running note

At every step it updates a single vector, h_t, blending what just arrived with whatever it had already summarized — a compressed record of everything relevant seen so far, carried forward.

♻

Same weights, every step

The exact same weight matrices update the note at step 1, step 50, and step 500. This weight sharing is what makes it "recurrent" rather than one enormous, step-specific feedforward stack.

∞

In principle, arbitrarily long history

Nothing in the recurrence caps how far back information could survive. What actually happens to information from far in the past is the entire subject of the next few slides.

Every tree-based or classical-statistics lesson elsewhere in this curriculum treats a row of features as a single, order-independent snapshot — shuffle the rows and a random forest doesn't notice or care. A time series is the opposite kind of object: the order of the observations is the information, and a recurrent network is the first architecture in this category built specifically around that fact.

Section 3

Backpropagation through time: the same weight, multiplied again and again

Training unrolls the recurrence across every step and backprops the loss all the way to step 1.

∂h_T ⁄ ∂h₁
=
W_h · W_h · W_h · … (T−1 copies)

Because h_t depends on h_(t−1), which depends on h_(t−2), and so on, the chain rule produces a gradient that is a product of many terms, one per time step — the same recurrent weight matrix multiplied in again at every single step. A product of many numbers each under 1 doesn't shrink gently; it shrinks exponentially in the number of steps.

Picture a whispered message passed down a long line of people, and each person repeats only about 70% of the volume they heard. By the tenth person the message is nearly silent — not from any single mistake, just from quietly repeating something a little quieter, over and over. That's the vanishing gradient: the training signal about step 1 gets multiplied down a little at every later step, and after enough steps there's essentially nothing left to learn from.
Vanishing vs. exploding

If the dominant eigenvalue of W_h (scaled by tanh′, always ≤ 1) is below 1, the product shrinks toward zero exponentially — vanishing. Above 1, it grows exponentially instead — exploding, which shows up as wildly unstable training updates. Exploding is the easy direction to patch, with gradient clipping; vanishing is not — it's the one that actually blocks learning long-range dependencies, and the one the rest of this lesson is about fixing (Bengio, Simard & Frasconi, 1994).

Three architectures, side by side

Same job — carry information through time. Very different answers to "how does a gradient survive the trip back?"

Same additive principle as the LSTM's cell state, achieved with one merged state and two gates instead of three plus a separate cell state — fewer parameters, usually faster to train, similar practical performance on many tasks.

Section 6

Inside the LSTM: three gates, one conveyor belt

Each gate is itself a small learned network — a sigmoid output between 0 and 1, read as "how much."

✕

Forget gate — f_t

f_t = σ(W_f[h_(t−1),x_t]+b_f) — a number per unit of the cell state; 0 erases it completely, 1 keeps it entirely.

+

Input gate & candidate — i_t, C̃_t

A candidate update C̃_t = tanh(...) proposes new content; the input gate i_t decides how much of that candidate actually gets written in.

➜

Cell-state update — additive

C_t = f_t⊙C_(t−1) + i_t⊙C̃_t — the old state is scaled down, the new candidate is added. Addition, not another matrix multiplication.

◎

Output gate — o_t

h_t = o_t⊙tanh(C_t) — the hidden state is a filtered, task-facing readout of the cell state, not the long-term memory itself.

Differentiate the cell-state update with respect to C_(t−1) and the result is exactly f_t — a single gate value, not a matrix product accumulated across every prior step. When the forget gate learns to stay close to 1 for information that matters, gradient flows across many steps largely undiminished.
Where this needs qualifying

"LSTM" doesn't mean "immune to vanishing gradients." The additive path substantially mitigates the problem compared to a plain RNN — it doesn't eliminate it for arbitrarily long sequences. That gap is real, and it's the honest motivation for the attention mechanism covered next, not an overstatement invented for this lesson.

Section 7

GRU: the same additive idea, fewer moving parts

Two gates instead of three, one merged state instead of two.

LSTM

Three gates (forget, input, output) plus a separate cell state — four learned weight matrices per layer. More expressive, more parameters, generally the safer default on larger datasets or longer sequences.

GRU

Two gates (reset r_t, update z_t), no separate cell state — three learned weight matrices per layer. h_t = (1−z_t)⊙h_(t−1) + z_t⊙h̃_t is itself a convex, additive blend of old and new — structurally the same kind of additive mechanism, with one fewer gate.

Neither is a strict improvement on the other. Fewer parameters typically means faster training and somewhat less overfitting risk on smaller datasets — but which one actually performs better is dataset- and task-dependent, and needs to be checked empirically, not assumed from general reputation.

If the LSTM is a note-taker with a separate long-term notebook (the cell state) and a short summary card they hand off (the hidden state), the GRU is a note-taker who keeps just one notebook and decides, page by page, how much of the old page to keep versus overwrite. Simpler machinery, usually just as good, occasionally not — and cheaper to run either way.

Section 10

Not obsolete — a real, current tradeoff

RNNs and LSTMs were the dominant sequence architecture before 2017 — the next lesson explains why that changed for long sequences. Attention lets every output position look directly at every input position, instead of relaying information step by step through a chain that still degrades gradually even with gating.

A real tradeoff, not an obsolete method

That's a genuine, current tradeoff — not a claim that LSTMs are obsolete. For shorter sequences, streaming or online settings that must process one step at a time as it arrives, and lower-compute environments where a transformer's data and compute appetite isn't affordable, a well-tuned LSTM or GRU remains a genuinely reasonable, still-current choice. A rolling 20-bar EUR/USD return window feeding a small LSTM forecaster, for instance, processes returns in order and can in principle learn to hold onto a large early move rather than treating it as one interchangeable column among twenty — whether that structural edge beats a well-tuned feedforward or gradient-boosted model on the same lagged features is a separate, empirical question.

Section 11

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

What specifically does an RNN's hidden-state recurrence add that a feedforward network structurally lacks?

A feedforward network treats every input dimension independently, with no built-in notion of "this came before that." The recurrence h_t = tanh(W_h h_(t−1) + W_x x_t + b) makes each step's output depend on a running summary of every earlier step — order shapes the computation itself, not just which column a value sits in.
Question 2

Why does a plain RNN's gradient with respect to an early hidden state shrink exponentially as the sequence gets longer?

∂h_T/∂h₁ is a product of T−1 copies of (roughly) W_h. If the dominant eigenvalue of that repeated term is below 1, the product shrinks toward zero exponentially in the number of steps — the vanishing gradient (Bengio, Simard & Frasconi, 1994).
Question 3

Why does the LSTM's cell-state update resist the vanishing-gradient mechanism the plain RNN suffers from?

∂C_t/∂C_(t−1) = f_t — a single gate value, not a matrix product accumulated across every prior step. When the forget gate learns to stay near 1 for information that matters, gradient flows across many steps largely undiminished.
Question 4

What is the core structural difference between a GRU and an LSTM?

Three learned weight matrices instead of four, and one state instead of two. Fewer parameters typically means faster training and somewhat lower overfitting risk on smaller datasets — but which architecture actually performs better is dataset- and task-dependent.
Question 5

Are LSTMs now obsolete, now that attention and transformers exist?

Attention supersedes gated RNNs specifically for long-sequence problems, because it lets every output position look directly at every input position. That doesn't erase the LSTM's genuine strengths for shorter sequences, streaming or online settings, and lower-compute environments — "superseded for long sequences" and "obsolete everywhere" are different claims.
Section 12

What to carry forward

01

A plain RNN carries a hidden state forward step by step, reusing the same weights at every step — in principle giving it access to arbitrarily long history.

02

Training via backpropagation through time unrolls the recurrence and backprops through every step — the gradient reaching an early step is a product of many repeated terms.

03

That repeated multiplication shrinks (or explodes) exponentially with sequence length — the vanishing/exploding gradient problem that cripples plain RNNs on long-range dependencies in practice.

04

The LSTM adds a separate cell state, updated additively and protected by forget, input, and output gates — an "error highway" that lets gradients survive far longer.

05

The GRU merges the cell and hidden state into one and uses just two gates instead of three — fewer parameters, often comparable performance, genuinely dataset-dependent.

06

Gating mitigates vanishing gradients — it doesn't eliminate them for very long sequences. That remaining gap is exactly what motivates attention, next.

What this now lets you do

Recognise when a sequence model actually needs an explicit memory pathway, rather than treating twenty lagged columns as interchangeable features. Explain, mechanically, why a plain RNN forgets — and why an additive cell state fixes it. Treat "LSTM or GRU" as a genuine empirical tradeoff instead of a default pick. And see exactly why the next lesson's attention mechanism had to be invented in the first place.
