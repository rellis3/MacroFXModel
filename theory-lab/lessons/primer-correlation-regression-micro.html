Correlation & Regression, Without Fooling Yourself

Every later lesson that asks "do these two things move together" or "what's the best straight line through this data" is quietly leaning on two tools: correlation and ordinary least squares regression. Cointegration's first step is a regression. The Kalman Filter tracks a hedge ratio that is a regression slope, just one allowed to drift. PCA decomposes a correlation matrix built from exactly the formulas below. This guide builds both tools from nothing — and spends just as much time on how convincingly they lie.

Section 1

"Moving together" is the whole question

Picture a stock's daily return plotted against the broad market's daily return, day after day.

↕

Above average together, below average together

On a day the market is up, is the stock usually also up? On a day it's down, is the stock usually also down? "Usually yes" is what correlation is built to detect.

?

It asks one question, and only one

Do these two series tend to move the same direction, opposite directions, or neither? It does not ask, and cannot answer, why.

🔢

One number, sounding like a fact

"r = 0.87" reads like a verdict about the world. It's actually a summary of one dataset, over one window, assuming the relationship is a straight line.

⛓

Three lessons downstream depend on this one

Cointegration, the Kalman Filter, and PCA all import this machinery by reference rather than re-deriving it — this is where it gets built, once.

A correlation coefficient silently assumes the relationship it's summarizing is linear, and says nothing whatsoever about which variable, if either, is causing the other. Keep both caveats in view — the rest of this guide is largely about what happens when they get forgotten.
Section 2

Covariance: the raw, unscaled version

For each day, multiply how far the stock was from its own average by how far the market was from its own average — then average that product across every day.

Cov(X,Y) = avg[ (X − x̄)(Y − ȳ) ]
Same side of average → positive
  • Both above average, or both below: the product of two positives or two negatives is positive
  • Most day-pairs land here → the average product comes out positive
  • Positive covariance = tend to move the same direction
Opposite sides → negative
  • One above its average while the other is below: one positive deviation times one negative gives a negative product
  • Most day-pairs land here → the average product comes out negative
  • Negative covariance = tend to move opposite directions

Notice this is the exact same machinery as variance, just applied to two variables instead of one — variance is nothing but the covariance of a variable with itself: Var(X) = Cov(X,X). The one problem covariance has as a report card: its size depends on units. The same relationship measured in basis points versus percent produces two completely different-looking numbers, even though nothing about the underlying relationship changed.

Why this needs fixing before it's useful

A covariance of 4.0 in one unit and 0.0004 in a rescaled unit can describe the identical relationship. Covariance's sign is meaningful on its own; its magnitude, by itself, is not comparable across two different pairs of variables. That's precisely the gap correlation exists to close.

Section 3

r: covariance, squeezed onto a ruler from −1 to +1

Divide covariance by the product of each variable's own standard deviation — that single division cancels the units out entirely.

r = Cov(X,Y) / (σX · σY)
∅

Unitless, by construction

σX and σY carry the same units as X and Y, so dividing by both cancels units out of the result completely — r is a pure number.

↔

Provably bounded, −1 to 1

This isn't an empirical observation — it falls directly out of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. r = 1 and r = −1 are the mathematical ceiling and floor, not rare extremes.

🏷

The Pearson correlation coefficient

This exact formalization — covariance rescaled by both standard deviations — is Karl Pearson's, from the 1890s, which is why it carries his name.

⚠

r = 0 means no LINEAR tendency — not "unrelated"

Let y = x² with x ranging symmetrically around zero. y is completely, deterministically fixed by x — yet r comes out near zero, because the positive and negative deviation-products cancel almost exactly.

That last point is the single most common misreading of a correlation coefficient, and it's worth sitting with before moving on: a low r never proves "no relationship." It only proves "no straight-line relationship" — always look at a scatter plot, not just the number.

Section 4

What different values of r actually look like

Same kind of scatterplot, three different strengths of relationship. Flip between them.

Try it
Strong positive. The cloud is a tight band hugging the fitted line — knowing X pins down Y almost exactly. This is the kind of relationship a hedge ratio is worth trusting.
Weak positive. There's a faint upward drift in the fitted line, but any single point tells you almost nothing about Y. This is roughly what "two currencies loosely correlated" looks like up close — real, but not tradeable on its own.
Moderate negative. As X rises, Y tends to fall, with visible but not extreme scatter around the trend. This is the shape a workable hedge relationship often has — negative, clear, but never perfectly clean.
Section 5

Regression: the one line that fits best

Correlation says how strongly two things move together. Regression goes further — it hands you a specific, usable line.

Y = α + βX + ε

α is the intercept — the predicted Y when X = 0. β is the slope — the predicted change in Y per one-unit change in X. ε is everything about Y this straight line doesn't capture. For each observation, the residual is the vertical gap between the actual point and the line's prediction: eᵢ = yᵢ − ŷᵢ.

"Ordinary least squares" is the name of the rule for picking α̂ and β̂: choose whichever values make the sum of the squared residuals as small as possible. Why squared, instead of just adding up the raw gaps? Raw gaps let a huge positive miss cancel a huge negative one, scoring a terrible line as "perfect." Squaring fixes that — and it also punishes big misses disproportionately: a residual twice as large contributes four times the penalty, which is exactly why an OLS line is more sensitive to outliers than an absolute-error fit would be.

What minimizing actually gives you

Setting the calculus to zero produces two conditions: the residuals must sum to exactly zero (the line runs through the data's "center of mass"), and — after substituting that in — the slope collapses to a strikingly clean result: β̂ = Cov(X,Y) / Var(X). The next slide puts that formula to work.

Section 7

R²: how much of the story the line actually explains

Split Y's total variation around its own mean into two pieces: what the line explains, and what's left over in the residuals.

R² = 1 − SSR / SST
1

R² = 1

The line passes through every point exactly — zero leftover residual variance.

0

R² = 0

The line explains nothing beyond just always guessing ȳ — X isn't helping.

=

R² = r², for one predictor only

For simple regression this isn't a coincidence — it falls straight out of substituting the OLS formulas back into the SSR/SST split.

The trap hiding inside that identity

R² can never fall when another predictor is added to a regression — OLS is always free to set a useless predictor's coefficient to exactly zero, reproducing the old fit, so the fit can only stay flat or improve. A regression stuffed with twenty near-random predictors will report a deceptively high in-sample R² for reasons that have nothing to do with any of them actually explaining Y. A suspiciously high R² with many predictors should raise scrutiny, not confidence — prefer adjusted R² or genuine out-of-sample performance.

Section 8

A great-looking correlation can still be lying to you

Two currencies correlated at 0.9 in a backtest could be causally linked, driven by a shared factor, or just coincidentally aligned over that one window — r alone cannot tell you which.

The classic case: across cities and months, ice cream sales and drowning deaths are strongly positively correlated. Ice cream doesn't cause drowning. Both are driven by a third, unmeasured variable — warm weather sends more people swimming and more people to buy ice cream at the same time. This is called confounding, and it's the default explanation to rule out, not a rare edge case.
What a high r does NOT prove
  • That one series causes the other to move
  • That a shared factor (broad USD strength, risk sentiment) isn't secretly driving both
  • That the relationship survives into the next window, out of sample
  • That the relationship is even linear — r ≈ 0 can still hide a strong nonlinear one
What to actually check first
  • Look at the scatter plot itself, not just the coefficient
  • Ask whether there's a clear economic or mechanical reason one should drive the other
  • Test the relationship on data the fit never saw
  • Treat "correlated in returns" as a cheap first filter, not a conclusion

A rolling correlation matrix across a universe of instruments is the standard, cheap first pass for finding relationships worth investigating — and precisely because it's cheap and fast, it's also easy to over-trust. "Correlated in returns" is a necessary but far from sufficient condition for a tradeable relationship.

Section 9

Where this shows up on a desk

⚖️

Hedge ratios — pairs trading's core input

Regress the instrument you're hedging on the hedge instrument. β̂ tells you exactly how many units of the second offset one unit of the first, on average, over the fitted window. This is Step 1 of Engle-Granger cointegration.

📈

Beta and factor exposure

Regress an asset's returns on a benchmark's returns and β̂ is the asset's sensitivity to that factor — literally CAPM beta, the same identity applied to a different X.

🧮

Risk/return decomposition

Regressing performance on known factors splits it into an explained part and a residual — sometimes optimistically labeled "alpha."

🔎

Screening and pair discovery

A rolling correlation matrix is the standard cheap first pass for finding candidate pairs — before the pitfalls on the previous slide get applied to them.

A pairs trade only works because a hedge ratio — a regression slope — is assumed stable enough that hedging one leg against the other cancels out most of the shared risk. When that slope drifts, the "hedge" quietly stops hedging, which is exactly why the Kalman Filter lesson tracks a time-varying version of this same β̂ instead of trusting one fixed-window estimate forever.
Section 10

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

What does a covariance of exactly 0 tell you?

Zero covariance means no linear co-movement in either direction — it says nothing about a nonlinear relationship, which could still be strong.
Question 2

Correlation is covariance rescaled by...

r = Cov(X,Y) / (σX·σY). Dividing by both standard deviations cancels the units out and bounds the result between −1 and 1.
Question 3

For y = x², with x ranging symmetrically around zero, the correlation r is close to...

y is identical for x and −x, so the positive and negative deviation-products in the covariance cancel almost exactly, driving r near zero despite a perfect (nonlinear) relationship.
Question 4

OLS chooses α̂ and β̂ to minimize...

Summing raw residuals lets huge positive and negative misses cancel out and score a bad line as "perfect." Squaring fixes that, at the cost of punishing outliers more heavily.
Question 5

A regression's R² rose from 0.40 to 0.55 after adding ten more predictors, most of which look like noise. What should you conclude?

OLS can always set a useless predictor's coefficient to zero and reproduce the old fit exactly, so R² is mechanically incapable of falling when predictors are added. A rise alone proves nothing — check adjusted R² or out-of-sample performance.
Section 11

What to carry forward

01

Covariance measures co-movement in raw units — its sign tells you direction, but its magnitude alone isn't comparable across different series.

02

Correlation r rescales covariance by both variables' spread into a unitless number bounded [−1, 1] — but r = 0 only rules out a linear relationship, never a relationship altogether.

03

The OLS regression slope, β̂ = Cov(X,Y) / Var(X), isn't a separate idea from correlation — it's the same covariance, normalized by X's own variance instead of by both variables' spreads.

04

R² = 1 − SSR/SST measures the fraction of Y's variance the line explains, and equals r² only for one predictor — adding more predictors can only push R² up, never down, whether or not they're real.

05

A high correlation proves only that two things moved together over one sample window — never that one caused the other, that a hidden factor didn't drive both, or that it survives into the next window.

06

A hedge ratio and a CAPM beta are both, literally, a regression slope — this is the exact machinery the Cointegration and Kalman Filter lessons build on next.

What this now lets you do

Read a correlation matrix without over-trusting a single number, compute a hedge ratio by hand from raw price pairs, and spot R²-inflation and spurious backtest correlation before they cost real money.
