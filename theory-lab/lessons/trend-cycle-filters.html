Trend/Cycle Filters: Hodrick-Prescott, Butterworth & Baxter-King

Splitting a series into "the slow-moving trend" and "the cycle riding on top of it" is one of the most-used, and most-criticized, moves in applied macroeconomics — the Hodrick-Prescott filter is everywhere, has a documented, load-bearing flaw, and the band-pass alternatives exist specifically because of it.

CONCEPT — classical macro/signal-processing filters, explained here as background knowledge, not built or tested anywhere in this repo ~25 min read Prereqs: Fourier Analysis & the Frequency Domain
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • Any series can be thought of as trend + cycle (+ noise), \(y_t = \tau_t + c_t + \varepsilon_t\); a trend/cycle filter is a specific rule for splitting a single observed series back into those unobserved pieces.
  • The Hodrick-Prescott (HP) filter finds the trend \(\tau_t\) that minimizes \(\sum(y_t-\tau_t)^2 + \lambda\sum\big[(\tau_{t+1}-\tau_t)-(\tau_t-\tau_{t-1})\big]^2\) — a direct tradeoff between fitting the data closely and keeping the trend smooth, dialed by \(\lambda\). The standard convention is \(\lambda=1600\) for quarterly data; a common, real mistake is reusing that number unscaled at a different observation frequency.
  • James Hamilton's 2018 paper is a genuine, load-bearing caveat, not a footnote: the HP filter suffers a well-documented end-of-sample bias, and can manufacture spurious cyclical dynamics that aren't in the true data-generating process at all — serious enough that Hamilton titled the paper "Why You Should Never Use the Hodrick-Prescott Filter."
  • Butterworth and Baxter-King filters are band-pass alternatives, designed to isolate one specific cycle-length band (classically the 6-to-32-quarter "business cycle" band) while damping frequencies outside it — motivated in part by dissatisfaction with HP's known flaws.
  • Explaining these filters is not a claim that trend/cycle decomposition produces trading edge — it's a description of a widely used (and widely debated) modeling choice, with an honestly stated, citation-backed flaw front and center.
  • Real-world episode: in the 1970s, real-time US output-gap estimates showed far more slack than later revised estimates did, and policy stayed loose while inflation rose (Orphanides, 2001). Orphanides & van Norden (2002) found the revisions came mainly from the end of the sample, the HP filter included, not from revised data.
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

Central banks keep talking about the output gap. Where does that number actually come from?

From a filter. Think of the sea at a pier. There's the tide, a slow rise and fall, and the waves on top. The gap is the waves. But you only ever see the total, so someone has to decide where the tide line is.

And the usual way to decide?

The Hodrick-Prescott filter. It draws a trend that stays close to the data but is punished for bending. One dial sets how hard bending is punished. Turn it to zero and the trend is just the data. Turn it to infinity and you get a straight line.

What do people set the dial to?

Sixteen hundred, for quarterly data. That's the convention. The classic mistake is reusing sixteen hundred on monthly data. The right value scales with the fourth power of the frequency ratio. Three months to a quarter, three to the fourth is eighty-one, so monthly needs about a hundred and thirty thousand.

That's a huge difference.

It is. But the bigger problem is the end of the sample. The filter looks both backwards and forwards in time. At the newest quarter there is no forwards. So the newest trend reading, the one policy actually uses, is the least reliable and gets revised as new data arrive.

Has that ever mattered?

The 1970s. Trend growth slowed: productivity faded from the late sixties and the 1973 oil shock hit supply. Trend lines fitted to the strong fifties and sixties sat too high. So the measured gap showed lots of spare capacity, and policy stayed loose. Inflation rose through the decade, into double digits by its end.

How do we know the slack wasn't there?

Athanasios Orphanides rebuilt the numbers policymakers had at the time, in 2001. The real-time gaps were far more negative than today's estimates for the same quarters. Then, with Simon van Norden, he showed the revisions came mainly from the end of the sample, not from revised data.

So should I stop using the filter?

James Hamilton's 2018 paper is literally titled "Why You Should Never Use the Hodrick-Prescott Filter". Besides the end problem, it can invent cycles in a pure random walk. The alternatives are band-pass filters, which keep only cycles of six to thirty-two quarters, but they lose data at both ends instead.

One line?

A trend is a modelling choice, not a measurement, and its newest point is the one most likely to be wrong.

Section 01

Why this matters

A huge amount of applied macroeconomic and business-cycle work rests on a single, deceptively simple move: take one observed series (GDP, industrial production, unemployment) and split it into a slow-moving "potential" or "trend" component and a faster "cyclical" or "gap" component — the output gap, in central-bank language. That split is never directly observed; it has to be estimated by some rule, and the rule chosen has real consequences, because policy decisions (is the economy overheating? is there slack to fill?) get made off the cyclical component's sign and size. This lesson covers the two families of rule used to do that splitting — a time-domain penalized-smoothness approach (Hodrick-Prescott) and frequency-domain band-pass approaches (Butterworth, Baxter-King) — building on the frequency-domain vocabulary from Fourier Analysis & the Frequency Domain, since a band-pass filter is defined entirely in terms of which frequencies it lets through and which it damps.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Separating the tide from the waves

Watch the sea level at a pier over a full day and you'll see two things layered on top of each other: the tide — a slow, smooth rise and fall over roughly twelve hours — and the waves, choppy up-and-down motion happening every few seconds on top of the tide. If you squint and mentally draw a smooth line through the choppy waves, tracking only their average height as it slowly rises and falls, you've just done, by eye, exactly what a trend/cycle filter does mathematically: separated a slow, smooth "trend" (the tide) from a faster "cycle" (the waves) riding on top of it. The hard part, and the part these filters formalize differently from each other, is deciding how smooth "smooth" has to be before something counts as trend rather than cycle — draw the line too wiggly and you've called some of the waves "tide"; draw it too flat and you've called some of the tide's own rise and fall "waves." Every filter in this lesson is a different, precise rule for making that call.

Section 02

The math, step by step

Watch the formula build
Where the Hodrick-Prescott trend comes from, one term at a time
\[ \color{#fbbf24}{\sum_{t=1}^{T} (y_t - \tau_t)^2} \]

Start with fit. On its own this is smallest when the trend copies the data exactly, \(\tau_t = y_t\), so the "trend" would include every wiggle.

\[ \sum_{t=1}^{T} (y_t - \tau_t)^2 + \color{#fbbf24}{\sum_{t=2}^{T-1} \big[(\tau_{t+1}-\tau_t) - (\tau_t-\tau_{t-1})\big]^2} \]

Add a charge for bending. The bracket is the trend's second difference: zero on a straight line, large wherever the slope changes.

\[ \sum_{t=1}^{T} (y_t - \tau_t)^2 + \color{#fbbf24}{\lambda} \sum_{t=2}^{T-1} \big[(\tau_{t+1}-\tau_t) - (\tau_t-\tau_{t-1})\big]^2 \]

Weight the charge with \(\lambda\). \(\lambda \to 0\) gives back the raw data; \(\lambda \to \infty\) forces a straight line. The quarterly convention is \(\lambda = 1600\); monthly data needs \(1600 \times 3^4 = 129{,}600\).

\[ \color{#10b981}{c_t = y_t - \hat\tau_t, \quad \hat\tau = \arg\min_{\tau} \Big\{ \sum (y_t - \tau_t)^2 + \lambda \sum \big[\Delta^2 \tau_t\big]^2 \Big\}} \]

The trend is whatever minimises the total, and the cycle, the "gap", is what is left. Every \(\hat\tau_t\) uses data on both sides of \(t\), which is why the newest one, with no data after it, is the least reliable.

Four pieces: (1) the general trend+cycle decomposition problem; (2) the Hodrick-Prescott filter and its \(\lambda\) parameter, including the frequency-rescaling rule; (3) Hamilton's documented critique; (4) Butterworth and Baxter-King as band-pass alternatives.

1. The general decomposition problem

\[ y_t \;=\; \tau_t \;+\; c_t \;+\; \varepsilon_t \]
y_tthe observed series
τ_tthe (unobserved) trend — the slow-moving component
c_tthe (unobserved) cycle — the faster component riding on the trend
ε_tirreducible noise, belonging to neither

None of \(\tau_t\), \(c_t\), \(\varepsilon_t\) is directly observed — only their sum, \(y_t\), is. Every filter below is a specific, explicit rule for recovering an estimate of \(\tau_t\) (and, by subtraction, \(c_t = y_t - \tau_t\)) from the one series that's actually available. Different rules embody different assumptions about what "trend" means, and — this is the crucial, easy-to-forget point — none of them recovers the "true" trend, because there is no ground truth to check against; each is a modeling choice, not a measurement.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Only the total (the tide-plus-waves reading you actually see) is ever measured directly — nobody gets to see "just the tide" or "just the waves" on their own to check an answer against. Every trend/cycle filter is really just a rule for guessing how to split that one number back into two, and different rules guess differently — there's no scorecard for which guess was exactly right, because the true split was never observed in the first place.

2. The Hodrick-Prescott filter

The HP filter (Hodrick & Prescott, developed in a 1980 working paper, published 1997) defines the trend \(\tau_t\) as whatever series minimizes this penalized objective:

\[ \sum_{t=1}^{T} (y_t - \tau_t)^2 \;+\; \lambda \sum_{t=2}^{T-1} \big[(\tau_{t+1}-\tau_t) - (\tau_t-\tau_{t-1})\big]^2 \]
Σ(y_t−τ_t)²fidelity term — penalizes the trend for straying from the data
(τ_{t+1}−τ_t)−(τ_t−τ_{t-1})the trend's second difference — its curvature, or how much its own slope is changing
λthe dial — how heavily curvature gets penalized relative to data-fit

The first term is a familiar least-squares fidelity term — it wants \(\tau_t\) to track \(y_t\) closely. The second term penalizes the trend's curvature: the bracketed expression is the second difference of \(\tau_t\), which is zero exactly when the trend is following a perfectly straight line (constant slope) and grows whenever the trend's slope itself is changing — i.e. whenever the trend is bending. \(\lambda\) is the single dial controlling the tradeoff between these two competing goals:

λ → 0
No smoothing
The curvature penalty vanishes; the "trend" becomes the raw data itself, τ_t = y_t
λ = 1600
Standard quarterly convention
Hodrick & Prescott's original choice, calibrated for quarterly U.S. business-cycle data
λ → ∞
Maximum smoothing
Curvature must be exactly zero everywhere; the trend collapses to a single straight line (linear trend)
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Think of λ as a dial on how stubbornly straight you insist the trend line has to be. Turn it all the way down and the "trend" just becomes the raw, wiggly data itself — no smoothing at all. Turn it all the way up and the trend gets forced into one perfectly straight line, ignoring every wiggle in the actual data. λ = 1600 is a specific, standard middle setting that's been the default for quarterly economic data for decades.

\(\lambda = 1600\) is the number virtually every textbook and paper quotes, and it is specific to quarterly observations — it is not a universal constant. A common, real mistake is reusing \(\lambda=1600\) unscaled on monthly or annual data, where it is simply the wrong smoothing strength for that frequency. Ravn & Uhlig (2002) proposed the standard rescaling rule: since higher-frequency data has more observations per cycle, \(\lambda\) should scale with the fourth power of the ratio of observation frequencies:

\[ \lambda_{\text{new}} \;=\; \lambda_{\text{quarterly}} \times s^{4} \]
λ_quarterlythe 1600 baseline, calibrated for quarterly data
show many "new-frequency" periods fit in one quarter (e.g. 3 for monthly)
λ_newthe rescaled penalty at the new observation frequency

Concretely: monthly data has 3 months per quarter, so \(s=3\), giving \(\lambda_{\text{monthly}} = 1600 \times 3^4 = 1600 \times 81 = 129{,}600\). Annual data has 4 quarters per year, so \(\lambda_{\text{annual}} = 1600 \times (1/4)^4 = 1600/256 \approx 6.25\). Skipping this rescaling — using \(\lambda=1600\) directly on monthly data, say — under-smooths the trend by roughly a factor of 81, letting far more monthly noise leak into what's supposed to be the smooth trend component.

3. Hamilton's critique — why this is a real, load-bearing caveat

James Hamilton's 2018 paper in the Review of Economics and Statistics, bluntly titled "Why You Should Never Use the Hodrick-Prescott Filter," documents two specific, serious problems, not a vague stylistic objection:

  • End-of-sample (end-point) bias. The HP filter as defined above is two-sided — the trend estimate at time \(t\) depends on data both before and after \(t\). Near the very end of a real, finite sample there is no "after" data available, so the filter's effective behavior near the most recent observations becomes far more one-sided than in the middle of the sample, producing a trend estimate that can swing noticeably once new data arrives and gets appended — exactly the region (the most recent data) that matters most for a real-time policy decision.
  • Spurious cyclical dynamics. Hamilton shows that applying the HP filter can manufacture cyclical-looking dynamics in the extracted \(c_t\) component even when applied to data that is, by construction, a pure random walk with no genuine cyclical structure at all — meaning some of what gets read as "the business cycle" in an HP-filtered series can be a mechanical artifact of the filter itself, not a feature of the underlying economy.
The load-bearing caveat: this is not a minor implementation detail — Hamilton (2018) argues the end-point bias hits hardest exactly where policymakers most need a reliable trend/cycle read (the most recent data), and that the filter can invent cyclicality that isn't in the true process at all. Cogley & Nason (1995) documented a related spurious-cyclicality result more than two decades earlier. Treat HP output — and especially its most recent few observations — as a debatable modeling choice with known, documented flaws, never as ground truth about "the" output gap.
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

The HP filter looks both backward and forward in time to smooth out today's trend estimate. That works great in the middle of a long dataset, where there's plenty of "forward" data to look at — but right at the very newest data point, there's no future to look at yet, so the filter has to guess with only half the information it normally uses. That's exactly why the most recent reading is the least trustworthy one, and why it tends to get revised later once more data arrives.

4. Butterworth and Baxter-King — band-pass alternatives

Both HP's flaws are part of the motivation for a different family of filter, defined directly in the frequency domain (see Fourier Analysis & the Frequency Domain): a band-pass filter that explicitly targets one specific range of cycle lengths and damps everything else, rather than implicitly defining "cycle" as "whatever the penalized-smoothness objective leaves over."

  • The Butterworth filter is borrowed directly from electrical engineering (originally designed for analog circuits), specified by an order (controlling how sharply it transitions from passing to blocking frequencies — a higher order gives a steeper roll-off at the cost of more complexity) and a cutoff frequency (or a pair of cutoffs, for a band-pass version) marking where that transition happens. Its defining engineering property is being maximally flat within the passband — it doesn't ripple or distort frequencies it's supposed to let through — while still rolling off outside it.
  • The Baxter-King filter (Baxter & King, 1999) is a finite moving-average filter specifically designed to approximate an ideal band-pass filter — one that would pass a target cycle-length band, classically 6 to 32 quarters ("the business cycle" band in their and much subsequent literature), essentially perfectly, and block everything outside it essentially completely. Because it's a finite (truncated) moving average using \(K\) leads and \(K\) lags, it necessarily loses \(K\) observations at each end of the sample — a straightforward data-loss tradeoff, not a bias — in exchange for a good approximation to that ideal band-pass shape.

Both trade the HP filter's implicit, smoothness-based notion of trend for an explicit statement of exactly which cycle lengths count as "the cycle" — a real conceptual clarity gain, purchased at the cost of having to state that band's boundaries as an assumption up front, and (for Baxter-King specifically) losing usable observations at the edges of the sample instead of Hamilton's end-point bias.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Instead of separating "smooth" from "wiggly" the way HP does, a band-pass filter is more like a radio tuner: you tell it exactly which range of cycle lengths counts as "the cycle" — say, patterns that repeat every 6 to 32 quarters — and it keeps only that range, filtering out anything faster or slower, the same way tuning a radio keeps one station and mutes the static around it.

Worked example

Rescaling λ for monthly and annual data

Using the Ravn-Uhlig fourth-power rule above, with \(\lambda_{\text{quarterly}}=1600\) as the baseline:

Monthly (3 months per quarter, ratio = 3): λ_monthly = 1600 × 3⁴ = 1600 × 81 = 129,600
Annual (4 quarters per year, ratio = 1/4): λ_annual = 1600 × (1/4)⁴ = 1600 / 256 ≈ 6.25

The size of that jump — nearly 130,000 for monthly data versus roughly 6 for annual data, an 80-fold-plus range around the quarterly baseline of 1600 — is exactly why "just use 1600" is a real, easy-to-make mistake at any frequency other than quarterly: reusing 1600 on monthly data would apply dramatically too little smoothing (letting much more month-to-month noise leak into the "trend"), and reusing it on annual data would apply dramatically too much (over-smoothing a series that already has fewer, lower-frequency observations to begin with).

Real-world trading scenario

A central bank's 2019 output-gap estimate, revised by data it didn't have yet

A central bank's research desk estimates the HP-filtered output gap using quarterly GDP data through 2019 Q4, concluding the economy is running close to potential — a real-time judgment that feeds directly into that quarter's policy discussion. Two years later, with 2020's COVID-driven collapse and 2021's rebound now part of the same dataset, the desk re-runs the identical HP filter over the extended sample and finds the trend estimate at 2019 Q4 itself has shifted meaningfully from what was reported in real time — not because the 2019 data changed, but because the two-sided filter's 2019 Q4 estimate was, at the time, being computed without any of the "future" data (2020-2021) it would eventually have access to. This is Hamilton's end-of-sample bias playing out exactly where it matters most: the reading a decision-maker actually had in hand at the moment the decision was made was the least reliable part of the whole filtered series, and it revised substantially once more data arrived — a genuine, documented reason real-time HP-based output-gap estimates get revised, not a hypothetical concern.

A band-pass filter's gain: what it keeps vs. what it damps
Schematic, conceptual gain curve for a band-pass filter (Butterworth or Baxter-King), not a fitted filter design. Frequencies corresponding to cycles longer than 32 quarters (the trend) and shorter than 6 quarters (noise) are damped toward zero gain; frequencies in between — the classic "business cycle" band — pass through close to unchanged. A higher Butterworth order sharpens the shoulders (a steeper transition); a longer Baxter-King truncation length \(K\) more closely approximates this idealized shape, at the cost of losing more observations at each end of the sample.
Section 03

It actually happened: the 1970s output gap that wasn't there

It actually happened

Policy set on the newest point of a trend, the least reliable one

The scenario above is a hypothetical desk revising its 2019 gap after 2020. The best-documented real case is fifty years older, and it shaped how economists think about this whole lesson.

In the 1970s US policymakers judged that the economy was running well below its potential: a large negative output gap, meaning plenty of spare capacity. The estimates came from trend lines fitted to past output and carried forward. But trend growth had slowed. Productivity growth fell from the late 1960s, and the 1973 oil shock hit supply further. A trend fitted to the strong 1950s and 1960s, extended to the newest quarter, sat too high, so the gap between that trend and actual output looked deeply negative. Policy that believes there is lots of slack stays looser than it otherwise would. Inflation rose through the decade, into double digits by its end.

Athanasios Orphanides, then a Federal Reserve economist, rebuilt the numbers policymakers actually had at the time ("Monetary Policy Rules Based on Real-Time Data", American Economic Review, 2001). The real-time gaps were far more negative than the gaps economists estimate today for the same quarters. Much of the "slack" had never existed.

Then, with Simon van Norden ("The Unreliability of Output-Gap Estimates in Real Time", Review of Economics and Statistics, 2002), he asked why. Across many detrending methods, the HP filter included, revisions to the gap were about as large as the gap itself. The main culprit was not revised GDP data. It was the end of the sample: the trend estimate at the newest observation changes as later data arrive. That is Section 02's end-point problem, measured on the numbers policy was really set on.

Where the textbook version breaks

The HP minimisation treats every \(\tau_t\) the same, but the newest one has data on only one side, and it is the one decisions use. The textbook also assumes trend and cycle are separable, with the trend smooth. A slowdown in trend growth (the 1970s) or a collapse like 2020's gets split between trend and cycle in a way only later data can correct, so the real-time gap mixes a genuine cycle with a trend error of unknown size.

Section 04

How practitioners actually use this

The HP filter has been, for decades, the default tool for output-gap and potential-GDP estimation across central banks and international institutions, precisely because it is simple, requires no assumption about which specific cycle length matters, and produces a smooth, intuitive-looking trend line. That dominance is exactly why Hamilton's 2018 critique landed as forcefully as it did in the academic literature — it wasn't attacking an obscure tool, it was attacking the field's default. In practice today, many researchers report HP-filtered results alongside a robustness check using Hamilton's suggested regression-based alternative, or a band-pass filter, rather than relying on HP alone; Baxter-King and Christiano-Fitzgerald (a related, asymmetric band-pass filter) remain the standard citation for an explicit "business cycle band" definition when a specific cycle-length range, rather than an implicit smoothness tradeoff, is what the analysis actually needs. Butterworth filters see more use in the signal-processing and engineering literature generally, and appear in economics mainly as one specific way to implement a band-pass idea computationally.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where trend/cycle filters go wrong

  • Reusing λ=1600 at the wrong observation frequency. The single most common HP-filter mistake — see the worked example above for exactly how far off an unscaled reuse can be (an 80-fold-plus range between the correctly rescaled monthly and annual values).
  • Trusting the HP filter's most recent observations. Per Hamilton (2018), the end-of-sample bias hits hardest exactly at the newest data points — the ones most likely to matter for a live decision — and those estimates should be expected to revise as more data arrives, not treated as settled.
  • Mistaking HP-filtered cyclicality for genuine business-cycle dynamics. Hamilton (and Cogley & Nason before him) showed the filter can manufacture cycle-looking patterns even from a pure random walk with no true cyclical structure at all — a smooth-looking extracted "cycle" is not, by itself, proof that a real cycle exists in the underlying process.
  • Treating the 6-32 quarter "business cycle" band as a law of nature. It's Baxter & King's chosen convention, motivated by prior macroeconomic research on typical U.S. cycle lengths — a reasonable default, but a modeling assumption stated up front, not a universal physical constant that applies unchanged to every economy or every series.
  • Losing observations at the edges with Baxter-King and not accounting for it. A truncation length \(K\) means the \(K\) most recent and \(K\) oldest observations in the sample have no filtered value at all — a real, visible cost that needs to be planned around, not an oversight to be surprised by later.
  • Treating any of these as a trading signal on their own. A trend/cycle decomposition is a descriptive modeling choice about how to split one series into two unobserved pieces — it is not, by itself, evidence of a tradeable edge until whatever rule is built on top of it clears an honest out-of-sample test.
Scenario drill · the mid-1970s

The gap says there is plenty of slack

You advise a US policymaker in the mid-1970s. Your potential-output trend is fitted to past output, mostly the strong growth of the 1950s and 1960s, and carried forward to the newest quarter. It shows a large negative output gap: lots of spare capacity. But productivity growth has been fading since the late 1960s, the 1973 oil shock has hit supply, and inflation is rising. What do you tell the policymaker?

  1. The gap is large and negative, so there is room to keep policy loose.
    This trusts the single least reliable number in the series: the trend at the newest quarter, estimated with no later data and anchored to growth rates that may no longer hold. If trend growth has slowed, the trend sits too high and the slack is partly an artefact.
  2. The newest gap reading is the least reliable point, and trend growth may have slowed. Treat the slack as uncertain and give real weight to rising inflation.
    This is the lesson's point in practice. The end of a trend estimate revises the most, and a slowdown in trend growth shows up first as a gap that is too negative. Rising inflation is direct evidence that the slack may not be there.
  3. Switch to a band-pass filter that keeps only 6 to 32 quarter cycles.
    A fair instinct, since it states the cycle band openly, but a finite band-pass filter such as Baxter-King loses its last K observations, so it gives no reading at all for the newest quarters. It does not answer the question you were asked.
  4. Raise the smoothing so the trend is less influenced by the latest wobbles.
    More smoothing pushes the trend closer to a straight line drawn through the fast-growth past, so it sits even higher at the newest quarter and the gap looks even more negative. It makes this particular error worse.

What happened. Policy that believed in large slack stayed looser than an accurate gap would have called for, and inflation rose through the decade, into double digits by its end. Athanasios Orphanides (American Economic Review, 2001) rebuilt the real-time data and found the gaps policymakers had were far more negative than today's estimates for the same quarters: much of the slack had never existed. With Simon van Norden (2002) he showed that across many detrending methods, the HP filter included, revisions were about as large as the gap itself, driven mainly by the end of the sample, not by revised GDP.

Sources: Orphanides, "Monetary Policy Rules Based on Real-Time Data", American Economic Review 91(4), 2001; Orphanides and van Norden, "The Unreliability of Output-Gap Estimates in Real Time", Review of Economics and Statistics 84(4), 2002.

Section 05

Key Takeaways

✓Trend/cycle decomposition is y_t = τ_t + c_t + ε_t, estimated by a chosen rule. None of the three components is directly observed, and no filter recovers a "true" trend — each is a modeling choice.
✓HP minimizes fit-vs-smoothness, dialed by λ. λ=1600 is the standard quarterly convention; rescale by the fourth power of the frequency ratio (Ravn-Uhlig) at any other frequency — monthly ≈129,600, annual ≈6.25.
✗Hamilton (2018): the HP filter has real, documented flaws. End-of-sample bias hits the most recent (most decision-relevant) observations hardest, and the filter can manufacture spurious cyclicality even from a pure random walk.
✓Butterworth and Baxter-King isolate an explicit cycle-length band instead. Baxter-King's classic 6-32 quarter "business cycle" band trades HP's end-point bias for a stated, cleaner data-loss tradeoff (losing K observations at each end).
⚠The "business cycle band" is a convention, not a law. 6-32 quarters is Baxter & King's chosen default, not a universal constant true of every series or every economy.
✗The newest gap estimate is the one that misleads (1970s). Real-time output gaps showed slack that revised data later erased; Orphanides & van Norden (2002) traced most of the revision to the end of the sample, not to revised GDP. Whether a gap reading predicts rates or FX is untested here.
ℹThis lesson explains modeling tools, not a claim of edge. A trend/cycle split is descriptive; turning it into a trading rule still needs the same honest out-of-sample bar as anything else in this curriculum.
Section 06

Self-test

Q1. What does the second-difference penalty term in the HP objective actually penalize, and what does λ→∞ do to the trend as a result?
The bracketed term \((\tau_{t+1}-\tau_t)-(\tau_t-\tau_{t-1})\) is the trend's second difference — how much its own slope changes from one period to the next. It is exactly zero when the trend has constant slope (a perfectly straight line) and grows whenever the trend bends. As \(\lambda \to \infty\), any nonzero curvature becomes infinitely costly, forcing the optimal trend toward the one shape with zero curvature everywhere: a single straight line, regardless of how the actual data wiggles around it.
Q2. Why is λ=1600 specific to quarterly data, and what goes wrong if it's applied unscaled to monthly data?
λ=1600 was calibrated by Hodrick and Prescott for the smoothness/fit tradeoff appropriate to quarterly observation spacing. Monthly data has three times as many observations per quarter, so a cycle of any given real-world length spans three times as many data points — the same λ would be far too weak a smoothing penalty relative to how "choppy" monthly data looks point-to-point. The Ravn-Uhlig rule fixes this by scaling λ with the fourth power of the frequency ratio, giving λ≈129,600 for monthly data; using 1600 directly on monthly data under-smooths by roughly a factor of 81, letting far more short-run monthly noise get labeled "trend" than the quarterly convention ever intended.
Q3. What specifically does Hamilton (2018) mean by "end-of-sample bias," and why does it matter more for policy than a bias in the middle of the sample would?
The HP filter's trend estimate at any point in the sample depends on data both before and after that point. Near the very end of a real, finite sample, there is no "after" data yet, so the filter's effective behavior there is much more asymmetric (closer to one-sided) than in the middle of a long sample where data exists on both sides. That means the trend estimate for the most recent observations is systematically less reliable and more likely to revise once new data arrives — and those most-recent observations are exactly the ones a real-time policy decision (is the economy overheating right now?) actually depends on, unlike a historical trend estimate from the middle of the sample which no live decision hinges on.
Q4. How can the HP filter produce a "spurious cycle" from data that has no genuine cyclical structure at all?
Hamilton (2018), building on Cogley & Nason (1995), shows that applying the HP filter's smoothing/fitting procedure to a series generated by a pure random walk — which by construction has no true cyclical component, just an accumulating random drift — can still produce an extracted cyclical residual \(c_t = y_t - \tau_t\) that displays cycle-like autocorrelation patterns. This happens because the filter's smoothing mechanism itself imposes structure on the residual, independent of whether the underlying process actually has any genuine periodic behavior; the resulting "cycle" is a mechanical artifact of the filtering procedure, not evidence of a real economic cycle.
Q5. What tradeoff does the Baxter-King filter accept in exchange for isolating a specific cycle-length band, and how is that tradeoff different in kind from Hamilton's HP critique?
Baxter-King is a finite (truncated) moving average using K leads and K lags, so it cannot produce a filtered value for the K most recent and K oldest observations in the sample — a straightforward loss of usable observations at each end. This is fundamentally different from Hamilton's HP critique: Baxter-King's cost is an explicit, known, and fully anticipated data loss (you simply don't get an answer for those points), whereas HP's end-of-sample problem is a bias — it still produces an answer for the most recent points, but that answer is systematically less reliable and prone to revision, without necessarily being obvious that it's unreliable at the time.
Q6. Why is the classic 6-to-32-quarter "business cycle" band described in this lesson as a convention rather than a fact?
Baxter & King chose that specific band based on prior macroeconomic research characterizing typical U.S. business-cycle lengths at the time — it's a reasonable, widely adopted default, but it's a modeling assumption about which cycle lengths are "the" business cycle, stated up front as a choice, not a universal physical constant. A different economy, a different historical period, or a different series entirely could plausibly have its economically meaningful cyclical activity concentrated in a different frequency band, which is exactly why the band should be treated as a stated assumption to be justified for the specific application, not applied automatically everywhere.
