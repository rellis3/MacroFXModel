Itô's Lemma

Ordinary calculus's chain rule quietly assumes a curve is smooth enough that its own squared wiggle vanishes in the limit. A Brownian path is not that smooth, and the term ordinary calculus throws away turns out to be exactly the \(\sigma^2/2\) the Black-Scholes lesson uses in its \(d_1\)/\(d_2\) formula without ever deriving it. This lesson derives it.

CONCEPT — the derivation Black-Scholes assumed ~18 min read Prereqs: Calculus for Finance, Black-Scholes-Merton Option Pricing
TL;DR
  • Flagged up front as the most mathematically demanding lesson in this curriculum — the result is one line, but getting there requires accepting a genuinely strange fact about Brownian motion.
  • The ordinary chain rule works because a smooth function's increment \(\Delta x\) is order \(\Delta t\), so its square is order \(\Delta t^2\) and vanishes in the limit — Brownian motion breaks this, since \(dW_t\) is order \(\sqrt{dt}\), making \((dW_t)^2\) order \(dt\), not negligible.
  • That single fact is written as the identity \((dW_t)^2 = dt\) (a quadratic-variation statement about the aggregate, not any one random draw), and it forces an extra term into the Taylor expansion that ordinary calculus is entitled to drop — this extra term is Itô's Lemma.
  • Applying Itô's Lemma to \(\ln S_t\) under geometric Brownian motion makes every \(S_t\) cancel and leaves \(d(\ln S_t) = (\mu - \sigma^2/2)\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t\) — the mechanical, non-arbitrary origin of the \((r\pm\sigma^2/2)\) terms inside Black-Scholes's \(d_1\)/\(d_2\), which that lesson simply asserted.
  • The same \(\sigma^2/2\) correction also appears informally elsewhere in this curriculum (volatility drag in the Kelly Criterion and Returns & Compounding lessons) — three independent appearances of one underlying piece of mathematics.
Section 01

Why this matters

Say this plainly up front: this is the most mathematically demanding lesson in this curriculum so far. It is not demanding because the final result is complicated — it fits in one line — but because getting there requires accepting a genuinely strange fact about Brownian motion that has no counterpart anywhere in ordinary calculus. There is no shortcut around that strangeness; the honest thing to do is derive it carefully rather than wave at it.

black-scholes.html, elsewhere in this Theory Lab, builds the entire option-pricing model on the assumption that the underlying follows geometric Brownian motion, \(dS_t = \mu S_t\,dt + \sigma S_t\,dW_t\), and then states — without deriving it — that "solving this SDE (a standard but non-trivial step using Itô's Lemma, which is outside this lesson's scope) shows that \(\ln S_t\) is normally distributed." That same lesson's closed-form formula contains a term, \(r + \sigma^2/2\) inside \(d_1\) and \(r - \sigma^2/2\) inside \(d_2\), that is simply asserted. Anyone reading carefully should ask: where does that \(\sigma^2/2\) actually come from? It is not an arbitrary correction, not a risk premium, not a fudge factor calibrated to make the model fit — it is the direct, mechanical consequence of applying the chain rule correctly to a random process, and "correctly" here requires a genuinely different piece of calculus than the one taught in an ordinary calculus course. That piece is Itô's Lemma, named for the Japanese mathematician Kiyosi Itô, who published the result in 1944. This lesson builds it from first principles, starting from the ordinary calculus chain rule everyone already knows.

💡
Plain-English intuition

A coastline that never gets straighter, no matter how far you zoom in

Mathematician Benoit Mandelbrot popularized a famous observation about measuring the coastline of Britain: the closer you look, the more jagged detail you find — bays within bays, inlets within inlets — so the "length" of the coastline keeps growing the finer your ruler gets, rather than converging to a fixed number the way it would for a smooth curve. A smooth curve behaves the opposite way: zoom in far enough on any point of a smooth curve (a parabola, a sine wave, the trajectory of a thrown ball) and it looks, for practical purposes, like a straight line. That "it becomes a straight line under a microscope" property is not a minor technical footnote — it is the entire justification for differential calculus. The derivative \(f'(x)\) is defined as the slope of that straight line, and the chain rule works because a composition of two "eventually straight under a microscope" objects is itself "eventually straight under a microscope."

A Brownian motion path is the coastline, not the parabola. However far you zoom in on a Brownian path, it never looks like a straight line — it stays exactly as jagged, at every scale, all the way down. This isn't poetic exaggeration; it is a precise mathematical property (Brownian paths are, with probability one, continuous everywhere but differentiable nowhere), and it is the single fact that forces a different calculus. Section 02

The math, step by step

Four steps: (1) recall exactly what the ordinary chain rule assumes, by re-deriving it from a Taylor expansion; (2) identify precisely which of that derivation's assumptions Brownian motion violates; (3) redo the Taylor expansion honestly, keeping the term ordinary calculus is entitled to drop; (4) apply the result to \(\ln S_t\) under geometric Brownian motion and watch the Black-Scholes \(\sigma^2/2\) term fall out.

1. Ordinary calculus's chain rule — and the assumption hiding inside it

Everyone learns the chain rule as a rule to apply, rarely as something with an assumption baked in. For a smooth, ordinary (deterministic) function of time \(x(t)\), and a smooth function \(f\) applied to it:

\[ \frac{d}{dt}f(x(t)) = f'(x(t))\,\frac{dx}{dt} \]
f'(x(t))ordinary derivative of f, evaluated at the current point
dx/dthow fast x itself is changing — nothing stochastic yet

Here is where that comes from. Taylor-expand \(f\) around the current point \(x\), for a small change \(\Delta x\):

\[ f(x+\Delta x) = f(x) + f'(x)\Delta x + \tfrac{1}{2}f''(x)(\Delta x)^2 + O(\Delta x^3) \]
f'(x)Δxfirst-order term — order Δt, survives the limit
½f''(x)(Δx)²second-order term — order Δt² for a smooth x(t), vanishes

Now let \(\Delta x\) be the change in \(x(t)\) over a small time step \(\Delta t\): to leading order, \(\Delta x \approx x'(t)\Delta t\). Plug that in and look at the size of each term as \(\Delta t \to 0\): the first term, \(f'(x)\Delta x\), is order \(\Delta t\) (it shrinks proportionally to \(\Delta t\)). The second term, \(\tfrac{1}{2}f''(x) (\Delta x)^2\), is order \((\Delta t)^2\) — because \(\Delta x\) itself is order \(\Delta t\), squaring it gives order \(\Delta t^2\), which shrinks much faster than \(\Delta t\) as \(\Delta t \to 0\) (if \(\Delta t = 0.01\), \(\Delta t^2 = 0.0001\) — a hundred times smaller). Dividing the whole expansion by \(\Delta t\) and taking the limit, every term of order \(\Delta t\) or smaller vanishes except the very first — which is exactly the chain rule above. The ordinary chain rule is really a statement that the second-order (and higher) Taylor terms are negligible in the limit, because a smooth function's own increment \(\Delta x\) is itself order \(\Delta t\), so its square is order \(\Delta t^2\). That is the assumption. It holds for every differentiable function you meet in an ordinary calculus course. It does not hold for Brownian motion.

2. Why Brownian motion breaks the assumption: \((dW_t)^2 = dt\)

A Wiener process (standard Brownian motion) \(W_t\) has independent, mean-zero increments with \(\text{Var}(W_{t+\Delta t} - W_t) = \Delta t\) over any interval of length \(\Delta t\). That variance fact is the whole story. It means a Brownian increment \(\Delta W\) is typically of size \(\sqrt{\Delta t}\) (its standard deviation), not of size \(\Delta t\) the way a smooth function's increment is. Consequently, its square is typically of size \(\Delta t\) — not \(\Delta t^2\). This single difference in scaling is the entire reason ordinary calculus fails for Brownian motion.

Make this precise the way stochastic calculus does, via quadratic variation. Take a partition of \([0,T]\) into \(n\) small subintervals of length \(\Delta t = T/n\), and sum the squared increments of \(W_t\) across the whole partition:

\[ \sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\left(W_{t_{i+1}} - W_{t_i}\right)^2 \]

Each term in that sum has expectation \(\Delta t\) (since each increment has variance \(\Delta t\)), and there are \(n = T/\Delta t\) terms, so the sum's expectation is exactly \(T\) — for every partition, no matter how fine. As the partition is refined (\(n \to \infty\), \(\Delta t \to 0\)), this sum doesn't just have expectation \(T\); it converges (in mean-square, a standard but technical mode of convergence covered rigorously in any stochastic calculus text — cited here rather than proven) to the fixed number \(T\) itself. Compare this to the same construction for an ordinary smooth function \(x(t)\): each term \((x(t_{i+1})-x(t_i))^2 \approx (x'(t_i))^2(\Delta t)^2\), so the whole sum is bounded by (a constant) \(\times \Delta t \times T \to 0\) as \(\Delta t \to 0\). A smooth function's quadratic variation is zero. Brownian motion's quadratic variation over \([0,T]\) is \(T\) — not zero. This is the single fact from which all of Itô calculus follows, and it is written, as shorthand, as the identity:

\[ (dW_t)^2 = dt \]
(dW_t)²squared Brownian increment — not negligible, unlike ordinary calculus
= dtquadratic variation accumulates at exactly this rate — "Itô's multiplication table"

Read this carefully for what it is and isn't. It is not a claim that any single random increment \(dW_t\), squared, literally equals the deterministic number \(dt\) — \(dW_t\) is still random, mean zero, with actual outcomes scattered on both sides of zero. It is shorthand for the quadratic-variation fact just derived: summed up over an interval, the squared increments accumulate at exactly rate \(dt\) per unit time, with no randomness surviving in the limit — sometimes called "Itô's multiplication table," alongside the companion (and far less surprising) facts \(dt \cdot dt = 0\) and \(dt \cdot dW_t = 0\), which say that terms with a \(dt\) factor are always negligible next to terms of pure order \(dt\). This is exactly why the chart below shows a jagged Brownian-style path staying jagged at every zoom level while a smooth curve visibly straightens: "staying jagged under magnification" and "having positive quadratic variation" are the same underlying fact viewed two ways, one visual and one algebraic.

It never resolves into a straight line, at any zoom level, because its quadratic variation over any interval is nonzero however small the interval — this is the visual content of \((dW_t)^2 = dt\).

3. Building Itô's Lemma: the same Taylor expansion, one extra term

Now redo the derivation from step 1, honestly, for a random process instead of a smooth function. Let \(X_t\) follow a general Itô process (this is the same form as the GBM in the Black-Scholes lesson, just written for a generic \(X_t\) rather than specifically the price \(S_t\)):

\[ dX_t = \mu\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t \]

Take a twice-differentiable function \(f(X_t)\) and Taylor-expand it — exactly the same second-order expansion used in step 1, and the same one used to derive volatility drag in the Calculus for Finance lesson and the Kelly Criterion lesson:

\[ df = f'(X_t)\,dX_t + \tfrac{1}{2}f''(X_t)\,(dX_t)^2 + \cdots \]

Everything so far is identical to ordinary calculus. The difference is entirely in what happens to \((dX_t)^2\). Expand it:

\[ (dX_t)^2 = \mu^2(dt)^2 + 2\mu\sigma\,dt\,dW_t + \sigma^2(dW_t)^2 \]

Apply Itô's multiplication table to each of the three terms. \((dt)^2\) is order \(dt^2\) — negligible, exactly as in ordinary calculus. \(dt\,dW_t\) is also negligible (a \(dt\) factor multiplying anything vanishes faster than order \(dt\)). But the last term, \(\sigma^2(dW_t)^2\), is where Brownian motion refuses to cooperate with the ordinary-calculus habit of dropping second-order terms: by the quadratic-variation fact from step 2, \((dW_t)^2 = dt\), so this term equals \(\sigma^2\,dt\) — order \(dt\), the same order as the drift term, not negligible.

\[ (dX_t)^2 = \sigma^2\,dt \]
(dX_t)²order dt — the same order as the drift term, not negligible

Substitute that back into the Taylor expansion:

\[ df = f'(X_t)\big(\mu\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t\big) + \tfrac{1}{2}f''(X_t)\,\sigma^2\,dt \]

Collect the \(dt\) terms together and the \(dW_t\) term separately. This is Itô's Lemma:

\[ df = \left(f'(X_t)\mu + \tfrac{1}{2}f''(X_t)\sigma^2\right)dt + f'(X_t)\sigma\,dW_t \]

\(X_t\)The underlying random (Itô) process — a variable that evolves partly predictably (drift) and partly randomly (diffusion). In the Black-Scholes context this is the asset price \(S_t\); Itô's Lemma is stated generally because it applies to a function of any Itô process, not just GBM.
\(dX_t = \mu\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t\)The assumed dynamics of \(X_t\): a drift \(\mu\) (predictable part, per unit time) plus a diffusion \(\sigma\,dW_t\) (random shock, scaled by \(\sigma\)). Same structural form as the GBM equation in the Black-Scholes lesson.
\(f(\cdot)\)Any twice-continuously-differentiable function you want to apply to \(X_t\) — e.g. \(f(x)=\ln x\) below, or a call option's value as a function of the underlying.
\(f'(X_t)\), \(f''(X_t)\)The first and second derivatives of \(f\), evaluated at the current value \(X_t\) — ordinary calculus objects, nothing new about them individually.
\((dW_t)^2 = dt\)The one substitution ordinary calculus has no rule for: Brownian motion's quadratic variation accumulates at rate \(dt\) per unit time, rather than vanishing, because a Brownian increment is order \(\sqrt{dt}\) rather than order \(dt\). Derived in step 2 above.
\(\tfrac{1}{2}f''(X_t)\sigma^2\,dt\)The Itô correction term — the entire content of this lesson. It is the ordinary second-order Taylor term, \(\tfrac{1}{2}f''(x)(\Delta x)^2\), evaluated with \((\Delta x)^2 \to \sigma^2 dt\) instead of \(\to 0\). Everything else in the formula (the \(f'(X_t)\mu\,dt\) drift term and the \(f'(X_t)\sigma\,dW_t\) diffusion term) is exactly what the ordinary chain rule would have produced.

It is worth restating the entire point of this derivation in one sentence, because it's easy to lose in the algebra: Itô's Lemma is the Taylor expansion you already know, applied to a random process instead of a deterministic one, plus exactly one extra term that ordinary calculus is entitled to throw away and stochastic calculus is not. There is no additional machinery beyond that one substitution — no new kind of derivative, no new function, nothing conceptually exotic. The entire apparent strangeness of "stochastic calculus" reduces to accepting that \((dW_t)^2 = dt\) rather than \(0\), and then doing completely standard algebra with that one fact in hand.

4. Applying Itô's Lemma to \(\ln S_t\) — where Black-Scholes's \(\sigma^2/2\) actually comes from

Now do exactly what black-scholes.html deferred. Let \(X_t = S_t\), following geometric Brownian motion \(dS_t = \mu S_t\,dt + \sigma S_t\,dW_t\), and apply Itô's Lemma with \(f(x) = \ln x\). Its derivatives are \(f'(x) = 1/x\) and \(f''(x) = -1/x^2\). Plug \(X_t = S_t\), \(f'(S_t) = 1/S_t\), \(f''(S_t) = -1/S_t^2\), drift \(\mu S_t\), and diffusion \(\sigma S_t\) into the formula from step 3:

\[ d(\ln S_t) = \left(\frac{1}{S_t}\cdot \mu S_t + \tfrac{1}{2}\cdot\left(-\frac{1}{S_t^2}\right)\cdot(\sigma S_t)^2\right)dt + \frac{1}{S_t}\cdot\sigma S_t\,dW_t \]

Every \(S_t\) cancels cleanly (the \(1/S_t\) in \(f'\) against the \(S_t\) in the drift and diffusion; the \(1/S_t^2\) in \(f''\) against the \(S_t^2\) from squaring the diffusion). What's left:

\[ d(\ln S_t) = \left(\mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)dt + \sigma\,dW_t \]
μ − σ²/2the drift of ln S_t — not μ itself
σ dW_tthe diffusion term, unchanged from ordinary calculus

There it is. The instantaneous drift of \(\ln S_t\) is not \(\mu\) — the price's own drift — it is \(\mu - \sigma^2/2\). The \(\sigma^2/2\) did not appear because of risk, discounting, or any financial argument; it appeared purely because \(f''(x) = -1/x^2\) is negative and \((dX_t)^2 = \sigma^2 dt\) is positive, so the Itô correction term \(\tfrac{1}{2}f''(S_t)\sigma^2 S_t^2\,dt\) is always negative for the log function — a direct, mechanical consequence of applying the chain rule correctly, not an assumption bolted on afterward. (This also explains, precisely, the black-scholes.html claim quoted at the top of this lesson: since \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) are constants, integrating \(d(\ln S_t)\) over \([0,T]\) gives \(\ln S_T - \ln S_0 = (\mu - \sigma^2/2)T + \sigma W_T\), so \(\ln S_T\) is normal — hence \(S_T\) is lognormal.)

Now take the same replication argument the Black-Scholes lesson builds — the one that swaps the real-world drift \(\mu\) for the risk-free rate \(r\) for pricing purposes (formally, a change of probability measure — the subject of the next lesson in this sequence, Girsanov's theorem, not derived here). Substituting \(\mu \to r\):

\[ d(\ln S_t) = \left(r - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)dt + \sigma\,dW_t \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ln\frac{S_T}{S_0} = \left(r-\frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)T + \sigma\sqrt{T}\,Z,\ \ Z\sim N(0,1) \]

This is the exact quantity Black-Scholes needs to compute \(N(d_2)\), the risk-neutral probability that the option finishes in the money (\(S_T > K\)):

\[ P(S_T>K) = P\!\left(\ln\tfrac{S_T}{S_0} > \ln\tfrac{K}{S_0}\right) = P\!\left(Z > \frac{\ln(K/S_0)-(r-\sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}\right) = N(d_2),\qquad d_2 = \frac{\ln(S_0/K)+\left(r-\dfrac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}} \]

That is exactly the \(d_2\) formula from black-scholes.html, with \((r-\sigma^2/2)\) sitting in the numerator precisely because that is the risk-neutral drift of \(\ln S_t\) — derived above, term for term, from Itô's Lemma applied to \(f(S)=\ln S\). \(d_1\) then follows from \(d_2\) by a further algebraic shift, \(d_1 = d_2 + \sigma\sqrt{T}\), which turns \((r-\sigma^2/2)\) into \((r+\sigma^2/2)\):

\[ d_1 = \frac{\ln(S_0/K)+\left(r-\dfrac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}} + \sigma\sqrt{T} = \frac{\ln(S_0/K)+\left(r+\dfrac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}} \]

matching the black-scholes.html formula exactly. Be precise about what has and hasn't been derived here: the \((r-\sigma^2/2)\) drift term inside \(d_2\) is a direct Itô's Lemma output, shown above line by line. The further shift from \(d_2\) to \(d_1\) by \(\sigma\sqrt{T}\) is a real, well-established result (it reflects pricing \(N(d_1)\) under a different probability measure — the "stock measure" rather than the risk-neutral money-market measure), but justifying that shift rigorously requires Girsanov's theorem, which is genuinely the next lesson's job, not this one's. What this lesson delivers in full is the piece black-scholes.html explicitly skipped: the \(\sigma^2/2\) is not asserted, it is derived, and it is derived from exactly one fact — \((dW_t)^2=dt\) — that ordinary calculus doesn't have.

Worked example

Reprising the Black-Scholes worked example, now with the algebra shown

black-scholes.html's worked example uses \(S_0=K=100\), \(r=0.02\), \(\sigma=0.20\), \(T=1\), and simply states \(d_1=0.20\), \(d_2=0.00\). Reconstruct those two numbers from the Itô-derived formulas above, term by term.

\(d_2\) first, since it's the one this lesson directly derives: \(\ln(S_0/K) = \ln(100/100) = \ln(1) = 0\) (spot equals strike). The risk-neutral drift term is \(\left(r-\sigma^2/2\right)T = (0.02 - 0.20^2/2)(1) = 0.02 - 0.02 = 0\) — in this particular example the Itô correction \(\sigma^2/2 = 0.02\) exactly cancels the risk-free rate \(r=0.02\), a coincidence of the chosen round numbers, not a general property. So the numerator of \(d_2\) is \(0+0=0\), and:

\[ d_2 = \frac{0 + 0}{0.20\sqrt{1}} = 0.00 \]

Then \(d_1\) is just \(d_2\) shifted by \(\sigma\sqrt{T} = 0.20\sqrt{1} = 0.20\):

\[ d_1 = d_2 + \sigma\sqrt{T} = 0.00 + 0.20 = 0.20 \]

Both numbers match the Black-Scholes worked example exactly — the difference is that here, \(d_2=0.00\) is visibly the risk-neutral drift of \(\ln S_T\) (which happens to be zero at this exact parameter combination), rather than a number that appears from nowhere.

🎯
Real-world trading scenario

The correction term shows up three times in this curriculum — that's not a coincidence

Picture a quant re-deriving why a stock's risk-neutral drift is \(r - \sigma^2/2\) rather than simply \(r\) — the exact question this lesson has just answered. Without Itô's Lemma, that \(\sigma^2/2\) looks like an arbitrary adjustment, the kind of thing a junior quant might suspect was reverse-engineered to make historical option prices fit, or a "volatility haircut" someone added by convention and everyone since has copied without questioning. With Itô's Lemma, it is not an adjustment at all — it is what the chain rule, applied correctly to a random process, mechanically produces the moment you take the log of anything that follows geometric Brownian motion. There is nothing to calibrate and nothing to justify empirically; it falls out of \(f''(x) = -1/x^2\) for \(f(x)=\ln x\) and the one substitution \((dW_t)^2=dt\), as derived step by step above.

Here is the part worth sitting with: this same \(\sigma^2/2\) term already appeared twice earlier in this curriculum, informally, before this lesson ever named Itô. The Returns & Compounding primer (primer-returns-compounding.html) derives "volatility drag," \(g \approx \bar{R} - \sigma^2/2\) — the gap between a strategy's arithmetic average return and its actual compounded growth rate — using a second-order Taylor expansion of \(\ln(1+R)\) around zero. The Kelly Criterion lesson (kelly-criterion.html) derives the identical \(g \approx \mu - \sigma^2/2\) for a continuous return stream, by the same Taylor-expansion logic, to explain why overbetting past the Kelly-optimal fraction can turn a positive-drift strategy into a long-run loser. Both of those derivations are doing, informally and in discrete time, exactly the Taylor expansion this lesson made rigorous in continuous time: \(f(x+\Delta x) \approx f(x) + f'(x)\Delta x + \tfrac{1}{2}f''(x)(\Delta x)^2\) with \(f=\ln\), and \((\Delta x)^2\) standing in, informally, for what Itô's Lemma makes precise as \(\sigma^2\,dt\). Three different parts of this curriculum — option pricing, position sizing, and basic compounding — independently run into the identical correction term, because all three are really asking the same underlying question: "what happens to a growth rate when you take the logarithm of something random?" Itô's Lemma is the one piece of machinery that answers that question rigorously, once, for all three.

How practitioners actually use this

No trading desk re-derives Itô's Lemma from scratch before pricing a trade — it is baked, permanently, into every derivatives pricing library in use anywhere, the way ordinary calculus is baked into a physics simulation without anyone re-deriving \(F=ma\) each time. Where it matters directly, constantly, to a working quant:

📐

Deriving the governing PDE for a new payoff

Every exotic option's pricing PDE — barriers, Asians, lookbacks — is derived the same way as Black-Scholes: apply Itô's Lemma to the payoff's value function, build the hedged portfolio, set its return to the risk-free rate.

🔗

Multivariate and correlated extensions

Pricing a basket or spread option needs the multivariate form (Itô-Doeblin), adding a covariance cross-term \(\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2\) alongside each asset's own \(\sigma^2/2\) term.

📊

Convexity adjustments

Futures-vs-forward convexity, CMS convexity, XVA — anywhere a desk needs the expected value of a nonlinear function of a random rate, Itô's Lemma (via Feynman-Kac) makes the adjustment rigorous.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where this result is misunderstood or misapplied

Treating \((dW_t)^2=dt\) as a statement about one random draw. It is not saying a single Brownian increment, squared, literally equals the deterministic number \(dt\) every time — any individual \(dW_t\) is still random and can be larger or smaller than \(\sqrt{dt}\) in magnitude. The identity is a statement about the limit of a sum of many squared increments (quadratic variation), which converges to \(T\) with no remaining randomness. Treating it as a single-draw fact rather than a limiting/aggregate one is the single most common source of confusion when first meeting Itô calculus.

Confusing "\(\ln S_T\) drifts at \(\mu-\sigma^2/2\)" with "the stock is expected to be worth less because of volatility." This is exactly the volatility-drag confusion flagged in the Kelly Criterion and Returns & Compounding lessons: \(E[S_T]\) under GBM still grows at the raw rate \(\mu\) (or \(r\), risk-neutrally) — that is unaffected by \(\sigma\). What shrinks by \(\sigma^2/2\) is the drift of the log, equivalently the median/typical path, not the mean. The gap between the two exists because \(E[e^X] \ne e^{E[X]}\) for a random \(X\) (Jensen's inequality, since \(e^x\) is convex) — a higher \(\sigma\) widens that gap without lowering the arithmetic mean at all.

Applying the single-variable form of Itô's Lemma to a function of two correlated random processes. The formula derived in this lesson, \(df = (f'\mu+\tfrac12f''\sigma^2)dt+f'\sigma\,dW_t\), is for a function of one Itô process. A function of two correlated processes (a spread, a basket, a ratio of two prices) needs the multivariate version, which adds a cross-partial-derivative term weighted by the covariance between the two processes' \(dW\)'s. Using the single-variable formula on a genuinely two-asset payoff silently drops that cross term and misprices the correlation risk.

Thinking the \(\tfrac12f''\sigma^2\) correction is specific to \(\ln(\cdot)\). It is not — it appears for any twice-differentiable \(f\) with \(f''\ne0\) applied to a diffusion; the log function is simply the case this lesson (and Black-Scholes) needs. A convex \(f\) (\(f''>0\), like \(f(x)=x^2\)) gets a positive Itô correction instead — the sign of the correction always matches the sign of \(f''\), which is worth checking explicitly rather than assuming it's always a downward \(\sigma^2/2\)-style adjustment.

Key Takeaways

✓The ordinary chain rule drops a term that isn't negligible for Brownian motion. A smooth function's increment is order Δt, so its square vanishes; a Brownian increment is order √dt, so its square, \((dW_t)^2\), is order dt — not negligible.
✓\((dW_t)^2=dt\) is a quadratic-variation statement, not a single-draw fact. Summed over a partition, squared increments converge to a fixed number — with no randomness surviving in the limit.
✓Itô's Lemma is the same Taylor expansion, plus one extra term. \(df = (f'\mu+\tfrac12f''\sigma^2)dt+f'\sigma\,dW_t\) — everything but the \(\tfrac12f''\sigma^2\) correction is what ordinary calculus would have produced.
✓Applying it to ln S_t derives Black-Scholes's σ²/2, mechanically. \(d(\ln S_t)=(\mu-\sigma^2/2)dt+\sigma\,dW_t\) — not asserted, not a fudge factor, a direct consequence of \(f''(x)=-1/x^2\) being negative.
✓The same σ²/2 term appears three times in this curriculum — Black-Scholes's \(d_1\)/\(d_2\), Kelly Criterion's volatility drag, and Returns & Compounding's log-return approximation — one piece of mathematics, not three coincidences.
⚠σ²/2 shrinks the drift of the log, not the mean of the price. \(E[S_T]\) under GBM is unaffected by σ — the gap exists because \(E[e^X]\ne e^{E[X]}\) (Jensen's inequality), not because volatility literally destroys expected value.
⚠The single-variable formula doesn't cover correlated processes. A basket, spread, or ratio of two correlated underlyings needs the multivariate Itô-Doeblin form with a covariance cross-term — using the plain version silently drops correlation risk.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. Why doesn't the ordinary calculus chain rule work for a function of Brownian motion, in precise terms?
The ordinary chain rule comes from a second-order Taylor expansion, \(f(x+\Delta x)\approx f(x)+f'(x)\Delta x+\tfrac12 f''(x)(\Delta x)^2\), in which the second-order term is dropped because, for a smooth deterministic function, \(\Delta x\) is itself order \(\Delta t\), so \((\Delta x)^2\) is order \((\Delta t)^2\) — negligible next to the order-\(\Delta t\) first term as \(\Delta t\to 0\). For Brownian motion, an increment \(dW_t\) has variance \(dt\), so it is typically of size \(\sqrt{dt}\), not \(dt\) — meaning \((dW_t)^2\) is order \(dt\), the same order as the first-order term, not negligible. Dropping it, as the ordinary chain rule does, throws away a real, same-order contribution.
Q2. Where does the \(\tfrac12 f''\sigma^2\) term in Itô's Lemma actually come from — is it a new kind of derivative, or something else?
It is nothing more exotic than the second-order term of an ordinary Taylor expansion, \(\tfrac12 f''(X_t)(dX_t)^2\), with \((dX_t)^2\) expanded out to \(\sigma^2(dW_t)^2\) (the \(\mu^2(dt)^2\) and \(2\mu\sigma\,dt\,dW_t\) cross terms vanish as negligible), and then \((dW_t)^2\) replaced with \(dt\) via the quadratic-variation fact from step 2. No new derivative or new function is introduced — it is completely standard calculus, applied to one substitution \((dX_t)^2=\sigma^2 dt\) that ordinary calculus never needs to make because ordinary functions have \((dx)^2\to 0\) instead.
Q3. Someone claims "(dW_t)^2 = dt means every single Brownian increment, squared, equals dt exactly." Is that a correct reading of the identity?
No. Any individual increment \(dW_t\) is still a random draw from a mean-zero distribution with variance \(dt\) — its square, for one particular draw, is not exactly \(dt\). The identity is shorthand for quadratic variation: summed over a partition of an interval, the squared increments \(\sum_i(W_{t_{i+1}}-W_{t_i})^2\) converge, as the partition is refined, to the fixed number \(T\) — with the randomness of individual terms averaging out in the limit. It is a statement about an aggregate/limiting quantity, not about any single increment.
Q4. Derive \(d(\ln S_t)\) from Itô's Lemma applied to geometric Brownian motion, and state its drift term precisely.
With \(f(x)=\ln x\), \(f'(x)=1/x\), \(f''(x)=-1/x^2\), and \(dS_t=\mu S_t\,dt+\sigma S_t\,dW_t\), Itô's Lemma gives \(d(\ln S_t) = \left(\tfrac{1}{S_t}\mu S_t + \tfrac12\left(-\tfrac{1}{S_t^2}\right) (\sigma S_t)^2\right)dt + \tfrac{1}{S_t}\sigma S_t\,dW_t\). Every \(S_t\) cancels, leaving \(d(\ln S_t) = \left(\mu-\tfrac{\sigma^2} {2}\right)dt+\sigma\,dW_t\). The drift of \(\ln S_t\) is \(\mu-\sigma^2/2\), strictly less than the price's own drift \(\mu\) — a direct consequence of \(f''(x)=-1/x^2\) being negative, which makes the Itô correction term always subtract for the log function.
Q5. How does the \((r-\sigma^2/2)\) drift term derived in this lesson relate to the \((r-\sigma^2/2)\) inside Black-Scholes's \(d_2\) and the \((r+\sigma^2/2)\) inside \(d_1\)?
Substituting the risk-neutral drift \(r\) for \(\mu\) in the Itô result gives \(\ln(S_T/S_0)=(r-\sigma^2/2)T+\sigma\sqrt{T}\,Z\). Computing \(P(S_T>K)\) from that normal distribution produces exactly \(d_2 = [\ln(S_0/K)+(r-\sigma^2/2)T]/(\sigma\sqrt{T})\) — the \((r-\sigma^2/2)\) is the risk-neutral drift of \(\ln S_t\), derived term-by-term from Itô's Lemma. \(d_1\) is then \(d_2+\sigma\sqrt{T}\) by definition, and adding \(\sigma\sqrt{T}\) to the \((r-\sigma^2/2)T\) numerator term turns it into \((r+\sigma^2/2)T\) algebraically. So \(d_2\)'s term comes directly from this lesson's Itô derivation; \(d_1\)'s term is that same quantity shifted by a probability-measure change (Girsanov's theorem, the next lesson), not a separate Itô computation.
Q6. The \(\sigma^2/2\) term also shows up in the Kelly Criterion lesson's "volatility drag" and the Returns & Compounding primer's log-return approximation. Is that the same term, or just a coincidental resemblance?
It's the same term, arrived at by the same underlying mathematics at different levels of formality. Both the Kelly Criterion and Returns & Compounding lessons derive \(g\approx \mu-\sigma^2/2\) (or \(\bar R-\sigma^2/2\)) informally, using a discrete-time second-order Taylor expansion of \(\ln(1+R)\) around zero — the same \(f(x+\Delta x)\approx f(x)+f'(x)\Delta x+\tfrac12 f''(x)(\Delta x)^2\) expansion this lesson uses, with \(f=\ln\). Itô's Lemma is the continuous-time, rigorous version of exactly that expansion, with \((\Delta x)^2\) made precise as \(\sigma^2\,dt\) rather than approximated informally. All three appearances trace back to the same fact: taking the logarithm of something random subtracts a variance/2 term from its drift, whether you derive that once rigorously (Itô's Lemma) or arrive at it informally three separate times.
