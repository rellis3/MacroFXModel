SDEs 1 · Stochastic Differential Equations

Does This Equation Even Have a Solution?

Every SDE lesson so far in this curriculum — Itô's Lemma, Ornstein-Uhlenbeck mean reversion, Black-Scholes's geometric Brownian motion — writes down dX_t = μ(X_t,t)dt + σ(X_t,t)dW_t and moves straight on to solving it, without ever asking whether a process satisfying that equation actually exists, or whether it's the only one. That assumption is not automatic. This deck states the precise conditions under which it holds, shows why the earlier lessons got it for free, and shows a coefficient choice where it genuinely breaks.

Section 1

Writing an equation down doesn't make it true

Every SDE in this curriculum is a special case of the same general form.

dX_t
=
μ(X_t,t)dt
+
σ(X_t,t)dW_t

The Itô's Lemma lesson works entirely with the simplest possible case — μ and σ are fixed constants, and existence is never in question. But ou-mean-reversion.html's mean-reverting drift θ(μ−X_t) and black-scholes.html's level-proportional GBM coefficients both let μ and σ depend on the current level X_t itself. The moment a coefficient is a function of X_t rather than a fixed number, a real question opens up: for this particular μ and σ, does the equation actually pin down one process, or could it pin down none, or several?

This is the exact same question ordinary differential equations had to answer first. Write dx/dt = f(x) with x(0) = x₀, and it's tempting to assume a curve x(t) simply exists for all time and is the only one doing so. Neither is automatic — and the classical fix, the Picard-Lindelöf theorem, is the direct ancestor of the SDE result this deck builds toward.
Section 2

Two different ways it can break

Worth keeping these separate — they're genuinely different failures, with different fixes.

⑂

Non-uniqueness

A rough enough coefficient can let more than one valid curve pass through the same starting point. Classic case: dx/dt = √|x|, x(0)=0 is solved both by x(t)≡0 forever and by x(t)=t²/4 peeling away — nothing in the equation picks one.

↑∞

Finite-time blow-up

A fast-growing enough coefficient can make the solution race to infinity before any fixed horizon is reached — not "hard to compute," but genuinely undefined past that point, for any method.

〜

A third failure, SDE-only

The diffusion term σ(X_t,t)dW_t can itself be badly behaved even when the drift μ is perfectly tame — there's no ODE counterpart to a noisy term misbehaving on its own.

The existence-and-uniqueness theorem coming up next is the stochastic-calculus analogue of Picard-Lindelöf, built to control all of these at once — with two conditions on μ and σ, checked together, with the same constant.

Section 3

Condition one — Lipschitz: no infinitely steep hills

Rules out non-uniqueness. Holding for all x, y and all t in the horizon of interest:

|μ(x,t)−μ(y,t)| + |σ(x,t)−σ(y,t)|
≤
K|x−y|

In plain terms: neither coefficient's output can change faster than a fixed multiple K of how far apart the two inputs x and y are. A ball rolling down a hill this smooth can slide quickly, but it can never suddenly teleport to a totally different spot — which is exactly what would let two different solution paths branch off from the same starting point.

Why this rules out non-uniqueness

√|x| near x=0 has infinite slope — its graph is vertical there. No finite K bounds |σ(ε)−σ(0)| / |ε−0| = σ/√ε as ε→0. That's precisely the coefficient behind the √|x| non-uniqueness example on the previous slide, and precisely why the CIR square-root diffusion (covered later in this category) fails this condition at the boundary that matters most for a short rate: zero.

Section 4

Condition two — linear growth: no unbounded horsepower

Rules out finite-time blow-up. Holding for all x and all t in the horizon of interest:

|μ(x,t)| + |σ(x,t)|
≤
K(1+|x|)

Neither coefficient is allowed to grow faster than linearly in x as x gets large. A car's engine can't have literally unlimited horsepower that keeps increasing the faster it's already going — without this bound, the process could accelerate away to infinity before any fixed time horizon T is even reached.

Given both conditions, holding with the same constant K, a unique strong solution X_t exists on [0,T] for any fixed T. The proof mirrors Picard-Lindelöf almost exactly: successive approximations (Picard iteration), with Lipschitz forcing them to converge and linear growth stopping the limit from escaping to infinity first.

Section 5

Three coefficients, three verdicts

Same two conditions, checked against three different real coefficient families from this curriculum.

Try it
Lipschitz ✓
Growth ✓
ou-mean-reversion.html's θ(μ−x) and black-scholes.html's GBM μx, σx are all exactly linear in x. A linear function's slope is a constant — the same K works for every pair x, y anywhere on the line, and the same K bounds the growth too. Sample paths stay well-defined and finite on any horizon, with room to spare.
Lipschitz ✗ at x=0
Growth ✓
σ(x)=σ√|x| has infinite slope right at zero, so no finite K bounds it there — the standard theorem from Section 3-4 doesn't apply. But it never grows faster than linearly, so the failure is narrow and local, not explosive. This is exactly why CIR needs the separate, weaker Yamada-Watanabe conditions instead of an outright rejection — a failed Lipschitz check means "use the more careful theorem," not "abandon the model."
Lipschitz ~ (local only)
Growth ✗
x^1.5 is smooth away from zero (locally Lipschitz), but it grows faster than linearly as x→∞ — the linear growth condition fails outright. A local-volatility desk proposal shaped like this is not automatically unusable, but the textbook existence-and-uniqueness guarantee simply does not cover it, and a real non-explosion check becomes genuine due diligence, not a formality.

Section 7

Cross the linear-growth threshold, live

Drag p. dX/dt = X^p, X(0)=1 is the deterministic illustration — the same shape of failure the SDE growth condition targets.

Try it
Growth exponent p in σ(X) = X^pp = 1.00
Linear growth condition: holds (p ≤ 1). Illustrative time-to-blow-up: ∞ (never)
p ≤ 1 is linear growth or slower — the theorem's condition holds, and the illustrative path never diverges in finite time. The instant p crosses above 1, growth is superlinear and a finite blow-up time appears, shrinking fast as p climbs toward 2.
Section 8

Check a mean-reverting SDE's coefficients, live

dX_t = θ(μ−X_t)dt + σdW_t — the same drift shape as ou-mean-reversion.html. Starts on θ=0.15, μ=0, σ=0.02, tested at x=5.

Try it — change any input, live
Lipschitz constant (drift slope, diffusion is constant so it adds 0): K = |θ| = 0.1500
Growth check at x: |θ(μ−x)| + |σ| = 0.7700 vs. K(1+|x|) = 0.9000
Growth condition with this K: holds
If the check fails, the fix isn't to abandon the coefficients — it's to enlarge K, since Lipschitz and growth just need some shared constant, not necessarily the tightest one for either condition alone.

Because θ(μ−x) is exactly linear in x, its Lipschitz constant is exact everywhere: K = θ, no region-dependence, no caveats. This is the same reason GBM's μx and σx are so clean — linear coefficients are the one case where the honest Lipschitz constant is trivial to state.

Section 9

Why the earlier lessons got this for free

OU — θ(μ−X_t)dt + σdW_t
  • Drift is exactly affine in X_t — constant slope θ everywhere
  • Diffusion is a flat constant σ — contributes 0 to the Lipschitz bound
  • Same K = θ works for Lipschitz and (with the "+1") for growth
  • ou-mean-reversion.html never has to mention any of this
GBM — μX_t dt + σX_t dW_t
  • Both coefficients exactly linear in X_t, K = |μ|+|σ|
  • Same K bounds Lipschitz and growth simultaneously, everywhere
  • black-scholes.html builds its closed form on this quiet guarantee
  • Well-posedness is essentially free — the honest reason GBM is the default
The caveat worth stating, not glossing over

GBM's Lipschitz constant is exact and region-independent because its coefficients are exactly linear — that's a property of linearity, not of every growth-condition-satisfying process. For coefficients that are only locally Lipschitz, a fully rigorous global argument needs a second ingredient — a separate non-explosion check — on top of the local property. GBM's coefficients are simple enough that this resolves cleanly, but "it's globally Lipschitz, done" skips a real step.

Section 10

Where this shows up as actual due diligence

🧪

Vetting a new coefficient specification

Any time a model departs from the standard families — a custom local-volatility surface, a bespoke jump-diffusion drift — checking Lipschitz and growth (or citing a paper that already has) is the first honest gate before trusting simulated output.

📉

Diagnosing a Monte Carlo that "blows up"

If simulated paths regularly produce NaNs or divergence, the first question is whether the underlying coefficients even satisfy a growth condition — sometimes the numerical scheme isn't the bug, the specification is.

⚠

"It ran without crashing" isn't proof

A coarse time step can silently step over a genuine finite-time blow-up or non-uniqueness and still produce a plausible-looking path. Well-posedness is a property of the true continuous-time equation, not of one lucky simulation run.

✗≠

Failed Lipschitz ≠ broken model

CIR's square-root diffusion fails the standard Lipschitz condition and is still one of the most widely used short-rate specifications in the industry. A failed check means "use Yamada-Watanabe instead," not "abandon this."

Section 11

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

What does the Lipschitz condition rule out?

A bound on how fast μ and σ can change output relative to input change stops the coefficients from being rough enough to let two different solution paths branch off the same starting point.
Question 2

What does the linear growth condition rule out?

Capping how fast μ and σ can grow in x is exactly what stops the process from being able to explode to infinity before reaching T — the dX/dt=X² illustration shows what happens when this fails.
Question 3

σ(x) = σ√|x| — the CIR-style square-root diffusion — at x = 0:

σ/√ε → ∞ as ε→0, so no finite K bounds the Lipschitz ratio near zero. But √|x| never grows faster than linearly, so growth holds fine — the failure is narrow, which is why the weaker Yamada-Watanabe conditions can still guarantee a solution.
Question 4

Why does OU's drift θ(μ−X_t) have an exact, region-independent Lipschitz constant?

For a linear function, |f(x)-f(y)|/|x-y| is a fixed number (the slope) for every pair x, y, no matter how far apart or how large — a property of linearity, not of any specific parameter value.
Question 5

A proposed local-volatility coefficient fails the Lipschitz check. Should the model be abandoned?

CIR's square-root diffusion fails the standard global Lipschitz condition and is nonetheless one of the most widely used, well-validated short-rate models in the industry, thanks to the separate Yamada-Watanabe conditions.
Section 12

What to carry forward

01

Writing dX_t = μ(X_t,t)dt + σ(X_t,t)dW_t down doesn't automatically mean a unique process satisfying it exists — that has to be checked, coefficient by coefficient.

02

Global Lipschitz + linear growth, with the same constant K, guarantees a unique strong solution — the SDE analogue of Picard-Lindelöf for ODEs.

03

Lipschitz rules out non-uniqueness (two solutions from one start); linear growth rules out finite-time blow-up (racing to infinity before time T).

04

OU's affine drift and GBM's level-proportional coefficients satisfy both conditions cleanly and cheaply — the quiet reason those closed-form solutions exist at all.

05

CIR's σ(x)=σ√|x| fails Lipschitz at x=0 but not growth — a narrow failure that needs Yamada-Watanabe, not a verdict that the model is broken.

06

Superlinear growth (like σ(x)=σ₀x^1.5, or the illustrative dX/dt=X^p for p>1) is the dangerous failure — no solution exists at all past some finite time, for any method.

What this now lets you do

Look at a new SDE specification — your own, or one proposed on a desk — and ask the right first question before trusting its simulated output: is this coefficient pair Lipschitz and linear-growth, and if not, which of the two failures is it, and does a weaker theorem still cover it? That's the honest gate every model in this curriculum quietly passed before you ever saw its closed form.
