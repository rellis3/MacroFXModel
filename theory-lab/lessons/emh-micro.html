Lesson 1 · Foundations & Epistemics

The Efficient Market Hypothesis, the Null Every Edge Must Beat

Prices already reflect what's knowable — so before you believe you've found an edge, the first question is why the market left it there. Every later lesson in this curriculum describes a way to extract a pattern from price data. This lesson is the thing all of them are arguing against, and the posture this entire repo's backtest discipline is built on.

Section 1

The $20 bill on a crowded sidewalk

EMH isn't something you "believe in" — it's the default assumption every trading idea has to argue its way past.

If someone really dropped a $20 bill on a busy sidewalk, it wouldn't stay there long — someone would pick it up within seconds. A market is about as crowded and well-incentivized a sidewalk as exists: thousands of professional traders, each rewarded for spotting free money, staring at the same prices you are. If a pattern in price data reliably predicted returns and cost nothing to exploit, the buying or selling pressure from people exploiting it would already have pushed the price to where the edge disappears.
⊘

Not a rule to accept or reject

Nobody in quantitative finance treats EMH as literally, perfectly true. It's the null hypothesis — the thing you argue past with evidence, not a verdict on markets.

💵

Only the free lunch is forbidden

EMH says a price already reflects available information, so there's no free, riskless way to profit from information you already have. It does not say nobody can ever earn a return.

🔍

The interesting question is friction

What happens when exploiting an edge isn't free — when it needs capital, patience, leverage, or tolerance for a risk other participants won't bear? That's where persistent effects tend to live.

Why this is Lesson 1

Momentum effects, mean-reversion patterns, volatility risk premia, macro signals — all of it is downstream of this lesson. Before any of it is useful, you need to understand the baseline every one of those ideas is implicitly claiming to beat.

Section 2

What "efficient" actually predicts: a random walk

Five simulated paths, same starting price, same process, no shared destination.

Same start, no shared destination
All five paths start at the exact same price, generated by the exact same random process — yet they drift to five different levels with no systematic push either way. Nothing changed mid-path; the spread is purely the accumulation of unpredictable, zero-mean surprises, one step at a time.

The plain-English version: tomorrow's price equals today's price, plus a surprise — and the honest forecast of that surprise, given everything currently known, is zero. Not "the surprise is always exactly zero" (it isn't; that's why prices move and risk exists) but "you cannot use what you already know to predict which way it will go." A process with that property is a martingale — your best forecast of tomorrow's price, given today's information, is simply today's price.

This is like a coin that's landed heads ten times in a row: the honest guess for the next flip is still 50/50, because the coin has no memory — even though the running tally keeps climbing. A market being "efficient" means prices have no memory in exactly that sense.
Section 3

Three nested claims, three different strategies ruled out

Fama's 1970 taxonomy splits EMH by what information is assumed already priced in. Each form is a stronger claim than the last.

Try it
Rules out: technical analysis — chart patterns, moving-average crossovers, momentum built purely from price history. Any predictable pattern in past prices alone would already be arbitraged away. This is the form the random-walk math directly tests.
Rules out: fundamental analysis on public data — everything anyone can legally read (earnings, filings, macro releases) is assumed to already be in the price. Tested via event studies: how fast and completely prices adjust after a public release.
Rules out: even insider trading. Essentially never taken as literally true — well-documented profitable insider trading shows private information has value the market hasn't priced in. Included mainly as the logical extreme, and the reason insider-trading law exists at all.

Think of it as three levels of a game being "unbeatable." Weak form says you can't win using only the scoreboard from past games. Semi-strong says you can't win even reading every public report on the players. Strong form says you couldn't win even with secret information nobody else has — which is basically nobody's serious claim.

Section 4

You can never test EMH alone — the joint-hypothesis problem

The single most important epistemic point in this lesson.

The joint-hypothesis problem

Testing whether "prices reflect available information" first requires a model of what the price should be given that information — CAPM, a multi-factor model, a discounted-cash-flow model, whatever. Every test of EMH is therefore secretly a joint test of two things bundled together: (1) is the market efficient, given the assumed pricing model, and (2) is the assumed pricing model itself correct. An anomalous result can never tell you which one failed.

Reading 1 — market is inefficient

You found a genuine mispricing. The market left information unexploited, and your test correctly caught it.

Reading 2 — model is wrong

The market is fine; your pricing model is missing a risk factor. What looks like "alpha" is compensation for a risk your model didn't account for — exactly how size and value factors got absorbed from single-factor CAPM into multi-factor models.

Imagine checking whether a bathroom scale is accurate, but the only way to test it is against a second scale that might itself be off. If the two disagree, you can't tell which one is wrong — maybe the first, maybe the second, maybe both a little. Testing "is the market efficient" has the same problem: a surprising result never proves the market messed up, it might just mean your ruler was bent.

This is exactly why professional researchers are reluctant to declare "EMH is false" from a single anomaly — and exactly why one profitable-looking backtest in this repo can never single-handedly prove an edge is real.

Section 5

Is 0.03 a signal, or routine noise?

A worked autocorrelation check — the same question every backtest secretly asks.

ρ̂₁ = 0.03, n = 500
  • Standard error under the null ≈ 1/√500 ≈ 0.045
  • 0.03 is smaller than one standard error
  • Nowhere near the ~2-SE threshold for "significant"
  • Exactly the small, noisy wobble an efficient series produces routinely
ρ̂₁ = 0.15, n = 500
  • Same standard error, ≈ 0.045
  • 0.15 is more than three standard errors from zero
  • A real statistical signal in this sample
  • Still no OOS test, no cost check, no mechanism — the start of a research question, not the end
Where this needs qualifying

Autocorrelation asks "does knowing yesterday's move help guess today's?" A statistically significant answer only tells you a pattern existed in this sample. It says nothing yet about whether it survives data the rule never saw, realistic transaction costs, or — per the joint-hypothesis problem — whether it's a real inefficiency or just compensation for a risk factor your simple model missed.

Section 6

The multiple-testing trap: how many "edges" would appear by luck alone?

Test enough random strategies against a significance threshold, and some will clear it by pure chance — not skill. How many, exactly?

Formula: expected false positives = N × α
Testing 100 strategies at α = 0.05 means about 5 of them would look "statistically significant" by pure chance alone — even if every single one had zero real skill.
This is the multiple-testing problem, and it's the joint-hypothesis problem's close cousin: a backtest scan across hundreds of parameter combinations is, statistically, exactly like running hundreds of independent significance tests. Some will clear the bar on chance alone — the next lesson in this series builds the correction for it.

This is why a "found in a backtest" result gets far more skepticism the more variations were tried to find it. A single pre-registered test clearing α = 0.05 is meaningful; the best of 100 tried variations clearing it is close to guaranteed, regardless of whether any of them has real skill.

Section 7

Anomalies exist — and mostly get traded away

Evidence for and against strict efficiency, in the same breath.

📉

Momentum & value effects

Documented, persistent-looking patterns in historical data — exactly the kind of thing that looks like a weak or semi-strong violation.

📰

Post-earnings-announcement drift

Prices keep moving for days after a public earnings release rather than adjusting instantly — a semi-strong-form violation, and one of the most robustly documented anomalies in the literature.

⚡

Capital chases what works

Once an anomaly is well known and cheap to exploit, capital floods in, and the effect typically shrinks or disappears — the $20 bill gets picked up.

🔄

Efficiency isn't a fixed switch

Andrew Lo's Adaptive Markets view: efficiency varies with the population of participants and how much capital is chasing an effect — inefficiencies can appear, get arbitraged away, and reappear as conditions shift.

A weak-form violation — predicting the future from past prices alone — is a much narrower, more surprising claim than a semi-strong violation, which just means some public information takes time to fully price in. Confusing the two overstates how strong a given piece of evidence actually is.
Section 8

Why an inefficiency survives at all

If profit-seeking arbitrageurs are everywhere, a durable inefficiency needs a reason it isn't instantly picked up.

💰

Funding constraints

The trade needs leverage or capital most participants can't or won't deploy.

🔐

Information asymmetry

The relevant data is genuinely hard to obtain or interpret — not secret, just costly to use.

🏦

Structural / forced flows

Rebalancing, hedging, or central-bank flows that aren't profit-motivated, and so don't get arbitraged away by pure profit-seekers.

📜

Regulatory limits

Position caps or capital rules that stop certain players from taking the other side of the trade.

⚠

Genuine risk premium

The "edge" is compensation for bearing tail risk, illiquidity, or career risk most participants avoid — not a free lunch at all.

The bar this sets

If you can't articulate why an effect you found should exist and persist, that's a reason for skepticism, not an automatic kill — but it raises the bar for what evidence would convince you it's real.

Section 9

This lesson, turned into this repo's engineering rules

EMH isn't philosophy here — it's why this codebase's validation discipline looks the way it does.

✂

The out-of-sample split

In-sample significance only shows a rule fits the specific sample it was tuned on — exactly what curve-fitting to noise also produces. OOS testing is the direct defense against that.

🔢

The ≥30-trade gate

A handful of lucky trades looks like skill by pure sampling variance. A minimum trade count is a floor under how much you can trust a Sharpe ratio at all.

🎯

A named no-skill benchmark

"Beat a bad strategy" and "beat efficient-market pricing" are different bars. Every claimed edge is judged against buy-and-hold or a simple fixed exposure — the second bar is the only one that means anything.

💸

Costs-on-by-default

EMH forbids the free lunch specifically — an edge that vanishes the moment realistic transaction costs and slippage are applied was never a free lunch to begin with.

The question this repo trains you to ask about your own backtest results: "if the equity curve goes up, what would have to be true about market structure for this to be real, and is there evidence for that beyond the curve itself — or did I just find noise, or a joint-hypothesis artifact?"
Section 10

Common ways people misread EMH

The misreading
  • "EMH says nobody can ever make money"
  • "One profitable backtest disproves market efficiency"
  • "Efficient" means prices are always rational and correctly valued
  • EMH is a fixed, permanent verdict on a market
What it actually claims
  • Only riskless, cost-free profit is forbidden — carry, illiquidity, and equity risk premia are entirely compatible with efficiency
  • One anomaly is equally consistent with "the pricing model used to judge 'abnormal' was incomplete" — the joint-hypothesis problem again
  • Prices can reflect information quickly and still look absurd in hindsight — a bubble doesn't require irrationality by the people setting the price at the time
  • Adaptive Markets: efficiency is ecological, varying with participants and competition, not a single on/off switch

Getting paid extra to drive a truck full of fireworks isn't "beating the system" — it's fair payment for taking on real danger other drivers won't. A lot of trading profit works the same way: compensation for holding a risk most other capital doesn't want, not a secret trick that breaks efficiency.

Section 11

Quick check

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Why can't a single backtest anomaly conclusively falsify EMH?

Every test of EMH secretly bundles two claims: the market is efficient, and the assumed pricing model is correct. An anomalous result can't tell you which one failed.
Question 2

A technical-analysis pattern built purely from past price history would, if genuinely reliable, violate which form of EMH?

Weak form claims price/volume history alone is already fully reflected in price — a reliable pattern using only that history would violate it directly.
Question 3

Does EMH forbid all profitable trading strategies?

Strategies that earn returns by bearing a genuine, priced risk — carry, illiquidity, tail risk — are entirely consistent with efficient markets. That's compensation for risk, not a free lunch.
Question 4

Testing 200 independent strategies at a significance threshold of α = 0.05, roughly how many would look "significant" by pure chance alone?

Expected false positives = N × α = 200 × 0.05 = 10. Significance at the single-test level says nothing once many tests are run — the multiple-testing problem.
Question 5

ρ̂₁ = 0.15 on n = 500 daily returns clears roughly how many standard errors from zero?

t = ρ̂ / SE = 0.15 / 0.045 ≈ 3.3 — a real statistical signal in-sample, though still not proof of a tradeable edge without OOS, cost, and mechanism checks.
Section 12

What to carry forward

01

EMH is the null hypothesis, not a verdict — prices already reflect available information, and every trading idea has to argue its way past that default before it earns trust.

02

Efficiency's testable core is a martingale — your best forecast of tomorrow's price, given everything known today, is today's price. Returns should show no exploitable autocorrelation.

03

Three nested forms rule out three different strategies — weak (technical analysis), semi-strong (fundamental analysis on public data), strong (even insider trading, essentially never taken literally).

04

The joint-hypothesis problem is unavoidable — you can never test "the market is efficient" without also assuming a pricing model, so one anomaly never proves which one is wrong.

05

Multiple testing manufactures fake edges — expected false positives = N × α. The more variations tried, the more skepticism a "found" pattern deserves.

06

EMH forbids the free lunch, not all profit — genuine risk premia are compatible with efficiency, and this repo's OOS split, trade-count gate, named benchmark, and costs-on-by-default are that logic turned into engineering rules.

What this now lets you do

Treat any backtested result with the correct starting skepticism: ask whether it survives out-of-sample data, realistic costs, and the joint-hypothesis question — and whether it's one carefully chosen test or the best of many, before ever calling it an edge.
