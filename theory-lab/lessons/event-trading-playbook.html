The Event-Trading Playbook: Payrolls, CPI and Central Banks

Before a scheduled release the options market has already priced how far price should move. After it, price moves on the surprise, not the number. Everything in between is liquidity, sizing and discipline.

CONCEPT — a framework for scheduled releases, not a tested strategy ~18 min read Prereqs: Vega & the Volatility Surface, Central Bank Policy

TL;DR
  • The move is priced before the number. An at-the-money straddle costs about \(0.8\,\sigma\sqrt{T}\,S\), so its breakeven is the market's expected absolute move. Over an event, short-dated implied vol jumps, then collapses once the number is out.
  • Back out the event from the term structure. Variance adds across days, so with one expiry before the event and one after it, \(\sigma_e^2 = \sigma_2^2T_2 - \sigma_1^2T_1 - \sigma_b^2(T_2-T_1-1)\). With 7% for 4 days and 9% for 5 days, the event day is worth 14.5% annualised: about ±98 pips on EUR/USD at 1.0800, against ±48 on an ordinary day.
  • Price reacts to the surprise, actual minus consensus, not the level. A 7.7% inflation print can send stocks to their best day in two years if 7.9% was expected. Standardise the surprise by its usual size before you compare releases.
  • Around the release, liquidity thins and spreads widen. The first move is fast and noisy; the second move comes from the details: revisions, the other half of the report, the press conference.
  • Size for the gap, not the stop. A stop is an order, not a guarantee. Size so a move of two or three event standard deviations is survivable, set blackout rules, and run a checklist before every release.
  • Real-world episode: US CPI on 10 November 2022 came in at 7.7% against roughly 7.9% expected. The S&P 500 rose 5.5%, the 2-year Treasury yield fell from 4.62% to 4.32%, and the dollar fell hard. Inflation was still nearly four times the Fed's target: only the surprise was good news.
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

Payrolls are on Friday. I think the number will be strong. Should I just get long the dollar now?

First ask what the market already expects. Not the number. The size of the move. The options market tells you that before the release.

How?

Take two option expiries on euro dollar. One expires Thursday, before the release. One expires Friday, after it. Say Thursday's is priced at seven percent volatility and Friday's at nine.

Nine doesn't sound much bigger than seven.

That's the trick. Volatility doesn't add up across days, but variance does. Square each one and multiply by the days. Five times nine squared, minus four times seven squared. What's left is the Friday on its own, and it comes out at about fourteen and a half percent.

And in pips?

Divide by about sixteen to turn a yearly number into a daily one. With euro dollar at one oh eight, that's about ninety-eight pips for one standard deviation. An ordinary day is about forty-eight. So the market expects payrolls day to move about twice as much.

So a straddle over the release is a bet that the move beats that.

Right. Its breakeven is about eight tenths of that standard deviation, roughly seventy-eight pips. And once the number is out, that extra volatility vanishes. If the move is small, the straddle buyer loses even though something happened.

Now direction. A strong number means a stronger dollar, surely.

Only if it's stronger than expected. Price reacts to the surprise: the actual minus the consensus. A strong number that everyone expected is already in the price.

Has that ever been really obvious?

The tenth of November, twenty twenty-two. US inflation came in at seven point seven percent. That's still nearly four times the Fed's target. But the forecast was about seven point nine. The S and P five hundred rose five and a half percent, its best day in two and a half years, and the dollar fell hard.

Good news that was really just less bad news.

Exactly. And the first few seconds are the worst time to trade. Spreads widen, quotes vanish, and the first move often reverses when people read the revisions or hear the press conference. That's the second move.

So what about my stop?

A stop is an order, not a guarantee. Price can jump straight through it. Size the position so a move of two or three event standard deviations still costs you an amount you can live with.

One-line version?

Know the priced move before the number, trade the surprise not the level, and size for the gap, not the stop.

Section 01

Why this matters

Most market days are a slow drift of information. A handful are not. At 8:30 a.m. New York time on the first Friday of most months, the US Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes the Employment Situation report: nonfarm payrolls, the unemployment rate, average hourly earnings. A week or so later it publishes the consumer price index, also at 8:30. Eight times a year the Federal Open Market Committee releases its decision at 2:00 p.m., and its Chair takes questions at 2:30. The ECB, the Bank of England, the Bank of Japan and the rest run their own calendars. Each of these is a moment when a large piece of information lands at a time everyone knows in advance.

That makes scheduled events unusual in a useful way. You can't know the number, but you can know almost everything else: when it arrives, what the consensus forecast is, how big a move the options market is charging for, how thin liquidity will get, and what the central bank says it cares about. A trader who has done that homework stops treating the release as a coin toss and starts treating it as a priced bet with a known shape. This lesson is that homework, written out: the arithmetic for the priced move, the rule that turns a number into a reaction, and the practical discipline around both.

💡
Plain-English intuition

The market bets on the size before it learns the direction

Think of a scheduled release as a sealed envelope that everyone knows will be opened at 8:30. Nobody knows what's inside. But everyone has a guess (the consensus), and everyone knows roughly how surprising these envelopes usually turn out to be. So two different things get priced before the envelope opens.

The direction is mostly already in the price, because the consensus is already in the price. If everyone expects a strong number, the currency has already moved as if the number will be strong. Only the part nobody expected, the surprise, is left to move it. The size is priced too, in options. Someone selling an option that expires just after the release wants to be paid for the jump it might bring, so options spanning the event cost more than options that expire before it. That extra cost is a direct, tradeable quote for how big the move "should" be.

Put those two ideas together and you have the whole playbook. Before the release: what size is priced, and is my position sized for it? At the release: was it a surprise, and which way? After the release: did the details or the press conference change the story? Everything else in this lesson fills in those three questions with numbers.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

If everyone already knows it will rain tomorrow, umbrella shops don't get a rush when it rains. The rush comes if it snows instead. Markets are the same: the forecast is already in the price, so only the surprise makes it jump. And the price of an umbrella in advance tells you how worried people are about the weather.

Section 02

The math, step by step

Watch the formula build
Where the event-day volatility comes from, one term at a time
\[ \sigma_e^2 = \color{#fbbf24}{\sigma_2^2\,T_2} \]

Start with everything the longer expiry prices: its implied variance times its days. This expiry spans the event, so the event is somewhere inside this total.

\[ \sigma_e^2 = \sigma_2^2\,T_2 \color{#fbbf24}{\;-\;\sigma_1^2\,T_1} \]

Take away the days both expiries share. The shorter expiry ends before the event, so its variance is all ordinary days. What remains is the forward variance between the two expiries.

\[ \sigma_e^2 = \sigma_2^2\,T_2 - \sigma_1^2\,T_1 \color{#fbbf24}{\;-\;\sigma_b^2\,(T_2-T_1-1)} \]

If the gap holds ordinary days as well as the event, strip them out at the base volatility. When the expiries sit one day apart, as in the example below, this term is zero.

\[ \color{#10b981}{\sigma_e = \sqrt{\sigma_2^2\,T_2 - \sigma_1^2\,T_1 - \sigma_b^2\,(T_2-T_1-1)}} \]

Take the square root to get the event day's annualised implied volatility. With 9% over 5 days and 7% over 4 days: \(\sqrt{0.0405 - 0.0196} = \sqrt{0.0209} = 14.5\%\).

\[ \text{move} = \frac{\sigma_e}{\color{#fbbf24}{\sqrt{252}}}\times S \;\Rightarrow\; \color{#10b981}{\pm 98 \text{ pips at } 1.0800} \]

Divide by the square root of 252 trading days to turn a yearly volatility into one day's standard deviation, then multiply by the price.

Step one: what a straddle says. An at-the-money straddle is a call and a put at the same strike, at the current price. It pays off if price moves far enough in either direction, so its cost is the market's price for the size of a move with no view on its direction. For an at-the-money option with a short expiry, Brenner and Subrahmanyam (1988) showed the Black-Scholes price collapses to a simple rule: a call is worth about \(0.4\,\sigma\sqrt{T}\,S\). A straddle is two of them:

\[ \text{Straddle} \;\approx\; 0.8\,\sigma\sqrt{T}\,S \]
σimplied volatility, annualised
Ttime to expiry, in years
Sspot price
0.8≈ √(2/π) = 0.798: the average size of a normal move, in standard deviations

The 0.8 is not arbitrary. If a move is normally distributed with standard deviation \(\sigma\sqrt{T}\,S\), its average absolute size is \(\sqrt{2/\pi}\approx 0.798\) of that. So the straddle's cost is the expected absolute move, and the straddle breaks even when price ends up exactly that far from the strike. Traders say "the straddle is pricing a 78-pip move" and mean exactly this. It is a mean, not a cap: about four moves in ten end up bigger than it.

Step two: why one expiry is not enough. A one-week option that spans payrolls prices the whole week, the quiet days as well as the event. Its volatility is an average, and the event gets diluted in it. To isolate the event you use the fact that variance, not volatility, adds up across time. If each day's move is independent, the variance to an expiry is the sum of each day's variance. Count time in trading days and quote volatility annualised, and each day contributes \(\sigma_{\text{day}}^2 \times 1\) to a total that the market quotes as \(\sigma^2 T\):

\[ \sigma_2^2\,T_2 \;=\; \sigma_1^2\,T_1 \;+\; \sigma_b^2\,(T_2 - T_1 - 1) \;+\; \sigma_e^2 \cdot 1 \]

The same idea is often quoted the other way round, as event weight: how many ordinary days the event is worth. That is just \(\sigma_e^2/\sigma_b^2\). FX option desks build their volatility term structure this way, giving each calendar day a weight (weekends and holidays low, data days high) so that implied vol for any expiry is the square root of the weighted sum. Whether the clock counts 252 trading days or 365 calendar days is a convention; what matters is using the same one for both expiries.

Step three: from volatility to pips. Annualised volatility becomes a one-day standard deviation by dividing by the square root of the number of days in the year's clock, about 15.87 for 252 trading days (the "divide by 16" rule from the VIX lesson). Multiply by spot and divide by the pip size:

\[ m_e \;=\; \frac{\sigma_e}{\sqrt{252}} \times \frac{S}{\text{pip}}, \qquad \text{breakeven} \;\approx\; 0.8\,m_e \]
mₑone standard deviation of the event-day move, in pips or points
√252≈ 15.87, turns a yearly vol into a daily one
pip0.0001 for EUR/USD, 0.01 for USD/JPY, 1 for an index point

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

A five-day ticket costs more than a four-day ticket. If each normal day costs the same, the extra cost of the fifth day tells you what that one day is worth. If the fifth day is payrolls day, it costs about four normal days. That's how you find the price of the event on its own.

The trap: event vol is a small difference of two big numbers. The event variance is what is left after subtracting one large total from another. A one-point error in either quote moves the answer a lot. In the example, marking the 5-day at 8% instead of 9% drops the event vol from 14.5% to 11.1%. Use mid-market quotes from the same moment, the same day-count clock for both expiries, and treat the result as a range, not a point.

Step four: surprise, not level. The options market prices the size. The direction comes from comparing the release with what was expected. Define the surprise as

\[ s \;=\; A - E, \qquad z \;=\; \frac{A - E}{\operatorname{sd}(A - E)} \]
Athe actual released number
Ethe consensus forecast: the median of economists' survey forecasts
sthe raw surprise, in the release's own units
zthe standardised surprise: raw surprise ÷ the usual size of past surprises

Standardising is what lets you compare releases. A 0.1-point miss on core CPI and a 50,000-job miss on payrolls are in different units; dividing each by the standard deviation of its own past surprises puts them on one scale. This is the measure Andersen, Bollerslev, Diebold and Vega used in their 2003 study of five-minute exchange rates around US announcements. They found that the surprise explains the jump in the conditional mean, that the adjustment is very fast, and that the response is asymmetric: bad news moved rates more than good news of the same size. The working model is a simple regression on the surprise:

\[ \Delta p \;=\; \beta\,z \;+\; \varepsilon \]
Δpthe price change in a short window after the release
βthe reaction per one standard deviation of surprise; its sign and size change with the regime
εeverything else: other parts of the report, revisions, positioning, noise

The level of the number does not appear. If the consensus is 7.9%, the price already reflects 7.9%. A print of 7.7% is new information only to the extent of the 0.2-point difference. Kuttner (2001) showed the same thing for Fed decisions: bond yields barely responded to the anticipated part of a rate change, and strongly to the unanticipated part measured from fed funds futures.

The level of the release is already in the price, because the consensus was. Only the part nobody forecast moves it. In real data the slope is not fixed: it is larger when the release matters more to the central bank, which is why the same-sized CPI surprise matters far more in a year when the central bank is fighting inflation than in a year when it isn't.

Worked example (illustrative quotes)

Payrolls Friday on EUR/USD: from two vol quotes to a move in pips

It is Monday morning. EUR/USD is at 1.0800 and payrolls are due on Friday at 8:30 New York time. FX options expire at the 10:00 New York cut, so an option expiring Thursday ends before the release and one expiring Friday spans it. Suppose the market quotes:

1

Write down both totals

Thursday expiry: \(\sigma_1 = 7\%\) over \(T_1 = 4\) trading days, so \(\sigma_1^2T_1 = 0.0049 \times 4 = 0.0196\). Friday expiry: \(\sigma_2 = 9\%\) over \(T_2 = 5\) days, so \(\sigma_2^2T_2 = 0.0081 \times 5 = 0.0405\).

2

Subtract to isolate the event

The expiries are one day apart, so there are no ordinary days in the gap: \(\sigma_e^2 = 0.0405 - 0.0196 = 0.0209\), and \(\sigma_e = \sqrt{0.0209} = 14.5\%\) annualised. That one day carries 52% of the week's priced variance.

3

Turn it into pips

\(14.46\% / 15.87 = 0.911\%\) for one day. At 1.0800 that is \(0.00984\), or ±98 pips for one standard deviation. The event straddle's breakeven is about \(0.798 \times 98 \approx\) 78 pips.

4

Compare with an ordinary day

An ordinary day at 7% is \(7\% / 15.87 = 0.441\%\), about ±48 pips. The event day's standard deviation is 2.07 times larger, and its variance \(0.0209/0.0049 = 4.3\) times larger: payrolls are priced like 4.3 ordinary days squeezed into one.

Now you have a yardstick. If you think the number will surprise strongly, the question is not "will EUR/USD move?" but "will it move more than the 78 pips the straddle already charges for?" And if you hold spot through the release, 98 pips is one standard deviation of what might hit you in a few seconds.

Real-world trading scenario

You think payrolls will beat. Three ways to act on it, and what each one costs

Same illustrative week: EUR/USD at 1.0800, the event priced at ±98 pips for one standard deviation, an ordinary day at ±48. You expect a strong US jobs number, which would push EUR/USD down. Your risk budget for the trade is $500 on a $100,000 account. A pip on 100,000 EUR/USD is worth $10.

1 · Short spot into the number

Size for the gap, not the stop. Plan for a three-standard-deviation adverse move, about 295 pips: \(\$500 / (295 \times \$10) \approx 0.17\) lots, about €17,000. A trader who sizes off a normal 30-pip stop would hold \(\$500/(30 \times \$10) \approx 1.7\) lots, ten times as much, and a 150-pip jump through the stop would cost about $2,500.

2 · Buy the event straddle

No direction needed, but you pay about 78 pips of premium on the event day, and the extra vol drains out the moment the number is known. You profit only if the move beats what was priced. A strong view on direction is not a view on size.

3 · Wait for the surprise, trade the second move

Stay flat into 8:30. Read the surprise, the revisions and the earnings figure. Enter only once spreads are back to normal, sized for an ordinary day's volatility plus a margin. You give up the first jump; you also avoid paying the widest spread of the day.

None of these is right every time. What's wrong every time is the fourth option: holding an ordinary-day position with an ordinary-day stop through a release priced at twice the ordinary move.

Section 03

Around the release: liquidity, the first move and the second

The arithmetic tells you how big the move should be. What actually happens in the minutes around a release follows a pattern documented in the Treasury market by Fleming and Remolona (1999) and seen across FX and futures since. It is worth knowing step by step, because each phase calls for different behaviour.

1

Before: liquidity is withdrawn

In the minutes before a major release, market makers widen their quotes or pull them, because nobody wants to be the resting order that gets hit by someone who reads the number a fraction of a second faster. Spreads widen and the order book thins. A market order placed now pays more and can move the price itself.

2

The first move: price jumps on the headline surprise

Fleming and Remolona found that the price adjusts sharply within a minute or two of the release, on reduced trading volume: quotes simply reset to the new level. In FX, Andersen, Bollerslev, Diebold and Vega found the conditional mean jumps almost immediately. This is the move a headline-reading algorithm makes. It is fast, and it is often a poor fill for anyone else.

3

The second move: the details arrive

Then trading volume surges, and volatility stays elevated for a while as traders digest everything that isn't the headline. This is where the first move gets confirmed, extended or reversed. Fleming and Remolona describe a second stage of prolonged, more moderate volatility, as participants with different interpretations trade with each other.

4

After: implied vol is crushed

Once the number is known, the event day's variance has been spent. The next expiry no longer contains it, so short-dated implied vol drops back towards the ordinary level. A straddle bought for the event loses that premium whether or not the move was big. This is theta and vega arriving together.

What drives the second move. Three things, in rough order of how often they matter:

🔁

Revisions in the same report

The Employment Situation report revises the previous two months of payrolls every month. A headline 50,000 above consensus with 80,000 of downward revisions to earlier months is, net, weaker news about the level of employment than expected. The headline algorithm trades the first number; the second move trades the sum.

🧩

The other half of the report

Payrolls come from the establishment survey; the unemployment rate from a separate household survey; average hourly earnings matter for inflation. CPI has a headline and a core figure. When the pieces disagree, the market settles on whichever one the central bank is watching most closely at that moment.

🏛️

The reaction function

A number only matters through what it does to the expected path of policy rates. In 2022, with the Fed fighting inflation, CPI was the release that moved everything. When a central bank says it is watching the labour market, payrolls take that role. Read the bank's own statements to know which release is in charge.

🎙️

The press conference

A rate decision is released as a short statement; the press conference follows. The FOMC statement comes at 2:00 p.m. and the Chair's press conference at 2:30, after every meeting since January 2019. The first move trades the statement; the second trades the answers.

Central-bank decisions are surprises against a priced path. For a rate decision the "consensus" is what futures and overnight index swaps already price. A hike that was fully priced moves little. A hold when a cut was priced is a hawkish surprise even though nothing changed. Gürkaynak, Sack and Swanson (2005) found that Fed announcements move markets along two separate dimensions: a surprise in the current rate decision, and a surprise in the signal about the future path, carried by the statement's wording. The second moved longer-dated yields more than the first. The press conference is where the path gets explained, which is why the second move on a central-bank day can be larger than the first.

A widely cited example: on 3 December 2015 the ECB cut its deposit rate by 10 basis points, to −0.30%, and extended its bond purchases to March 2017 without raising their monthly size of €60 billion. Markets had priced more easing. The euro rose about 3% against the dollar that day, its biggest one-day rally since 2009 on Bloomberg's figures: an easing decision that sent the currency up, because it was less easing than expected.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

When the teacher hands back test results, the first thing everyone looks at is the big number at the top. That's the first move. Then they read the comments, and sometimes the comments change the story: "great score, but you copied question three." That's the second move.

Section 04

It actually happened: US CPI, 10 November 2022

The cleanest modern demonstration that markets trade the surprise and not the level came from an inflation report that, on its level, was terrible.

It actually happened

7.7% inflation, and the best day for US stocks in two and a half years

By November 2022 US consumer price inflation had peaked at 9.1% in the year to June and was still 8.2% in the year to September. The Fed had just raised its target range by 75 basis points for the fourth meeting in a row, to 3.75–4.00%, on 2 November. Every CPI release that year had become the most important event on the calendar, because each one decided how much further the Fed would go.

At 8:30 a.m. on Thursday 10 November the Bureau of Labor Statistics published October's figures. Headline CPI rose 0.4% in the month and 7.7% over the year. Economists had expected about 0.6% for the month and around 7.9% for the year. Core CPI, excluding food and energy, rose 0.3% in the month and 6.3% over the year, against about 6.5% expected. On the level, inflation was still nearly four times the Fed's 2% target. As a surprise, it was about two tenths of a point below forecast on both measures, and in a market that had been positioned for the opposite, that was enough.

The S&P 500 closed up 5.5% at 3,956.37, its biggest one-day gain since April 2020. The Nasdaq Composite rose 7.4% and the Dow 1,201 points. The 2-year Treasury yield fell from 4.62% to 4.32% and the 10-year from 4.15% to 3.82%, on AP's figures. The dollar fell broadly; Reuters reported the yen's biggest one-day gain against it since 2008. None of this was about inflation being low. It was about inflation being lower than the price had assumed, which shifted the expected path of Fed hikes.

The next day the move extended rather than reversing: the S&P 500 rose almost 1% more and the Nasdaq 1.9%. In December the Fed slowed to a 50 basis-point hike. The second move, in this case, confirmed the first, because the details of the report (core and headline both soft) agreed with the headline.

⚠️
Where the textbook version breaks

Normal moves, a reliable consensus, and a stop that fills where you put it

The event-vol arithmetic treats the event move as one normal draw with a known standard deviation. Real event moves are fatter-tailed: most releases are quiet, and a few move several standard deviations. The consensus is a median of surveys taken days before, and the "whisper" the market actually positions for can differ from it. Revisions and the other half of the report can swamp the headline surprise, so the clean regression in the chart above has a much noisier error term in real data.

Above all, gaps. On 15 January 2015 the Swiss National Bank, without warning, removed the 1.20 floor it had held under EUR/CHF since 2011. The euro fell far below 1.00 francs within minutes. Stop-loss orders resting just under 1.20 were filled hundreds of pips away, because there was nobody to trade with in between. The retail broker FXCM was left with client accounts owing it about $225 million and needed a $300 million rescue loan from Leucadia. That was not a scheduled release, which is the point: the stop that "protects" you is an order to trade at the next available price, and around a shock the next available price can be a long way off.

Not tested here Whether event-day implied vol over- or under-prices the realised event move has not been tested on this desk. A fair test would back out event vol from the two expiries' quotes before each release, record the realised move from the release to the next option cut, and compare the straddle's cost with its payoff across many releases, after spreads.
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Everyone thought the inflation number would be 7.9. It came out 7.7. That's still way too high, like a fever of 39 instead of 39.2. But everyone had already planned for 39.2, so the slightly-less-bad news felt like great news, and prices jumped.

Section 05

How practitioners actually use this

Desks that trade macro releases tend to converge on the same habits, whatever their style. The options market's priced move is the yardstick for everything: how big a position can be, where a stop is meaningless, whether a straddle is cheap or dear. The consensus is the reference point for direction. And a fixed routine keeps a trader from improvising at the worst possible moment.

Sizing and stops around events. The rule is to size for the gap, not for the stop. Take the event's standard deviation from the term structure, decide how many of them you want to survive (two or three is common, with the knowledge that real tails are fatter than normal ones), and size so that move costs no more than your risk budget. Place stops outside the priced event range, or accept that a stop inside it is a coin toss that will often be filled at a worse price. Some traders replace stops with options for the event window, buying protection whose loss is capped at the premium. That works, but the protection is priced at event vol, so it is expensive exactly when you want it.

Blackout rules. The word is used two ways. Central banks have their own: Federal Reserve officials observe a blackout from midnight Eastern time on the second Saturday before each FOMC meeting until midnight on the day after the meeting, and do not discuss policy in public in that window, so there is no last-minute guidance to trade on. The ECB keeps a similar quiet period in the week before its meetings. Traders use personal blackout rules too: for example, no new positions from 15 minutes before a tier-one release until spreads are back to normal after it, and no holding an ordinary-sized position through one. Some brokers and proprietary-trading firms write similar restrictions into their terms. The exact window matters less than deciding it in advance.

On the desk, the Event Response page measures each high-impact release's half-hour move as a multiple of an ordinary half-hour at the same clock, with sample sizes. It is the realised counterpart of the event weight computed here from implied vols.

📋
The pre-event checklist

Seven questions to answer before every tier-one release

1

What exactly is released, and when?

The time to the minute, in your own time zone, and every figure in the report: headline, core or the other survey, and revisions.

2

What is the consensus, and how big is a normal surprise?

Write down the median forecast and the usual size of past surprises, so you can call a print "one standard deviation hot" in the moment.

3

What move are options pricing?

Back out the event vol from the expiries either side, and convert it to pips or points. That is your yardstick for size and stops.

4

Which figure is the central bank watching?

Read the latest statement and speeches. That decides which part of the report drives the second move.

5

What am I holding, and what does a 3 sd move cost?

Every open position that responds to this release, at the event standard deviation, not the ordinary one. Cut anything you can't afford to see gapped.

6

What is my plan for each outcome?

Hot, in line, soft: what you do in each case, written before 8:30, including "nothing".

7

When does my blackout end?

The time or condition (spreads back to normal, the press conference over) after which you may act. Until then, hands off.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where event trading quietly goes wrong

Trading the level. "Inflation is 7.7%, that's terrible, sell stocks" lost a great deal of money on 10 November 2022. The level was in the price; the surprise wasn't. Always ask "compared with what was expected?"

Using one expiry's vol as the event vol. A one-week vol of 9% does not mean the event is priced at 9%. It averages the event with the quiet days. Back the event out with the variance arithmetic, or you will underestimate the event move by half.

Buying the straddle because "something will happen". Something always happens. The question is whether it beats the move already priced, after the vol crush. Straddles over events are often priced richly enough that the average buyer loses.

Trusting the stop. In a thin market a stop fills at the next available price. Size so a gap through the stop is survivable; don't size off the stop distance.

Reacting to the first print alone. Revisions, the other survey, core versus headline, and the press conference can each reverse the first move. The first minute rewards speed you probably don't have; the second move rewards reading the whole report.

Scenario drill · 10 November 2022

Long dollars into a CPI release, with an ordinary-day stop

It is 8:20 a.m. in New York. You are long US dollars against the euro and the yen, betting that October's CPI will be hot and push the Fed to keep hiking by 75 basis points. Your position is sized for an ordinary day, with stops about one ordinary day's move away. The consensus is about 0.6% for the month and 7.9% for the year. CPI has been the most market-moving release of the year. What do you do in the next ten minutes?

  1. Hold. The stops limit the damage if you're wrong.
    Your stops sit inside the move the options market is pricing for this release. A stop is an order to trade at the next available price, and in the first seconds after a big surprise there may be little liquidity between your level and where price lands. You have sized for a stop that may not fill where you set it.
  2. Add to the position. Inflation has been running hot all year, so the number will be high.
    That is a view on the level, and the level is already in the consensus and the price. Only a hotter-than-7.9% print helps you. Adding doubles your exposure to a coin flip on the surprise, at the widest spreads of the day.
  3. Cut the position to a size where a move of two or three event standard deviations is affordable, or go flat, and decide again after the number is out.
    This sizes for the gap, not the stop. You keep the option to trade the second move once you know the surprise and spreads have normalised. If CPI is hot, you can re-enter with information; if it is soft, a small position limits the damage.
  4. Keep the position but buy options that pay if the dollar falls.
    Defensible: the protection caps the loss at its premium. But you are buying it at event vol, the most expensive moment, and you are still carrying a full-size bet on the surprise. Cutting size does the same job more cheaply.

What happened. CPI rose 0.4% in October and 7.7% over the year; core rose 0.3% and 6.3%. All were below consensus. The 2-year Treasury yield fell from 4.62% to 4.32%, the dollar fell broadly, with the yen's biggest one-day gain against it since 2008, and the S&P 500 rose 5.5%. A long-dollar position sized for an ordinary day took a move far larger than an ordinary day in the first minutes. The next month, the Fed slowed to a 50 basis-point hike.

Section 06

Key Takeaways

✓The size of the move is priced before the release. An at-the-money straddle costs about \(0.8\,\sigma\sqrt{T}\,S\): the market's expected absolute move. Read it before you trade.
✓Variance adds, so the event can be backed out. \(\sigma_e^2 = \sigma_2^2T_2 - \sigma_1^2T_1 - \sigma_b^2(T_2-T_1-1)\). With 7% over 4 days and 9% over 5, payrolls are priced at 14.5%: ±98 pips at 1.0800, about 4.3 ordinary days of variance.
✓Price reacts to the surprise, actual minus consensus, not to the level. Standardise it by the usual surprise size to compare releases, and read central-bank decisions against the rate path futures already price.
✓There are two moves. The first is a fast jump on the headline in thin liquidity. The second comes from revisions, the rest of the report and the press conference, and it can confirm or reverse the first.
⚠Event vol is a small difference of two big numbers. One vol point on the weekly quote moved the example's event vol from 14.5% to 11.1%. Use fresh mid quotes on the same day-count clock and treat the answer as a range.
⚠Implied vol collapses after the release. A straddle bought for the event loses its event premium the moment the number is known. It pays only if the move beats what was priced.
✗A stop is not a guarantee. Around a shock it fills at the next available price, which can be hundreds of pips away, as EUR/CHF showed in January 2015. Size for the gap, set a blackout, and run the checklist. Event-vol pricing against realised event moves is untested on this desk.
Section 07

Self-test

Q1. Why did US stocks have their best day in two and a half years on a day inflation was reported at 7.7%?
Because prices react to the surprise, not the level. The consensus was about 7.9% for the year and 0.6% for the month, and markets were already priced for that. The release came in about 0.2 points lower on both headline and core. That changed the expected path of Fed hikes: the 2-year yield fell 30 basis points, which lifted stocks and sank the dollar. Inflation at 7.7% was still terrible news in absolute terms; it was good news only relative to what was expected.
Q2. A 3-day expiry is quoted at 6% and a 4-day expiry, which also spans a CPI release, at 8%. What is the event-day vol, and the one-day move on an asset at 100?
The expiries are one day apart, so \(\sigma_e^2 = 0.08^2 \times 4 - 0.06^2 \times 3 = 0.0256 - 0.0108 = 0.0148\), and \(\sigma_e = \sqrt{0.0148} = 12.2\%\) annualised. Dividing by \(\sqrt{252} = 15.87\) gives 0.77% for the day, so about ±0.77 for one standard deviation on an asset at 100. The straddle breakeven is about \(0.8 \times 0.77 \approx 0.61\). The event is worth \(0.0148/0.0036 = 4.1\) ordinary days of variance.
Q3. Payrolls come in 50,000 above consensus, but the previous two months are revised down by a combined 80,000. EUR/USD first drops, then rallies. Why?
The first move trades the headline: a beat means a stronger dollar, so EUR/USD drops. The second move trades the whole report. Adding the revisions, total employment is 30,000 lower than the market thought before the release, so the net news about the labour market is weak. As traders read the details, the dollar gives back its gain and more. Revisions are part of the same release, and they are surprise too.
Q4. You buy the event straddle the day before payrolls. EUR/USD moves 60 pips on the release. Why might you still lose money?
Because the straddle was priced for the event. In the worked example its breakeven is about 78 pips: you paid for a move of that size. A 60-pip move is real, but it is smaller than what was priced. And once the number is out, the event variance is spent, so the implied vol of whatever time is left drops back to the ordinary level (the vol crush), taking the rest of the event premium with it. A straddle is a bet that the move beats the price, not that a move happens.
Q5. Why is "set a tight stop" bad risk control for a position held through a tier-one release?
A stop is an order to trade at the next available price once your level is touched. Around a release, liquidity is withdrawn and price can jump past your level with nothing traded in between, so the fill can be far worse than the stop. Sizing off the stop distance therefore understates the real risk. The fix is to size for the event's priced move, two or three event standard deviations, so a gap is survivable. The SNB's removal of the EUR/CHF floor in January 2015 is the extreme case: stops just under 1.20 filled far below.
Q6. The ECB cut its deposit rate on 3 December 2015 and the euro rose about 3%. How can an easing decision strengthen the currency?
Because the market reacts to the decision relative to what was priced. Markets had expected more easing than the ECB delivered: a 10 basis-point deposit cut to −0.30% and a longer, but not larger, bond-buying programme. Relative to expectations, that was a hawkish surprise, so the expected path of euro rates rose and the euro rallied, its biggest one-day gain against the dollar since 2009 on Bloomberg's figures. Central-bank decisions are surprises against the path that futures and swaps already price.
