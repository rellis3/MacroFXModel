Market Impact & Execution, One Trade-Off, One Dial

Trade a large order all at once and you pay for the impact of demanding immediacy right now. Spread it out over a long window instead, and you pay a different price — exposure to whatever the market does while you're still holding the unexecuted remainder. Almgren-Chriss turns that tension into a single risk-aversion dial and a closed-form schedule for exactly how fast to trade.

The whole idea
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

I have to sell a position much bigger than anything showing in the book. All at once, or slowly?

Either way you pay. Sell it all now and you walk deep into the book. That is impact cost, big but predictable. Sell slowly and each slice barely moves the price, but you are holding the rest the whole time while the market wanders. That is timing risk.

How big is the impact, roughly?

Studies across markets find it grows with the square root of your size relative to daily volume. Four times the size moves the price about twice as far. So your total cost, size times impact, goes up about eight times.

And Almgren and Chriss?

In 2000 they put both costs into one sum. Impact grows with the square of how fast you trade. Risk grows with how much you still hold, weighted by how much you dislike uncertainty. Then they found the schedule that makes the total as small as possible.

What does it look like?

If you don't mind risk at all, it is a straight line: the same amount every hour. That's TWAP. Turn up your dislike of risk and the schedule front-loads. You sell a lot early and very little late.

Give me the number.

Take the lesson's example: two percent daily volatility and a moderately risk-averse trader. Halfway through the day, the straight-line seller still has half the order left. The front-loaded seller has only about twenty-one percent left. Both finish at the close.

So front-loading is the safe choice?

It costs more on average, by design. It is the right choice when you genuinely dislike the risk, say with a big data release coming. Neither setting is wrong; it is a policy you should choose on purpose.

Has this gone wrong in real life?

Archegos, March 2021. Several prime brokers held big blocks of the same few stocks when the fund couldn't meet its margin calls. Some sold early, in large blocks, and later reported no material loss. The slower ones sold into prices everyone else had already pushed down. Credit Suisse lost about five and a half billion dollars, Nomura about two point nine.

So patience was the mistake.

There, yes. The model assumes you're the only seller, so waiting only exposes you to random noise. With rivals, waiting exposes you to their selling, which is a predictable push against you.

One line?

Alone, choose your speed by how much risk you can stand; in a crowd heading for the same exit, the patient seller pays the most.

Section 1

Two ways to get soaked

There is no third option that avoids both costs — only a spectrum between them.

Trade fast

Sell it all right now and you walk deep through the order book, paying a large, immediate, fairly predictable cost for demanding that much immediacy at once. This is impact cost — concentrated into a short window.

Trade slow

Sell a sliver every hour over a month and each sale barely moves the price — but you're now sitting on a large unsold position, fully exposed to ordinary price drift for the entire time. This is timing risk — exposure stretched over a long window.

The tension

You cannot minimize both at once. Trading faster shrinks the window you're exposed to volatility but raises the cost of demanding liquidity right now; trading slower does the reverse. Every execution schedule is a specific, implicit answer to how much of one you're willing to accept to reduce the other.

Section 2

Two kinds of impact — one reverts, one doesn't

Every trade moves the price. Whether that move sticks around depends on why it happened.

Try it
The price concession needed to get filled right now. Once trading stops, liquidity refills and the price drifts back — this component reverts.
A lasting repricing, because the trade itself is informative — others rationally update their belief about fair value partly because you traded. This component does not revert.

Press your thumb into a memory-foam pillow. Some of the dent springs back the moment you let go — that's temporary impact. But if pressing hard enough actually changed something about the foam itself, part of the dent stays forever — that's permanent impact, because your trade told everyone something real, and nobody un-learns it.

Section 3

Impact grows with the square root of size, not size itself

A large body of empirical research finds impact scales roughly as I ≈ Y·σ·√(Q / ADV) — not linearly with order size.

Empirical square-root law vs. a naive linear guess
Both curves are pinned to agree at 0 and at 0.5 ADV by construction. In between, the concave square-root curve bows above the straight chord — at a quarter of ADV it implies ~100bps versus ~71bps for a naive linear model.

Because √x is concave, quadrupling order size only roughly doubles the impact, i.e. the cost per unit (√4Q = 2√Q; total cost, size × impact, rises about 8×) — very large orders are cheaper per share than a linear model would suggest. But the same curve cuts the other way for smaller orders: they already cost more per share than a linear model calibrated off a bigger reference size would predict.

Section 5

Almgren-Chriss: put a number on both sides of the trade-off

Liquidate X units over a horizon [0,T]. Two costs compete, and one dial decides how heavily each counts.

Total cost — minimize over the whole schedule
Trading faster (a larger rate) raises impact cost, modeled as growing with the square of the trading rate. Every unit still unsold is exposed to volatility σ, so holding inventory longer raises the variance term. λ is the dial: how heavily the trader weights that variance against expected impact cost.
η

Temporary-impact coefficient

Cost per unit of (trading rate)² — how expensive it is, per instant, to demand a given speed of execution.

λ

Risk aversion — the dial

How heavily the trader penalizes variance (timing risk) relative to expected impact cost. λ = 0 is risk-neutral; larger λ means more nervous about uncertainty.

σ

Volatility

The instrument's price volatility over the execution horizon — the thing you're exposed to for as long as inventory remains unsold.

Permanent impact doesn't appear here at all — for a linear permanent-impact model, its total cost depends only on the fixed total size X, not on the shape of the schedule, so it's a sunk cost every trajectory pays identically. It still costs real money; it just doesn't affect which shape is optimal.
Section 6

One shape parameter decides everything

Solving the trade-off above (a calculus-of-variations problem) collapses to one clean closed form.

x(t)

Remaining inventory

Units still left to trade at time t. Starts at X, must reach exactly 0 by time T.

κ

κ = √(λσ²/η) — the shape parameter

Folds risk aversion, volatility, and the impact coefficient into a single number, in units of 1/time.

∿

x(t) = X · sinh(κ(T−t)) / sinh(κT)

A straight line at κ→0; increasingly front-loaded — fast early, nearly flat later — as κ grows.

κ is basically a dial for "how nervous are you." Turn it all the way down and you sell at a perfectly steady pace, like a metronome — that's TWAP. Turn it up and you rush to sell most of it early, accepting a worse average price, just to stop worrying about what the market might do later.
Two limits tell the whole story

As κ→0 (λ=0, risk-neutral): sinh(z)≈z for small z, so x(t)/X → (T−t)/T — a perfectly straight line, constant trading rate, i.e. TWAP. As κ grows large: sinh grows exponentially, so the trajectory front-loads hard — most of the order sold early, a long, nearly-flat tail after.

Section 8

Three ways to execute the same order

Same total size, same horizon, three different schedules — with three different cost/risk profiles.

Try it
One instant, maximum immediacy: you walk deep through the book and pay the full impact cost in a single concentrated hit — but timing risk is essentially zero, because you're done before the market has time to move against you.
Constant trading rate for the whole window (κ = 0 — a risk-neutral trader): impact cost per share is much lower, but you carry meaningful inventory for the entire horizon, fully exposed to ordinary price drift throughout.
A moderately risk-averse trader (κ > 0): most of the order gets done early, at a slightly worse expected impact cost than TWAP, specifically to cut the time spent holding inventory — and with it, timing-risk exposure.
Bars are illustrative, not fitted to real execution data — they show the direction and relative size of the trade-off, not calibrated magnitudes.
Section 9

How desks actually use this

Almost nobody solves the Euler-Lagrange equation by hand for every order — but the framework's outputs shape real execution-algorithm design.

📐

Calibrating η and σ from real fills

Temporary-impact coefficient and volatility are estimated empirically from historical executions, often using the square-root law as the starting functional form.

🎚️

Choosing λ as an explicit policy

A desk sets risk aversion deliberately — based on genuine risk tolerance and situational context, like a known upcoming volatility event — not whatever a default happens to produce.

🤖

Building TWAP/VWAP/IS algorithm families

TWAP is the κ→0 limit; VWAP adapts the same trade-off to typical intraday volume curves; "implementation shortfall" algorithms minimize this exact impact-vs-risk objective, often with front-loading built in.

🔄

Re-solving the schedule in real time

Live algorithms adapt as conditions change mid-execution — volatility spiking, liquidity thinning — rather than committing blindly to a static plan computed once.

Section 10

Where this gets misread

Easy to misread
  • Treating the closed-form sinh/cosh trajectory as literally optimal in live markets — it assumes constant σ and η for the whole horizon
  • Assuming permanent impact "doesn't matter" because it drops out of the shape optimization — it's still a real, often large cost
  • Assuming higher risk aversion is always "safer" — a high-κ schedule pays more expected impact cost by construction, not a free lunch
  • Using Kyle's linear impact and the square-root law interchangeably, ignoring the size regime each fits
The correct reading
  • Treat the closed form as a starting schedule, adapted as real intraday volatility and liquidity actually evolve
  • Budget for permanent impact separately as a real, trajectory-independent cost on the full order size
  • Pick λ deliberately to match genuine risk tolerance or a specific situational reason — not by default
  • Use the linear approximation for smaller orders relative to depth; use the square-root law at larger sizes relative to ADV
Factual grounding — checked against this repo's actual code

The full lesson tags this concept "not built or tested in this repo," and that check holds up: no square-root impact law, no Kyle's-Lambda-style size-scaled cost, and no Almgren-Chriss-style execution-trajectory optimizer exists anywhere in this codebase. Cost modeling here is flat — bot/backtest.py's apply_costs() charges the same spread-plus-slippage debit on every trade regardless of size. Two namesakes are worth ruling out: VWAP appears widely (e.g. vumanchuLab/, Gold/modules/session_engine.py) purely as a technical-analysis anchor level, and "TWAP slope" in backtestSystem/engine.py is a smoothed trend indicator — neither is an execution-scheduling algorithm. This repo trades single orders at flat cost, not sliced institutional blocks.

Section 11 · It actually happened

Archegos, March 2021: one exit, several sellers

It actually happened

The family office Archegos held huge, concentrated stock positions through total return swaps with several prime brokers, each of which held the shares as a hedge. When ViacomCBS sold new stock and its shares fell, Archegos couldn't meet its margin calls. The banks discussed an orderly joint wind-down on Thursday 25 March and didn't agree. On Friday some, Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley among them, sold billions of dollars of stock in big blocks. The early sellers (Goldman, Deutsche Bank, Wells Fargo) reported no material loss. The slow ones sold into prices everyone else had already pushed down: Credit Suisse lost about $5.5 billion, Nomura about $2.9 billion.

Section 12

Where the textbook breaks, and what's untested here

Where the textbook version breaks

Almgren-Chriss assumes one liquidator: timing risk is zero-mean volatility, ½λσ²x(t)², and the only permanent impact is your own. With rival forced sellers in the same names, each of their blocks is permanent impact on your remaining x(t), a predictable drift against you rather than noise. Then the patient schedule is the worst one, and a race to the exit is rational.

Not tested here "Trading slower lowers total execution cost": not tested on this desk, which sends single small orders at a flat modeled cost and has never sliced an order or measured impact. A fair test needs sliced parent orders with arrival price, every child fill and market volume. It would compare implementation shortfall across schedule speeds at matched size/ADV and volatility, flag days with other large sellers, and fix all of that in advance.
Section 13

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Under the square-root law, quadrupling an order's size relative to ADV roughly does what to its impact?

Impact ∝ √(Q/ADV), and √(4Q) = 2√Q — quadrupling the input to a square root only doubles the output.
Question 2

Which component of market impact reverts once you stop trading?

Temporary impact is the concession needed for immediacy right now — it unwinds as the book refills. Permanent impact is a lasting, information-driven repricing that doesn't revert.
Question 3

In x(t) = X·sinh(κ(T−t))/sinh(κT), what happens as κ → 0?

sinh(z) ≈ z for small z, so the ratio collapses to (T−t)/T — a straight line, i.e. a constant trading rate the whole horizon.
Question 4

A highly risk-averse trader (high κ) typically chooses a trajectory that is:

High κ means sinh grows fast, front-loading the schedule — trading faster early to cut how long inventory sits exposed to timing risk, accepting more impact cost in exchange.
Question 5

Why does permanent impact drop out of the Almgren-Chriss optimal-trajectory calculation?

Every candidate trajectory pays the identical permanent-impact cost (fixed at total size X), so it contributes nothing to choosing between trajectories — it drops out of the shape optimization, but it's still a real cost paid on the full order.
Your call
Scenario drill · 26 March 2021

You hold the same blocks as every other prime broker

You run equity financing at a prime broker. Your client, Archegos, built huge positions in a handful of stocks through total return swaps, and you hold the shares as your hedge. ViacomCBS sold new shares, its stock fell sharply, and Archegos cannot meet its margin calls. Yesterday the banks discussed an orderly joint wind-down and did not agree. Several other banks hold large blocks of the same stocks. It is Friday morning. How do you sell?

  1. Follow a patient, low-urgency schedule over the coming weeks to keep your impact cost down.
    That is the right answer for a lone seller facing random volatility. Here the risk of waiting is not random: every block another bank sells pushes down the price of what you still hold. The patient schedule sells into everyone else's impact.
  2. Sell early and in size, in large blocks, accepting a steep discount now.
    With rival forced sellers, the first to sell gets the best prices still on offer. You pay a known, large impact cost to avoid a larger, predictable loss from selling after the others.
  3. Run a strongly front-loaded schedule through the day, finishing by the close.
    The right instinct: high urgency, done fast. But spread over a whole day, much of it can still land after rivals' blocks have hit the price. Better than a slow schedule, worse than moving first.
  4. Hold off and keep pushing for an orderly joint wind-down with the other banks.
    Talks had already failed, and nothing stops a rival from selling while you negotiate. Waiting leaves your whole position exposed to their selling.

What happened. On Friday 26 March some banks, Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley among them, sold billions of dollars of stock in large blocks at steep discounts, before the market had absorbed what was happening. Goldman, Deutsche Bank and Wells Fargo later reported no material loss. The slower banks sold into prices already pushed down by everyone else's blocks: Credit Suisse lost about $5.5 billion and Nomura about $2.9 billion.

Section 14

What to carry forward

01

Impact splits into temporary (the cost of immediacy, which reverts) and permanent (an information-driven repricing, which doesn't).

02

The empirical square-root law says impact ∝ √(Q/ADV), not linear — big orders are cheaper per share than a linear model implies; small orders are costlier.

03

Optimal execution is a genuine trade-off — trading fast concentrates impact cost into a short window, trading slow spreads timing-risk exposure over a long one, and you can't shrink both at once.

04

Almgren-Chriss minimizes expected impact cost plus λ × the variance of execution price (timing risk) over the whole trajectory — λ is the one risk-aversion dial.

05

The optimal schedule has a closed form: x(t) = X·sinh(κ(T−t))/sinh(κT), κ = √(λσ²/η) — a straight line (TWAP) at κ = 0, increasingly front-loaded as κ rises.

06

Permanent impact doesn't change which shape is optimal, since it's a sunk cost every schedule pays identically — but it still costs real money on the full order.

07

The model assumes you're the only seller. At Archegos (March 2021) early block sellers reported no material loss and the slowest lost billions: rivals' selling is permanent impact on your inventory. Untested on this desk.

What this now lets you do

Read any execution schedule and recognize what risk-aversion assumption it implies. Roughly estimate impact cost from order size, ADV, and a calibrated coefficient using the square-root law. Recognize that front-loading isn't "wrong" or "overcautious" — it's a legitimate, quantifiable choice of a different λ, not a mistake.
