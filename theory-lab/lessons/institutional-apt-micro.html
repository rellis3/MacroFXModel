Arbitrage Pricing Theory, No Equilibrium Required

CAPM derives its one factor from an equation about how every investor in the economy behaves. Fama-French hand-picks specific factors because they empirically work. APT does neither — it needs only one, much weaker assumption, no arbitrage, to force expected returns into an approximately linear function of exposures to some small number of pervasive risk factors, without ever saying which ones, or how many.

Section 1

Three routes to one equation

Same destination — an equation for expected return — three completely different ways to get there. Pick a model.

Compare
Derived from full market equilibrium. Assume every investor is mean-variance optimizing and holds some mix of the risk-free asset and the single market portfolio — that alone pins down exactly one factor, and one price of risk for it. No choices left for the researcher to make.
Chosen empirically — no equilibrium argument at all. Sort stocks by size, value, momentum; keep whichever sorted long-short portfolios earn a persistent excess average return; call the winners factors. Three or four specific, named, fixed-in-advance factors, the same ones every time.
Derived from no-arbitrage alone — far weaker than full market equilibrium. Proves returns must be approximately linear in exposures to some small number of pervasive factors — but never says which ones, or how many. A theorem about the shape of the answer, not a specific fitted model.

APT only becomes a specific, testable model once you plug in a concrete choice of factors — the market portfolio alone (nearly CAPM again), the Fama-French factors, or — this deck's focus from here on — observable macroeconomic surprises.

Section 2

Diversification erases the noise

Every asset's return splits into a common-factor part and its own private noise. Average enough assets and the noise disappears.

➗

One return, two parts

Rᵢ = E[Rᵢ] + β₁F₁ + β₂F₂ + … + εᵢ — a systematic part every asset shares some exposure to, plus εᵢ, pure asset-specific noise, uncorrelated across assets.

📉

Average enough assets…

A portfolio of N assets weighted ~1/N each has idiosyncratic-noise variance shrinking toward zero as N grows — the exact same "average away the noise" mechanic random forests use on tree predictions, applied here to portfolios instead.

✅

…and only the systematic part survives

A large, well-diversified portfolio's return collapses to E[R_P] + Σ β_Pk·Fk — no εᵢ term left to explain away, only exposure to the common, pervasive factors.

If you flip one coin, the outcome is genuinely unpredictable. Flip a thousand and look at the average — that average lands remarkably close to 50% almost every time, because each coin's own randomness is a different, unrelated flip. A diversified portfolio does the same thing to a thousand unrelated, asset-specific noise terms.
Section 3

The no-arbitrage argument, drawn

Same factor exposure, different expected return — that gap cannot survive competitive trading.

Visualize it
Long $1 of P, short $1 of Q: zero net investment, zero net factor exposure (the identical β's cancel), zero idiosyncratic risk (both are already diversified) — yet a certain 2-percentage-point profit. Competitive arbitrage trading closes gaps exactly like this one, and that closing pressure is the entire mechanism behind APT's pricing equation.
Section 4

What the argument forces the pricing equation to look like

The equation
E[Rᵢ]
≈
Rf
+
Σ βᵢₖ · λₖ
Expected return = the risk-free rate, plus (loading × price of risk) summed across every pervasive factor. Compare this to CAPM's Rf + β·(market premium): APT is exactly this idea, generalized from one factor to K factors — with K deliberately left unspecified by the theory itself.
Rf

Risk-free rate

What any asset with zero exposure to every factor should earn — no risk taken, no premium owed.

β

Factor loading

Asset i's sensitivity to factor k, estimated from a time-series regression of the asset's returns on the factor's surprise series.

λ

Factor risk premium

The expected excess return of a portfolio with unit exposure to factor k alone, and zero exposure to every other factor — the price the market pays for that one risk.

Section 5

Chen, Roll & Ross: a macro face on the factors

The 1986 paper that made APT genuinely useful: five observable macro surprise variables as the priced factors.

🏭

Unexpected industrial production

The surprise component of real output growth — the same "how hot is the economy running" question the Taylor Rule's output gap asks, expressed as a surprise instead of a level.

💵

Unexpected inflation

Actual minus already-priced-in — not the inflation rate itself. A print landing exactly at consensus carries no new information.

📐

Unexpected term-spread change

The surprise in long-minus-short government yields — a shift in the expected future path of short rates beyond what was already anticipated.

⚠️

Unexpected default-spread change

The surprise in the credit-risk premium — a read on unanticipated shifts in aggregate default risk and risk appetite.

📊

A market-return residual

Whatever broad equity-market exposure isn't already captured by the four macro surprises above — so the macro story isn't quietly smuggling in the whole market factor unlabeled.

Section 6

Why "surprise," never the level

The single most important idea in this whole factor list.

Markets are forward-looking. By the time a macro release prints, the part everyone already expected is already priced in — through futures curves, consensus surveys, a central bank's own guidance. Only the part nobody saw coming still represents genuine, un-hedged risk at the moment it's realized, and only that part is capable of carrying a risk premium.
Where this connects

This is the identical logic the Central Bank Policy lesson uses for rate decisions: "it is the hawkish or dovish surprise relative to what was already priced — not the absolute stance itself — that moves a currency." A hike exactly matching what was priced moves almost nothing. Chen-Roll-Ross applies the same reasoning to a whole factor model at once — the Taylor Rule lesson's own "gap between implied and actual, not the level" is this same idea again, one layer removed.

Section 10

Two honest cracks in the theory

The generality problem

There is no single, universally agreed "the APT model," the way there is exactly one standard Fama-French three-factor specification. Because the theory itself never specifies which factors or how many, different researchers running the identical no-arbitrage theorem routinely choose different macro variables, different factor counts, and get different implied risk premia as a result.

Verified against this repo's actual code

This repo has real macro-surprise construction (js/econSurprise.js standardizes actual-minus-consensus by each series' own dispersion) and a real joint multi-factor OLS engine (js/mve/ols.js). But it never joins them: the surprise/regime studies (js/macroRegimeFx.js) report mean FX returns bucket-by-bucket, one macro state at a time, while the multi-factor OLS engine runs on macro levels (real-rate diff, DXY, curve) for fair-value estimation, not on surprises for a Chen-Roll-Ross-style expected-return decomposition. Both halves are real; the specific joint regression this lesson describes is a real but different thing this codebase hasn't built.

Section 11

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

What single assumption is APT derived from?

This is a much weaker assumption than CAPM's full-market-equilibrium requirement — it needs nothing about how any individual investor behaves, only that riskless arbitrage gets competed away.
Question 2

Why is APT called "a framework, not a fitted model"?

APT proves returns must be approximately linear in exposures to SOME pervasive factors — it becomes a specific, testable model only once a researcher picks the factors.
Question 3

Two diversified portfolios have identical factor exposures but different expected returns. What does a long/short position across them look like?

That combination — no cost, no risk, but a positive expected payoff — is exactly the free lunch competitive arbitrage trading closes.
Question 4

Why does Chen-Roll-Ross use the SURPRISE in inflation and industrial production, not their raw levels?

Markets are forward-looking — by release time, the anticipated part is already reflected in prices. This is the same "surprise, not stance" logic the Central Bank Policy lesson uses for rate decisions.
Question 5

Does having a macro-surprise index AND a multi-factor OLS engine mean this repo runs a Chen-Roll-Ross-style APT regression?

The surprise index and regime studies report bucketed means, one macro state at a time; the multi-factor OLS engine regresses on macro levels for fair value, not on joint surprise factors for expected-return decomposition.
Section 12

What to carry forward

01

APT is derived from no-arbitrage alone — far weaker than CAPM's full-equilibrium assumption, which is what makes it more general and less specific at once.

02

It's a framework, not a fitted model — unlike CAPM's one derived factor or Fama-French's specific chosen factors, APT never says which factors or how many.

03

The mechanism is a free-lunch argument: identical factor exposure but different expected return lets you build a zero-cost, zero-risk position with a nonzero expected payoff.

04

Chen-Roll-Ross (1986) gave APT a genuinely useful macro face: unexpected industrial production, inflation, term-spread, and default-spread surprises, plus a market residual.

05

Only the surprise carries a risk premium, never the level — the same logic this curriculum's Central Bank Policy and Taylor Rule lessons use for rate decisions.

06

There's no single, standard "the APT model" — treat any specific specification as one modeling choice among many, not a settled consensus.

What this now lets you do

Recognize APT for what it actually is — a theorem about the shape of the pricing relationship, not a specific list of factors — and read any paper's "APT test" as one modeling choice among many rather than a settled result. Apply the "surprise, not level" logic to any macro-driven factor story, not just Chen-Roll-Ross's original five. Tell a genuine joint multi-factor regression apart from separate one-factor-at-a-time checks, which sets up the next lesson's risk-model machinery.
