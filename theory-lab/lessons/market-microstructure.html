Market Microstructure: Price Impact & the Bid-Ask Spread

A market maker facing an anonymous order never knows whether it comes from someone who knows something they don't, or someone who simply needs to trade — and that single piece of uncertainty is the formal origin of both the bid-ask spread and the fact that trading a bigger size moves the price against you.

CONCEPT — the theory behind this repo's fill-realism assumptions ~17 min read Prereqs: none required — Correlation & Linear Regression primer helpful

Why this matters

This repo already behaves as if trading costs real money in a way that varies with circumstance, not just a flat commission. The house rules in this project's own memory file are explicit about it: "costs on by default... plus slippage on stop/breakout entries. Free fills are not honest," and a standing anti-pattern warns against "assuming the intrabar take-profit was hit on a daily bar (path is unknown) — prefer M1 fills." fill-realism.html goes further and empirically tests whether a strategy's headline Sharpe survives being re-priced through progressively more realistic, finer-grained fills. All of that is sound practical discipline. None of it, as currently written anywhere in this repo, explains why a spread exists in the first place, or why slippage should be modeled as getting systematically worse with order size and worse still in thin liquidity — as opposed to just being a fixed friction you subtract off the top.

Market microstructure theory answers both questions with two results from the same year, 1985, that between them are the formal foundation under almost every practical execution rule a trading desk follows. Glosten and Milgrom explain why a bid-ask spread exists at all — not as an arbitrary fee, but as a market maker's rational, calculated defense against systematically losing money to counterparties who know more than they do. Kyle explains why the size of an order matters — why trading twice as much moves the price roughly twice as far, and why that relationship is steeper in a thin market than a deep one. Together they are the rigorous "why" behind a rule this repo already enforces as pure practical hygiene: that fills are not free, that they get worse with size, and that they get worse still exactly when liquidity is thin.

Plain-English intuition

The market maker's dilemma: who am I actually trading against?

Picture yourself as a market maker in some FX pair. Someone submits an order to buy from you. You have no idea who they are or why they're trading. Maybe they are a corporate treasury converting export revenue back to their home currency — a reason completely unrelated to whether the "true" value of the pair is about to move. Or maybe they are a trader who, for whatever reason (a faster news feed, a better model, an order flow signal, in the extreme case genuine inside information about an imminent central bank action) actually knows the fair price is higher than where you're currently quoting it. Call the first kind a noise trader (also called a liquidity trader — they trade for reasons orthogonal to value) and the second kind an informed trader.

If you quote a single "best guess" price and trade with anyone who shows up at it, you will do fine, on average, against noise traders — their buys and sells are unrelated to where the price is really headed, so you win as often as you lose. But you will systematically lose against informed traders: they only show up on the side of the trade that is bad for you, by construction, precisely because they know something you don't. If informed traders make up any meaningful share of your flow, quoting at your single best-guess price is a losing strategy over time — you are, without realizing it, giving free options to anyone with better information.

The fix is to not quote a single price at all. Quote two prices — a bid (where you buy) below your best-guess fair value, and an ask (where you sell) above it — and set them far enough apart that the money you make, on average, from noise traders passing through that gap is enough to cover the money you expect to lose, on average, to informed traders trading against you. The width of that gap — the spread — is not an arbitrary fee. It is calibrated specifically to survive adverse selection: the structural fact that the people most likely to trade with you when it's bad for you are exactly the people who know something you don't. The more of your flow you suspect is informed, the wider that protective gap has to be. This is the whole idea behind Glosten-Milgrom, made precise below.

The math, step by step

Two separate results are covered here, in the order they build on each other conceptually: (1) Glosten-Milgrom, a discrete, one-order-at-a-time model that derives the bid-ask spread from adverse selection using nothing but Bayes' theorem, and (2) Kyle's Lambda, a continuous model of order size that generalizes the same adverse-selection logic into a linear price-impact relationship. Glosten-Milgrom explains why there is a gap between bid and ask even for a single share; Kyle explains why that gap effectively widens further, and keeps widening, the more you try to trade at once.

1. Setting up the market maker's problem

Assume there is some "true" (fundamental, fully-informed) value of the asset, \(V\), which for tractability takes one of two possible values with equal prior probability: a high value \(V_H\) or a low value \(V_L\), with \(V_H > V_L\). The market maker does not know which is true — only that each is equally likely going in. A stream of orders arrives one at a time, and each order is either a buy or a sell. Crucially, each order comes from one of two types of trader, and the market maker cannot tell which type is in front of them:

Informed traderOccurs with probability \(\alpha\) for any given order. Knows \(V\) exactly. Always trades in the direction that is profitable given that knowledge: buys if \(V=V_H\) (the asset is worth more than it looks), sells if \(V=V_L\).
Noise (liquidity) traderOccurs with probability \(1-\alpha\). Trades for reasons unrelated to \(V\) — buys or sells with equal probability regardless of what \(V\) actually is.
\(\alpha\)The market maker's belief about the proportion of order flow that is informed. This single number is the whole story: it is exogenous here (assumed known or estimated from context — a busy pre-news session, a name with a history of informed trading, and so on), and everything about the resulting spread is driven by it.

The market maker is assumed to be risk-neutral and competitive — free entry from other market makers drives expected profit on each quote to exactly zero. That zero-expected-profit condition is what pins down the bid and ask: each one must equal the market maker's best updated estimate of \(V\), conditional on having just observed a buy (for the ask) or a sell (for the bid). "Best updated estimate conditional on new evidence" is exactly a job for Bayes' theorem.

2. Bayesian updating: why a trade is itself evidence

This is a direct application of the machinery from the Bayesian Inference lesson in this Theory Lab, and it is worth stating the callback plainly: every order the market maker sees is a piece of evidence that updates their belief about \(V\), in exactly the same prior-times-likelihood-over-evidence sense as any other Bayesian update. This is the deep reason prices move on trades at all, even absent any new public news — the trade itself is informative, because it is more likely to have come from an informed trader who knows something, than not.

Let \(H\) be the hypothesis "\(V=V_H\)" and \(E\) be the evidence "the order just observed was a buy." First, the two likelihoods — how likely a buy order is, under each possible true value:

\[ P(\text{buy}\mid V_H) = \alpha\cdot 1 + (1-\alpha)\cdot\tfrac12 = \frac{1+\alpha}{2} \]
\[ P(\text{buy}\mid V_L) = \alpha\cdot 0 + (1-\alpha)\cdot\tfrac12 = \frac{1-\alpha}{2} \]

Read the first line: if \(V\) is truly high, a buy order arrives either because an informed trader showed up (probability \(\alpha\), and they always buy in this case) or because a noise trader showed up and happened to buy (probability \((1-\alpha)\times\tfrac12\)). The second line is the mirror image: if \(V\) is truly low, a buy order can only come from a noise trader who happened to buy — an informed trader would never buy when they know the asset is overvalued. This asymmetry — a buy is more likely when \(V_H\) is true than when \(V_L\) is true, and by exactly how much more depends on \(\alpha\) — is the entire engine of the model.

Apply Bayes' theorem exactly as derived in the Bayesian Inference lesson, \(P(H\mid E) = P(E\mid H)P(H)/P(E)\), with the equal \(\tfrac12\) prior on each state (the two \(\tfrac12\) priors cancel with the \(P(\text{buy})\) normalizer, which itself works out to exactly \(\tfrac12\) — noise-trader symmetry means a buy and a sell are unconditionally equally likely):

\[ P(V_H \mid \text{buy}) = \frac{P(\text{buy}\mid V_H)\,P(V_H)}{P(\text{buy})} = \frac{1+\alpha}{2} \]

Before the order, the market maker's belief was a flat 50/50 on \(V_H\) versus \(V_L\). After observing a single buy order, that belief shifts to \(\tfrac{1+\alpha}{2}\) in favor of \(V_H\) — strictly above 50%, and by more the larger \(\alpha\) is. If \(\alpha=0\) (nobody is ever informed), the posterior stays at exactly 50% — a buy tells you nothing, because it's exactly as likely to come from a noise trader regardless of \(V\). If \(\alpha\) is large, a buy is strong evidence for \(V_H\), because a large share of buys are coming from people who know it's true. This is the formal version of "prices move on trades even without public news": the trade itself is the news, filtered through how much of the market's flow the market maker believes is informed.

3. From the posterior to the quoted spread

The zero-expected-profit condition says the ask must equal the market maker's expected value of \(V\) given a buy just arrived — set the ask any lower and informed buyers eat you alive; any higher and a competing market maker undercuts you. Using the posterior above:

\[ \text{Ask} = E[V\mid\text{buy}] = \frac{1+\alpha}{2}V_H + \frac{1-\alpha}{2}V_L \]

By the exact mirror-image argument (a sell order shifts belief toward \(V_L\) by the same logic), the bid is:

\[ \text{Bid} = E[V\mid\text{sell}] = \frac{1+\alpha}{2}V_L + \frac{1-\alpha}{2}V_H \]

Subtract one from the other and almost everything cancels, leaving a remarkably clean result:

\[ \text{Spread} = \text{Ask} - \text{Bid} = \alpha\,(V_H - V_L) \]

The spread is exactly the fraction of informed trading, \(\alpha\), times the total uncertainty about fundamental value, \((V_H-V_L)\). Two consequences worth sitting with: first, the midpoint of the bid and ask, \(\tfrac{\text{Ask}+\text{Bid}}{2}\), works out to exactly \(\tfrac{V_H+V_L}{2}\) — the unconditional prior mean, unaffected by \(\alpha\). The market maker isn't shading their central estimate up or down; they're widening symmetrically around it. Second, and this is the headline result: the spread scales linearly with \(\alpha\) holding value-uncertainty fixed. Double the market maker's belief that flow is informed, and the spread they need to quote to survive doubles with it. A spread is not an administrative fee — it is the price of protection against adverse selection, sized exactly to the perceived risk of it.

4. Kyle's Lambda: the same logic for a continuum of order sizes

Glosten-Milgrom prices one indivisible order at a time — a single buy or sell, no notion of "how much." Real order flow has a size, and the same adverse-selection logic implies something practically more important for execution: the price concession a market maker demands should grow with the size of the order, not just with whether it's a buy or a sell. This is Kyle's (1985) contribution, and its headline object is a single linear equation:

\[ \Delta p = \lambda\, Q \]
\(Q\)The signed order flow — positive for net buying, negative for net selling, and larger in magnitude for a bigger order (or a bigger net imbalance of orders over some window).
\(\Delta p\)The resulting price impact — how far the price moves away from where it was, as a direct consequence of that order flow.
\(\lambda\)(lambda) Kyle's Lambda — the price-impact coefficient, in price-units-moved per unit of order flow. This is the object the whole model exists to explain.

Kyle's original setup swaps Glosten-Milgrom's binary informed/noise-order sequence for a continuous, one-period version: an insider who knows the asset's true liquidation value \(v\) (drawn from a distribution with standard deviation \(\sigma_v\) — how much uncertainty there is about fundamental value) chooses an order size to submit; simultaneously, noise traders submit an aggregate random order flow with standard deviation \(\sigma_u\) (how much random, value-unrelated trading volume the market naturally has). The market maker sees only the total combined order flow — insider plus noise, indistinguishable from one another — and sets a single linear price rule \(p = \bar v + \lambda\,Q\) to break even in expectation, exactly the same zero-expected-profit discipline as Glosten-Milgrom, just solved as a continuous equilibrium instead of a single Bayesian update. Solving that equilibrium (a fixed point: the insider's optimal trade size depends on \(\lambda\), and \(\lambda\) in turn depends on how aggressively the insider is expected to trade — the full derivation is a Bayesian Nash equilibrium and is outside this lesson's scope) yields:

\[ \lambda = \frac{\sigma_v}{2\,\sigma_u} \]

Read this the way the model intends: \(\lambda\) rises when \(\sigma_v\) is large (a lot of fundamental uncertainty — there's more for an insider to potentially know, so the market maker must protect against it harder) and falls when \(\sigma_u\) is large (a deep pool of noise/liquidity trading — any given order is more likely to just be noise, diluting the informed share, so it can be absorbed with less price concession). Kyle himself defined market depth as \(1/\lambda\) — literally "how much order flow it takes to move the price by one unit." A market with many potentially-informed participants and thin noise-trading volume has a small \(\sigma_u\) relative to \(\sigma_v\), hence a high \(\lambda\) and shallow depth: the same-size order moves the price a lot. A deep, mostly-noise-trader-dominated market has a low \(\lambda\) and can absorb size with barely a ripple. This is the formal object underneath the everyday trading intuition that "slippage gets worse the bigger you trade, especially in a thin or illiquid moment" — that intuition is \(\lambda\), just not usually named as such.

Worked example

Computing a spread and a price impact by hand

Part 1 — Glosten-Milgrom spread. Suppose value uncertainty puts \(V_H = 101\) and \(V_L = 99\) (a symmetric \(\pm 1\) band around a par value of 100 — think of this in pip-scaled FX terms). Compare two beliefs about informed-trading share, \(\alpha=0.3\) versus \(\alpha=0.6\):

\[ \alpha=0.3:\quad \text{Ask}=0.65(101)+0.35(99)=100.30,\quad \text{Bid}=0.35(101)+0.65(99)=99.70,\quad \text{Spread}=0.60 \]
\[ \alpha=0.6:\quad \text{Ask}=0.80(101)+0.20(99)=100.60,\quad \text{Bid}=0.20(101)+0.80(99)=99.40,\quad \text{Spread}=1.20 \]

Exactly as the formula \(\text{Spread}=\alpha(V_H-V_L)\) predicts: \(0.3\times 2=0.60\) and \(0.6\times 2=1.20\). Doubling the market maker's belief about informed-trading share exactly doubles the spread they need to quote to stay at zero expected profit — with the midpoint pinned at 100.00 in both cases.

Part 2 — Kyle's Lambda price impact. Take value uncertainty \(\sigma_v = 2\) (price units) as fixed, and compare two liquidity regimes via the noise-trading volume \(\sigma_u\): a normal, liquid session with \(\sigma_u=100\) (units of order flow), versus a thin, illiquid session with \(\sigma_u=20\):

\[ \text{Liquid: } \lambda = \frac{2}{2(100)} = 0.01 \qquad\qquad \text{Thin: } \lambda' = \frac{2}{2(20)} = 0.05 \]

Now push the same order, \(Q=500\) units, through both regimes:

\[ \text{Liquid: } \Delta p = 0.01 \times 500 = \$5 \qquad\qquad \text{Thin: } \Delta p' = 0.05 \times 500 = \$25 \]

Identical order, five times the price impact, purely because the thin session has a fifth as much noise-trading volume to absorb it into. Nothing about the order itself changed — only the depth of the market it landed in. This is precisely the chart below, and precisely the mechanism behind the real-world trading scenario that follows it.

Both lines pass through the origin — no order, no impact — and both are straight, which is Kyle's linear price-impact assumption: impact is proportional to size, not a step function or a curve. The steeper red line is a strictly thinner market by the same 500-contract order: five times the impact, from a market with a fifth as much noise-trading volume to absorb it. Real markets deviate from perfect linearity at very large sizes (empirical microstructure research finds impact grows somewhat sub-linearly, closer to a square-root law, for the biggest orders) — Kyle's linear model is the tractable first-order approximation, not a claim that the line never bends.

How practitioners actually use this

Nobody at a real trading desk solves the Glosten-Milgrom Bayesian update or Kyle's fixed-point equilibrium by hand before placing an order. What both models motivate, and what desks do use constantly, is a small set of execution habits that only make sense once you understand adverse selection and price impact are real, quantifiable forces rather than vague folklore: (1) widening quoted spreads mechanically around scheduled information events — a market maker quoting FX pairs ahead of a central bank rate decision or a jobs report widens because they know \(\alpha\), the informed-trading share, is about to spike as participants trade on leaked or fast-processed information; (2) slicing large orders over time (algorithms like TWAP, VWAP, and implementation-shortfall schedules exist specifically to avoid dumping one large \(Q\) into the market at once, since \(\Delta p = \lambda Q\) says a single big clip costs more, in total, than the same size spread across many smaller clips that each let the market partially absorb and recover); and (3) estimating \(\lambda\) empirically — the microstructure literature runs exactly the regression the formula suggests, \(\Delta p_t = \lambda\, Q_t + \varepsilon_t\), on real trade or bar data, to get a live, asset- and regime-specific estimate of price impact rather than relying on a textbook constant. All three habits are the same underlying idea in different clothes: informed-trading risk and thin liquidity both raise the true cost of demanding immediacy, and a disciplined desk prices that cost in rather than discovering it the hard way in a bad fill.

Real-world trading scenario

The same order, twice — why the London/Asia session gap is where slippage bites

A retail trader places a moderately large market order in a thinly-traded pair right around the low-liquidity transition between the Asia and London sessions — a window with materially fewer active participants on both sides of the book than during peak London/New York overlap hours. The fill comes back meaningfully worse than the quoted price at the moment the order was sent.

This is not a broken platform, a scam broker, or bad luck. It is Kyle's Lambda, exactly as derived above, playing out in real time: \(\lambda = \sigma_v/(2\sigma_u)\) is high in that thin window specifically because \(\sigma_u\) — the depth of ordinary, value- unrelated liquidity trading available to absorb the order — has collapsed. The identical order size that would barely move the price during peak overlap liquidity (a low-\(\lambda\) regime, plenty of noise-trader depth to absorb it) produces real, structural slippage during the thin session (a high-\(\lambda\) regime), for the same structural reason the worked example above showed a five-fold gap in impact from the same 500-unit order in a liquid versus a thin market. Nothing about the trader's order was unusual; the market it landed in was thin, and thin markets have high \(\lambda\) by construction.

The practical, actionable takeaway is not "avoid the market" — it's size down, or avoid market/stop orders specifically, during known thin-liquidity windows (session transitions, pre-market gaps, illiquid pairs generally). The same order that is cheap to execute at 9am London is not cheap to execute at 5am, purely as a function of how much depth is available to trade against — a fact this lesson gives you the formal vocabulary to reason about explicitly, rather than only as an anecdotal "it always seems to fill worse around then."

Glosten-Milgrom and Kyle's Lambda are that "why": the spread every backtest here pays on entry and exit is, in theory, exactly the adverse-selection premium derived in section 3 above — a cost that exists because whoever is quoting the other side of the trade cannot tell an informed order from a noise one, and prices accordingly. And the additional slippage this repo specifically flags for stop and breakout entries — orders that, by design, fire during fast, directional moves, exactly the moments when momentary liquidity thins out and suspected informed flow spikes — is precisely a high-\(\lambda\) event: the same order size that would be cheap in calm, deep conditions costs more exactly when a stop is triggering, because \(\lambda\) is elevated in exactly that instant.

The engineering already reflects this even without naming it. Kyle's Lambda is the formal parameter that predicts exactly this kind of collapse should be expected wherever a strategy's simulated fills understate how thin liquidity actually was on the path between entry and exit. What changes is having a rigorous, citable reason for a rule that was previously enforced purely as hard-earned practical caution.

Common pitfalls

Where these models are misread or over-applied

Treating \(\lambda\) as a fixed constant of an instrument. \(\lambda = \sigma_v/(2\sigma_u)\) depends on time-varying inputs — how much fundamental uncertainty exists right now, and how much noise- trading depth is currently available — not on some permanent property of "EUR/USD" as an instrument. The same pair has a low \(\lambda\) during London/NY overlap and a high \(\lambda\) at a thin session transition, exactly as the scenario box above describes. A single "typical slippage" number for an instrument, applied at every hour of the day, throws away the single most useful implication of the model.

Confusing the spread with the total transaction cost. Glosten-Milgrom isolates one specific component of the spread — compensation for adverse selection. Real quoted spreads also bake in order-processing costs and inventory-risk compensation (a market maker holding an unwanted position also charges for the risk of carrying it, a separate strand of the microstructure literature associated with Ho-Stoll-style inventory models, not covered in this lesson). Treating the whole observed spread as pure adverse-selection premium overstates how much of it is really about informed-trading risk specifically.

Assuming price impact is purely linear at every size. Kyle's \(\Delta p = \lambda Q\) is a first-order, tractable approximation, deliberately simple to make the equilibrium solvable. Empirical market-impact research generally finds impact grows somewhat sub-linearly for very large orders relative to typical daily volume (often described as closer to a square-root relationship) — the straight lines in the chart above are the model's assumption, not a guarantee that doubling an already-enormous order exactly doubles its cost.

Treating Kyle's Lambda as only permanent impact. The model's \(\lambda\) is a single-period, permanent price-impact coefficient — the new price stays there because the market maker has permanently updated their belief about \(V\). Real executed trades also incur temporary impact (price concessions that partially revert once the immediate liquidity demand is satisfied) layered on top of the permanent component. Conflating "the price moved while I was trading" with "the price permanently repriced by that amount" overstates how much of an observed intrabar move a single order's impact actually explains.

Reading a wide spread as proof of informed trading, full stop. A wide spread is consistent with a market maker's belief that \(\alpha\) is high, but that belief can be wrong, stale, or driven by generic uncertainty (a scheduled event with unknown outcome) rather than any specific informed trader actually being present. The model describes the market maker's rational defensive posture given their belief, not a guarantee that informed traders are in fact currently active.

Self-test

Q1. Why does a market maker widen the bid-ask spread when they believe a larger fraction of incoming order flow is informed rather than noise?
Because the market maker's quotes are set by a zero-expected-profit condition: the ask must equal the expected value of the asset conditional on a buy order having just arrived, and the bid the mirror image for a sell. Informed traders only ever trade in the direction that's unprofitable for the market maker, so any informed share \(\alpha\) creates a systematic expected loss on every trade unless it's priced in. The derivation shows this precisely: Spread \(= \alpha(V_H-V_L)\) — the spread is exactly the informed-trading probability times the total fundamental-value uncertainty. A wider spread isn't greed; it's the exact protective gap needed so that profits earned from noise traders (who trade both directions equally, regardless of the truth) offset expected losses to informed traders on average, leaving zero expected profit overall.
Q2. In the Glosten-Milgrom model, why does observing a single buy order shift the market maker's belief about \(V\) from a 50/50 prior to \(\tfrac{1+\alpha}{2} : \tfrac{1-\alpha}{2}\) — and why does that shift grow as \(\alpha\) increases?
A buy order is more likely to occur when \(V=V_H\) than when \(V=V_L\), because an informed trader always buys when they know the value is high, adding to the noise-trader baseline of a 50/50 buy/sell split. The likelihood of a buy under \(V_H\) is \(\tfrac{1+\alpha}{2}\) and under \(V_L\) is \(\tfrac{1-\alpha}{2}\) — an asymmetry entirely caused by the informed traders. Bayes' theorem converts that asymmetric likelihood into an updated (posterior) belief, and because the size of the asymmetry between the two likelihoods is controlled directly by \(\alpha\), a larger \(\alpha\) means a buy order is stronger evidence for \(V_H\), producing a bigger belief shift. At \(\alpha=0\) there is no asymmetry at all (both likelihoods collapse to \(\tfrac12\)) and a buy order is uninformative — the posterior stays at 50/50.
Q3. Why is Kyle's Lambda higher in a thin/illiquid market than in a deep, liquid one, holding fundamental-value uncertainty fixed?
\(\lambda = \sigma_v/(2\sigma_u)\): with \(\sigma_v\) (value uncertainty) held fixed, \(\lambda\) is inversely proportional to \(\sigma_u\), the standard deviation of noise/liquidity trading volume — Kyle's proxy for how much value-unrelated order flow is available to "hide" informed trades inside and absorb size generally. A thin market has a small \(\sigma_u\): little ordinary trading volume to dilute or absorb any given order, so the market maker must assume a larger share of any order they see is potentially informed, and price a bigger concession accordingly. A deep market has a large \(\sigma_u\), diluting the informed share and letting size move through with much less price concession. Kyle's own term for \(1/\lambda\) is market depth — precisely "how much order flow it takes to move the price by one unit," which is small (shallow) exactly when \(\lambda\) is large.
Q4. What is the practical difference between the bid-ask spread (Glosten-Milgrom) and price impact (Kyle's Lambda) as sources of trading cost?
The spread is a roughly fixed, per-trade cost that you pay once by crossing from bid to ask (or ask to bid), largely independent of how large your order is, at least for small orders relative to typical depth. Price impact, \(\Delta p = \lambda Q\), is a cost that scales with the size of the order itself — it captures the fact that a bigger order moves the price further against you as you execute it, on top of whatever the quoted spread already costs. A small retail order mostly pays the spread; a large institutional order can pay far more in price impact than in spread, which is exactly why execution algorithms (TWAP/VWAP/implementation-shortfall) exist specifically to manage \(\lambda Q\) by slicing size over time, rather than to manage the spread, which slicing doesn't materially change.
Q5. In the worked example, the same 500-unit order produced $5 of impact in one session and $25 in another. What changed, and what didn't?
The order itself didn't change — same size, \(Q=500\), same asset, same fundamental-value uncertainty \(\sigma_v=2\). What changed was \(\sigma_u\), the depth of ordinary noise/liquidity trading available to absorb the order: 100 units in the "normal" session versus 20 units in the "thin" session, a fifth as much. Because \(\lambda=\sigma_v/(2\sigma_u)\) is inversely proportional to \(\sigma_u\), the thin session's \(\lambda\) is five times larger (0.05 versus 0.01), and since impact is \(\lambda\) times the (unchanged) order size, the impact is five times larger too ($25 versus $5). This is the formal mechanism behind the real-world scenario in this lesson: the same order costs more, structurally, when it lands in a thinner market, with nothing about the trader's decision needing to have been wrong.
Q6. Why does CLAUDE.md's rule to model extra slippage specifically on stop and breakout entries reflect Kyle's Lambda, rather than being an arbitrary conservative padding?
Stop and breakout entries fire, by design, during fast, directional price moves — exactly the moments when momentary liquidity tends to thin out (other participants pull quotes or step back rather than absorb a fast move) and when the market is most likely to be responding to genuine new information rather than routine noise trading. Both of those are precisely the conditions that raise \(\lambda\): lower \(\sigma_u\) (thinner momentary depth) and effectively higher perceived \(\sigma_v\)/informed-trading share (a fast move is more likely to be information-driven). So the same order size that would be cheap during calm, orderly trading costs more exactly at the instant a stop or breakout order triggers — which is exactly why the repo's fill discipline singles those entry types out for extra slippage rather than applying a flat cost model uniformly to every order type.
