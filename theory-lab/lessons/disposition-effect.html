Selling Winners, Holding Losers

The disposition effect: why you cash in what's working and keep what isn't, how it was measured in 10,000 real brokerage accounts, and how to measure it in your own last 20 trades.

CONCEPT — explained here; desk verdicts quoted where tested ~18 min read Prereqs: Why a Loss Hurts Twice as Much
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • The disposition effect (Shefrin and Statman, 1985) is the habit of selling winners too early and riding losers too long. It is the previous lesson's S-curve, acted out on a portfolio.
  • Terrance Odean (1998) measured it in about 10,000 accounts at a US discount broker (1987–1993). On days they sold, investors realised 14.8% of the gains available to them but only 9.8% of the losses: PGR 0.148 vs PLR 0.098.
  • It cost them. The winners they sold went on to beat the losers they kept by about 3.4 percentage points over the following year.
  • It is also tax-backwards: realised gains are taxed and realised losses can offset them, so tax logic says defer gains and take losses. In Odean's data the pattern reversed only in December, the tax-loss-selling month.
  • Two hands-on tools below: a portfolio exercise that computes your PGR and PLR and the tax bill, and a self-audit of your last 20 closed trades.
  • Desk verdict: untested. No ledger entry measures the desk's own trades this way.
Section 01

Why this matters

The previous lesson drew the value curve: concave for gains, so you lock them in; convex and steeper for losses, so you gamble rather than book them. That was about single choices. This lesson is about what the curve does to a whole portfolio over months: it leaves you holding a collection of your worst ideas while your best ones have been sold.

It also matters because it is one of the best-measured biases in finance. It has been found in real brokerage records, in other countries, in laboratory markets and among professionals. You do not have to take anyone's word for whether you have it. By the end of this page you can measure it in your own trades with two numbers.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Selling a winner feels like being right. Selling a loser feels like admitting you were wrong.

When you sell a stock for a profit, the trade becomes a story with a happy ending: you were right, and the gain is now safe. When you sell for a loss, it becomes a story with an unhappy ending, and the regret is real and immediate. Holding the loser keeps the story open. Maybe it comes back. Hersh Shefrin and Meir Statman, who named the effect in 1985, explained it with exactly these ingredients: the prospect-theory curve, mental accounting (each position is its own little account you want to close in the black), regret aversion, and the difficulty of self-control.

The market does not know or care what price you paid. Whether you should hold a stock tomorrow depends on its future, not on your entry. The disposition effect is what happens when your entry price gets a vote anyway.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

You have a box of toys to swap. Some are now worth more than you paid, some less. You keep swapping away the good ones because swapping feels like winning, and you keep the broken ones because giving them up would mean admitting you made a bad swap. After a while your box is full of broken toys.

Section 02

How to measure it: PGR and PLR

A naive test would count how many winners and losers people sell. That fails: in a rising market most positions are winners, so people sell more winners for no psychological reason at all. Odean's fix was to compare what was sold with what could have been sold on the same day.

On each day an investor sells anything, every position in the account falls into one of four boxes, measured against its purchase price:

✓

Realised gain (RG)

A position above its buy price that was sold that day.

⏸

Paper gain (PG)

A position above its buy price that was not sold that day.

✗

Realised loss (RL)

A position below its buy price that was sold that day.

⏸

Paper loss (PL)

A position below its buy price that was not sold that day.

Add up the counts over every sale day, then compute two proportions:

\[ \text{PGR} = \frac{RG}{RG + PG} \qquad\qquad \text{PLR} = \frac{RL}{RL + PL} \]
PGRproportion of gains realised: of the winners you could have sold, the share you did
PLRproportion of losses realised: of the losers you could have sold, the share you did
PGR
How readily you bank gains
Odean's investors: 0.148
PLR
How readily you take losses
Odean's investors: 0.098
PGR − PLR
The disposition gap
Above zero: you sell winners more readily than losers

If your entry price did not affect your selling, PGR and PLR would be about equal. A gap of 0.148 against 0.098 means a winner was roughly 1.5 times as likely to be sold as a loser on any given sale day.

\(RG\)Realised gains: count of winning positions sold on sale days.
\(PG\)Paper gains: count of winning positions held on sale days.
\(RL\)Realised losses: count of losing positions sold on sale days.
\(PL\)Paper losses: count of losing positions held on sale days.
\(\text{PGR}\)Proportion of gains realised, \(RG/(RG+PG)\).
\(\text{PLR}\)Proportion of losses realised, \(RL/(RL+PL)\).
\(t\)Tax rate on realised gains (illustrative, used in the exercise below).

Worked example

The instinctive sale versus the tax-aware sale

1

Instinct: sell the two big winners

Alder (+$1,200) and Cedar (+$1,200) raise $11,400. Realised P&L +$2,400; at 20% the tax bill is $480. Three winners were available and two were sold: PGR = 2/3 = 0.67. Three losers were available and none was sold: PLR = 0/3 = 0.

2

Tax-aware: sell the two biggest losers

Birch (−$2,000) and Delta (−$1,200) raise $9,800. Realised P&L −$3,200, which at 20% is worth up to $640 of tax saved against gains elsewhere. PGR = 0, PLR = 2/3.

3

The difference

Same cash need, and the portfolio's total value is identical either way, but the tax outcome is $1,120 apart. And you now own the opposite set of stocks. Odean's data says which set tends to do better next: the winners.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

When you sell something for more than you paid, the tax collector takes a slice. When you sell for less, the tax collector gives you a little credit. So if you have to sell something anyway, selling the loser is usually cheaper, even though it feels worse.

Section 04

It actually happened: 10,000 accounts, seven years

It actually happened

Odean's brokerage records, 1987–1993

In the 1990s Terrance Odean obtained the trading records of 10,000 randomly selected accounts at a large US discount brokerage, covering 1987 to 1993. These were real investors trading their own money, with no advice from the broker. His 1998 paper, Are Investors Reluctant to Realize Their Losses?, applied the PGR/PLR method to every sale day in the data.

Across the year the answer was clear: PGR 0.148 against PLR 0.098. Investors realised gains about one and a half times as readily as losses. Odean then checked the obvious innocent explanations. Rebalancing (trimming a position that has grown too big) did not account for it, and neither did the higher trading costs of low-priced shares. The pattern held up.

The one month it reversed was December, when losses were realised more readily than gains. December is when US investors sell losers to cut their tax bill. In other words, people knew losses were worth taking. They just needed a deadline to overcome the reluctance.

The cost: a natural defence is "I sold the winner because it had run its course, and kept the loser because it was due a bounce." Odean tested it. Over the following year the winners investors sold beat the losers they kept by about 3.4 percentage points (in returns above the market). The belief that losers would bounce back was not borne out by what happened next. These investors would have done better doing the exact opposite of their instinct.

It is not just one dataset

Mark Grinblatt and Matti Keloharju (2001) found the same reluctance to realise losses in Finland's complete national shareholding records. Martin Weber and Colin Camerer (1998) produced it in a laboratory market where the "stocks" were designed so that a past rise made a further rise more likely, not less, so the behaviour cannot be a sensible bet on reversal. And it reaches prices: Andrea Frazzini (2006) used mutual-fund holdings to estimate the gains and losses sitting in each stock, and found that prices under-react to news when holders are sitting on gains (they sell into good news too early) or losses (they refuse to sell into bad news). Grinblatt and Bing Han (2005) linked the same mechanism to momentum in stock returns.

Where the textbook version breaks

Not every sale of a winner is the bias. Trimming a position that has grown to an oversized share of the book is risk management, and a profit target set before entry is a plan, not a feeling. PGR and PLR measure a tendency across many decisions; they cannot tell you whether any one sale was right. And for a trader who holds one position at a time, there are no paper gains or losses to count on the day of a sale. For that trader the fingerprint is different: losers held longer than winners. Section 06

How practitioners deal with it

📋

Exits written at entry

A stop and a target (or a trailing rule) decided while flat. The exit is then a plan being carried out, not a judgement made while feeling the P&L.

🔄

The "fresh eyes" test

For each holding: if I had this much cash instead, would I buy this today? A "no" is a sell, whatever the entry price.

🧾

Tax-loss harvesting

Deliberately realising losses to offset gains, in the spirit of George Constantinides's (1983) result that tax-optimal trading takes losses and defers gains. Check local wash-sale rules.

📊

Measure, don't guess

Run the self-audit every quarter. Professionals review the exits, not just the entries. Most disposition is invisible until it is counted.

🎯
Real-world trading scenario

The swing trader with a lopsided payoff

A trader's journal shows 60% winners, which feels like a good strategy, but the account is flat over the year. The audit explains it: winners were held 3 days on average, losers 9, and the average loss was about 1.5 times the average win. At 60% wins and a 1.5× loss size, the expected result per trade is \(0.6 \times 1 - 0.4 \times 1.5 = 0\). The edge is real; the disposition effect is giving all of it back. The fix is not a new signal. It is a written exit rule applied to both sides.

⚠️
Common pitfalls
  • Counting sales instead of opportunities. "I sold 12 winners and 8 losers" says nothing in a rising market. Compare with what you could have sold: that is the whole point of PGR and PLR.
  • Over-correcting. Dumping every loser instantly is not the cure; following a pre-set exit rule is.
  • Anchoring on the entry when averaging in. If you bought at three prices, which one is "the" entry? Your brain picks the one that tells the nicest story. Use the average cost, and better still, ignore it and use the fresh-eyes test.
  • Treating tax as an afterthought. The tax difference between selling winners and selling losers is real money, every year, and it compounds.

Key Takeaways

✓The disposition effect is the S-curve on a portfolio. Bank gains (concave), avoid booking losses (convex, steeper): Shefrin and Statman, 1985.
✓Measure it with PGR and PLR. Odean's 10,000 accounts: 0.148 vs 0.098, a winner about 1.5 times as likely to be sold as a loser.
✗It costs money twice. The winners sold beat the losers kept by about 3.4 percentage points the next year, and selling gains first pays tax that selling losses would have saved.
✓December shows it is a choice. Faced with a tax deadline, the same investors took losses readily.
✓Audit your own exits. PGR vs PLR if you hold several positions; days held for winners vs losers if you hold one at a time.

Section 08

Self-test

Q1. Why is "I sold more winners than losers last year" not evidence of the disposition effect?
In a rising market most positions are winners, so you would sell more winners even at random. PGR and PLR divide by what was available to sell on each sale day, so they compare like with like.
Q2. On a sale day you hold 5 winners and 3 losers and sell 2 winners and 0 losers. What are PGR and PLR for that day?
RG = 2, PG = 3, so PGR = 2/5 = 0.40. RL = 0, PL = 3, so PLR = 0/3 = 0. A strong disposition-style day.
Q3. What did Odean find about the stocks investors sold versus the ones they kept?
Over the following year the winners they sold outperformed the losers they kept by about 3.4 percentage points in returns above the market. The usual justification, that losers were due a bounce, was not supported.
Q4. Why is the disposition effect tax-inefficient, and what does the December result add?
Realised gains are taxed and realised losses can offset them, so tax logic says defer gains and take losses: the opposite of the bias. In December, when the tax deadline approached, Odean's investors realised losses more readily than gains, showing they could take losses when the incentive was salient.
Q5. You only ever hold one position at a time. How do you check yourself for the disposition effect?
PGR and PLR need other open positions on the sale day, so they do not apply. Compare the average holding time of losing trades with winning trades (and average loss size with average win size). Losers held clearly longer than winners is the fingerprint.
Q6. What is this desk's verdict on "cut losers, let winners run"?
Untested. The ledger has no entry on how the desk's own trades are closed. The published evidence is strong, but it is about investors in those studies, not a measured result on this desk.
