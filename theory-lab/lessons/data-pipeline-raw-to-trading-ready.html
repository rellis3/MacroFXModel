The Data Pipeline: Raw Feeds to Trading-Ready Data

A raw vendor feed and a backtest-ready dataset are not the same object — this is the map of every stage a tick has to pass through in between, and why the most common bar-construction choice is quietly the statistically weakest one.

TL;DR

🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

I download prices from a vendor and backtest on them. What's wrong with that?

Nothing, if six things already happened in between. A raw feed is groceries off the truck, not a plated meal.

Six things?

Capture the feed exactly as it arrived. Check it: missing fields, gaps, impossible prices. Clean what the checks flagged. Adjust for splits, dividends and futures rolls. Store one versioned copy everyone uses. And finally, when someone asks about a past date, only hand out what was knowable on that date.

Why not fix bad ticks the moment they arrive?

Because then you lose the original record. You can never check your fix, or show what the feed really said. Capture first, fix later, in a separate step you can audit.

Where do bars come in?

That is a choice hiding inside capture. Most people close a bar every minute. That is a time clock. You can also close a bar every so many trades, or every so much volume, or every so many dollars traded.

Does it matter which?

Take an hour with a data release in it. About four thousand of the hour's four and a half thousand ticks land in five minutes around the release. That is eighty-nine percent of the activity. One-minute bars give those five minutes just five bars out of sixty. A volume clock tuned to sixty bars an hour gives them about fifty.

So time bars blur the busiest part.

And over-sample the quiet parts. Returns from volume or dollar bars tend to sit closer to the well-behaved shape most statistical tools assume. Time bars aren't wrong, they're just the weaker default.

Has the raw record itself ever changed?

The Flash Crash, sixth of May, twenty ten. The Dow fell close to a thousand points in minutes. Accenture traded at one cent. After the close, the venues cancelled over twenty thousand trades that were more than sixty percent away from the twenty to three price. So there are two versions of that afternoon: the raw tape and the corrected one.

And my dataset holds which one?

That's the point. It depends on when it was built and how. A pipeline that kept the raw prints and applied the cancellations as a dated step can tell you. One that didn't, can't.

One line to remember?

Capture everything as it arrived, correct it in separate steps you can audit, and choose your bar clock on purpose.

Section 01

Why this matters

Every lesson elsewhere in this Theory Lab that talks about a feature, a signal, or a backtest quietly assumes something upstream: that the price series feeding it is complete, correctly timestamped, free of corporate-action jumps, and dated to when the information was actually knowable. Feature Engineering, Leakage & Reproducibility is explicit about this boundary — it covers what it takes to build a feature honestly from data that is already clean. This category is the layer underneath that: what it takes to get from a raw vendor feed to a dataset that is even eligible to have a feature built on it in the first place. Get this layer wrong and no amount of discipline at the feature stage rescues the result — a leak, a gap, or a stale value baked into the raw series propagates into every feature and every backtest built downstream of it, silently.

This lesson is the map of the whole pipeline. Each stage after ingestion gets its own dedicated lesson later in this category, so treat this one as the table of contents: read it end to end once, then come back to the specific stage lesson when that's the problem in front of you.

💡
Plain-English intuition

A kitchen line, not a vending machine

A raw market data feed is closer to a delivery of unwashed vegetables and uncut meat than to a finished meal. Nobody eats straight off the delivery truck. Between "raw ingredients arrive" and "plated dish goes to the table," a real kitchen has a sequence of stations: check the delivery against the order and reject anything spoiled (validation), wash and trim (cleaning), portion and standardize cuts so every dish uses the same units (normalization), store what isn't used immediately at the right temperature (storage), and only pull from what's actually ready when an order comes in — never serve today's dish using an ingredient substitution decided next week (point-in-time serving). A trading data pipeline has the exact same shape, and skipping a station doesn't make the meal arrive faster — it makes a customer sick two stages later, usually somewhere much harder to trace back to the missed step.

Section 02

The six pipeline stages, in order

A raw feed passes through six stages before it is "trading-ready" — usable to build a feature, run a backtest, or drive a live signal. Each stage exists to close a specific way the previous ones can still leave the data unfit to use.

1

Ingestion — raw ticks, quotes, and trades land as-is

The exchange or vendor feed delivers raw ticks (individual trade prints), quotes (bid/ask updates), or both, exactly as transmitted, with no correction applied yet. This stage's only job is capture: get every message that arrived, in the order and with the timestamp it arrived, onto durable storage. Nothing gets fixed here — fixing anything before it's captured risks losing the original record of what the feed actually said, which is the only ground truth a later validation or cleaning step can be checked against.

2

Validation — schema checks, gap detection, sanity bounds

Before anything is trusted, it's checked structurally: does every record have the fields it's supposed to (a schema check), are there suspicious gaps in the timestamp sequence (a session that should have continuous quotes but goes quiet for an hour), and does every price fall inside a plausible bound for that instrument (a sanity check that catches a feed error before it's ever treated as a real price). Validation doesn't try to fix anything — it flags what looks wrong so the next stage knows what needs attention.

3

Cleaning — fixing what validation flagged

Bad ticks, stale quotes, halts, duplicate or out-of-order timestamps, and missing values all get handled here, using a specific, defensible method for each failure mode — never a blanket rule. This stage is large enough, and easy enough to get subtly wrong, that it has its own lesson: Data Cleaning: Outliers, Bad Ticks & Missing Data.

4

Normalization — adjusting for corporate actions and contract rolls

A raw price series is not directly comparable across a stock split, a dividend, or (for futures) a contract roll — each of those events puts a fake, non-tradeable jump in the raw series that a naive backtest would misread as a real return. Normalization is the step that produces a continuous, tradeable-return series across those events. This is its own lesson too: Corporate Actions & Series Continuity.

5

Storage — where the cleaned, normalized series actually lives

Cleaned, normalized data needs a storage format and architecture that keeps a single, versioned source of truth — not five teams independently recomputing "the adjusted close" and slowly drifting apart. Storage format choices (time-series-oriented vs. general columnar), lineage, and schema versioning are covered in Data Architecture for Systematic Trading.

6

Point-in-time serving — only handing out what was actually knowable then

The final stage answers a question that's easy to get wrong even with perfectly clean, correctly normalized data sitting in a well-architected store: for a query "as of date X," does the answer reflect only what was actually known on date X, or does it quietly include a later revision, a later restatement, or an instrument that only exists in today's universe because it survived? This is the deepest and most commonly mishandled stage, covered fully in Point-in-Time Data & Survivorship Bias.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Think of a raw data feed like a delivery of groceries dumped straight off the truck. First you write down exactly what arrived, unopened (ingestion). Then you check nothing's obviously wrong — no torn bags, no missing items (validation). Then you actually wash, trim, and fix anything bad (cleaning). Then you cut everything into the same standard sizes so recipes work the same way every time (normalization). Then you put it away somewhere organized, not just piled on the counter (storage). And finally, when someone asks "what did we have in the fridge on Tuesday," you answer using only what was actually in the fridge on Tuesday — not something you bought yesterday (point-in-time serving).

Section 03

Bar construction: how raw ticks become the bars a strategy sees

Watch the rule build
How much activity one bar carries, under each clock
\[ \text{ticks per bar} = \color{#fbbf24}{\frac{\text{ticks in a window}}{\text{bars in that window}}} \]

The question that separates the clocks: how much real trading sits inside one bar? Use the worked example below: one hour, about 4,500 ticks.

\[ \text{release, 1-min bars:}\quad \frac{4{,}000}{5} = \color{#fbbf24}{800} \]

The five minutes around the release hold about 4,000 ticks, and a one-minute clock gives them 5 bars. Each bar is stuffed with about 800 ticks.

\[ \text{quiet stretch, 1-min bars:}\quad \frac{300}{40} = \color{#fbbf24}{7.5} \]

The last 40 minutes hold about 300 ticks across 40 bars. Same clock, about a hundred times less activity per bar.

\[ \text{volume clock:}\quad \frac{4{,}500 \text{ ticks}}{60 \text{ bars}} = \color{#fbbf24}{75 \text{ ticks per bar}} \]

Now fix the activity per bar instead of the time. Sized to give about 60 bars an hour, each bar closes after about 75 ticks, assuming volume roughly tracks tick count.

\[ \color{#10b981}{\text{release, volume bars:}\quad \frac{4{,}000}{75} \approx 53 \text{ bars}} \]

The burst now gets about 53 of the hour's bars, in line with the worked example's 50 to 55. The clock speeds up when trading does, so every bar carries a comparable slice of activity.

Alongside ingestion sits one foundational choice (the bars themselves are best built from ticks that have already been validated and cleaned, so a bad print never lands in a bar's high or low): how are individual ticks grouped into the discrete "bars" (open/high/low/close observations) that everything downstream — a chart, a feature, a backtest — actually operates on? Marcos López de Prado's Advances in Financial Machine Learning (Wiley, 2018), Chapter 2, lays out four standard sampling clocks for this, each grouping ticks by a different criterion reaching a threshold:

🕒

Time bars

Close a bar every fixed wall-clock interval (every minute, every hour) regardless of how much trading happened inside it.

🔢

Tick bars

Close a bar after a fixed number of individual trade prints, however long or short that takes in wall-clock time.

📦

Volume bars

Close a bar once a fixed number of shares/contracts/units has traded, so a bar represents a constant amount of activity.

💵

Dollar bars

Close a bar once a fixed notional value has traded (price × volume), which stays comparable even as an instrument's price level itself drifts over years.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

A "bar" is just one snapshot of price activity — like one photo in a flipbook. The question is what triggers the camera to click. Time bars click the shutter every fixed number of minutes, no matter what's happening. Tick bars click after a fixed number of trades occur. Volume bars click once a fixed amount of stuff has traded. Dollar bars click once a fixed amount of money has traded. Same underlying action, four different rules for when to take the next picture.

Time bars are the default almost everyone starts with — they're the most intuitive and the easiest to build from a raw feed. They also have a specific, well-documented statistical weakness. Trading activity is not smooth across a session: it clusters around the open, the close, and news releases, and thins out during quiet stretches. A fixed wall-clock sampling interval doesn't know this — it samples the same way regardless. The result is that a time bar during a quiet period is built from very few underlying ticks (oversampling a period with little genuine information turnover), while a time bar during a burst of activity is built from a huge number of ticks compressed into the same fixed window (undersampling a period where a great deal actually happened). Volume bars and dollar bars sample by activity itself rather than by the clock, so each bar represents a comparable amount of genuine information turnover — which is why López de Prado's Chapter 2 argues, citing earlier empirical work such as Ané & Geman (2000), that returns built from volume/dollar bars sit closer to independent and identically distributed (IID) and closer to Gaussian than returns built from time bars sampled at a comparable average frequency. This matters directly for anything built downstream: many statistical tools (a lot of the parametric machinery covered elsewhere in this Theory Lab) assume something close to IID, roughly-normal returns, and feeding them time-bar returns is quietly handing them a worse-behaved input than the same underlying data would have produced sampled a different way.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine counting how many people walk into a store by checking the door every 5 minutes, no matter what. On a slow Tuesday morning, those 5-minute checks are basically fine. But during a Black Friday doorbuster rush, hundreds of people might pile through in those same 5 minutes — your once-every-5-minutes counting method can't tell the difference between "quiet" and "mobbed," because it's watching the clock instead of watching the door. Volume and dollar bars watch the door instead — they take a new snapshot every time a fixed amount of actual activity has happened, so a rush gets counted properly as a rush.

The trap: time bars are not "wrong," and most of the industry still runs on them for good practical reasons (simplicity, universal tooling, human-readable intervals) — but choosing them without knowing why volume/dollar bars exist means silently accepting a statistically worse-behaved return series, every single day, for every instrument sampled this way.

Worked example

Same hour, two sampling clocks, very different bar counts

Predict first. The hour below has about 200 ticks in a 15-minute lull, about 4,000 in the 5 minutes around a data release, and about 300 in the last 40 minutes. What share of the hour's ticks lands in those 5 release minutes? Lock in a guess, then check it against the time-bar paragraph below.

About 89%. \(4{,}000 / 4{,}500 \approx 0.89\). Yet a one-minute clock gives those minutes 5 of its 60 bars, about 8%. Read on to see how a volume clock hands the same burst 50 to 55 bars instead.

Consider one trading hour in a liquid FX pair that includes a scheduled data release 20 minutes in. Suppose the raw tick feed shows roughly 200 ticks in the 15 minutes before the release, a burst of roughly 4,000 ticks in the 5 minutes around the release itself, and roughly 300 ticks in the remaining 40 minutes — a realistic activity-clustering pattern.

Time bars, 1-minute clock. This produces exactly 60 bars for the hour, no matter what. The 5 minutes around the release get exactly 5 bars — the same number of observations as any other 5 quiet minutes — even though those 5 minutes contained roughly 4,000 of the hour's approximately 4,500 total ticks (about 89% of the hour's entire information content compressed into 5 of 60 bars, or about 8% of the bar count).

Volume bars, sized to produce roughly 60 bars/hour on average. Using a threshold tuned so a typical hour produces about 60 bars, the pre-release lull (200 ticks in 15 minutes) might only close 2-3 bars, while the release burst (4,000 ticks in 5 minutes) closes roughly 50-55 bars (assuming volume roughly tracks tick count) — the sampling clock speeds up exactly when genuine activity speeds up, so each bar keeps representing a comparable slice of actual information turnover instead of a comparable slice of the clock.

The time-bar version has quietly under-represented the single most informative 5 minutes of the hour, and over-represented the quiet stretches either side of it — exactly the oversampling/undersampling pattern this section describes, made concrete with tick counts.

🎯
A real-world trading scenario

The volatility-clustering model that "worked" better once the bars changed, not the model

A research team builds a short-horizon volatility model on 5-minute time bars for a currency pair. The residuals from the model come back visibly fat-tailed and autocorrelated in a way that doesn't match what the model's assumptions expect, and several rounds of re-specifying the model (adding lags, trying a different volatility estimator) only partially help. Eventually the team reruns the exact same model specification, unchanged, on dollar bars sized to produce a comparable average bar count per session. The residual diagnostics improve meaningfully — not because the model got better, but because the input return series is now closer to the well-behaved, activity-normalized series the model's assumptions were implicitly built for. The lesson isn't "dollar bars fix everything" — it's that a model's poor fit can sometimes be a data-representation problem hiding as a model-specification problem, and bar choice is one of the first places to check before adding more complexity to the model itself.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

The team kept trying to fix their model, but the model wasn't actually broken — the ruler they were measuring with was. Once they switched to a ruler (dollar bars) that stretched and shrank with how busy the market actually was, instead of a ruler that just marked off equal chunks of time, the same model suddenly measured things much more sensibly. Sometimes the fix isn't a smarter model — it's a fairer way of chopping up the data you feed it.

Section 04

It actually happened: the afternoon the official tape changed after the close

The six-stage map treats the raw record as fixed the moment it is captured, and validation as a quiet back-office step that flags problems for later. On one afternoon in 2010 validation was a live market actor, and the authoritative record of what traded was rewritten hours after the fact.

It actually happened

6 May 2010: the Flash Crash

The afternoon was already nervous, with European debt worries in the headlines. According to the joint SEC–CFTC staff report published that September, a large fund began selling 75,000 E-mini S&P 500 futures contracts, worth about $4.1 billion, through an algorithm that targeted a share of trading volume without regard to price or time. Liquidity thinned, and within minutes the Dow Jones Industrial Average fell close to 1,000 points, about 9%, before recovering most of the drop in roughly half an hour.

The report also described what happened inside trading firms' own pipelines. Many liquidity providers ran automated integrity checks on the data they were receiving; when prices and feeds stopped looking consistent, those checks paused their trading, and their quotes left the market. With real bids gone, market orders reached placeholder "stub" quotes far from any sensible price. Accenture, a large and liquid stock, traded at one cent.

After the close the exchanges and FINRA cancelled trades executed more than 60% away from each stock's price at 2:40 p.m.: over 20,000 trades in more than 300 securities. So two versions of 6 May exist: the raw tape as it was printed, and the corrected tape with those trades removed. Single-stock circuit breakers and a ban on stub quotes followed later that year. Which version of the afternoon a dataset holds today depends on when it was built and whether its pipeline applied the cancellations as a separate, dated step.

Where the textbook version breaks

The map assumes the stages are passive and in order: capture fixes the truth, and validation and cleaning only ever look backwards at it. On 6 May both assumptions failed. Firms' validation stages were part of the market, and their pauses helped remove the liquidity whose absence made the prints extreme. And the cleaning stage needed information, the venues' cancellation decisions, that arrived after the close, so "clean as of the close" and "clean as of the next morning" are different datasets. A one-minute time bar built from the raw tape for that afternoon contains a range no one could have traded at the size a backtest assumes.

Not tested here Not tested here: that the historical bars this desk backtests on match what its live feed showed at the time. The ledger has no entry on data-pipeline parity.
How to test it: For four to eight weeks, store the live feed's M1 bars for the desk's FX pairs as they form. Days later, fetch the same windows from the historical source and count the bars whose open, high, low or close differ, with the size of each difference in pips. The gate, fixed in advance, is that fewer than 1% of bars differ by more than half a pip, and that rerunning one short-horizon backtest on each copy moves its headline metric by less than its own confidence interval. The baseline is the historical copy itself, and no trading costs enter, since this checks the data and not a trade.
Section 05

How practitioners actually use this

Institutional data-engineering teams treat the pipeline in this lesson as infrastructure, not a one-time script: ingestion runs continuously and append-only, validation alerts are monitored like any other production system health check, and the choice of bar construction is usually made once, deliberately, per research use case, rather than re-decided ad hoc for every new study. Many desks maintain time bars as the default "human-readable" view for charts and monitoring while maintaining volume- or dollar-bar series in parallel specifically for statistical modeling work, precisely because the two serve different purposes — a person scanning a chart wants a fixed, familiar time axis; a model consuming returns wants the better-behaved statistical properties. Neither is fully correct or fully wrong in isolation.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where a pipeline quietly breaks down

  • Treating "I have a chart" as "I have trading-ready data." A chart tolerates far more sloppiness than a backtest — a chart renderer silently interpolates over gaps and rarely flags a stale quote, so a series that looks fine on a chart can still fail every later stage in this pipeline.
  • Fixing problems at the ingestion stage instead of just capturing them. Correcting a suspicious tick before it's durably stored destroys the original record needed to audit that correction later — capture first, fix in a separate, logged stage.
  • Assuming time bars are statistically neutral because they're the most common choice. Popularity isn't evidence of statistical soundness — the oversampling/undersampling effect is real and documented, not a matter of opinion.
  • Never revisiting the bar-construction choice once made. A bar scheme picked for one research question (a slow macro-driven strategy) may be a poor fit for a different one (a short-horizon microstructure study) built later on the same infrastructure.
  • Skipping straight to modeling without confirming each pipeline stage actually ran. A dataset that "looks clean" by eye has not necessarily passed explicit validation, cleaning, and normalization steps — verify the stages happened, don't assume it from the data's appearance.
Scenario drill · 6 May 2010

Your backtest loves the Flash Crash afternoon

You are building one-minute bars for US stocks to backtest a buy-the-dip strategy. Your vendor's tape for 6 May 2010 shows Accenture trading at one cent in the afternoon, and the backtest books a spectacular profit buying it there. You know the venues later cancelled many trades from that afternoon. What do you do with the data?

  1. Keep the raw prints in the bars. They really printed, so they are real data.
    They printed, and many were then cancelled. A bar built from them has a low no one could have traded at the size a backtest assumes. The profit is an artefact of the data, not of the strategy.
  2. Delete the whole day from the dataset so it can't distort anything.
    Removing a stressed day quietly makes the backtest look calmer than history was. The strategy's behaviour on a day like that is exactly what you need to see, on a version of the tape that reflects tradeable prices.
  3. Keep the raw capture, apply the venues' cancellations as a separate, dated cleaning step, build bars from the corrected tape, and record which version the backtest used.
    Capture first, correct in a logged step. You can serve either version, explain the difference, and the backtest no longer books fills at prices that were cancelled.
  4. Swap in a vendor's already-corrected tape and discard the raw one.
    The bars will be closer to tradeable, but you lose the record of what a live system would have seen that afternoon, and you can't audit how the correction was made.

Section 07

Self-test

Q1. Name the six pipeline stages in order and state, in one sentence each, what each stage is responsible for.
Ingestion captures raw ticks/quotes/trades exactly as transmitted. Validation runs structural checks — schema, gap detection, sanity bounds — flagging but not fixing problems. Cleaning fixes what validation flagged (bad ticks, stale quotes, halts, duplicates, missing data), using a defensible method per failure mode. Normalization adjusts for corporate actions and futures rolls so the series becomes continuous and tradeable-return-consistent. Storage keeps the cleaned, normalized data in a versioned, single-source-of-truth architecture. Point-in-time serving answers "as of date X" queries using only what was genuinely knowable at that date, with no later revision or survivorship creeping in.
Q2. Why is fixing a suspicious record during ingestion itself considered a mistake, even if the fix is correct?
Ingestion's only job is to durably capture what the feed actually said. If a correction is applied before that capture happens, the original record — the only ground truth a later validation or cleaning step could be audited against — is lost. Even a technically correct fix applied at this stage removes the ability to later verify what was actually flagged, why, and whether the fix itself was reasonable; cleaning belongs in its own separate, logged stage precisely so that audit trail survives.
Q3. What are the four standard bar-construction sampling clocks, and what does each one hold fixed while a bar is being built?
Time bars hold a fixed wall-clock interval fixed (close a bar every minute/hour regardless of activity). Tick bars hold a fixed count of trade prints fixed. Volume bars hold a fixed traded quantity (shares/contracts) fixed. Dollar bars hold a fixed traded notional value (price × volume) fixed, staying comparable even as an instrument's price level drifts over long periods.
Q4. Explain, without using the word "IID," why a fixed-clock sampling scheme produces a statistically worse-behaved return series than an activity-based one.
Trading activity clusters unevenly through a session — bursts around news and the open/close, lulls in between. A fixed wall-clock interval samples at the same rate regardless of how much genuine activity is happening inside it, so a quiet period gets the same number of observations as a very active one, while an active period's huge amount of real information gets compressed into the same small number of bars a quiet period would produce. An activity-based clock (volume or dollar bars) speeds up exactly when genuine activity speeds up, so each bar keeps representing a comparable amount of real information turnover — which is why its returns behave more consistently from bar to bar than a fixed-clock scheme's returns do.
Q5. In the worked example, why did the 1-minute time-bar hour under-represent the release burst even though it used the same total ticks?
The time-bar scheme allocates a fixed number of bars per fixed time (5 bars for any 5 minutes), regardless of how many ticks arrive in that window. The release burst packed roughly 89% of the hour's total tick count into the same 5 bars a quiet 5-minute stretch would also receive, so those 5 bars had to compress vastly more information into the same observation count as any other 5 bars in the hour — the opposite of what an activity-based clock like volume bars would do, where a burst simply produces far more bars in the same wall-clock span.

Further reading
  • Marcos López de Prado, Advances in Financial Machine Learning (Wiley, 2018) — Chapter 2, "Financial Data Structures," is the primary source for the time/tick/volume/dollar bar taxonomy and the argument for why activity-based bars produce statistically better-behaved returns than time bars.
  • David Easley, Marcos López de Prado & Maureen O'Hara, "The Volume Clock: Insights into the High-Frequency Paradigm," Journal of Portfolio Management, 39(1), 2012 — the original applied argument for sampling by volume/activity rather than by wall-clock time in high-frequency microstructure research.
  • Robert F. Engle & Jeffrey R. Russell, "Autoregressive Conditional Duration: A New Model for Irregularly Spaced Transaction Data," Econometrica, 66(5), 1998 — foundational work on modeling the irregular, clustered timing of transactions directly, the statistical phenomenon that motivates activity-based bar sampling in the first place.
