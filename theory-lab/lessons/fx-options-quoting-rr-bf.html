FX Options Quoting: Delta, Risk Reversals & Butterflies

Why an FX options screen shows volatilities instead of prices and deltas instead of strikes, how three numbers per expiry rebuild a whole smile, and how to read a risk reversal as the market paying up for one side.

CONCEPT · how the market quotes FX options, not a trading signal ~18 min read Prereqs: Black-Scholes-Merton, Interest Rate Parity
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • FX options trade in implied volatility, not money. Both sides agree a vol, then the Garman–Kohlhagen formula (Black-Scholes with two interest rates) turns it into a premium. A vol quote survives a moving spot rate; a price does not.
  • Strikes are named by delta. A "25-delta call" is the strike whose delta is 0.25. At the money usually means the delta-neutral straddle (DNS) strike, where a call and a put have equal and opposite deltas, not spot or the forward.
  • Each expiry is quoted as three numbers: the ATM vol (level), the 25-delta risk reversal (call vol minus put vol: the tilt) and the 25-delta butterfly (how far the wings sit above ATM: the curvature). Then \(\sigma_{25C} \approx \sigma_{ATM} + \sigma_{BF} + \tfrac12\sigma_{RR}\) and \(\sigma_{25P} \approx \sigma_{ATM} + \sigma_{BF} - \tfrac12\sigma_{RR}\). The same holds at 10 delta.
  • That formula is exact only for the smile's own strangle. The quoted butterfly is a "market strangle" priced at one vol, and the two drift apart when the risk reversal is large: in a published USD/JPY example (January 2009, RR −5.3) the quoted fly was 0.184 vol but the smile's own was 0.419.
  • Delta itself depends on the pair: spot or forward delta, and premium-included ("premium-adjusted") when the premium is paid in the base currency, as in USD/JPY and EUR/JPY but not EUR/USD or GBP/USD. Use the wrong one and every strike lands in the wrong place.
  • Real-world episode: before the June 2016 Brexit vote, the GBP/USD one-month 25-delta risk reversal went from about −1.3 on 25 May to −5.4 on 26 May, the day the one-month expiry first covered the vote, and to −7.9 by 10 June. On 24 June sterling fell from 1.4877 to 1.3622 against the dollar, about 8.4%.
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

I asked for a price on a one-month euro dollar option and the broker said twenty-one point six. That isn't a price.

It is, just not in money. FX options are quoted in implied volatility. Both sides agree the vol, then plug it into the currency version of Black-Scholes to get the premium.

Why not just quote the premium?

Because the premium changes every time spot ticks, and the vol barely does. A vol quote stays good while the market moves.

He didn't give me a strike either. He said twenty-five delta.

Same idea. Delta is a strike measured in risk. A twenty-five delta call is out of the money, far enough that it moves about a quarter as much as the currency. Name the strike by its delta and the name still means the same thing tomorrow.

So what is actually on the screen?

Three numbers per expiry. The at-the-money vol is the level. The risk reversal is the twenty-five delta call vol minus the put vol: the tilt. The butterfly is how far the two wings sit above the middle: the curvature.

Give me numbers.

At the money ten, risk reversal minus two, butterfly a half. Start at ten. Add the half to both wings, so ten and a half. Then split the risk reversal in two. The put gets one more, the call one less. Put eleven and a half, call nine and a half.

So minus two means the puts are dearer.

Right. The market is paying up for one side. A deep negative risk reversal says people want protection against the base currency falling, and will pay extra for it. It is the price of insurance, not a forecast.

Is that little formula exact?

Only for the smile's own strangle. The butterfly the broker quotes is defined through a strangle priced at a single vol. When the risk reversal is big, the two come apart. In one published dollar yen example from January two thousand and nine, the quoted butterfly was about point one eight, but the smile's own was about point four two.

And delta isn't even one thing, is it?

No. Dollar yen deltas include the premium, because the premium is paid in dollars, the base currency. Euro dollar deltas don't. Read the wrong convention and every strike lands in the wrong place.

Has a risk reversal ever told a real story?

The Brexit vote in June twenty sixteen. The one-month sterling risk reversal was about minus one point three on the twenty-fifth of May. The next day the one-month expiry first stretched past the vote, and it jumped to about minus five point four. By the tenth of June it was near minus eight. After the result, sterling fell more than eight percent in a day.

The one-line version?

Three numbers give you a smile: level, tilt and curvature. Check which delta and which at-the-money before you use them, and read the risk reversal as the price of fear, not a forecast.

Section 01

Why this matters

Open an FX options broker screen and you will not find a single premium. You will find rows of tenors (overnight, one week, one month, three months, a year) and, for each, a handful of numbers that look like percentages: an at-the-money volatility, a 25-delta risk reversal, a 25-delta butterfly, often the same pair at 10 delta. A client asking for "a one-month 25-delta EUR put" is also not naming a strike. Every one of those numbers is a coordinate in a quoting system that the foreign-exchange options market built so that thousands of quotes can stay valid while spot moves underneath them.

If you trade spot or forwards, this screen is still useful to you. The risk reversal is the cleanest public measure of which way the market is scared: how much more it pays for protection against a fall than for upside. The ATM term structure shows when the market expects trouble, tenor by tenor. Strategists quote both every day. But reading them correctly needs the conventions: which delta, which "at the money", which currency the premium is paid in, and what the butterfly quote actually means. Get those wrong and a perfectly good number gives you a wrong strike or a wrong vol.

This lesson builds the quoting system from the ground up: why vol, why delta, how ATM is defined, how risk reversals and butterflies rebuild a smile, where the standard shortcut is only approximate, which conventions apply to which pairs, and how to read a risk reversal and a term structure. It ends with a live calculator that turns three quotes into strikes under Garman–Kohlhagen, and the Brexit vote of 2016, when the sterling risk reversal told the story weeks in advance.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Quoting the recipe, not the cake

Bond traders quote yields, not prices, because a yield means the same thing for every bond and changes slowly, while prices jump around and depend on each bond's details. FX options traders do the same thing with volatility. An option's premium depends on spot, two interest rates, the strike and time, all of which move. Implied volatility is the one ingredient that is genuinely up for negotiation. So they agree the vol and let a standard formula bake the premium when the deal is done.

Delta does the same job for strikes. "The 1.3677 strike" means something different every time spot moves. "The 25-delta call" always means "the out-of-the-money call whose value moves about a quarter as much as the currency", wherever spot is. So the market quotes a grid of vols against deltas, and anyone can turn it back into strikes and premiums with the same formula.

And the three headline numbers? Think of the smile as a cheap rope held at three points. ATM sets the height of the middle. The butterfly lifts both ends together. The risk reversal tips it, one end up, the other down.

Step 1: the premium is a formula of the vol. A European FX option gives the right to buy (call) or sell (put) one unit of the base currency (the first in the pair, "foreign") for \(K\) units of the terms currency (the second, "domestic") at expiry. Garman and Kohlhagen (1983) showed it is priced exactly like Black-Scholes on a stock that pays a continuous dividend equal to the foreign interest rate:

\[ v = \phi\left[S\,e^{-r_f\tau}N(\phi d_+) - K\,e^{-r_d\tau}N(\phi d_-)\right],\qquad d_\pm = \frac{\ln(F/K) \pm \tfrac12\sigma^2\tau}{\sigma\sqrt{\tau}} \]
vpremium, in terms currency per 1 unit of base currency
φ+1 for a call on the base currency, −1 for a put
S, Fspot, and the forward F = S·e^((r_d − r_f)τ)
r_d, r_fterms (domestic) and base (foreign) interest rates
σ, τimplied vol, and time to expiry in years

Every input but \(\sigma\) is observable: spot is on the screen, the two deposit rates are on another screen, the strike and expiry are in the contract. The only thing left to negotiate is \(\sigma\), so that is what gets quoted. The forward \(F = S e^{(r_d-r_f)\tau}\) is covered interest parity, the subject of the Interest Rate Parity lesson: the high-yielding currency trades at a forward discount, and the option formula inherits that through \(F\).

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

An option's price is like a cake made from five ingredients. Four of them (the exchange rate, two interest rates and the time left) everyone can see on a screen. Only one, how jumpy people think the exchange rate will be, is a matter of opinion. So traders argue about that one ingredient and let the recipe make the cake.

Step 2: delta names the strike. Delta is how much the option's value moves per unit move in the exchange rate, in units of the base-currency notional. FX uses two versions. Spot delta is the hedge in the spot market; forward delta is the hedge in the forward market:

\[ \Delta_S = \phi\,e^{-r_f\tau}N(\phi d_+), \qquad \Delta_F = \phi\,N(\phi d_+) \]
Δ_Sspot delta: base-currency units to trade in spot per unit notional
Δ_Fforward delta: the same hedge done in the forward
e^(−r_f τ)discounts the base currency you would receive at expiry

Because \(N\) is invertible, a delta and a vol together pin down exactly one strike. Solve the spot-delta expression for \(d_+\), then for \(K\):

\[ K = F\exp\!\left(-\phi\,N^{-1}\!\left(\phi\,e^{r_f\tau}\Delta_S\right)\sigma\sqrt{\tau} + \tfrac12\sigma^2\tau\right) \]
N⁻¹inverse of the standard normal distribution function
φ e^(r_f τ) Δ_Sconverts the quoted spot delta back into N(φd₊)
Kthe strike that has this delta at this vol

For a forward delta drop the \(e^{r_f\tau}\). Notice that the vol appears in the strike formula. A "25-delta" strike at 22% vol is further from the forward than a 25-delta strike at 10%, because a jumpier currency needs a strike further away before the option moves only a quarter as much. That is why you cannot convert deltas to strikes until you know the vol at that delta, and why the smile is quoted in delta space in the first place.

Delta also carries the sign of the side: a 25-delta call has \(\Delta = +0.25\), a 25-delta put has \(\Delta = -0.25\). With forward deltas, a call and a put at the same strike always differ by exactly 1, so a 25-delta put is the same strike as a 75-delta call. That symmetry is why smile tables are often drawn on a forward-delta axis.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Instead of saying "the house at number 37", FX traders say "the house a quarter of the way to the edge of town". If the town grows or shrinks overnight, the second description still points to the same kind of house. A 25-delta option is always the one a quarter of the way out, however far that is today.

Step 3: what "at the money" means. Spot? The forward? In FX, for short-dated options, it is usually neither. The default is the delta-neutral straddle (DNS): the strike at which a call and a put have equal and opposite deltas, so a straddle (long both) has zero delta and is a pure bet on volatility. Setting \(N(d_+) = \tfrac12\) gives \(d_+ = 0\), so:

\[ K_{ATM}^{DNS} = F\,e^{\frac12\sigma_{ATM}^2\tau} \qquad\text{(regular delta)},\qquad K_{ATM}^{DNS} = F\,e^{-\frac12\sigma_{ATM}^2\tau}\qquad\text{(premium-adjusted delta)} \]
K_ATMthe ATM strike under the delta-neutral straddle convention
e^(±σ²τ/2)a small shift off the forward, a few pips at one month
σ_ATMthe quoted ATM vol, used to find its own strike

The shift is tiny at short tenors (at one month and 21.6% vol it moves the EUR/USD ATM strike about 26 pips above a forward of 1.3070) but grows with \(\sigma^2\tau\). Some markets quote ATM at the forward instead (\(K = F\), "ATMF"), which Reiswich and Wystup describe as very common for long maturities and for pairs with a large interest-rate differential, such as many emerging-market pairs.

Step 4: the risk reversal and the butterfly. Fix an expiry. Let \(\sigma_{25C}\) be the vol of the 25-delta call and \(\sigma_{25P}\) the vol of the 25-delta put (delta −0.25). The two non-ATM quotes are:

\[ \sigma_{RR} = \sigma_{25C} - \sigma_{25P}, \qquad \sigma_{BF} = \frac{\sigma_{25C} + \sigma_{25P}}{2} - \sigma_{ATM} \]
σ_RRrisk reversal: call vol minus put vol, the smile's tilt (skew)
σ_BFbutterfly (strangle): average wing vol over ATM, the curvature
σ_25C, σ_25Pvols of the 25-delta call and the −25-delta put

Two equations, two unknowns. Add and subtract them and the wing vols fall straight out:

\[ \sigma_{25C} = \sigma_{ATM} + \sigma_{BF} + \tfrac12\sigma_{RR}, \qquad \sigma_{25P} = \sigma_{ATM} + \sigma_{BF} - \tfrac12\sigma_{RR} \]

The same structure repeats at 10 delta: \(\sigma_{10C} = \sigma_{ATM} + \sigma_{10BF} + \tfrac12\sigma_{10RR}\) and \(\sigma_{10P} = \sigma_{ATM} + \sigma_{10BF} - \tfrac12\sigma_{10RR}\). The 10-delta quotes describe the far wings, where crash protection lives, and they are quoted separately because the shape near the middle does not tell you the shape far out. Five quotes per expiry (ATM, two RRs, two flies) give five anchor points.

Sign convention. A positive risk reversal means calls on the base currency trade at a higher vol than puts. Desks say a risk reversal is "in favour of" a side: "GBP/USD one-month 25-delta at 7.9 GBP puts" is a risk reversal of −7.9. In USD/JPY a negative number means yen calls (dollar puts) are bid.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Picture a seesaw with a spring under each end. The ATM vol is how high the middle sits. The butterfly pushes both springs up the same amount. The risk reversal tips the seesaw, one end up and one end down. Tell me those three numbers and I can tell you where both ends are.

Step 5: the honest caveat about the butterfly. The formula above is exact for the smile strangle, which is what \(\sigma_{BF}\) means in the definition: the average of the smile's two 25-delta vols minus ATM. But the butterfly a broker quotes is a market strangle (also called the broker or quoted strangle). It is defined by a trade: take the single vol \(\sigma_{ATM} + \sigma_{BF}^{quoted}\), find the 25-delta call and put strikes at that one vol, and price both options at that one vol. The quote says a correct smile must reprice that strangle to the same total premium, whatever vols it assigns to those two strikes.

When the risk reversal is near zero, the two definitions agree, and the shortcut is exact. When the risk reversal is large they drift apart, and a desk solves for the smile numerically (Reiswich and Wystup 2009; Clark 2011). Their published one-month example from 20 January 2009 shows how far apart they can be:

The trap: the quoted butterfly is not the smile's butterfly when the skew is big. USD/JPY one month, 20 January 2009: ATM 21.00%, 25-delta RR −5.3, quoted BF 0.184. Plugging the quoted fly into the shortcut gives 18.534% for the 25-delta call and 23.834% for the put. The smile that actually reprices the market strangle has 18.769% and 24.069%: a smile strangle of 0.419, more than double the quoted 0.184, and both wings about 0.24 vol higher. For EUR/USD on the same day (RR only −0.5) the two strangles were 0.7375 and 0.7377, effectively identical.

Section 03

Which delta? Spot, forward and premium-included conventions

So far delta has been the textbook kind: how many units of base currency to trade to hedge. FX adds one more wrinkle. The premium is a sum of money, and in some pairs it is paid in the base currency. Reiswich and Wystup give a clean example. A EUR/USD call on €1,000,000 with spot 1.3900, strike 1.3500 and value 0.1024 USD per EUR costs $102,400, which is €73,669. If its delta is 60%, a dealer who sold it would buy €600,000 to hedge. But if the client paid the premium in euros, the dealer already received €73,669 and needs to buy only €526,331: a premium-adjusted delta of 52.63%.

In formulas, the premium-adjusted delta is the ordinary delta minus the premium measured in base currency, \(v/S\). For a call and a put this simplifies to:

\[ \Delta_{S,pa} = \phi\,e^{-r_f\tau}\,\frac{K}{F}\,N(\phi d_-), \qquad \Delta_{F,pa} = \phi\,\frac{K}{F}\,N(\phi d_-) \]
K/F · N(φd₋)the delta once the premium you already hold is netted off
d₋uses d₋ (= d₊ − σ√τ) instead of d₊
pa"premium-adjusted", also called premium-included

Two consequences. First, a premium-adjusted delta is always smaller (for calls) or more negative (for puts) than the regular one, so the "25-delta" strike moves: closer to ATM for calls, further out for puts. Second, the premium-adjusted call delta is not monotonic in the strike. It rises, peaks, then falls, so a given call delta can match two strikes. Desks take the one on the out-of-the-money side of the peak. The ATM delta-neutral strike moves too, to \(F e^{-\frac12\sigma^2\tau}\), just below the forward instead of just above it.

Regular delta (premium in terms currency)

  • EUR/USD, GBP/USD, AUD/USD: premium in USD, the terms currency
  • Delta = e^(−r_f τ)N(φd₊) (spot) or N(φd₊) (forward)
  • ATM DNS strike F·e^(+σ²τ/2), just above the forward

Premium-adjusted delta (premium in base currency)

  • USD/JPY, USD/CHF, USD/CAD: premium in USD, the base currency
  • EUR/JPY, EUR/CHF, EUR/GBP, AUD/JPY; USD/BRL, USD/MXN
  • ATM DNS strike F·e^(−σ²τ/2), just below the forward

The rule of thumb behind that table (Reiswich and Wystup, following Clark): the premium is usually paid in the more commonly traded currency of the pair. Almost every USD pair is premium-paid in USD; pairs with EUR but no USD are paid in EUR; JPY is never the premium currency. Whenever that premium currency is the base currency, deltas are premium-adjusted by default. Exceptions exist, so always check the broker's convention.

Spot or forward? For pairs of OECD currencies (USD, EUR, JPY, GBP, AUD, NZD, CAD, CHF, NOK, SEK, DKK), the default is spot delta out to and including one year and forward delta beyond. For pairs that include an emerging-market currency, forward delta is the default at every tenor. Forward deltas are popular for smile tables because a 25-delta put and a 75-delta call are then exactly the same strike.

How much does it matter? Take the USD/JPY one-month quote from Section 02 (spot 90.68, 31 days, JPY rate 0.42875%, USD rate 0.3525%). At the smile's 25-delta call vol of 18.77%, the premium-adjusted spot-delta strike is 94.10; read the same vol as a regular spot delta and you get 94.23. For the put at 24.07% it is 86.51 against 86.71, and the ATM DNS strike is 90.52 against 90.86. Thirteen to thirty-four yen pips of misplacement, with nothing wrong except the convention.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

If someone pays you for a promise using the very thing you might have to hand over later, you already hold some of it. So you need to go and get a bit less. Premium-adjusted delta is just "how much more do I need, now that I have been paid partly in the thing itself?"

Section 04

Reading a risk reversal, and the term structure

A risk reversal is a price, and like any price it reflects supply and demand. A deeply negative 25-delta risk reversal means people are paying several vols more for options that pay off if the base currency falls than for options that pay off if it rises by a similar amount in delta terms. Three overlapping reasons, which the number alone cannot separate:

1

Crash risk

The market thinks a big move one way is more likely, or would be faster and gappier, than the same move the other way. Moves that come with jumps in volatility (a currency dropping in a panic) make those options worth more.

2

Hedging demand and positioning

Someone with real exposure is buying protection: importers, investors with unhedged foreign assets, or traders long a carry position who want insurance. Dealers who sell it charge for carrying that risk.

3

Spot–vol correlation

If implied vol tends to rise when spot falls, a put gains twice in a sell-off: from spot and from vol. The market prices that in. In equity indices this is persistent; in FX it changes sign with the pair and the regime.

What the risk reversal is not is a forecast of direction. It says the downside is expensive to insure, not that it will happen. Before the 2016 Brexit vote the sterling risk reversal was deeply negative while spot was actually rising (1.4390 on 10 June to 1.4877 by the close on polling day) as polls drifted towards Remain. Both readings were right: the market thought Remain more likely and paid heavily for protection in case it was wrong.

Because the risk reversal is quoted in vol, its money value depends on vega. In the EUR/USD example, a one-month 25-delta option on €10 million has a vega of about $12,100 per vol point (identical for the call and the put, since both are 25-delta with the same tenor). A risk reversal of −0.5 means the put costs roughly $6,000 more in vol terms than a call of the same delta; a risk reversal of −5 would mean about $60,000.

The term structure: one smile per expiry

Every tenor on the screen has its own ATM, risk reversal and butterfly. Read across the ATM column and you get the volatility term structure. In calm markets it usually slopes up (longer tenors a little higher); in a crisis it inverts, with near-dated vol above far-dated, the same pattern described for the VIX in Vega & the Volatility Surface. Scheduled events leave fingerprints: an expiry that spans a central-bank meeting, an election or a referendum carries that day's extra variance, and the first tenor to cover the event jumps.

Variances add over time, so two adjacent tenors imply the vol between them, the forward vol:

\[ \sigma_{1\to 2} = \sqrt{\frac{\sigma_2^2\,T_2 - \sigma_1^2\,T_1}{T_2 - T_1}} \]
σ₁, σ₂ATM vols for the shorter and longer expiry
T₁, T₂their times to expiry in years
σ₁→₂the implied vol for the stretch between the two expiries

With one-month vol at 10% and two-month at 12%, treating the months as equal, \(\sigma_{1\to2} = \sqrt{0.0288 - 0.0100} = 13.71\%\): the market is pricing the second month as much rougher than the first. If an election falls in that second month, that is where its premium lives. Risk reversals show the same footprint: the tenor whose expiry first covers an event usually moves most, which is exactly what happened in 2016.

A client wants a one-month 25-delta EUR put on €10 million (illustrative trade on real quotes)

It is 20 January 2009 and the screen shows the EUR/USD quote above: ATM 21.62, 25-delta RR −0.5, BF 0.74. A corporate client asks for a one-month 25-delta EUR put (USD call) on €10 million, premium in USD.

Step 1, convention. EUR/USD under one year: regular spot delta, ATM delta-neutral, premium in USD. Step 2, vol. \(21.62 + 0.74 + 0.25 = 22.61\%\). Step 3, strike. At 22.61% the −25% spot-delta strike is 1.2530. Step 4, premium. What the risk reversal costs the client. The option's vega is about $12,100 per vol point, so the skew adds about 0.25 × $12,100 ≈ $3,000 over pricing it at ATM + BF, and the full half-point gap to the equivalent call is worth about $6,000. Section 06

It actually happened: the sterling risk reversal before Brexit, 2016

The cleanest public example of a risk reversal telling a story in advance is sterling before the referendum on leaving the European Union. Iain Clark and Saeed Amen tracked the GBP/USD smile through the first half of 2016 and published the numbers (working paper June 2016; Risks, 2017).

It actually happened

Each tenor flinched on the day its expiry first covered the vote

On 20 February 2016 the government set the referendum for 23 June. Until then, short-dated GBP/USD 25-delta risk reversals had sat in a band of roughly −0.5 to −1.5: sterling puts a little dearer than calls, as usual for a currency the market saw as vulnerable. From then on, each tenor moved sharply on the day its expiry first reached past 23 June. The two-month risk reversal moved dramatically between 25 and 26 April. The one-month went from −1.3 on 25 May to −5.4 on 26 May: an option dealt on 25 May expired just before the result, one dealt on 26 May just after it.

It did not stop there. By 10 June, with spot near 1.4390, the one-month risk reversal had reached −7.9: the market was paying about eight vols more for 25-delta sterling puts than for 25-delta sterling calls. Clark and Amen used the whole smile to back out what the market expected. Their reading on 13 June was that a Leave vote could take GBP/USD into a 1.10 to 1.30 range; their two-scenario model, fitted to data from 24 February to 22 June, put the Leave outcome near 1.37.

Meanwhile spot rallied as polls drifted towards Remain, to 1.4877 by the close on polling day. The result came through the night of 23 to 24 June: Leave. GBP/USD closed 24 June at 1.3622, down about 8.4%, after trading briefly around 1.32 to 1.33 overnight, its weakest since 1985. The options market had not predicted the result. It had priced, for weeks and in public, how expensive the wrong side would be.

⚠️
Where the textbook version breaks

Three numbers are not a distribution

Before Brexit the true distribution was two-humped: one outcome near 1.47, another far below. A smile built from ATM, a risk reversal and a butterfly, interpolated with a smooth curve, cannot show two humps. It can only say "wide, and lopsided". That is why Clark and Amen fitted a mixture of two scenarios instead of a single smile model. The quotes are honest; the shortcut from three quotes to a full distribution is where the information gets lost.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Before a big storm, umbrella shops raise their prices. The price of umbrellas doesn't tell you it will rain, but it tells you lots of people are worried it might. In June 2016 the "umbrella" against the pound falling got very expensive, right on the day the umbrellas started to cover the big vote.

Section 07

How practitioners actually use this

Dealers and brokers quote and trade the three building blocks directly. A risk reversal trades as a package (long a 25-delta call, short a 25-delta put, or the reverse), usually delta-hedged, and is quoted as the vol difference. A butterfly trades as a strangle against an ATM straddle. The screen therefore shows real, tradable prices for level, skew and curvature at each tenor, and everything else (a 1.3500 strike, a 17-day expiry) is interpolated from them. Desks fill the smile with a model: Malz's parabola for a quick picture, the vanna-volga method of Castagna and Mercurio (2007) which prices off the three liquid points, SABR fitted to the quotes, or Reiswich and Wystup's parabolic method.

Spot traders and strategists read risk reversals as a gauge of how lopsided the market's fear is. A risk reversal moving towards one side, especially when spot is not moving, says demand for protection is building. Comparing tenors shows when it is expected. Changes matter more than levels: some pairs carry a persistent skew (the yen has long tended to rally in sell-offs, so USD/JPY risk reversals have for long stretches favoured yen calls), and a "normal" level for one pair is extreme for another.

Hedgers use the same screen to choose instruments. When puts are expensive relative to calls, a forward hedge (which has no vol in it at all) or a structure that sells the expensive side can be cheaper ways to protect, at the cost of giving up some upside. Understanding the quote is what lets a treasurer see that the "cheap" zero-premium collar is in fact selling them a call at a low vol and buying a put at a high one.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where reading an FX vol screen quietly goes wrong

Using the wrong delta convention. A USD/JPY 25-delta strike is premium-adjusted; a EUR/USD one is not. One month out, the difference was 13 to 34 yen pips in the example above, and it grows with vol and tenor. Beyond one year, OECD pairs switch from spot to forward delta. Check before converting.

Taking "ATM" to mean spot or the forward. For most short-dated quotes it is the delta-neutral straddle strike, slightly above the forward with regular deltas and slightly below it with premium-adjusted ones. Some long-dated and emerging-market quotes use the forward instead.

Plugging the quoted butterfly into the shortcut when the skew is large. With RR −5.3 the USD/JPY wings came out about 0.24 vol too low. Fine for a quick read; not fine for pricing a strangle.

Reading the risk reversal as a forecast. It is the price of protection. In June 2016 it was deeply negative while spot rose for two weeks. It tells you how expensive being wrong is, not which way the market will go.

Comparing tenors without checking the calendar. A one-month risk reversal that jumps overnight with no news may simply have rolled over an event. Look at which expiry first covers the election or meeting before reading the move as new information.

Scenario drill · 10 June 2016

Sterling insurance has never looked so expensive

You run a US fund holding £50 million of UK assets, currency unhedged. The referendum is on 23 June. GBP/USD is near 1.4390, and the one-month 25-delta risk reversal is about −7.9: sterling puts cost about eight vols more than equivalent calls. A colleague says: "The puts are absurdly rich. Let's sell some and collect the premium." What do you do?

  1. Sell 25-delta GBP puts to harvest the rich skew.
    You already own the downside through the £50 million of assets. Selling puts doubles that bet, and the skew is rich precisely because the market sees a real chance of a large, fast fall. You would be paid well for standing in the road.
  2. Hedge some or all of the sterling with forwards, which carry no implied vol, and buy options only for the part where you want to keep the upside.
    The risk reversal says option protection on this side is expensive, not that you should go unprotected. A forward hedge costs nothing in vol terms; the price is giving up the rally if Remain wins. Using options only where you need the optionality keeps the skew bill small.
  3. Buy 25-delta GBP puts on the whole £50 million.
    It protects you, and it keeps the upside, but you pay the richest vol on the screen for all of it. Defensible for a fund that cannot give up the upside; expensive for one that can.
  4. Do nothing. A −7.9 risk reversal means the market is too bearish, so sterling should rally.
    The risk reversal is a price of insurance, not a forecast, and reading it as a contrarian signal is still a forecast. Staying unhedged into a binary event is a directional bet with the fund's whole sterling position.

What happened. Sterling first rallied, to 1.4877 by the close on polling day, as polls leaned towards Remain. Then Leave won. On 24 June GBP/USD closed at 1.3622, down about 8.4%, after trading briefly around 1.32 to 1.33, its weakest since 1985. Forward hedges locked in rates above 1.40; puts paid out; anyone who had sold sterling puts on top of a long sterling book lost twice. Figures from Clark and Amen (2017) and contemporary market reports.

Section 08

Key Takeaways

✓FX options are quoted in vol and delta. Vol is the only negotiable input to Garman–Kohlhagen, and delta names a strike in a way that survives spot moving. Turn a delta and a vol back into a strike with \(K = F\exp(-\phi N^{-1}(\phi e^{r_f\tau}\Delta_S)\sigma\sqrt\tau + \tfrac12\sigma^2\tau)\).
✓Three quotes per expiry rebuild the smile. ATM is the level, the risk reversal the tilt, the butterfly the curvature: \(\sigma_{25C,25P} \approx \sigma_{ATM} + \sigma_{BF} \pm \tfrac12\sigma_{RR}\). The 10-delta pair does the same for the far wings.
✓ATM usually means the delta-neutral straddle. \(F e^{+\frac12\sigma^2\tau}\) with regular deltas, \(F e^{-\frac12\sigma^2\tau}\) with premium-adjusted ones; at the forward for many long-dated and emerging-market quotes.
✓A risk reversal is the market paying up for one side. It mixes crash risk, hedging demand and spot–vol correlation, and its jumps are timed by which expiry first covers an event, as sterling's one-month did on 26 May 2016.
⚠Check the conventions before you convert. Premium-adjusted for USD/JPY, EUR/JPY, USD/CHF and EUR/GBP; regular for EUR/USD, GBP/USD, AUD/USD; spot delta to one year then forward for OECD pairs; forward delta throughout for emerging-market pairs.
⚠The shortcut is exact only for the smile strangle. The quoted butterfly is a market strangle; with a large risk reversal the two differ (USD/JPY, January 2009: 0.184 quoted against 0.419 in the smile).
✗A risk reversal is not a forecast. Sterling's one-month risk reversal was near −7.9 while spot rallied for two weeks; it priced how costly the wrong side would be, not which side would win.
Section 09

Self-test

Q1. A one-month quote shows ATM 8.0%, 25-delta risk reversal +1.0, 25-delta butterfly 0.30. What are the 25-delta call and put vols, and which side is the market paying up for?
\(\sigma_{25C} = 8.0 + 0.30 + 0.5 = 8.8\%\) and \(\sigma_{25P} = 8.0 + 0.30 - 0.5 = 7.8\%\). Their difference is the risk reversal (+1.0) and their average minus ATM is the butterfly (0.30). A positive risk reversal means calls on the base currency are dearer: the market pays up for protection against the base currency rising.
Q2. Why is ATM in FX usually defined as the delta-neutral straddle rather than spot or the forward?
A straddle struck where the call and put deltas are equal and opposite has zero delta, so buying or selling it is a pure volatility trade with no spot exposure to hedge. That is what an ATM vol quote is meant to price. With regular deltas the strike is \(F e^{\frac12\sigma^2\tau}\), slightly above the forward; with premium-adjusted deltas it is \(F e^{-\frac12\sigma^2\tau}\), slightly below. Some long-dated and emerging-market quotes use the forward itself.
Q3. Why are USD/JPY deltas premium-adjusted while EUR/USD deltas are not?
In both pairs the premium is normally paid in USD. In EUR/USD the dollar is the terms currency, so the premium is not part of the hedge quantity, which is measured in euros. In USD/JPY the dollar is the base currency: a dealer who sells a USD call and receives the premium in dollars already holds some of the dollars needed to hedge, so the hedge (and the delta that names the strike) is reduced by the premium. That shifts every 25-delta strike and the ATM strike.
Q4. A one-month risk reversal jumps from −1.3 to −5.4 overnight with no news. What is the most likely mechanical reason?
The one-month expiry has just rolled over a scheduled event. An option dealt the day before expired before the event; one dealt today expires after it, so it now carries the event's risk. This is what happened to GBP/USD between 25 and 26 May 2016, a month before the 23 June referendum. Read moves in a given tenor against the calendar before treating them as new information.
Q5. Why can \(\sigma_{25C,25P} = \sigma_{ATM} + \sigma_{BF} \pm \tfrac12\sigma_{RR}\) give the wrong wing vols when the risk reversal is large?
The formula is exact for the smile strangle (the smile's own average 25-delta vol minus ATM). The broker's butterfly is a market strangle: both strikes found and priced at the single vol ATM + BF. A correct smile only has to reprice that strangle's total premium, and when the smile is strongly tilted its 25-delta strikes differ from the market-strangle strikes, so the two strangle numbers diverge. In the USD/JPY example of 20 January 2009 (RR −5.3) the quoted 0.184 corresponded to a smile strangle of 0.419, putting both wings about 0.24 vol higher.
Q6. One-month ATM vol is 10% and two-month is 12%. What is the implied vol for the second month alone, and what might explain it?
Variances add, so \(\sigma_{1\to2} = \sqrt{0.12^2 \times 2 - 0.10^2} = 13.71\%\) (treating the two months as equal length). The market is pricing the second month as much rougher than the first, which is what you would see if a scheduled event (an election, a referendum, a central-bank meeting) falls inside it.
