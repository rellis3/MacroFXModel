Fractional Brownian Motion & Long Memory

The Hurst exponent lesson measured persistence with a slope fit to historical rescaled ranges — a descriptive statistic, not a model of anything. This lesson gives \(H\) a real generating process: a Gaussian process with a precisely defined covariance structure, fractional Brownian motion, plus its discrete-time econometric cousin ARFIMA — and uses both to turn "long memory" from a loose adjective into a formally testable property that a finite-order ARMA model can never possess, no matter how many parameters it is given.

CONCEPT — formalizes the Hurst exponent lesson's empirical statistic ~17 min read Prereqs: Hurst Exponent & Variance Ratio Test, Stationarity, ACF & PACF

Why this matters

The previous lesson in this series measured how "persistent" a price path has been using R/S analysis and the variance ratio test — both are, deliberately, model-free: they estimate a single number, \(H\), directly from historical data without ever committing to a specific mathematical process that could have generated that data. That's a genuine strength (you don't need to believe a specific model to trust the measurement) but it is also a limitation: a bare number with no generating model behind it is hard to reason about precisely. What, exactly, does \(H = 0.7\) imply about the correlation between today's move and a move 50 days from now? Does that correlation ever really vanish, or does it just get small? Is "persistence" the same thing as "long memory," or only sometimes? None of those questions have crisp answers without a model. Fractional Brownian motion is that model — a continuous-time stochastic process built so that its Hurst exponent is not fit after the fact but is a parameter of its exact covariance structure, from which every one of those questions above has a precise, derivable answer. This lesson builds that process from its defining formula, uses it to state "long memory" exactly (a genuine mathematical distinction from mere autocorrelation), and introduces ARFIMA, the tool econometricians actually reach for when they want to fit long-memory behavior to real, noisy, discretely-sampled data like a daily volatility series.

Plain-English intuition

A random walk that never fully forgets

Ordinary Brownian motion is amnesiac: at every instant, it draws a fresh, independent increment, with zero regard for which direction it has been moving. Its whole future is statistically indifferent to its whole past, given only where it stands right now. Fractional Brownian motion keeps the same rough visual texture — a continuous, jagged, random path — but bakes in a controllable amount of memory. Turn the memory dial to "persistent" (\(H>0.5\)) and the process behaves like a random walker who is subtly reluctant to reverse course: having drifted up recently makes a further upward increment slightly more likely, and — this is the part that makes it a genuinely different object from an AR(1) process — that subtle reluctance to reverse does not fade away exponentially fast as you look further into the past; it fades so slowly that, added up over all lags, it never stops mattering. Turn the dial to "anti-persistent" (\(H<0.5\)) and the walker becomes subtly self-correcting, the drunk-on-a-leash from the previous lesson. Set the dial to exactly \(H=0.5\) and every trace of memory vanishes and you are left with vanilla Brownian motion. The dial itself — a single real number between 0 and 1 — is built directly into the process's covariance function, which is the entire mathematical content of this lesson.

The math, step by step

Four steps: (1) the precise definition of fractional Brownian motion via its covariance function, and the algebra showing it collapses to ordinary Brownian motion at \(H=0.5\); (2) what non-independent increments mean for \(H \ne 0.5\), split into the persistent and anti-persistent cases; (3) a rigorous definition of "long memory" — the exact sense in which it is a different mathematical category from "somewhat autocorrelated," not just a matter of degree; (4) ARFIMA, the discrete-time model practitioners actually fit, and its precise link back to \(H\).

1. Defining \(B_H(t)\) by its covariance

Fractional Brownian motion (fBm), usually written \(B_H(t)\), is the unique (up to a scale constant) centered Gaussian process with \(B_H(0)=0\) and covariance function

\[ \operatorname{Cov}\big(B_H(s), B_H(t)\big) = \tfrac{1}{2}\Big(|s|^{2H} + |t|^{2H} - |t-s|^{2H}\Big), \qquad s, t \ge 0,\ H \in (0,1) \]

This single formula is the entire definition — everything else in this lesson is a consequence of it. \(H\) is called the Hurst index (or self-similarity index) of the process, and it is the same \(H\) the previous lesson estimated empirically from real price data via R/S analysis: fBm is the generating model whose parameter that estimation procedure is, whether or not the person running it realizes it, trying to recover.

The \(H=0.5\) reduction, checked exactly. Set \(H = 0.5\), so \(2H = 1\), and take \(0 \le s \le t\) without loss of generality (so \(|t-s| = t-s\)):

\[ \operatorname{Cov}\big(B_{0.5}(s), B_{0.5}(t)\big) = \tfrac{1}{2}\Big(s + t - (t-s)\Big) = \tfrac{1}{2}\big(2s\big) = s = \min(s,t) \]

That is exactly the covariance function of ordinary (standard) Brownian motion, \(\operatorname{Cov}(W(s),W(t)) = \min(s,t)\). This is not a coincidence engineered for this lesson — it is the defining check that any claimed formula for fBm must pass, and it is the precise sense in which "fBm generalizes Brownian motion" is a true mathematical statement rather than a hand-wave: at \(H=0.5\), the general formula reduces algebraically, term for term, to the special case everyone already knows. For any other value of \(H\), that cancellation does not happen and the process is a genuinely different object with genuinely different correlation behavior, worked out next.

A second consequence of the same formula, obtained by setting \(s=t\) inside \(\operatorname{Var}(B_H(t)-B_H(s)) = \operatorname{Var}(B_H(t)) + \operatorname{Var}(B_H(s)) - 2\operatorname{Cov}(B_H(s),B_H(t))\) and substituting the covariance formula, is the increment-variance scaling law

\[ \operatorname{Var}\big(B_H(t) - B_H(s)\big) = |t-s|^{2H} \]

which says the standard deviation of an increment over a span of length \(n\) scales as \(n^H\) — exactly the power law the previous lesson's R/S statistic was built to detect empirically. Fractional Brownian motion is, in this precise sense, the continuous-time object whose existence R/S analysis and the variance ratio test were always implicitly testing for.

2. What non-independent increments actually mean

For ordinary Brownian motion, non-overlapping increments are independent — that is the entire content of \(H=0.5\). For \(H \ne 0.5\), fBm's increments (the process is sometimes analyzed through its increments, fractional Gaussian noise, \(X_i = B_H(i+1)-B_H(i)\)) are correlated, and the sign of that correlation is exactly the fade/follow split the Hurst lesson introduced empirically:

  • \(H > 0.5\): persistent, positively correlated increments. A positive increment makes the next increment more likely to also be positive. Correlations between increments decay — but, critically, they decay as a power law in the lag, not exponentially. This is qualitatively different from the short memory of an AR(1) process (covered in the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck and stationarity lessons), whose autocorrelation decays as \(\phi^k\) — a geometric, exponentially fast decay that makes lag-60 correlation numerically indistinguishable from zero for any \(|\phi|<1\). A persistent-fBm-consistent series can still show a real, non-negligible correlation at lag 60 that no AR(1) fit, however carefully tuned, could ever produce.
  • \(H < 0.5\): anti-persistent, negatively correlated increments. A positive increment makes the next increment more likely to be negative — consistent with the Hurst lesson's \(H<0.5\) mean-reverting case. Here the correlations are negative but, as the next section makes precise, they decay fast enough to sum to a finite value — which matters for whether this case counts as "long memory" in the strict sense below.

Both cases stand in contrast to \(H=0.5\), where the increment autocorrelation is exactly zero at every nonzero lag — no persistence, no anti-persistence, nothing to model.

3. Long memory, defined precisely — not just "somewhat persistent"

"Long memory" has a specific formal meaning, and it is worth stating exactly because the phrase gets used loosely in practice. A stationary process is said to have long memory (equivalently, long-range dependence) if its autocovariance function \(\gamma(k)\) is not absolutely summable:

\[ \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} |\gamma(k)| = \infty \]

For fractional Gaussian noise, \(\gamma(k)\) behaves asymptotically like \(H(2H-1)\,k^{2H-2}\) for large \(k\). Whether that sum diverges or converges is a direct, checkable consequence of the exponent \(2H-2\): the sum \(\sum_k k^{2H-2}\) diverges exactly when \(2H-2 \ge -1\), i.e. when \(H \ge 0.5\). This gives a precise, non-obvious refinement of the persistent/anti-persistent split from the section above:

  • \(H > 0.5\) gives \(2H-2 > -1\): the autocovariance sum diverges. This is genuine long memory in the strict, formal sense.
  • \(H < 0.5\) gives \(2H-2 < -1\): the autocovariance sum converges absolutely, even though the correlations are negative and real. Anti-persistence is a genuine, useful, mean-reverting property — but it is technically short memory by this definition, not long memory with a negative sign. "Long memory" strictly names the \(H>0.5\) case.
  • \(H = 0.5\): \(\gamma(k)=0\) for all \(k \ne 0\) — no memory of any kind, the sum is trivially finite (it's zero).

Now contrast this with any finite-order, stationary ARMA process (the AR(1)/OU and ARIMA machinery from earlier lessons in this Theory Lab). A stationary ARMA process's autocorrelation function is always a mixture of damped exponentials/sinusoids — it decays geometrically, as \(\phi^k\) for the dominant root \(\phi\) with \(|\phi|<1\). A geometric series is always absolutely summable, \(\sum_k |\phi|^k = \frac{1}{1-|\phi|} < \infty\), for any finite \(\phi\) short of exactly 1. This is the crux of the whole lesson: no ARMA model, however many autoregressive or moving-average terms you add, can ever produce a non-summable autocorrelation function — exponential decay is exponential decay, and adding more lag terms only changes the rate and shape of that decay, never its fundamentally summable character. Long memory in the strict sense above requires a genuinely different kind of process — a power-law-decaying one like fBm/fractional Gaussian noise — not a bigger ARMA model. That is a formal distinction, not a matter of degree, and it is the reason this lesson exists as a separate piece of machinery rather than "just use a higher-order AR model."

Exponential decay (short memory) vs. power-law decay (long memory)
Both curves start at \(\rho(0)=1\) by construction. The blue curve is a stylized AR(1) autocorrelation, \(\rho(k) = 0.7^k\) — essentially indistinguishable from zero by lag 10, exactly the exponential-decay signature no finite ARMA order can escape. The red curve is a stylized long-memory autocorrelation, \(\rho(k) \propto (k+1)^{-0.3}\) (consistent with an fBm/ARFIMA process around \(H \approx 0.85\)) — still at roughly 0.3 by lag 50–60, a level an AR(1) fit would have long since forgotten. Summed to infinity, the blue curve's tail contributes a finite, small amount; the red curve's tail, decaying as a power law, keeps adding up forever — the formal divergence described above, made visible.

4. ARFIMA — the discrete-time cousin practitioners actually fit

Fractional Brownian motion is a continuous-time construction, which makes it awkward to fit directly to daily or weekly bars. The ARIMA lesson elsewhere in this Theory Lab covers the standard discrete-time toolkit: an ARIMA(\(p,d,q\)) model applies \(d\) rounds of first-differencing (\(d\) is a non-negative integer — 0, 1, or occasionally 2) to induce stationarity before fitting an ARMA(\(p,q\)) model to what's left. ARFIMA (AutoRegressive Fractionally Integrated Moving Average) is the direct generalization: it allows \(d\) to be any real number, not just an integer, via the fractional differencing operator \((1-L)^d\) (where \(L\) is the lag operator), expanded as an infinite binomial series rather than the familiar finite first-difference. The practical payoff is that ARFIMA can fit the slow, power-law-decaying autocorrelation described above using ordinary discrete-time estimation methods (maximum likelihood, or the Geweke–Porter-Hudak semiparametric estimator), without ever touching the continuous-time fBm machinery — an econometrician's tool for a physicist's process.

The fractional order \(d\) is directly, exactly related to the Hurst index:

\[ d = H - 0.5 \]

so \(d \in (0, 0.5)\) corresponds to \(H \in (0.5, 1)\) — genuine long memory, positive \(d\) meaning the series needs a fraction of a differencing pass to induce stationarity, less than the whole integer difference an ordinary ARIMA model would apply. \(d \in (-0.5, 0)\) corresponds to \(H \in (0, 0.5)\) — the anti-persistent, short-memory-but-negatively-correlated case from the section above. And \(d=0\) corresponds exactly to \(H=0.5\): no fractional differencing needed at all, which is just an ordinary stationary ARMA model — the same reduction-to-the-familiar-case check as the covariance formula's \(H=0.5\) reduction, now on the econometric side of the same coin.

Worked example

Converting between \(H\) and \(d\), and checking the covariance reduction numerically

The \(H=0.5\) reduction, with numbers. Take \(s=2\), \(t=5\). Plugging into the general fBm covariance formula at \(H=0.5\): \(\operatorname{Cov}(B_{0.5}(2), B_{0.5}(5)) = \tfrac{1}{2}(2^1 + 5^1 - 3^1) = \tfrac{1}{2}(2+5-3) = \tfrac{1}{2}(4) = 2\), and \(\min(2,5)=2\) — they match exactly, as the general algebra above guarantees for any \(s,t\).

\(H \to d\) conversions. A researcher who has estimated \(H \approx 0.85\) from a volatility series (strongly persistent, per the Hurst lesson's regimes) is, via \(d = H - 0.5\), looking at \(d \approx 0.35\) in ARFIMA terms — a real, long-memory-consistent fractional order comfortably inside the stationary-and-invertible range \(d \in (-0.5, 0.5)\). A different series estimated at \(H \approx 0.3\) (mean-reverting) maps to \(d \approx -0.2\) — a legitimate, if less commonly discussed, negative fractional order. Both conversions run in the other direction just as easily: an ARFIMA fit reporting \(\hat{d} = 0.4\) is reporting \(H \approx 0.9\), i.e. very strong persistence, in exactly the language the previous lesson's R/S regression would have used.

How practitioners actually use this

In practice, almost nobody trades a literal simulated path of fractional Brownian motion — it shows up as the theoretical justification behind two very practical activities. First, it is the formal backstop for exactly the kind of Hurst-exponent regime-gating covered in the previous lesson: knowing that a real generating process exists whose parameter is \(H\) is what makes "estimate \(H\), use it to gate fade vs. follow" a principled thing to do rather than an ad hoc curve-fit. Second, and more concretely useful day to day, it motivates fitting ARFIMA (or ARFIMA-GARCH hybrids, and specifically FIGARCH — Fractionally Integrated GARCH — for volatility) whenever a series' own autocorrelation function refuses to decay the way a standard, finite-order ARMA or GARCH model assumes it should. The tell is exactly the pattern in the chart above: fit an AR(1) or a low-order ARMA, look at the residual ACF, and if it is still visibly elevated at lags of 40, 50, 60+ where a correctly-specified short-memory model's residual ACF should be indistinguishable from noise, that is the practical, empirical signature of long memory the formal definition above describes — and the appropriate next model to reach for is ARFIMA, not a bigger ARMA order (per the point above, a bigger ARMA order cannot fix this; only a different kind of process can).

Real-world trading scenario

A GARCH residual that refuses to go away

A researcher is modeling a currency pair's realized-volatility series and starts, reasonably, with a short-memory approach: an AR(1) model on log-volatility, or the GARCH(1,1) framework covered in the next lesson in this series, both of which capture volatility clustering — today's volatility being informative about tomorrow's — through exponentially decaying persistence. The fit looks fine at short lags, but the residual autocorrelation refuses to die out: even at 60-plus trading day lags, there is still a small but persistent, clearly non-zero autocorrelation left over, well beyond what the GARCH(1,1) persistence parameter can honestly explain at that horizon. This is not a sign the GARCH fit is broken — it is the well-documented "long memory in volatility" stylized fact (see the Ding, Granger & Engle reference below): realized volatility across essentially every liquid market shows autocorrelation that decays as a slow power law, not the fast geometric decay any finite-order GARCH's short memory implies, distinct from and layered on top of GARCH's own (real, but short-range) clustering effect.

Recognizing this as long memory — rather than concluding the GARCH model just needs another parameter or a longer lag — motivates fitting an ARFIMA (or FIGARCH) specification instead. Suppose the fractional differencing order comes back estimated at \(\hat{d} \approx 0.35\). Via \(H = d + 0.5\), that implies \(H \approx 0.85\) — strongly persistent, in the same units as the Hurst lesson's regime split. The payoff for taking this seriously rather than papering over it with a bigger short-memory model: an ARFIMA/FIGARCH fit that has actually matched the series' true, slowly-decaying dependence structure supports forecasting volatility meaningfully further out than a short-memory GARCH(1,1) could honestly justify, because the model itself now encodes the correct rate of memory decay rather than truncating it exponentially fast by construction. This is a genuinely different, better-specified model of a real, well-documented empirical pattern — not a way of manufacturing extra predictive power out of nothing, and it still requires the same out-of-sample validation discipline as any other model before its longer-horizon forecasts are trusted.

A descriptive statistic already in production, a generative model not yet built

That is a reasonable scope for a live entry-bias feature: it needs a fast, robust regime read, not a full model fit. It does mean the live feature's \(H\) is a description of the recent past's scaling behavior, not a forecast derived from a fitted long-memory process — a distinction worth keeping straight given how easily "the Hurst exponent is 0.85" can be misread as "and therefore the model predicts continued persistence for N more days," which computeHurst's descriptive R/S procedure does not, by itself, license.

Common pitfalls

Where fBm and ARFIMA reasoning goes wrong in practice

  • fBm itself is not stationary (only its increments are). \(B_H(t)\) has variance \(t^{2H}\), which grows with \(t\) — the process itself is a non-stationary random walk in the same sense ordinary Brownian motion is. "Long memory," "stationary," and "fractional Gaussian noise" (the increment process) are the objects with a well-defined ACF to discuss; the raw fBm level series is not one of them. This mirrors the stationarity lesson's warning about running memory diagnostics on price levels instead of returns.
  • Structural breaks and regime switching can produce spurious long memory. A series made of several genuinely short-memory regimes stitched together (e.g. calm-market AR(1) volatility alternating with crisis-period AR(1) volatility at a different level) can show an aggregate autocorrelation function that decays very slowly — visually identical to true long memory — purely as an artifact of the regime switching, with no single long-memory process actually generating the data. This confound is itself well documented in the time-series literature; it means "the ACF decays slowly" should prompt a check for regime instability before concluding ARFIMA is the right model, not an automatic ARFIMA fit.
  • \(d\) is estimated with real uncertainty, and different estimators disagree. Maximum likelihood, the Geweke–Porter-Hudak log-periodogram regression, and other semiparametric estimators of \(d\) can give meaningfully different point estimates on the same finite sample, especially with the heavy tails typical of financial returns — a single reported \(\hat{d}\) deserves the same skepticism as a single point estimate of \(H\) from R/S analysis in the previous lesson (small-sample bias, no free lunch just because the model is fancier).
  • Anti-persistence (\(H<0.5\), \(d<0\)) is not "long memory with a minus sign." As derived above, the \(H<0.5\) case has a formally summable autocovariance — it is short memory by the strict definition, even though it is real, negatively-correlated, mean-reverting structure. Calling it "long memory" is a common looseness worth avoiding in precise writing.
  • A good ARFIMA in-sample fit is not automatically forecast edge. Per this repo's working agreement (a method is not a strategy), a better-specified statistical description of volatility's dependence structure is a legitimate methodological improvement over a misspecified GARCH — it is not, by itself, evidence that trading on the resulting longer-horizon forecast makes money. That still requires the same honest, costed, out-of-sample test as anything else in this repo.

Self-test

Q1. Why does fBm reduce to ordinary Brownian motion exactly at H = 0.5, algebraically?
Setting \(H=0.5\) makes \(2H=1\), so the general covariance formula \(\tfrac{1}{2}(|s|^{2H}+|t|^{2H}-|t-s|^{2H})\) becomes \(\tfrac{1}{2}(s+t-(t-s))\) for \(0\le s\le t\). The \(-(t-s)\) term cancels the \(t\) term and reinforces the \(s\) term: \(s+t-t+s = 2s\), and dividing by 2 gives exactly \(s = \min(s,t)\), which is the defining covariance function of ordinary Brownian motion. The cancellation is exact and algebraic, not approximate — it is the formal check any candidate fBm covariance formula must satisfy, confirming fBm is a strict generalization rather than merely "similar to" Brownian motion at \(H=0.5\).
Q2. What's the precise mathematical distinction between "long memory" and just "somewhat persistent" or "somewhat autocorrelated"?
Long memory has a specific formal definition: a stationary process's autocovariance function \(\gamma(k)\) is not absolutely summable, \(\sum_k |\gamma(k)| = \infty\). This is a categorical, checkable property, not a matter of degree. Any stationary finite-order ARMA process has geometrically (exponentially) decaying autocorrelation, and a geometric series is always absolutely summable for any decay rate strictly between 0 and 1 — so no ARMA process, regardless of how "persistent" its autocorrelation looks at short lags, can ever have long memory in this strict sense. Only a process whose autocorrelation decays as a power law slow enough (exponent \(\ge -1\)) qualifies — which for fractional Gaussian noise happens precisely when \(H > 0.5\). "Somewhat persistent" is a vague, comparative description; "long memory" is a specific claim about a divergent infinite sum.
Q3. Why aren't fBm's increments independent for H ≠ 0.5, and is the H < 0.5 case actually "long memory" too?
Independence of increments is a special property of \(H=0.5\) alone — it's the case where the covariance formula's cross-terms cancel perfectly, leaving zero correlation between non-overlapping increments. For any other \(H\), that cancellation does not happen, and the increment autocovariance \(\gamma(k)\) is nonzero and decays as \(k^{2H-2}\). But \(H<0.5\) (anti-persistent) is technically not long memory in the strict sense: the exponent \(2H-2 < -1\) makes the autocovariance sum converge absolutely, so it is short-memory, negatively-correlated structure — real and useful for mean-reversion reasoning, but a formally different category from the \(H>0.5\) long-memory case, where \(2H-2 \ge -1\) makes the sum diverge.
Q4. What is the exact relationship between ARFIMA's fractional order d and the Hurst index H, and what happens at d = 0?
\(d = H - 0.5\), equivalently \(H = d + 0.5\). At \(d=0\), \(H=0.5\) — no fractional differencing is applied at all, and the model collapses to an ordinary stationary ARMA process, the exact discrete-time analogue of fBm's own reduction to ordinary Brownian motion at \(H=0.5\). Positive \(d \in (0,0.5)\) (equivalently \(H\in(0.5,1)\)) is the genuine long-memory, persistent case; negative \(d \in (-0.5,0)\) (equivalently \(H\in(0,0.5)\)) is the anti-persistent, short-memory case.
Q5. Why can't a finite-order AR or ARMA model, no matter how many lags you add, ever exhibit true long memory?
A stationary ARMA(p,q) process's autocorrelation function is always built from a finite mixture of geometrically decaying terms (powers of the model's characteristic roots, all with modulus less than 1 for stationarity). A geometric series \(\sum_k |\phi|^k\) always converges to \(1/(1-|\phi|)\) for any \(|\phi|<1\), regardless of how close \(\phi\) is to 1 or how many AR/MA terms are combined — adding more lag terms changes the shape and rate of the decay but never removes its fundamentally summable, exponential character. Long memory requires a slower, power-law decay rate that no finite combination of exponentials can replicate; it requires a structurally different kind of process (fractional differencing / fBm), not a bigger version of the same one.
Q6. A researcher sees a volatility series' residual autocorrelation still elevated at lag 60 after fitting GARCH(1,1), and estimates ARFIMA's d ≈ 0.35. Is fitting ARFIMA automatically the right next step, and is a good in-sample d estimate itself evidence of trading edge?
Not automatically on either count. First, slowly-decaying residual autocorrelation is also the signature of structural breaks or regime switching stitched together from several genuinely short-memory regimes — a well-documented confound that can mimic true long memory without any single long-memory process actually being present, so it's worth checking for regime instability before committing to ARFIMA. Second, even a well-estimated \(\hat{d}\approx 0.35\) (implying \(H\approx 0.85\)) is a statement about the series' dependence structure being better described by ARFIMA than by GARCH(1,1) alone — a legitimate methodological improvement, consistent with the well-documented "long memory in volatility" literature. It is not, by itself, evidence that forecasting further out with that model produces a tradeable edge; per this repo's working agreement, that claim still needs a real, costed, out-of-sample test before being treated as anything more than a better-specified model.
