Model Selection: AIC & BIC

Adding another free parameter to a model can never make its in-sample fit worse — which means raw fit alone will always vote for the most complicated model you're willing to build. AIC and BIC are the two standard tools for penalizing that complexity honestly, and they don't always agree on the answer.

CONCEPT — how to penalize complexity honestly ~27 min read Prereqs: Regression Diagnostics
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • Adding a free parameter to a model can never make its in-sample fit (log-likelihood, \(R^2\)) worse — a larger model always nests and can at least match a smaller one — so raw in-sample fit alone always favors the most complex candidate, whether or not the extra parameters describe anything real.
  • AIC \(= 2k - 2\ln\hat L\) charges a flat penalty of 2 per parameter and is oriented toward out-of-sample prediction (asymptotically equivalent to leave-one-out cross-validation); BIC \(= k\ln(n) - 2\ln\hat L\) charges \(\ln(n)\) per parameter — stricter than AIC once \(n>e^2\approx7.4\) — and is oriented toward finding the true, most-parsimonious model (it's model-selection consistent as \(n\to\infty\), AIC is not).
  • The file's worked example (\(n=100\)): a 2-parameter Model A (\(-2\ln\hat L=100\)) and a 5-parameter Model B (\(-2\ln\hat L=94\)) tie exactly on AIC at 104 each, but BIC prefers Model A by about 7.8 points (109.21 vs. 117.03) — the same three extra parameters and the same 6-point deviance improvement read as "a dead heat" under AIC and "a clear win for the simpler model" under BIC.
  • Neither criterion is a substitute for a genuine out-of-sample check — they're a cheap, principled first filter (Lego Principle 5 still applies), and this repo currently has no coded AIC/BIC step in any engine.
  • How it reached trading: AIC (1973–74) and BIC (1978) became the standard way to choose lag lengths in ARIMA, VAR and GARCH forecasting models, and Pesaran & Timmermann (1995) used them to pick return-forecasting models in real time.
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

I've got two models for a spread. One uses one lag, the other uses five. The five-lag one fits better. Easy choice?

It always fits better. The bigger model contains the smaller one. It can set the extra coefficients to zero and match it, then nudge them to chase noise. So a better in-sample fit tells you almost nothing.

Then how do I choose?

You charge rent for every parameter. A model has to buy more fit than it pays in rent. That's what AIC and BIC do: a misfit score plus a penalty, and lower is better.

What's the rent?

AIC charges a flat two points per parameter. BIC charges the natural log of your sample size. With a hundred observations, that's about four point six.

Give me numbers.

A hundred days of data. Model A has two parameters and a misfit score, called the deviance, of one hundred. Model B has five parameters and a deviance of ninety-four. So three extra parameters bought six points of fit.

And the rent on those three?

Under AIC, three times two is six. Exactly what they bought. Both models score one hundred and four. A dead heat.

And under BIC?

Three times four point six is about thirteen point eight. More than double what they bought. BIC scores Model A at about one hundred and nine, Model B at about one hundred and seventeen. A clear win for the simpler model.

So which judge do I trust?

They answer different questions. AIC asks which model will forecast best on new data. It behaves like leave-one-out cross-validation. BIC asks which model is most likely the true one, and it gets pickier as you collect more data. With more than about eight observations, BIC is always the stricter judge.

Any traps?

Two big ones. Only compare models fitted on exactly the same data. A five-lag model uses its first five days as inputs, so refit both on the same trimmed window. And a low score only means best of the bunch. The best of five bad models is still a bad model.

So for my spread?

Neither judge thinks lags two to five earn their keep. They were probably fitting noise in that hundred-day window. Take the one-lag model, then test it out of sample before you risk money on it.

One line?

More knobs always improve the fit, so make every knob pay rent, and let an out-of-sample test have the final word.

Section 01

Why this matters

Every model-fitting procedure you'll use here — OLS regression, an ARIMA order, a GARCH variance equation, a hidden Markov model's number of states — has a knob for complexity: how many parameters it's allowed to estimate. Give a model more knobs and its in-sample fit can only get better, or at worst stay exactly the same. It can never get worse, because a model with more free parameters always contains every simpler model as a special case (just set the extra parameters to whatever value reproduces the simpler model), so the more complex model's best achievable fit is a lower bound on nothing except itself. That single fact is the entire root of overfitting: if you judge candidate models purely by in-sample goodness-of-fit — raw likelihood, raw \(R^2\), raw sum of squared residuals — the fanciest, most parameter-hungry model in your candidate set wins by construction, every time, whether or not its extra parameters describe anything real.

This is exactly the mechanism already flagged in this Theory Lab's Correlation & Regression primer, in its pitfall box on why "\(R^2\) can never go down when you add another predictor, even a useless one" — OLS is free to set a new predictor's coefficient to zero if it adds nothing, so the fit can only stay flat or improve. AIC and BIC are the formal answer to that pitfall: instead of asking "does adding this parameter help the fit?" (the answer is always "yes, weakly"), they ask "does adding this parameter help the fit by more than the going rate for a free parameter?" That reframing is what turns "always improves" into a real, decidable choice between competing models of different sizes.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Connect-the-dots with enough dots

Give a curve enough free parameters and it will pass through every single data point exactly — a polynomial of degree \(n-1\) fit to \(n\) data points achieves a perfect \(R^2=1\), zero residual error, regardless of whether the underlying relationship is a straight line with noise or genuinely wiggly. That "perfect fit" is not evidence the curve found real structure; it is a mechanical consequence of having as many knobs as data points to turn. A ruler-straight line through the same noisy data will have a worse in-sample fit and will almost always generalize better to a new data point the curve has never seen, because the wiggly curve spent its extra parameters chasing this particular sample's noise, not the underlying relationship. AIC and BIC are the two standard ways of putting a number on "how many dots is too many" — a formal penalty that grows with the number of free parameters, so that a model only wins by having more parameters if those parameters earn their keep by more than the penalty charges for them.

How it reached trading

How many lags before you're fitting noise?

AIC (Akaike, 1973–74) · BIC (Schwarz, 1978) · standard for lag choice in econometric forecasting by the 1980s

Every forecasting model on a desk has a dial: how many lags of the rate differential, how many factors, how many regimes. Adding one always improves the in-sample fit, so in-sample fit alone cannot set the dial — and a model with too many parameters forecasts worse out of sample.

Akaike's criterion, developed in the early 1970s for choosing the order of autoregressive models in engineering control, and Schwarz's Bayesian criterion of 1978 gave a penalised answer. By the 1980s they were the default way to choose lag lengths in ARIMA, VAR and GARCH work, built into econometric software. Pesaran and Timmermann (1995) used AIC, BIC and related criteria to re-select stock-return forecasting models month by month, as a real-time investor would have had to.

On a desk today, AIC and BIC are the quick first screen on a model's size: AIC when the goal is forecast accuracy, BIC when you want the smallest model the data support — with walk-forward testing as the final judge.

The math, step by step

Watch the formula build
From raw fit to AIC and BIC, one term at a time
\[ \text{score} = \color{#fbbf24}{-2\ln\hat L} \]

Start with the deviance, the in-sample badness of fit. Lower means a better fit. On its own it is useless for choosing a model size, because it can only fall as you add parameters.

\[ \text{AIC} = \color{#fbbf24}{2k} - 2\ln\hat L \]

Charge a flat 2 points per free parameter. That is Akaike's correction for how much better a model looks on its own data than on new data.

\[ \text{BIC} = \color{#fbbf24}{k\ln(n)} - 2\ln\hat L \]

Swap the flat charge for \(\ln(n)\) per parameter. It equals AIC's charge at \(n = e^2 \approx 7.4\) and grows with the sample, so BIC demands more proof as data piles up.

\[ 3\ \text{extra parameters:}\quad \underbrace{6}_{\text{fit gained}} \ \text{vs}\ \underbrace{\color{#fbbf24}{3\times2=6}}_{\text{AIC}} \ \text{vs}\ \underbrace{\color{#fbbf24}{3\ln(100)\approx13.8}}_{\text{BIC}} \]

Apply both to the worked example, \(n=100\): Model B's three extra parameters cut the deviance from 100 to 94. AIC prices them at exactly what they bought; BIC at more than double.

\[ \color{#10b981}{\text{AIC}_A = \text{AIC}_B = 104, \qquad \text{BIC}_A \approx 109.21 < \text{BIC}_B \approx 117.03} \]

The verdicts: a dead heat on AIC, a clear 7.8-point win for the simpler model on BIC. Same data, same fit, different price for complexity.

Start with the likelihood of a fitted model: given the model's estimated parameters, the likelihood \(L\) is the probability (density) of observing the data you actually observed. Fitting a model by maximum likelihood means choosing the parameters that make \(L\) as large as possible; call that maximized value \(\hat L\), and its natural log \(\ln \hat L\) the maximized log-likelihood. A higher \(\ln \hat L\) means the fitted model assigns higher probability to the data actually seen — a purely in-sample notion of "fits well."

Now suppose Model B contains Model A as a special case — for instance, Model B is Model A plus three extra lag terms, and setting those three extra coefficients to zero recovers Model A exactly. Model B's maximum-likelihood search happens over a strictly larger parameter space that includes every point Model A could reach. So whatever log-likelihood Model A achieves, Model B can always match it (by setting the extra parameters to zero) and, generically, can do at least slightly better by nudging them away from zero to chase whatever pattern — real or noise — happens to exist in this particular sample:

\[ \ln \hat L_{B} \ \ge\ \ln \hat L_{A} \quad \text{whenever B nests A} \]
ln L̂_B, ln L̂_Athe two models' maximized log-likelihoods
≥B can always match A's fit, and generically beats it, purely by having more parameters
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Give someone a 3-piece jigsaw puzzle and a picture, and they might match it roughly. Give them a 300-piece version of the same picture and they can trace every tiny detail — but that's not because they understand the picture better, it's just because more pieces let you match anything more closely, including random smudges on the original photo that were never really part of the picture. A bigger model can always match a smaller one's fit, and usually beat it slightly, purely because it has more pieces to work with — not because it found something truer.

This is the log-likelihood analogue of the \(R^2\) fact from the regression primer, and it is exactly why raw log-likelihood (or raw \(R^2\), or raw sum-of-squared-residuals) is not a valid way to choose between models of different sizes — it will always point toward the largest model in the candidate set, regardless of whether the extra parameters represent anything real. AIC and BIC both fix this the same conceptual way: goodness of fit minus a complexity penalty, so that extra parameters have to buy enough of an improvement in fit to be worth their cost. Both are scaled so that lower is better — this lesson uses \(-2\ln\hat L\) (a quantity called the deviance) as the "badness of fit" term that both criteria then add a penalty to.

Deriving AIC: an out-of-sample-oriented penalty

Akaike's (1974) argument starts from a different question than "which model fits my sample best?" — it asks "which model will predict new data, drawn from the same true but unknown process, best?" Formally, the target is the expected Kullback-Leibler divergence between the fitted model and the true data-generating process — a measure of how much information is lost by using the fitted model as an approximation to reality. Akaike showed that the raw in-sample log-likelihood \(\ln\hat L\) is an optimistic estimate of that out-of-sample quantity — it looks better than the model will actually perform on new data — and that the size of that optimism bias is, asymptotically, approximately equal to \(k\), the number of fitted parameters. Correcting for that bias by adding \(k\) back (doubled, by convention, so it lines up with the \(-2\ln\hat L\) scale used for likelihood-ratio tests) gives:

\[ \text{AIC} = 2k - 2\ln\hat L \]
2k
The complexity penalty
A flat charge of 2 points per free parameter, regardless of sample size
−2ln L̂
The deviance
In-sample badness-of-fit — can only fall (or stay flat) as k grows
AIC
The score
Lower is better — an estimate of expected out-of-sample badness-of-fit

Read AIC as "twice the number of parameters, plus the deviance" — the deviance is the in-sample badness-of-fit, and \(2k\) is the estimated correction needed to make that number a fair estimate of expected out-of-sample badness-of-fit rather than an in-sample-flattering one. A model that adds a parameter for pure noise will typically improve \(-2\ln\hat L\) by less than 2 (in expectation, under the null that the parameter is truly zero, the improvement in \(-2\ln\hat L\) from one extra parameter follows a \(\chi^2_1\) distribution with mean 1, i.e. it improves \(-2\ln\hat L\) by about 1 on average) — smaller than the 2-point penalty it costs — so on average AIC is not fooled by a genuinely useless parameter, even though it can still be fooled on any particular noisy sample.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

AIC is basically asking: "if I tried this model on a fresh, never-before-seen batch of data, how well would it actually do?" It knows that a model always looks a bit better on the exact data it was trained on than it will on new data, so it deliberately docks two points for every extra knob the model has, as a rough correction for that "grading on your own homework" effect.

Deriving BIC: a Bayesian-consistency penalty

Schwarz's (1978) argument starts from Bayesian model comparison: given a set of candidate models, each with its own prior probability, Bayes' rule says you should compare their marginal likelihoods — the probability of the data averaged (integrated) over each model's own prior on its parameters, not just evaluated at the single best-fitting point. That integral is generally intractable in closed form, but Schwarz showed that, for large \(n\), a Laplace approximation to the log marginal likelihood reduces to the maximized log-likelihood minus a penalty of \(\tfrac{k}{2}\ln(n)\) plus terms that vanish as \(n\) grows. Doubling that (again, to match the \(-2\ln\hat L\) convention) gives:

\[ \text{BIC} = k\ln(n) - 2\ln\hat L \]
k ln(n)the penalty — grows with sample size, unlike AIC's flat 2 per parameter
−2ln L̂the same deviance term AIC uses
BIClower is better; asymptotically picks the true model if it's in the candidate set

Read BIC as "the deviance, plus a penalty of \(\ln(n)\) per parameter" rather than AIC's flat 2 per parameter. Because it approximates a Bayesian posterior-probability comparison under (implicitly) equal priors on the candidate models, BIC has a property AIC does not: it is model-selection consistent — if the true data-generating model is actually one of the candidates, the probability that BIC picks it approaches 1 as \(n\to\infty\). AIC has no such guarantee; even with infinite data, AIC retains a fixed, nonzero probability of preferring an overly complex model, because it is optimizing a different target (expected prediction error, not "recover the exact true model").

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

BIC asks a slightly different question than AIC: not "which model predicts best," but "which model is most likely to be the actual, true, correct one." Because it's hunting for the genuinely right answer rather than just a good-enough forecaster, it demands more proof before it'll accept an extra parameter — and the more data you collect, the pickier it gets, on the logic that with lots of evidence, a truly useless parameter has nowhere left to hide.

Why they disagree: the penalty each parameter has to clear

Both formulas are "deviance plus a per-parameter penalty" — they differ only in how big that penalty is. AIC charges a flat \(2\) per parameter, no matter how much data you have. BIC charges \(\ln(n)\) per parameter, which grows with sample size. The two penalties are equal exactly when \(\ln(n) = 2\), i.e. \(n = e^2 \approx 7.39\) — so for essentially any dataset with more than about 8 observations, \(\ln(n) > 2\) and BIC's per-parameter penalty is already stricter than AIC's, and the gap widens (logarithmically) as \(n\) grows further. With \(n=100\) observations, for example, BIC charges \(\ln(100)\approx4.605\) per parameter — more than double AIC's flat charge of 2. This is the entire mechanical reason BIC systematically favors simpler (more parsimonious) models than AIC, especially in larger samples: it is not a different philosophy about what makes a good model so much as a stricter, sample-size-aware price tag on every additional parameter.

The practical consequence is a genuine, well-known split in emphasis, not just arithmetic trivia: AIC is oriented toward good out-of-sample predictive performance — it is asymptotically equivalent to leave-one-out cross-validation (a result due to Stone, 1977), making it a reasonable default when the goal is forecasting accuracy and you don't believe any candidate model is the literal, exactly-true process. BIC is oriented toward finding the most probable / most parsimonious model given the data, under the assumption that a "true," comparatively simple model exists among your candidates — which is why it is the more common default in fields that care about parsimony and interpretability (economics, some of time-series order selection) and tends to pick smaller models than AIC once samples get large. Neither is "the correct one" in an absolute sense; they answer subtly different questions, and reporting both — and noting when they disagree — is more honest than picking whichever one happens to endorse the model you already wanted.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Think of AIC and BIC as two different judges scoring the same complexity, with BIC simply charging a steeper "cover charge" per extra parameter once you have more than about 8 data points, and that cover charge keeps climbing the more data you collect. That's the entire reason they can disagree — same underlying idea (fit minus a penalty), just a different, and increasingly different, price tag on complexity.

Worked example

A genuine tie on AIC, a clear preference on BIC

Suppose you have two candidate models fit to the same \(n=100\) observations. Model A has \(k=2\) parameters and achieves a deviance of \(-2\ln\hat L_A = 100\) (equivalently \(\ln\hat L_A = -50\)). Model B has \(k=5\) parameters — three more than A — and, exactly as the nesting argument above predicts, fits slightly better in-sample: \(-2\ln\hat L_B = 94\) (equivalently \(\ln\hat L_B = -47\)). Model B's extra three parameters bought it a 6-point improvement in deviance. Is that worth it?

AIC:

\[ \text{AIC}_A = 2(2) + 100 = 4 + 100 = 104 \]
\[ \text{AIC}_B = 2(5) + 94 = 10 + 94 = 104 \]

A genuine tie: \(\text{AIC}_A = \text{AIC}_B = 104\). Model B's 6-point deviance improvement was worth exactly its 6-point AIC penalty increase (\(2\times3\) extra parameters) — no more, no less. AIC is indifferent between them; neither model has "won." That a model with three extra free parameters and a genuinely better maximized likelihood still only manages a tie is the complexity penalty doing precisely its job.

BIC (with \(n=100\), so \(\ln(100)\approx4.605\)):

\[ \text{BIC}_A = 2\ln(100) + 100 \approx 2(4.605) + 100 = 9.21 + 100 = 109.21 \]
\[ \text{BIC}_B = 5\ln(100) + 94 \approx 5(4.605) + 94 = 23.03 + 94 = 117.03 \]

BIC prefers Model A by roughly \(117.03 - 109.21 \approx 7.8\) points — a clear, non-trivial margin, not a tie. The same 6-point in-sample improvement that exactly offset AIC's penalty is nowhere near enough to offset BIC's steeper, sample-size-scaled charge for those same three extra parameters (\(3 \times \ln(100) \approx 13.8\) points of penalty, versus AIC's flat \(3\times2=6\)). This is the worked arithmetic behind the earlier claim: the same pair of models, the same data, and AIC calls it a dead heat while BIC calls it a clear win for the simpler model — because BIC's penalty for those three extra parameters is more than double AIC's at this sample size.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

In applied work, AIC and BIC are computed for every model in a candidate set (e.g. every ARIMA order up to some maximum, every number of hidden Markov states, every lag length for a regression), and the model with the lowest value is preferred. A common refinement, from Burnham & Anderson's model-selection literature, is to look at the gap \(\Delta_i = \text{AIC}_i - \text{AIC}_{\min}\) for every model rather than just crowning the single winner — the same "don't over-read one number" discipline as reading a p-value without its context:

Δ ≤ 2
substantial support
effectively tied with the best model
Δ 4–7
considerably less support
a real but not overwhelming gap
Δ > 10
essentially no support
safely ruled out relative to the leader

Two further practical caveats matter as much as the formula itself:

AIC/BIC only rank a candidate set against each other — they say nothing about whether any of them fit well in an absolute sense. The best model in a set of five bad models is still the best of five bad models; a low AIC is a relative, not absolute, statement. And the comparison is only valid across models fit on the identical response variable and identical usable sample — you cannot compare AIC across a model fit on levels versus one fit on differences, or (a subtlety specific to time series and directly relevant to the trading scenario below) across an AR(1) and an AR(5) fit carelessly, because the AR(5) model consumes 5 initial observations as lagged inputs rather than as fitted data points, effectively shrinking its usable \(n\) relative to the AR(1) model unless both are re-fit on the same trimmed, aligned sample. For small samples relative to \(k\) (a common rule of thumb: \(n/k < 40\)), the standard refinement is the corrected \(\text{AIC}_c = \text{AIC} + \frac{2k(k+1)}{n-k-1}\), which adds a further, sharper penalty that matters more when data is scarce relative to the number of parameters being estimated.

Real-world trading scenario

Choosing a mean-reversion spread's lag length

A researcher is deciding how many lags to use in an autoregressive model of a mean-reversion spread — say, a demeaned FX cross-pair spread believed to revert toward zero. Two candidates are on the table, fit on the same \(n=100\) days of the (already demeaned, so no separate intercept is needed) spread: Model 1, a 1-lag AR model with \(k=2\) free parameters (the lag-1 mean-reversion coefficient, plus the residual variance the Gaussian likelihood also estimates), and Model 2, a 5-lag AR model with \(k=5\) free parameters (five lag coefficients spanning the last week; for simplicity, and to keep this example directly comparable to the one worked through above, the residual-variance parameter is rolled into that same \(k=5\) count rather than treated as a separate 6th parameter — a common simplification, though careful software will report it separately). Reusing the worked example's exact deviance numbers for a direct, checkable comparison: \(-2\ln\hat L_1 = 100\) and \(-2\ln\hat L_2 = 94\).

Model 2's extra lags give it a better in-sample deviance, exactly as the nesting argument guarantees they must: \(-2\ln\hat L_1 = 100\) versus \(-2\ln\hat L_2 = 94\). Read naively — "the 5-lag model explains the data better" — that sounds like a reason to prefer it. But running the same AIC/BIC arithmetic as the worked example: AIC ties the two models exactly at 104, and BIC prefers the 1-lag model by roughly 7.8 points. Both criteria say the same thing here: the extra four lags improved the in-sample fit by less than the going rate for four more free parameters — AIC says "no better than a coin flip between them," and BIC, with its steeper, sample-size-scaled penalty, says "meaningfully prefer the simpler model." Neither criterion finds evidence that lags 2 through 5 are pulling their weight.

The trading interpretation is direct: those four extra lag coefficients were very likely fitting sampling noise in this particular 100-day window, not real, persistent mean-reversion structure in the spread. A model that spent its extra parameters on noise will typically fit that specific noise's shape and nothing that recurs in a new sample — so choosing the 1-lag model here is not "settling for less"; it is choosing the model more likely to have the same coefficients still describe the spread on data the researcher hasn't seen yet, which is the entire point of the exercise. This is the same trade-off Lego Principle 5 requires everywhere else in this repo: a model selection criterion is a cheap, honest first filter for exactly this question, standing in for — not replacing — a genuine out-of-sample check before either model is trusted with real risk.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where this goes wrong in practice

Treating AIC/BIC as an absolute goodness-of-fit measure. A low AIC only means "best among the candidates you actually compared" — it says nothing about whether any of them describe the data well in an absolute sense. A set of five badly-specified models still has a "winner" by AIC; that win doesn't make it a good model.

Comparing models fit on different effective sample sizes. An AR(1) and an AR(5) fit to "the same" time series do not automatically share the same usable \(n\) — the AR(5) model loses its first 5 observations to lagged inputs unless both models are re-fit on the same trimmed, aligned window. Comparing their raw AIC/BIC values without aligning \(n\) first silently compares apples to a slightly different set of apples.

Comparing non-nested models, or models on different response variables, as if the comparison were automatically valid. AIC/BIC assume the likelihoods being compared are computed on the identical data and response (e.g. you cannot validly compare an AIC computed on levels against one computed on first differences, or two models built on different link functions, without adjusting for the change of variables in the likelihood).

Over-interpreting a small gap as decisive. A \(\Delta\)AIC or \(\Delta\)BIC of 1 or 2 is not meaningfully different from a tie (as the worked example's exact AIC tie shows directly) — treat small gaps as "roughly indistinguishable," not as a confident preference for whichever model happened to land a fraction of a point lower.

Letting AIC/BIC substitute for a genuine out-of-sample check. Both criteria are principled, cheap, in-sample-computable estimates of an out-of-sample-oriented quantity — they are not the same thing as actually holding out data and checking. A model that "wins" on AIC/BIC still has to prove itself (per Lego Principle 5) on a true in-sample/out-of-sample split with realistic costs before it earns any trust; the criteria narrow the candidate set honestly, they don't replace validating the survivor.

Scenario drill · an illustrative lag-length choice

Five lags fit better. Do you trade them?

You model a demeaned FX cross spread that you believe reverts to zero. On the same 100 days you fit a one-lag model (2 parameters, deviance 100) and a five-lag model (5 parameters, deviance 94). AIC scores both at 104. BIC scores them 109.2 and 117.0. A colleague wants to trade the five-lag model because it "explains the data better". What do you do?

  1. Trade the five-lag model. Its in-sample fit is better.
    It had to be better. A model that nests another can never fit worse in sample. The extra lags bought 6 points of fit, no more than AIC's price for them and well under BIC's.
  2. Take the one-lag model, make sure both were fitted on the same trimmed window, then test it out of sample before risking capital.
    Neither criterion finds that lags 2 to 5 earn their keep. Aligning the sample removes the unequal-n trap, since the five-lag model uses its first five days as inputs. The out-of-sample test is the real verdict.
  3. Call it a tie, since AIC cannot separate them, and pick either one.
    A fair reading of AIC alone. But BIC's 7.8-point gap is a clear preference, and in a tie the simpler model is the safer bet. It also skips the out-of-sample check.
  4. Keep adding lags until the fit stops improving, then trade that.
    The fit never stops improving: each extra lag can only match or beat the last model in sample. This rule always ends at the biggest model you are willing to build.

What the numbers say. This is an illustrative case built on the lesson's worked example, not a historical event. The four extra lags were most likely fitting noise in that 100-day window. The one-lag model is the one more likely to keep the same coefficients on data it has not seen, and it still has to pass a genuine out-of-sample test before it earns any trust.

Section 04

Key Takeaways

✓Raw fit always favors the bigger model. A nesting model can match or beat a smaller one's log-likelihood by construction — raw likelihood or \(R^2\) alone can never validly choose between models of different sizes.
✓AIC and BIC both do "fit minus a complexity penalty," they just price it differently. AIC charges a flat \(2\) per parameter; BIC charges \(\ln(n)\) per parameter — stricter than AIC for any \(n>e^2\approx7.4\).
✓AIC and BIC optimize different targets. AIC is tuned for out-of-sample predictive accuracy (≈ leave-one-out CV); BIC is tuned for recovering the true, most-parsimonious model and is consistent as \(n\to\infty\), which AIC is not.
✓The worked example shows they can disagree outright. A tie on AIC (104 vs. 104) becomes a clear ~7.8-point BIC win for the simpler model — same data, same fit improvement, different verdict.
⚠AIC/BIC only rank a candidate set against itself. A low AIC is a relative statement — the best of five bad models still "wins," which doesn't make it a good model.
⚠Comparisons require identical usable data. An AR(1) vs. AR(5) comparison is invalid unless both are re-fit on the same trimmed, aligned sample — the AR(5) model otherwise silently loses observations to lagged inputs.
⚠Neither criterion replaces a genuine OOS check. A model that "wins" on AIC/BIC still has to prove itself (per Lego Principle 5) on a true in-sample/out-of-sample split with real costs before it earns any trust — and this repo has no coded AIC/BIC step yet.
ℹHow it reached trading: setting the lag dial. By the 1980s AIC and BIC were the default for choosing lags in forecasting models; AIC for prediction, BIC for parsimony, walk-forward tests as the final judge.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. Why does raw log-likelihood (or raw \(R^2\)) always weakly favor a model with more parameters, even parameters that describe nothing real?
Whenever one model nests another (the larger model reduces to the smaller one by setting some parameters to a fixed value, typically zero), the larger model's maximum-likelihood search happens over a strictly larger parameter space that includes every point the smaller model could reach. It can therefore always match the smaller model's fit exactly and, generically, do at least marginally better by moving the extra parameters away from zero to track whatever pattern — real or pure sampling noise — exists in that specific sample. This is a mechanical consequence of optimizing over a bigger space, not evidence the extra parameters are meaningful; it's the same reason OLS \(R^2\) can never fall when a predictor is added.
Q2. Write down the AIC and BIC formulas and explain, in one sentence, why lower values are preferred for both.
\(\text{AIC} = 2k - 2\ln\hat L\) and \(\text{BIC} = k\ln(n) - 2\ln\hat L\). Lower is better for both because each is "badness of fit" (\(-2\ln\hat L\), which falls as fit improves) plus a complexity penalty that rises with \(k\) — so a lower total means a model achieved good fit without spending too many parameters to get there, while a higher total means either a poor fit, an over-parameterized model, or both.
Q3. Why does BIC penalize complexity more harshly than AIC as sample size \(n\) grows, and at roughly what sample size does BIC's per-parameter penalty first exceed AIC's?
AIC charges a flat 2 points per parameter regardless of sample size, while BIC charges \(\ln(n)\) per parameter — a penalty that grows (albeit slowly, logarithmically) as more data is collected. The two penalties are equal exactly when \(\ln(n)=2\), i.e. \(n=e^2\approx7.39\); for essentially any dataset with more than about 8 observations, \(\ln(n)>2\) and BIC is already the stricter of the two, with the gap widening as \(n\) increases further (e.g. at \(n=100\), BIC's per-parameter charge of \(\ln(100)\approx4.6\) is more than double AIC's flat 2).
Q4. In the worked example, Model A (\(k=2\)) and Model B (\(k=5\)) tie exactly on AIC at 104 each, but BIC prefers Model A by about 7.8 points. Why did the same three extra parameters "buy" so much less under BIC?
The three extra parameters improved the deviance by 6 points (from 100 to 94). Under AIC, three extra parameters cost \(3\times2=6\) points of penalty — an exact wash, hence the tie. Under BIC at \(n=100\), three extra parameters cost \(3\times\ln(100)\approx13.8\) points of penalty — more than double the 6-point improvement they bought, leaving Model B clearly worse off net. The improvement in fit was identical in both cases; only the price tag for the parameters that produced it changed, because BIC's per-parameter charge (\(\ln n\)) is larger than AIC's (2) once \(n>e^2\).
Q5. In what sense is AIC "oriented toward out-of-sample predictive performance" while BIC is oriented toward "finding the true model"? Are these the same goal?
They are related but distinct goals. AIC is derived to approximately minimize expected Kullback-Leibler divergence between the fitted model and the true (possibly infinitely complex, never exactly capturable) data-generating process, and is asymptotically equivalent to leave-one-out cross-validation — it is tuned for good prediction on new data, without assuming any candidate model is exactly correct. BIC is derived from a Bayesian model-comparison argument and is model-selection consistent: if the true model actually is among the candidates, BIC's probability of selecting it approaches 1 as \(n\to\infty\). AIC has no such consistency guarantee — even with infinite data it retains a fixed, nonzero chance of preferring an overly complex model, because "predicts best" and "is exactly the true model" are not the same target once the true model's exact form isn't assumed to be in the candidate set.
Q6. In the AR(1) vs. AR(5) mean-reversion spread scenario, why does the 5-lag model's better in-sample log-likelihood not mean it is the better model to actually trade?
The 5-lag model is mechanically guaranteed to fit at least as well in-sample as the 1-lag model it nests, whether or not lags 2 through 5 describe any real, persistent structure in the spread. Both AIC (an exact tie) and BIC (a clear preference for the simpler model) show that the improvement those four extra lags bought was no better than — and by BIC's stricter accounting, considerably worse than — the going rate for four more free parameters, meaning the improvement is plausibly explained by the model fitting this specific sample's noise rather than real mean-reversion dynamics. A model that fit noise in-sample has no reason to keep fitting a *different* sample's noise out-of-sample, so the simpler model is the one more likely to still work on data the researcher hasn't seen — which is what the researcher actually needs before risking capital on it.
