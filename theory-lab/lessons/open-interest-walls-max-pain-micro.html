Open Interest Walls & Max Pain, Read as a Map

The previous lesson derived why dealer hedging creates real, mechanical pressure around concentrated open interest. This one applies that mechanism to an actual options chain — turning a table of strikes and open-interest numbers into three tradable landmarks: where the walls sit, where max pain sits, and what happens once a wall breaks instead of holding.

Section 1

A chain of numbers is really a map of hedging pressure

Every large open-interest figure in a chain is a place where the previous lesson's mechanism is doing work.

🧱

A wall is a strike, not a level someone drew

Just a strike whose call or put open interest is an outlier next to its neighbors — the number is public, the "wall" label is a read of that number.

🧲

Max pain is a different kind of landmark

Not the biggest single pile of OI — the one settlement price that would leave option writers, summed across the entire chain, paying out the least.

🗺️

The map redraws itself constantly

Open interest opens and closes every session. A wall or a max-pain strike is a snapshot of right now, not a fixed structural level.

Section 2

Finding a wall: an outlier, not just a big number

A strike counts as a wall when its OI is roughly 3× or more of its immediate neighbors — not simply "the biggest bar."

Illustrative example — invented numbers, a generic instrument
260 (7,800 calls) is ~3.5× its neighbor at 255 (2,200) — a call wall. 240 (6,500 puts) is ~2.5× its neighbor at 245 (2,600) — a put wall. Neither is the max-pain strike, which lands at 250 (worked out on a later slide) — the normal case, not an exception.

This same seven-strike table — invented for this deck, not pulled from any live feed — is reused for the max-pain calculation two slides ahead, so the numbers stay traceable end to end rather than resetting with every example.

Section 3

Two shapes the same ladder can take

The mechanism is identical either way — dealer hedging around concentrated OI. The shape of the concentration is what changes the outcome.

Compare
OI fans out fairly evenly across nearby strikes. No single strike towers over its neighbors, so the dealer-hedging flow at any one strike stays modest — a broken level nudges price, it doesn't launch it.
One strike — usually a put wall a single strike from spot — holds an outsized share of the chain's OI, much of it written by dealers running a continuous short-gamma hedge. Breach that level and the same flow that was dampening moves near it can flip to accelerating them, because dealers must trade to stay hedged either way.
Section 4

What actually happens when a wall breaks

1

Heavy short OI sits near spot

A large chunk of the wall's open interest is held by dealers who are net short those contracts and delta-hedge continuously.

2

While it holds, hedging dampens the move

As price approaches, dealer rebalancing trades against the move — the previous lesson's mechanism, working as designed and producing the "wall" behavior.

3

Once it's decisively broken, the same flow reverses role

The dealers unwinding or re-hedging that position must now trade with the move to stay hedged — buying into a rally, selling into a decline — instead of fading it.

4

That reversal is the "squeeze"

Not a short-stock squeeze in the classic sense — a short-gamma squeeze, where forced hedging flow, not new information, is what accelerates the move past the old wall.

The strike that was resistance on the way up can become fuel on the way through it — same open interest, same hedging mechanism, opposite effect, purely because the direction dealers must trade to stay hedged flips once the level gives way.
The load-bearing assumption

This entire mechanism only runs on the fraction of that OI actually held by delta-hedging market makers. Some of the same headline number belongs to directional traders, covered-call writers, or unrelated hedges — none of whom rebalance this way. The split isn't observable from the OI figure alone, so a big wall is an upper bound on squeeze potential, not a guarantee of it.

Section 5

Max pain: sum the whole chain, then minimize

At expiry, a writer owes exactly the intrinsic value of every option that finished in the money — nothing more, nothing less.

The formula, piece by piece
CallPain(S)
=
Σ call OIk × max(0, S−k)
PutPain(S)
=
Σ put OIk × max(0, k−S)
TotalPain(S)
=
CallPain(S) + PutPain(S)
🔵

CallPain(S) — payout owed on ITM calls

Every strike below the candidate S contributes S−k times its call OI; strikes at or above S contribute nothing.

🟠

PutPain(S) — payout owed on ITM puts

The mirror image — every strike above S contributes k−S times its put OI.

🟢

Max pain = the S that minimizes TotalPain(S)

Evaluate S at each strike in the chain, summing across the entire chain each time — not one strike's own OI compared to today's price.

A strike can carry a huge single-side concentration and still be an expensive candidate settlement price overall, because settling there also puts every strike below it deep in the money for calls (or above it, for puts). That's exactly why max pain in the earlier chart (250) landed away from both the call wall (260) and the put wall (240).

Section 6

Why "minimize," not "maximize"

Two genuinely different mechanisms, often discussed as if they're one.

Dealer-hedging channel

A mechanical, moment-to-moment rebalancing process that runs continuously as price moves — regardless of expiry. This is the previous lesson's entire mechanism, in force at any time, on any day.

Max-pain channel

A payout-incentive argument about one moment: settlement. Writers collectively pay out exactly the aggregate intrinsic value of whatever finishes ITM. The S that minimizes that is the one outcome uniformly in sellers' favor.

Since a payout to a writer is exactly the gain to the corresponding holder, the price that minimizes aggregate writer payout is also the price at which option buyers, collectively, gain the least. That symmetry — not a conspiracy — is the entire origin of "price gravitates toward max pain": if writers (typically larger, better-capitalized participants) have any influence on where price settles, this is the one outcome uniformly in their favor.
Where this needs qualifying

The two channels can reinforce each other, and often get talked about as if they were the same thing. They aren't: one is a mechanical flow that never stops; the other is an aggregate incentive that only bites at one instant. Conflating them is a common, avoidable source of overconfidence in "the price has to go there."

Section 9

How solid is "gravitates toward max pain," really?

The economic logic from Section 6 is real. The evidence that it actually moves price is much thinner than trading folklore implies.

📉

The evidence is thin and mixed

Academic studies of expiry-day "pinning" do find some effect for some names — but attribute most of it to dealer gamma hedging, the mechanical channel, not to the max-pain aggregate specifically. Direct tests of the max-pain hypothesis on its own generally don't find it reliably beating chance.

🙅

Nobody is actually enforcing it

Unlike dealer hedging — a literal, mechanical trade someone places — max pain describes an incentive spread across thousands of anonymous, uncoordinated writers. There's no single desk trading "to hit max pain."

🔁

It's easy to confirm after the fact

Because it's recomputed continuously and every chain has many candidate strikes, some strike is nearly always "close" to wherever price actually lands — which makes confirmation cheap and a genuine advance prediction hard.

🧭

Best read as incentive, not forecast

Useful for describing who benefits where in the chain. Weak, on its own, as a standalone signal for where price is headed next.

Where this needs qualifying

Treating max pain — or any wall — as a guaranteed target is the most common way this gets over-trusted. A scheduled data release or enough directional conviction from other participants can override either one entirely. And a wall computed from yesterday's close can already be stale: OI closes out constantly, so the same strike can be a strong wall one session and a materially weaker one the next, with no change in price at all.

Section 10

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

What makes a strike a "wall"?

The threshold is local — OI compared to nearby strikes — not a raw, chain-wide maximum.
Question 2

Max pain is the settlement price that...

It's a whole-chain minimum of aggregate writer payout — which is also, by construction, the minimum aggregate gain to holders.
Question 3

A heavily-shorted OI wall right next to spot breaks decisively. What tends to happen to dealer hedging flow there?

Dealers who were fading the move to stay hedged now have to trade with it instead — the "squeeze" is that reversal, not new information hitting the tape.
Question 4

Which is the more accurate description of the evidence behind "price gravitates toward max pain"?

The writer-incentive logic is sound math; whether it's a demonstrated price-moving force, distinct from dealer hedging, is genuinely contested.
Question 5

Three strikes: 245 (call 1,500 / put 4,000), 250 (call 2,200 / put 2,200), 255 (call 5,000 / put 1,200). Which is max pain?

Same numbers as the live calculator two slides back — 250 minimizes the whole-chain payout even though it isn't the biggest single OI figure anywhere in the table.
Section 11

What to carry forward

01

A wall is a strike whose OI is an outlier next to its neighbors (roughly 3×+) — a local property, not a chain-wide maximum.

02

Max pain minimizes aggregate writer payout across the entire chain — evaluate every candidate S, sum CallPain(S) + PutPain(S) over every strike, then take the minimum.

03

Dealer hedging and max pain are two distinct mechanisms that can reinforce each other — one is continuous and mechanical, the other is a one-instant payout incentive.

04

A squeeze is a role reversal, not new information — hedging flow that was dampening a move near a wall can flip to accelerating it once the wall breaks.

05

The evidence that max pain itself moves price is thin and contested — treat it as a description of writer incentives, not a forecast.

06

Walls and max pain are live snapshots — open interest closes out every session, so re-check rather than assume persistence.

What this now lets you do

Read a real options chain as a rough expected range — put wall to call wall — with max pain as a secondary pull as expiry nears, while knowing exactly how much confidence that pull deserves. Tell a mechanical dealer-hedging effect apart from a payout-incentive argument when someone conflates the two. Recognise a squeeze setup before it breaks, not just after, by checking how concentrated and how close to spot the short OI actually is.
