Open Interest Walls & Max Pain, Read as a Map

The previous lesson derived why dealer hedging creates real, mechanical pressure around concentrated open interest. This one applies that mechanism to an actual options chain — turning a table of strikes and open-interest numbers into three tradable landmarks: where the walls sit, where max pain sits, and what happens once a wall breaks instead of holding.

The whole idea
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

An options chain is just a spreadsheet of strikes and open interest. What am I meant to see in it?

A map. Every big number is a place where hedging flow can build up. The two landmarks people look for are the walls and max pain.

Start with the walls.

A wall is an outlier, not just a big number. In the lesson's chain, the two-sixty strike has seven thousand eight hundred calls, against two thousand two hundred next door. That's three and a half times, past the usual three-times bar, so it's the call wall. Below spot, two-forty has six thousand five hundred puts against two thousand six hundred: a put wall.

And the usual read is a range between them.

Roughly two-forty to two-sixty, while that open interest stays put. That last part matters more than people think.

So what is max pain?

A different idea. At expiry, option writers pay out on everything that finishes in the money. Pick a settlement price and add up what they'd owe across the whole chain. Do it at every strike. The cheapest one for the writers is max pain.

Give me the number.

Settle at two-fifty and the calls below cost thirty-four thousand, the puts above forty thousand five hundred. Seventy-four thousand five hundred in all, the lowest of any strike. So max pain is two-fifty. Notice it isn't either wall.

And price gets pulled there into expiry?

That's the folk claim. The logic is real, it's the outcome writers like best. But tests of max pain as a magnet haven't supported it. It describes who loses least. Nobody enforces it.

What about walls breaking?

Walls are snapshots. In the lesson's scenario the put wall holds once, then profit-taking thins it to about five thousand one hundred, and the next test goes straight through. The strike didn't change. The open interest did.

And GameStop?

January twenty twenty-one. Retail buyers piled into short-dated calls, the call walls were overrun and rebuilt higher within days, and the stock went from under twenty dollars to an intraday four hundred and eighty-three. Whether dealers hedging those calls drove it is still contested.

So the one-line version?

Walls and max pain tell you where positions sit today. Re-read them every session, and don't mistake them for a forecast.

Section 1

A chain of numbers is really a map of hedging pressure

Every large open-interest figure in a chain is a place where the previous lesson's mechanism is doing work.

🧱

A wall is a strike, not a level someone drew

Just a strike whose call or put open interest is an outlier next to its neighbors — the number is public, the "wall" label is a read of that number.

🧲

Max pain is a different kind of landmark

Not the biggest single pile of OI — the one settlement price that would leave option writers, summed across the entire chain, paying out the least.

🗺️

The map redraws itself constantly

Open interest opens and closes every session. A wall or a max-pain strike is a snapshot of right now, not a fixed structural level.

Section 2

Finding a wall: an outlier, not just a big number

A strike counts as a wall when its OI is roughly 3× or more of its immediate neighbors — not simply "the biggest bar."

Illustrative example — invented numbers, a generic instrument
260 (7,800 calls) is ~3.5× its neighbor at 255 (2,200) — a call wall. 240 (6,500 puts) is ~2.5× its neighbor at 245 (2,600) — a put wall. Neither is the max-pain strike, which lands at 250 (worked out on a later slide) — the normal case, not an exception.

Section 3

Two shapes the same ladder can take

The mechanism is identical either way — dealer hedging around concentrated OI. The shape of the concentration is what changes the outcome.

Compare
OI fans out fairly evenly across nearby strikes. No single strike towers over its neighbors, so the dealer-hedging flow at any one strike stays modest — a broken level nudges price, it doesn't launch it.
One strike — usually a put wall a single strike from spot — holds an outsized share of the chain's OI, much of it written by dealers running a continuous short-gamma hedge. Short gamma means that hedging trades with the move, and it is most intense right around the strike, so a push through the level can accelerate sharply.
Section 4

What actually happens when a wall breaks

1

Heavy short OI sits near spot

A large chunk of the wall's open interest is held by dealers who are net short those contracts and delta-hedge continuously.

2

A wall that holds needs long gamma

Dealer rebalancing only trades against the move (the "wall" behavior) where dealers are long gamma. Where they are short the OI, it trades with the move from the start.

3

Near and through the strike, short-gamma hedging peaks

For dealers short that OI, gamma is largest around the strike, so their hedging trades with the move — buying into a rally, selling into a decline — hardest exactly as the level is crossed.

4

That reversal is the "squeeze"

Not a short-stock squeeze in the classic sense — a short-gamma squeeze, where forced hedging flow, not new information, is what accelerates the move past the old wall.

The strike that was read as resistance on the way up can become fuel on the way through it — same open interest, same hedging mechanism, with the forced trading heaviest right where the level gives way.
The load-bearing assumption

This entire mechanism only runs on the fraction of that OI actually held by delta-hedging market makers. Some of the same headline number belongs to directional traders, covered-call writers, or unrelated hedges — none of whom rebalance this way. The split isn't observable from the OI figure alone, so a big wall is an upper bound on squeeze potential, not a guarantee of it.

Section 5

Max pain: sum the whole chain, then minimize

At expiry, a writer owes exactly the intrinsic value of every option that finished in the money — nothing more, nothing less.

The formula, piece by piece
CallPain(S)
=
Σ call OIk × max(0, S−k)
PutPain(S)
=
Σ put OIk × max(0, k−S)
TotalPain(S)
=
CallPain(S) + PutPain(S)
🔵

CallPain(S) — payout owed on ITM calls

Every strike below the candidate S contributes S−k times its call OI; strikes at or above S contribute nothing.

🟠

PutPain(S) — payout owed on ITM puts

The mirror image — every strike above S contributes k−S times its put OI.

🟢

Max pain = the S that minimizes TotalPain(S)

Evaluate S at each strike in the chain, summing across the entire chain each time — not one strike's own OI compared to today's price.

A strike can carry a huge single-side concentration and still be an expensive candidate settlement price overall, because settling there also puts every strike below it deep in the money for calls (or above it, for puts). That's exactly why max pain in the earlier chart (250) landed away from both the call wall (260) and the put wall (240).

Section 6

Why "minimize," not "maximize"

Two genuinely different mechanisms, often discussed as if they're one.

Dealer-hedging channel

A mechanical, moment-to-moment rebalancing process that runs continuously as price moves — regardless of expiry. This is the previous lesson's entire mechanism, in force at any time, on any day.

Max-pain channel

A payout-incentive argument about one moment: settlement. Writers collectively pay out exactly the aggregate intrinsic value of whatever finishes ITM. The S that minimizes that is the one outcome uniformly in sellers' favor.

Since a payout to a writer is exactly the gain to the corresponding holder, the price that minimizes aggregate writer payout is also the price at which option buyers, collectively, gain the least. That symmetry — not a conspiracy — is the entire origin of "price gravitates toward max pain": if writers (typically larger, better-capitalized participants) have any influence on where price settles, this is the one outcome uniformly in their favor.
Where this needs qualifying

The two channels can reinforce each other, and often get talked about as if they were the same thing. They aren't: one is a mechanical flow that never stops; the other is an aggregate incentive that only bites at one instant. Conflating them is a common, avoidable source of overconfidence in "the price has to go there."

Section 9

How solid is "gravitates toward max pain," really?

The economic logic from Section 6 is real. The evidence that it actually moves price is much thinner than trading folklore implies.

📉

The evidence is thin and mixed

Academic studies of expiry-day "pinning" do find some effect for some names — but attribute most of it to dealer gamma hedging, the mechanical channel, not to the max-pain aggregate specifically. Direct tests of the max-pain hypothesis on its own generally don't find it reliably beating chance.

🙅

Nobody is actually enforcing it

Unlike dealer hedging — a literal, mechanical trade someone places — max pain describes an incentive spread across thousands of anonymous, uncoordinated writers. There's no single desk trading "to hit max pain."

🔁

It's easy to confirm after the fact

Because it's recomputed continuously and every chain has many candidate strikes, some strike is nearly always "close" to wherever price actually lands — which makes confirmation cheap and a genuine advance prediction hard.

🧭

Best read as incentive, not forecast

Useful for describing who benefits where in the chain. Weak, on its own, as a standalone signal for where price is headed next.

Where this needs qualifying

Treating max pain — or any wall — as a guaranteed target is the most common way this gets over-trusted. A scheduled data release or enough directional conviction from other participants can override either one entirely. And a wall computed from yesterday's close can already be stale: OI closes out constantly, so the same strike can be a strong wall one session and a materially weaker one the next, with no change in price at all.

Section 10 · It actually happened

GameStop, January 2021: walls overrun and rebuilt

It actually happened

A heavily shorted stock (reported short interest above 100% of float) met a wave of retail buying in shares and short-dated out-of-the-money calls. As each call strike was overrun, buyers moved higher. The price went from under $20 to an intraday $483 on 28 January. Melvin Capital needed about $2.75bn from Citadel and Point72; several brokers restricted buying that day.

Section 11

Where the textbook breaks, and what this desk found

The folk claim: price gravitates to max pain into expiry, and walls fence the range.

Desk test: nothing found oi-max-pain: tested properly for the first time 2026-09-10: null. The OI publish-lag timing risk was tested directly and survives; DTE, gamma, sign convention and spot basis all verified. A level to know about, not a magnet.
Not tested here Walls as range fences: queued, not yet tested. A fair test: point-in-time walls from that morning's OI vs random bands of the same width, matched volatility, rule fixed in advance.
Where the textbook version breaks

Walls and max pain treat the chain as a fixed snapshot held by continuous hedgers, on a stock anyone can trade. In January 2021 open interest was rebuilt higher daily, short interest exceeded the float, and buying was partly switched off. One morning's chain described a market gone by the afternoon.

Section 12

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

What makes a strike a "wall"?

The threshold is local — OI compared to nearby strikes — not a raw, chain-wide maximum.
Question 2

Max pain is the settlement price that...

It's a whole-chain minimum of aggregate writer payout — which is also, by construction, the minimum aggregate gain to holders.
Question 3

A heavily-shorted OI wall right next to spot breaks decisively. What tends to happen to dealer hedging flow there?

Dealers short that OI must trade with the move to stay hedged, and gamma is largest right around the strike — the "squeeze" is that forced flow, not new information hitting the tape.
Question 4

Which is the more accurate description of the evidence behind "price gravitates toward max pain"?

The writer-incentive logic is sound math; whether it's a demonstrated price-moving force, distinct from dealer hedging, is genuinely contested.
Question 5

Three strikes: 245 (call 1,500 / put 4,000), 250 (call 2,200 / put 2,200), 255 (call 5,000 / put 1,200). Which is max pain?

Same numbers as the live calculator two slides back — 250 minimizes the whole-chain payout even though it isn't the biggest single OI figure anywhere in the table.

Scenario drill · 27 January 2021

The chain says one thing, the tape says another

It is the evening of 27 January 2021. GameStop has gone from under \$20 at the start of the month to hundreds of dollars. Its short interest is reported above 100% of the free float, Melvin Capital has needed about \$2.75 billion from Citadel and Point72, and retail buyers keep piling into short-dated, out-of-the-money calls. A max-pain calculation for Friday's expiry sits far below the price, and there are big call walls above it. How do you use the chain for tomorrow?

  1. Short the shares and wait for the price to drift down to max pain into Friday.
    Max pain only describes where writers would lose least, and tests of it as a magnet have not supported the pull. Here the chain is also rebuilt higher every day, and with short interest above the float you would be joining the most crowded side of the trade.
  2. Write calls at the next big strike up, because a call wall is resistance.
    A call wall leans against a rally only when dealers are long those calls. Here customers bought them, so the writers who hedge are short them and must buy shares as price climbs toward the strike. Each strike was being overrun and rebuilt higher within days.
  3. Treat the chain as a snapshot that may be stale by the afternoon: stand aside, or keep any position small with risk you can define in advance.
    This is the lesson's own warning at its most extreme. Walls and max pain describe where positions sat this morning. When open interest is rebuilt daily and trading conditions can change overnight, neither one gives you a level you can lean on.
  4. Buy the shares to ride the squeeze, with a stop below.
    The wall-break momentum read is a real one, and the call walls are a plausible accelerant. But the SEC staff later found no evidence that dealer hedging drove the rise, and in a market this violent a stop can fill far from where you set it. Defensible only at a small size.

What happened. On 28 January GameStop hit an intraday high of \$483. That day Robinhood and several other brokers restricted customers to selling the stock, not buying it; Robinhood said its clearing-house deposit requirement had jumped. The price fell sharply from its high. The SEC staff report in October 2021 attributed the rise mainly to a broad wave of buying, with short covering only a small part, and found no evidence of a gamma squeeze: market makers were, on its data, buying calls rather than writing them. Nobody was enforcing any max-pain level.

Sources: SEC staff report on equity and options market structure conditions in early 2021, 14 Oct 2021 (intraday high of $483.00 on 28 January; short interest above 100% of float; short covering a small fraction of buying; no evidence of a gamma squeeze); Benzinga, "GameStop Short Seller Melvin Gets $2.75B Funding From Citadel, Point72", 25 Jan 2021; CNBC, "Robinhood says restrictions on GameStop due to tenfold increase in deposit requirements", 30 Jan 2021.

Section 13

What to carry forward

01

A wall is a strike whose OI is an outlier next to its neighbors (roughly 3×+) — a local property, not a chain-wide maximum.

02

Max pain minimizes aggregate writer payout across the entire chain — evaluate every candidate S, sum CallPain(S) + PutPain(S) over every strike, then take the minimum.

03

Dealer hedging and max pain are two distinct mechanisms that can reinforce each other — one is continuous and mechanical, the other is a one-instant payout incentive.

04

A squeeze is forced hedging, not new information — short-gamma hedging trades with the move and peaks around the strike, so a broken wall can accelerate.

05

The evidence that max pain itself moves price is thin and contested — treat it as a description of writer incentives, not a forecast.

06

Walls and max pain are live snapshots — open interest closes out every session, so re-check rather than assume persistence.

07

GameStop, January 2021: call walls overrun and rebuilt higher within days; the "gamma squeeze" share of it is contested. Desk verdict: max pain is NOT a magnet (oi-max-pain, null); walls as range fences remain untested.

What this now lets you do

Read a real options chain as a rough expected range — put wall to call wall — with max pain as a commonly claimed pull that tests have not supported, and knowing exactly how much confidence that deserves. Tell a mechanical dealer-hedging effect apart from a payout-incentive argument when someone conflates the two. Recognise a squeeze setup before it breaks, not just after, by checking how concentrated and how close to spot the short OI actually is.
