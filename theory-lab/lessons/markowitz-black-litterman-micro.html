Markowitz & Black-Litterman, From One Position to a Whole Book

Harry Markowitz gave portfolio construction its first real mathematics in 1952 — and practitioners almost immediately discovered that mathematics was so sensitive to its own inputs it was barely usable as written. This is the story in two halves: why holding several positions "together" is a genuinely different problem from holding any one of them alone, why the clean textbook answer to that problem quietly breaks in practice, and the Bayesian fix — starting from what the market already believes, then nudging it — that turned it into something real desks actually run.

Section 1

A portfolio's risk is not the average of its parts

Two positions can look identical in isolation and behave completely differently once they're held together.

⚖

Same stats, different job

Two pairs with the same expected return and the same volatility can still be very different portfolio holdings — one might amplify everything else you own, the other might quietly cancel it out.

☔

The umbrella test

N people all packing the same umbrella carry N× the bulk of one umbrella. Half packing umbrellas and half packing sunscreen prepares the group for more, with less total stuff — because the two kinds of gear partly substitute for each other.

±

Co-movement is the missing variable

A position's risk contribution to a portfolio isn't its own volatility — it's that volatility filtered through how it moves with everything else already held.

↘

Sometimes it subtracts

When two positions tend to move opposite each other, combining them can make the portfolio less risky than either position held alone. That's diversification benefit, not a hedge you engineered on purpose.

Markowitz's core insight, in one sentence: a portfolio's variance is not a weighted average of its parts' variances — it's a weighted sum that also depends on every pairwise co-movement between those parts, and that extra term is exactly where diversification benefit, or its absence, actually lives.
Section 2

Two formulas: one an average, one that isn't

Expected return adds up the easy way. Variance doesn't — and the term that breaks the pattern is the whole point.

Expected return — a plain weighted average

+

E[R_p]
60% long a pair returning 2%, 40% long a pair returning 5%: 0.6(2%) + 0.4(5%) = 3.2%. Nothing subtle — written as a dot product, E[R_p] = wᵀμ.
Variance — a quadratic form, with a cross term
w₁²σ₁²
+
w₂²σ₂²
+
2w₁w₂·Cov(R₁,R₂)
=
Var(R_p)
The first two terms are exactly what you'd expect — each position's own variance, scaled by its weight squared. The amber term is the one a "risk is just an average" intuition misses: it can be positive (correlated positions compounding each other), zero (no interaction), or negative — actively subtracting from total portfolio variance.
Written as matrices

Both formulas above are the scalar (2-asset) expansion of one compact expression covering any number of assets: Var(R_p) = wᵀΣw, where Σ is the full covariance matrix — a grid whose diagonal holds each instrument's own variance and whose off-diagonal entries hold every pairwise covariance. First Σw scales each instrument's exposure by the weights; then wᵀ(·) folds the result into one number.

Section 3

Trace every mix, and the outer edge is the frontier

Two assets: a "safe" pair (μ = 3%, σ = 8%) and a "risky" pair (μ = 4%, σ = 15%). Every weight split between them traces one point on a risk/return map — switch correlation and watch the curve bend.

When two assets move in perfect lockstep (ρ = 1), there's no diversification benefit at all — the curve is a straight line, and the lowest-risk mix is simply 100% in the lower-vol asset (σ = 8.0%). Adding the riskier asset only adds risk.
With a realistic modest positive correlation (ρ = 0.3, this lesson's running example), the curve bows left of both assets. The minimum-variance mix — 87.1% safe / 12.9% risky — actually has lower volatility (7.77%) than the "safe" asset held alone (8.0%).
Uncorrelated assets bow further left still — the minimum-variance mix is 77.9% safe / 22.1% risky, at just 7.06% volatility, comfortably below either asset's own vol on its own.
Perfectly negatively correlated assets can be combined into a genuinely near-riskless mix (σ ≈ 0.0%) at 65.2% safe / 34.8% risky — the textbook case of a perfect hedge. Real asset pairs are never quite here, but it's the limit the earlier panels are bending toward.

Section 6

The optimizer takes your guesses far too literally

Everything so far is clean, correct mathematics — and for decades it was also close to unusable in practice, for one specific reason.

🎯

Extremely sensitive to E[R], not to Σ

Covariances can be estimated with reasonable stability from historical data. Expected returns cannot — a return forecast is the least reliable, most opinion-laden number in the whole model, yet it's what the optimizer's output depends on most violently.

🌀

It's solving a ratio

The optimizer maximizes return-per-unit-of-risk. A ratio is acutely sensitive to small numerator changes when assets have similar risk profiles — a rounding-level nudge to a forecast can flip which asset looks "obviously" better.

📐

"Error-maximization," not malfunction

The optimizer isn't broken — it's doing exactly what was asked: squeeze the most return-per-variance out of whatever numbers it's given, taken as exact. It just concentrates the portfolio precisely where your estimation error happens to look most favorable.

📉

Corner solutions

The practical result: extreme long/short positions, often paired with an equally extreme offsetting position elsewhere just to keep the return target and budget constraint satisfied.

This isn't a minor footnote — it's the single most cited practical criticism of mean-variance optimization, sometimes called the "error-maximization" property, and it's the reason "just run Markowitz" was, for decades, something academics recommended and desks quietly avoided doing literally.
Section 7

A half-point forecast nudge, a ten-point weight swing

Same tangency (maximum-Sharpe) portfolio as the full lesson's worked example — safe pair μ=3%/σ=8%, risky pair σ=15%, ρ=0.3, 2% risk-free rate. Only the risky pair's forecast changes.

Forecast: risky pair returns 4.0%

Tangency weights: 62.45% safe / 37.55% risky. A sensible-looking, moderate allocation — nothing here screams "instability."

Forecast: risky pair returns 4.5%

Tangency weights: 52.12% safe / 47.88% risky. A 0.5-percentage-point forecast change — well inside any honest analyst's error bar — swung the risky weight by more than 10 points.

Nothing about the real relationship between these two pairs changed. A rounding-level adjustment to one input the forecaster was never confident about produced a materially different "optimal" portfolio. Scale this to a basket of eight or ten correlated FX pairs, where forecasts differ from each other by fractions of a percent, and the instability compounds — this is exactly the mechanism behind the notorious extreme long/short corner solutions naive Markowitz optimization is known to produce.

Section 8

Reverse the optimizer to get a prior, then nudge it

Black & Litterman's 1992 fix doesn't touch the frontier or the covariance matrix — it changes where the expected-return vector comes from.

🌐

Start from equilibrium: Π = δΣw_mkt

If today's market weights are already roughly mean-variance-optimal, they imply a specific expected-return vector Π — recovered by literally running the optimization backwards. δ is a single risk-aversion scalar, calibrated from the market portfolio's own return and variance.

🗣

Ask for views, not forecasts

Instead of a precise expected return for every instrument, the investor states a handful of specific views — "EURUSD outperforms GBPUSD by 2%" — each with its own stated confidence.

⚗

Blend by precision, not by fiat

Prior and view are combined the same way any two noisy estimates of the same quantity are combined in Bayesian updating: weighted by precision (1/variance) — the more confident side pulls harder.

🎚

The posterior feeds the optimizer

The blended result, E[R]_BL — never the raw prior alone, never the raw view alone — is what gets handed to the exact same Step-3 optimizer from Section 3.

A single strong view on one pair no longer reaches the optimizer as an exact, isolated fact — it's blended with the market's own belief about that instrument and everything correlated with it, moderated by exactly how confident the investor said they were.

Section 10

Same math underneath, very different practical behavior

Raw Markowitz
  • Needs a precise expected return for every instrument — the input it's most sensitive to and least able to estimate reliably
  • A fraction-of-a-percent forecast nudge can flip weights into extreme long/short corners
  • No native concept of "confidence" — every number is treated as exact
  • Mathematically optimal given its inputs, which is exactly the problem when the inputs are noisy guesses
Black-Litterman
  • Starts from market-implied equilibrium returns — a stable, defensible default for everything you have no view on
  • Only needs a handful of specific, limited views, each with a stated confidence
  • A modest-confidence view produces a modest tilt; the market prior still dominates unless the view is both large and confident
  • Doesn't validate the view itself — a confident but wrong view still pulls the posterior the wrong way, just not to an absurd degree

Neither model is "solved" in the sense of removing judgment — Black-Litterman moves the judgment call from "guess every instrument's return" to "state a few views and how sure you actually are of them," which is a smaller, more honest thing to be asked for.

Section 11

Nobody runs the raw optimizer on real money

🧭

Black-Litterman / Bayesian shrinkage

Moderate the expected-return inputs themselves before they ever reach the optimizer.

🔒

Hard position & turnover constraints

Cap any single position's weight, or how far the optimizer can move from a benchmark or last period's holdings, regardless of what the raw math wants.

⚖

Risk-parity / minimum-variance-only

Skip the fragile expected-return vector entirely and optimize only on the comparatively stable covariance matrix — less "optimal," far more robust.

Where this repo actually stands

Checked against the current code, not just repeated from the full lesson: nothing in this repo runs either model. portfolioBacktest/ sizes a pair trade beta-neutral off an OLS hedge ratio; the shared portfolio simulators (pylego/portfolio_sim.py and its motif variants) combine trades with fixed per-trade risk sizing and a concurrent-risk cap — none of it estimates a covariance matrix and solves for optimal weights, and none of it reverse-engineers an equilibrium prior. Every strategy here is still effectively single-instrument or single-pair; this remains the theory a future multi-instrument portfolio-construction layer would sit on, not a description of something already running.

Section 12

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Why does portfolio variance depend on more than just each position's own weight and volatility?

The cross-covariance term is exactly where diversification benefit (or its absence) lives — a weighted-average intuition misses it entirely.
Question 2

Two assets with ρ = 1 (perfectly correlated) — what does the minimum-variance mix look like?

At ρ = 1 the frontier is a straight line — mixing in the higher-vol asset can only add risk, so the lowest-risk point is holding the safer asset outright.
Question 3

Why is the raw Markowitz optimizer so much more sensitive to expected-return inputs than to the covariance matrix?

The optimizer is solving a ratio — return per unit of risk — and a ratio is acutely sensitive to small changes in a numerator built from the least reliable input in the whole model.
Question 4

What role does the market-equilibrium prior Π play in Black-Litterman?

Π = δΣw_mkt is the prior; views are the likelihood; the two are precision-weighted together into a posterior that's what actually reaches the optimizer.
Question 5

A view stated with 100% confidence, in the simplified blending calculator on this page — what happens to the posterior?

At confidence = 100%, the blend collapses to the view alone — which is also, structurally, what happens whenever raw Markowitz has no market anchor to compete against a forecast.
Section 13

What to carry forward

01

Portfolio variance is a quadratic form, wᵀΣw, not a weighted average — the cross-covariance terms are exactly where diversification benefit lives, and they can be negative.

02

The efficient frontier traces the minimum-variance mix at every achievable return; its leftmost point is the minimum-variance portfolio, and correlation controls how far left it bows.

03

Raw Markowitz is extremely sensitive to the expected-return inputs specifically — a fraction-of-a-percent forecast nudge can flip "optimal" weights into extreme corners, the well-documented "error-maximization" flaw.

04

Black-Litterman fixes this by starting from a market-equilibrium prior, Π = δΣw_mkt, and Bayesian-blending it with a handful of confidence-weighted investor views.

05

Confidence is a genuine, separate lever — the same view, stated with low versus high confidence, produces a smaller versus larger tilt away from equilibrium.

06

Black-Litterman tames the optimizer's instability; it does not validate the view — a confidently wrong view still pulls the posterior in the wrong direction, just not to an absurd degree.

What this now lets you do

Read a portfolio-optimization output with the right skepticism: an extreme allocation is usually a sign the inputs were more uncertain than the model was told, not evidence of a hidden edge. Recognise why "just run mean-variance" is a red flag on a desk, and why Black-Litterman's fix — market prior plus confidence-weighted views — is the practical default instead. Build toward a real multi-instrument allocator with the right mental model already in place, before writing a line of the optimizer itself.
