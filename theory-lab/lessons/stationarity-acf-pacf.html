Stationarity, ACF & PACF

Every mean-reversion signal, every lookback window, every Cointegration test and every ARIMA/GARCH model quietly assumes something about how a series behaves through time — the ACF and PACF are the two plain diagnostic plots that check whether that assumption holds, and if it does, exactly how much of a series' own past is genuinely informative rather than noise carried along for the ride.

TL;DR
  • Stationarity means a series' mean, variance, and autocovariance stay constant through time (depend only on lag \(k\), never on the calendar date); most modeling — AR/ARIMA, GARCH, the Cointegration residual test — quietly assumes it.
  • The ACF at lag \(k\) is the total correlation between a series and its \(k\)-lagged self, mixing direct dependence with everything carried indirectly through intervening lags; a slowly-decaying ACF (still elevated 20-30 lags out) is the classic visual tell of non-stationarity (a unit root).
  • The PACF at lag \(k\) strips out that indirect chain, measuring only what lag \(k\) adds beyond what closer lags already explain — which is exactly what lets it identify an AR model's order \(p\).
  • The Box-Jenkins signature of an AR(\(p\)) process: the ACF decays gradually (never cleanly stops) while the PACF cuts off sharply after lag \(p\) — for AR(1) specifically, PACF is large at lag 1 and near-zero from lag 2 on, even though the ACF stays visibly nonzero for several more lags.
  • Practically, reading where the PACF cuts off tells you how many of a series' own past values are genuinely informative as predictors (the model's lag order) — a principled alternative to guessing or grid-searching that number. It is not the estimation window for a rolling mean, volatility or z-score, which is a separate choice.
  • How it reached trading: Granger & Newbold's 1974 spurious-regression warning and Dickey & Fuller's 1979 unit-root test made "is it stationary?" the first question a desk asks before trading a spread or fitting a forecast model.
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

Everyone I know uses a twenty-day lookback for everything. Is that wrong?

Maybe not wrong, but it is a guess. This lesson is about checking the guess against the data.

Checking what, exactly?

Two things. First, does the series behave the same way whenever you look at it? Same average, same size of swings, same link between today and a few days ago. That is what stationary means.

And if it doesn't?

Then a twenty-day average today means something different from a twenty-day average last year. Picture a dog on a leash tied to a post. It wanders, but it always gets pulled back. That is stationary. A dog with no leash just keeps wandering. That is a random walk, and most raw price levels look like the second dog.

OK. What's the second thing?

How far back the series' own memory reaches. The first plot, the autocorrelation function, asks how much today looks like the value one day back, two days back, and so on.

Give me a number.

Take a series where each day keeps seventy percent of yesterday's distance from the mean. One day back, the correlation is point seven. Two days back, seventy percent of that, about point four nine. Three days back, about point three four. It fades, but it never stops cleanly.

So three days back still matters?

That is the trap. Three days back only looks related because it fed two days back, which fed yesterday, which fed today. It is an echo along a chain.

How do I tell an echo from real memory?

The second plot, the partial autocorrelation function. It asks: once you already know the days in between, does the value from further back add anything new? For our series you get one tall bar at lag one, then nothing.

So the first plot fades and the second one cuts off.

Exactly. That is the Box-Jenkins signature. Where the second plot cuts off tells you how many past values deserve to be predictors. Here, just one. How long a window you use to estimate the average is a separate question.

And if the first plot doesn't fade at all?

Then stop. A plot still high twenty or thirty lags out is the classic sign of a random walk. Granger and Newbold showed in 1974 that regressing one independent random walk on another routinely gives an impressive-looking fit. Pure nonsense. Run a proper unit-root test, like Dickey and Fuller's, before you trust anything.

So the one-line version?

Check the series has a home level first, then let the partial autocorrelation tell you how many past days carry real information.

Section 01

Why this matters

Almost every piece of time-series machinery in this repo and in quantitative finance generally — a rolling z-score, a lookback-window feature, an AR/ARIMA model, a GARCH volatility forecast, and the Cointegration lesson's residual test — rests on an assumption that rarely gets stated out loud: that the statistical properties of the series being modeled don't themselves drift over time. If they do drift, a "20-day average" computed today doesn't mean the same thing it meant a year ago, and any model fit on the whole history is quietly averaging together several different regimes as if they were one. Stationarity is the formal name for the assumption holding; this lesson defines it precisely, contrasts it with the non-stationary case already introduced informally by this Theory Lab's random-walk and Ornstein-Uhlenbeck material, and then introduces the two diagnostic tools — the autocorrelation function (ACF) and the partial autocorrelation function (PACF) — that let you look at a series and answer a very practical question: how far back does this series' own history actually carry usable information, and where does it stop?

That practical question shows up constantly and usually gets answered by guesswork instead of evidence — "let's use a 20-day lookback" is a decision made in almost every rolling-window feature in this codebase, and it is, whether stated or not, an implicit claim about autocorrelation structure. The ACF and PACF are the tools that let you check that claim against the data rather than assume it. They are also the direct prerequisite for reading the Cointegration lesson's Step 2 with real understanding: the Augmented Dickey-Fuller test used there to test a spread's residual for stationarity is, under the hood, built from exactly the autocorrelation ideas developed here.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Does today's weather tell you anything about next Tuesday's?

Think about how useful yesterday's temperature is for predicting today's temperature, compared to how useful the temperature from ten days ago is. Intuitively, yesterday matters a lot — weather has real short-run persistence, a hot day is usually followed by another warm one. Ten days ago matters much less directly, but it isn't zero either, because of the chain in between: ten days ago influenced nine days ago, which influenced eight days ago, and so on, link by link, down to yesterday, which influences today. Some of the apparent relationship between "ten days ago" and "today" is real direct memory, and some of it is just riding along on that chain of intermediate days. The ACF measures the total relationship at each lag — direct memory plus everything carried through the chain. The PACF asks the sharper question: once you already know all the days in between, does the value from exactly \(k\) days ago add anything extra? For weather (and for a great many financial series), the honest answer past a short horizon is no — the intervening days already captured everything the more distant one had to offer. That's exactly the distinction this lesson formalizes.

How it reached trading

A spread that might never come back

Unit-root testing · Granger & Newbold, 1974; Dickey & Fuller, 1979 — then every relative-value and macro desk

Before the 1970s, a trader or economist who regressed one trending price on another usually got an impressive fit — a high R², a large t-statistic — and took it as a relationship. Clive Granger and Paul Newbold showed in 1974, by simulation, that regressions between two completely independent random walks routinely look that significant. Yule had warned of "nonsense correlations" in 1926; Granger and Newbold made the warning impossible to ignore for anyone working with prices.

David Dickey and Wayne Fuller supplied the test in 1979: does a series have a unit root (shocks never fade) or does it revert? Nelson and Plosser (1982) then found that many US macro and price series could not reject a unit root. Engle and Granger's 1987 cointegration framework built directly on this — a spread between two prices is tradable only if the spread itself passes a stationarity test — and that became the statistical basis of pairs and relative-value trading.

On a desk today the checks come first: an ADF or KPSS test before trading a spread or regressing one market on another, and the ACF/PACF to see how long a shock persists, how many lags a signal deserves, and whether a forecast model's residuals are still hiding structure.

Section 02

The math, step by step

Watch the formula build
From the three stationarity conditions to the AR(1) autocorrelation, one piece at a time
\[ E[X_t] = \color{#fbbf24}{\mu} \]

First condition: one fixed long-run mean. The series wanders around the same level whenever you look at it.

\[ E[X_t] = \mu, \qquad \mathrm{Var}(X_t) = \color{#fbbf24}{\sigma^2} \]

Second condition: a fixed variance. The swings around that mean do not grow over time. A random walk fails here, because its variance is \(t\sigma^2\).

\[ E[X_t] = \mu, \qquad \mathrm{Var}(X_t) = \sigma^2, \qquad \mathrm{Cov}(X_t, X_{t+k}) = \color{#fbbf24}{\gamma(k)} \]

Third condition: the link between two observations depends only on how far apart they are, the lag \(k\), never on the date.

\[ \mathrm{ACF}(k) = \frac{\gamma(k)}{\color{#fbbf24}{\gamma(0)}} \]

Divide by the lag-zero autocovariance, which is just the variance. Now it is a pure correlation between −1 and 1, and \(\mathrm{ACF}(0) = 1\).

\[ \mathrm{ACF}(k) = \frac{\gamma(k)}{\gamma(0)} = \color{#10b981}{\phi^k} \quad (\text{AR}(1),\ |\phi| < 1) \]

For an AR(1) series the answer is a geometric decay. With \(\phi = 0.7\): 0.70 at lag 1, 0.49 at lag 2, 0.343 at lag 3. It fades but never cleanly stops, which is why the PACF is needed to find the cut-off.

This section builds up four ideas in order: what stationarity means precisely, how it applies to processes already seen elsewhere in this Theory Lab, what the ACF is and what its shape reveals, and what the PACF adds on top — culminating in the classic Box-Jenkins identification rule that ties ACF and PACF shape directly to model order.

1. Stationarity, precisely. A time series \(X_t\) is (weakly, or "covariance") stationary if three statistical properties hold constant through time — they depend only on how far apart two observations are, never on the calendar date itself:

\[ E[X_t] = \mu, \qquad \mathrm{Var}(X_t) = \sigma^2, \qquad \mathrm{Cov}(X_t, X_{t+k}) = \gamma(k) \]
μfixed long-run mean, constant through time
σ²fixed variance, doesn't grow or drift over time
γ(k)autocovariance — depends only on the lag k, never on the date

In words: a fixed long-run mean \(\mu\) the series fluctuates around, a fixed variance \(\sigma^2\) describing how far it typically strays from that mean, and an autocovariance \(\gamma(k)\) — how related an observation is to the one \(k\) periods away — that depends only on the lag \(k\), not on which point in the sample you check it at. This is exactly the same definition the Cointegration lesson opens with, because it is the same concept doing double duty: it's the property a mean-reverting spread needs in order to be tradeable at all, and it's also the property the ACF and PACF below are only meaningfully interpretable under. Compute an ACF on a non-stationary series and the plot itself becomes unreliable, for reasons this lesson returns to below.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Stationary just means "behaves the same way no matter when you look at it." Picture a bouncy ball dropped in the same spot every day — it always settles around the same height, bounces about the same amount, and how today's bounce relates to yesterday's is always about the same story, whether you check on a Monday or a Friday. A series that keeps drifting to new heights, or gets wilder and calmer at random over the years, is the opposite — the rules of the game are quietly changing underneath you.

2. Two processes already in this Theory Lab, reframed as stationary vs. not. The Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) lesson's process and the pure random walk covered in the Cointegration lesson are the cleanest possible contrast. An OU process, \(dX_t = \theta(\mu - X_t)\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t\), has a pull-back-to-\(\mu\) term built directly into its drift: the further \(X_t\) strays from \(\mu\), the harder it gets pulled back. That mechanism gives it a fixed long-run mean and a variance that settles to a constant \(\sigma^2/2\theta\) rather than growing forever — it is stationary by construction, which is precisely why the OU lesson could treat "the mean" and "the standard deviation" of the process as fixed numbers worth trading bands around. A pure random walk, \(X_t = X_{t-1} + \epsilon_t\), has no such pull-back term at all; as the Cointegration lesson derives, its variance grows linearly with \(t\) — \(\mathrm{Var}(X_t) = t\sigma^2\) — so there is no fixed "normal level" for it to revert to, and the first requirement of stationarity (constant variance) fails outright. Every raw FX rate, equity index, or bond-yield level in this repo is generally treated as behaving like the second case; most well-specified spreads, and most return series, are treated as closer to the first. The ACF and PACF below are the tools that let you check which world a given series is actually in, rather than assuming it.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Think of a dog on a stretchy leash tied to a post versus a dog that's wandered off with no leash at all. The leashed dog can roam a bit, but it always gets pulled back toward the post — that's a stationary process, with a home base it keeps returning to. The unleashed dog just keeps wandering farther and farther away, with nowhere it's likely to circle back to — that's the random walk, and it's exactly why it has no fixed "normal level" to measure distance from.

3. The autocorrelation function (ACF). For a (assumed-stationary) series \(X_t\), the ACF at lag \(k\) is simply the correlation between the series and a copy of itself shifted back \(k\) steps:

\[ \mathrm{ACF}(k) \;=\; \mathrm{Corr}(X_t, X_{t-k}) \;=\; \frac{\gamma(k)}{\gamma(0)} \;=\; \frac{\mathrm{Cov}(X_t, X_{t-k})}{\mathrm{Var}(X_t)} \]
γ(k)autocovariance at lag k
γ(0)variance — autocovariance at lag zero, used to normalize
ACF(k)the normalized result — a pure correlation between -1 and 1

\(\mathrm{ACF}(0)\) is always exactly 1 (any series is perfectly correlated with itself at zero lag); every other lag's value falls between -1 and 1 and answers "how related is today's value to the value \(k\) steps back." Plotting \(\mathrm{ACF}(k)\) against \(k\) for a range of lags — usually the first 10-40 — produces what's called a correlogram, and its shape is genuinely diagnostic rather than decorative:

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

The ACF just answers "how much does today's value look like the value from a few days ago" — for every possible number of days back you might ask about. It bundles together everything that could connect the two: today looking like yesterday, which looked like the day before, and so on, all chained together, without trying to tell those pieces apart.

  • White noise (no memory at all — each value is an independent draw) produces a correlogram that's near zero at every lag beyond zero, scattered randomly inside a thin significance band around zero, with no discernible pattern.
  • An AR(1)-like stationary series (this period's value depends partly on last period's, with a fixed pull toward a mean) produces a correlogram that decays gradually — high at lag 1, smaller at lag 2, smaller still at lag 3, and so on, typically shrinking roughly geometrically (each lag's value roughly a constant fraction of the previous one) toward zero, eventually disappearing into the noise band at higher lags.
  • A trending or non-stationary series produces a correlogram that decays very slowly — still meaningfully elevated even 20 or 30 lags out, refusing to die down toward zero within any reasonable window. This is the classic visual tell-tale of non-stationarity: a series with a genuine unit root (a random walk, or close to one) is correlated with its own distant past not because of any real short-memory structure, but because a random walk's whole history is baked into its current level — today's value literally contains the sum of every shock since the series began, so it is mechanically related to everything that came before it, at every lag, forever. Practitioners often check a correlogram before running a formal unit-root test like ADF for exactly this reason — a slowly-decaying ACF is the cheap first-pass signal that a stationarity test is worth running properly.

4. The partial autocorrelation function (PACF). The ACF at lag \(k\) mixes together the direct relationship between \(X_t\) and \(X_{t-k}\) with everything carried indirectly through the intervening lags \(1, \ldots, k-1\) — exactly the weather-chain intuition above. The PACF at lag \(k\) is the correlation between \(X_t\) and \(X_{t-k}\) after removing (partialling out) the linear effect of every lag in between. Conceptually, one way to get there: regress \(X_t\) on \(X_{t-1}, X_{t-2}, \ldots, X_{t-k}\) all at once, and the coefficient on the very last term, \(X_{t-k}\), is the PACF at lag \(k\) — it captures whatever \(X_{t-k}\) still explains about \(X_t\) once every closer lag has already had its say. (In practice this is computed more efficiently via the Durbin-Levinson recursion rather than one large regression per lag, but the regression framing is the right way to understand what the number means.) Because it strips out the indirect, chained-through-intermediate-lags contribution that the plain ACF cannot separate out, the PACF answers a sharper question than the ACF does: not "is lag \(k\) related to today," but "does lag \(k\) add anything beyond what the closer lags already told you."

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

If yesterday tells you a lot about today, and the day before yesterday mostly just tells you about yesterday, then the day before yesterday isn't really giving you anything new once you already know yesterday. The PACF is the tool that checks exactly that — it only counts a day from further back as "informative" if it tells you something you couldn't already guess from the closer days in between.

5. The Box-Jenkins identification rule — why the contrast matters. This is the payoff of defining both functions rather than just one: for a genuine AR(\(p\)) process (today's value depends directly on the last \(p\) values plus noise), the two functions behave in a specifically different way, and that difference is what lets you read the model's order directly off the two plots:

  • The ACF decays gradually — it never cleanly stops, because the direct dependence at lag \(p\) still echoes forward through every later lag via the same chained-correlation mechanism described above (an AR(1) process, for instance, has \(\mathrm{ACF}(k) = \phi^k\), a smooth geometric decay that is technically nonzero at every lag, however small).
  • The PACF cuts off sharply after lag \(p\) — once you've partialled out lags \(1\) through \(p\), there's genuinely nothing left for lag \(p+1\) to explain, because the true process only ever depended on the first \(p\) lags directly. For an AR(1) process specifically, this means the PACF is large at lag 1 and then drops abruptly to (statistically indistinguishable from) zero at every lag from 2 onward — even though the ACF at lag 2, 3, 4… is still visibly nonzero from the decay described above.

That contrast — ACF decays, PACF cuts off — is the classic Box-Jenkins signature of an autoregressive process, and reading where the PACF cuts off is the standard way to identify \(p\), the order of an AR model, directly from data rather than by guessing a lag count. (The mirror-image rule holds for a moving-average MA(\(q\)) process — ACF cuts off after lag \(q\), PACF decays gradually — which is why the two plots together, not either alone, are the classical starting point for choosing an ARIMA model's \((p,q)\) before ever running a fit.)

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Picture dropping a pebble in a pond. The ripple (the ACF) keeps spreading outward, getting fainter and fainter, but never really stops all at once — it fades away slowly. But if you ask "did the pebble itself splash again a moment ago" (the PACF), the answer is a clean yes right after the drop, and then a flat no forever after — the single splash is the whole cause, even though its ripple keeps echoing outward for a while.

The signature to memorize: ACF decays, PACF cuts off — that single contrast is what lets you read an AR model's order \(p\) straight off two plots, instead of guessing a lag count or grid-searching one.

🎯
Real-world trading scenario

How many days actually matter for a mean-reversion signal?

A trader is building a mean-reversion signal on a spread — say, a cross-asset spread that has already cleared a Cointegration-style stationarity check, or a stationary residual from some other model. The natural next question is a design choice every such signal has to make somehow: how far back should the signal look? Ten days? Twenty? A common but unexamined approach is to just pick a round number that "feels reasonable" and move on, or to grid-search a handful of window lengths and keep whichever number back-tested best — the latter is itself a quiet form of overfitting, since it is fitting a tunable parameter to history rather than reading the answer off the series' actual structure.

Plotting the spread's own ACF and PACF answers the question directly instead of guessing. Suppose the correlogram looks close to the AR(1) signature described above: the ACF is meaningfully elevated at lag 1, decays gradually through lags 2-5, and fades into the noise band by lag 7 or so — that alone might tempt a 5-to-7-day lookback. But the PACF tells a sharper story: it shows one tall, clearly significant bar at lag 1, and then drops inside the noise band at every lag from 2 onward. That combination is the textbook signature of an AR(1) process — the spread's own history has real, direct predictive structure only at lag 1 (yesterday); everything the ACF shows at lags 2 through 7 is the same lag-1 relationship echoing forward through the chain, not new independent information at those later lags. A signal that uses the last 10 days as separate predictors on this spread is mostly re-adding information the model already has from yesterday alone, several times over, while contributing very little that's genuinely new — extra parameters (and extra estimation noise) bought for very little real signal. The PACF is what tells you "stop at lag 1" with actual evidence, instead of guessing a window and hoping it holds up out of sample. What the PACF settles is the lag order — how many past values the signal should use as predictors. It does not set the estimation window for the spread's rolling mean and standard deviation in a z-score; that window is a separate trade-off between estimation noise and how long the spread's level and volatility stay stable, and even an AR(1) spread needs many observations to pin down its mean.

  • The same gradual fade, just a little lower at every lag.
  • One tall bar at lag 1, then bars inside the noise band from lag 2 on.
  • Bars that stay high all the way out to lag 10.

One tall bar, then nothing. The ACF at lags 2, 3 and 4 is the lag-1 link echoing down the chain. With \(\phi = 0.7\), for example, lag 2 is \(0.7 \times 0.7 = 0.49\) and lag 3 is about 0.34, all inherited from lag 1. The PACF removes that echo, so for an AR(1) only lag 1 is left. Bars that stay high all the way out would be the unit-root signature instead, a sign to test for stationarity before reading either plot.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

🔢

Model order identification

Box-Jenkins: a PACF cutoff plus a decaying ACF picks the AR order \(p\) directly off the plots.

✅

Pre-model sanity check

GARCH, VAR and the Cointegration residual test all assume stationarity — a correlogram is the cheapest check before trusting any of them.

📏

ADF lag length

The PACF of a differenced residual is a principled way to choose how many lagged-difference terms the Dickey-Fuller regression needs.

🪟

Lookback-window design

Using the last N values as predictors is an implicit autocorrelation claim — ACF/PACF is the checkable version. A rolling z-score's estimation window is a separate choice.

Model order identification (Box-Jenkins). Before fitting an ARIMA\((p,d,q)\) model, plotting the (differenced, if necessary) series' ACF and PACF is the classical first step: a PACF that cuts off after lag \(p\) with a decaying ACF suggests an AR\((p)\) component; an ACF that cuts off after lag \(q\) with a decaying PACF suggests an MA\((q)\) component; both decaying gradually suggests a mixed ARMA process, where the cutoff heuristic weakens and information criteria (AIC/BIC) across candidate orders typically take over.

Checking a modeling assumption before it's used, not after. GARCH volatility models, VAR systems, and the Cointegration lesson's residual test all assume some notion of stationarity for the object being modeled. Looking at a correlogram is the cheapest possible sanity check before committing to any of those — a series whose ACF hasn't meaningfully decayed by lag 20 or 30 is telling you, visually, that whatever comes next (a differencing step, a detrending step, a formal ADF test) needs to happen before the fancier model is trusted.

Choosing lag length for the Augmented Dickey-Fuller test itself. The Cointegration lesson's Step 2 regression, \(\Delta \hat\epsilon_t = \gamma\,\hat\epsilon_{t-1} + \sum_{i=1}^{p}\delta_i\,\Delta \hat\epsilon_{t-i} + u_t\), needs enough lagged difference terms \(p\) to soak up any short-run autocorrelation left in the residual — too few and the test statistic is distorted by that leftover structure; too many and the test loses power. The PACF of the differenced residual is a standard, principled way to gauge how many lags actually need including, rather than picking \(p\) arbitrarily.

Feature and lookback-window design generally. Any quantitative feature that uses the last \(N\) values of a series as predictors — lagged returns in a regression, an AR-style reversal score — is implicitly assuming that those lags carry real information and lags beyond them don't. The ACF/PACF pair is the direct, checkable version of that assumption, as the trading scenario above works through concretely. It does not choose the estimation window of a rolling volatility, mean or z-score: that window trades estimation noise against how long the series' level and volatility stay stable, a different question from lag order.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where reading an ACF/PACF plot quietly goes wrong

Interpreting an ACF/PACF computed on a non-stationary series. The ACF and PACF are only cleanly interpretable under the stationarity assumption this lesson opens with. Run them on a raw, trending price level instead of a return or a properly-tested spread, and the correlogram's slow decay mostly just reflects the trend/unit-root behavior described above, not any genuine short-memory structure worth building a lookback window around. The standard fix is to difference (or otherwise detrend) the series first, and check the ACF/PACF of the transformed series, not the raw one.

Treating every bar near the significance band as meaningful. The \(\pm1.96/\sqrt{N}\) band is the threshold for a single lag tested in isolation under a white-noise null. Checking 20 or 40 lags at once and treating every bar that pokes outside the band as "real" ignores that testing many lags simultaneously is itself a multiple-comparisons problem — see the Multiple Testing & the Look-Elsewhere Effect lesson. Roughly 1 in 20 lags will clear a 5% band by chance alone even for genuine white noise; an isolated spike at, say, lag 17 with nothing meaningful at nearby lags is a much weaker claim than a clean, monotonic decay pattern.

Seasonal or periodic spikes mistaken for short-memory structure. A distinct ACF spike at a specific lag — say, lag 5 in a daily series, or a lag corresponding to a weekly/monthly cycle — often reflects genuine periodicity (a weekday-of-week effect, a monthly rebalancing flow) rather than the smooth AR-style decay this lesson describes. That's a real and sometimes tradeable pattern, but it's a different phenomenon from generic serial correlation and calling for different handling (a seasonal term, not a longer plain AR lookback).

Expecting the clean Box-Jenkins cutoff from a real market series. The sharp "ACF decays, PACF cuts off" signature is exact for a pure AR(1) process and close to exact for low-order AR processes generally, but most real financial series are not a clean textbook AR(\(p\)) — they're closer to a mixed, noisy approximation of one, at best. In practice both functions typically show some decay and the "cutoff" is a fade into the noise band rather than a hard stop. Use the shapes as a strong heuristic for how many lags carry predictive information, not as literal proof of an exact underlying model order.

Scenario drill · an illustrative morning

A beautiful regression between two price levels

You regress one currency pair's daily price level on another's over five years. The fit looks superb: an R² of 0.8 and a t-statistic of 12. You plan to trade the gap between them, the regression residual, as a mean-reverting spread. Before sizing it, you plot the residual's ACF. It is still well outside the noise band at lag 25. What do you do?

  1. Trade it. A t-statistic of 12 cannot be luck.
    Granger and Newbold showed that regressions between two independent random walks routinely look this significant. On non-stationary levels the usual significance numbers mean very little, so the fit on its own is not evidence.
  2. Treat the slow-fading ACF as a unit-root warning: run an ADF test on the residual, and trade the spread only if it passes.
    A correlogram still elevated 20 to 30 lags out is the cheap first-pass sign of a unit root. The formal test is the next step, and a spread is only tradeable if it is stationary.
  3. Use a longer window, say 60 days, for the z-score so it captures the slow decay.
    A longer window does not create a home level. If the residual has a unit root there is no fixed mean for any window to estimate, and the bands drift along with the spread.
  4. Difference both series and study the ACF and PACF of the daily changes instead.
    Differencing is the standard fix for reading the plots, so this is sound statistics. But it throws away the level relationship you wanted to trade. Testing the residual answers the question you actually have.

What the lesson says. This is an illustrative situation, not a dated event. The history in this lesson gives the answer: Granger and Newbold (1974) showed by simulation that regressions between independent random walks routinely look this significant, Dickey and Fuller (1979) supplied the unit-root test, and Engle and Granger (1987) made it the rule for spreads: a spread is tradeable only if it passes a stationarity test. A residual whose ACF has not faded by lag 25 is unlikely to pass.

Section 04

Key Takeaways

✓Stationarity means constant mean, variance, and autocovariance. All three must depend only on lag \(k\), never on the calendar date — most time-series modeling quietly assumes it.
✓The ACF measures total relationship. Correlation between a series and its \(k\)-lagged self, mixing direct dependence with everything carried through intervening lags.
✓The PACF strips out the chain. It measures only what lag \(k\) adds beyond what closer lags already explain — the sharper of the two questions.
✓ACF decays, PACF cuts off — the AR(\(p\)) signature. Reading where the PACF cuts off tells you the model order \(p\) directly from data, not a guess.
✓A slowly-decaying ACF is the cheap first-pass unit-root tell. Still elevated 20-30 lags out means run a formal test (ADF) before trusting anything downstream.
⚠Many lags at once is a multiple-comparisons problem. About 1 in 20 lags clears a 5% significance band by chance alone, even for genuine white noise — a single isolated spike is weak evidence.
⚠Real market series rarely show the clean textbook cutoff. Expect a fade into the noise band, not a hard stop — use the shapes as a strong heuristic, not literal proof of an exact model order.
ℹHow it reached trading: test before you trade a spread. Granger & Newbold (1974) showed regressions between unrelated random walks look highly significant; Dickey–Fuller (1979) supplied the unit-root test that cointegration-based pairs and relative-value trading are built on.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. Why does a random walk's ACF decay so slowly compared to a stationary AR(1) process's ACF?
A random walk's current value is literally the sum of every shock since the series began, \(X_t = X_0 + \sum_{i=1}^t \epsilon_i\), so it is mechanically tied to its entire history — there is no decaying "memory," just an ever-accumulating sum, and its correlation with values many lags back stays high because those past values are still fully embedded in the current level. A stationary AR(1) process, by contrast, has a pull-back term (\(|\phi|<1\)) that causes each shock's influence to shrink geometrically over time (\(\mathrm{ACF}(k) = \phi^k\)), so its correlation with the distant past genuinely fades out rather than persisting. The slow-decay ACF is a direct symptom of the same non-stationarity (growing, unbounded variance) the Cointegration lesson describes for a random walk.
Q2. What does a PACF cutting off after lag 2 (with nonzero values at lags 1 and 2, then dropping into the noise band from lag 3 onward) suggest about model order?
By the Box-Jenkins identification rule, a PACF that stays significant through lag 2 and then cuts off is the signature of an AR(2) process — today's value directly depends on the last two values, and once those two lags are partialled out, no further lag adds anything. The accompanying ACF for such a process would be expected to decay gradually (not cut off) rather than dropping sharply, since the AR(2) dependence still echoes forward through later lags via the chained correlation mechanism, the same way it does for AR(1).
Q3. Why is a slowly-decaying, still-elevated-at-large-lags ACF considered a classic visual sign of non-stationarity?
Because that shape is exactly what a unit-root (random-walk-like) process produces: since today's value carries forward the accumulated sum of essentially all past shocks rather than letting their influence decay, correlation with distant lags stays meaningfully above zero for far longer than a genuinely stationary, mean-reverting series would allow. A properly stationary series' ACF fades into the significance band within a modest number of lags; a series whose ACF is still clearly outside the band 20-30 lags out is showing the telltale fingerprint of a unit root, and is the cheap first-pass check practitioners run before committing to a formal test like ADF.
Q4. What does "partial" mean in "partial autocorrelation," and how does that make PACF a different question from ACF?
"Partial" refers to partialling out — statistically removing — the linear effect of the intervening lags before measuring the relationship at lag \(k\). The ACF at lag \(k\) measures the total relationship between \(X_t\) and \(X_{t-k}\), which mixes together whatever direct effect exists at lag \(k\) with everything carried indirectly through the chain of lags \(1,\ldots,k-1\) in between. The PACF strips that indirect, chained-through contribution out first (via the coefficient on \(X_{t-k}\) in a regression of \(X_t\) on \(X_{t-1},\ldots,X_{t-k}\)) and reports only what's left — the effect lag \(k\) has beyond what the closer lags already explain. That's why an AR(1) process can show a decaying ACF at every lag while its PACF is essentially zero past lag 1: the ACF is still picking up the lag-1 relationship's echo, but the PACF has already accounted for it.
Q5. Why can't you just always use more lags "to be safe" when building a predictor from a series' own history?
Because, per the trading scenario above, extra lags on a series whose PACF cuts off early (say, at lag 1) mostly re-adds information the model already has from the closer lags, restated several times over through the chained-correlation mechanism the ACF picks up — it does not add proportionally more genuine information. What it does add is estimation noise (each extra lag/parameter is estimated from finite, noisy data) and more room to fit noise in the sample. The PACF is the tool that tells you where the genuinely new information actually runs out, so extra lags beyond that point is closer to overfitting risk than to added signal.
Q6. Why doesn't a single bar clearing the significance band in an ACF or PACF plot prove that lag is a "real" effect?
The standard \(\pm1.96/\sqrt{N}\) band is the threshold for one lag tested on its own under a white-noise null at roughly 5% significance — but a correlogram typically shows many lags at once (10, 20, 40), and checking many thresholds simultaneously is a multiple-comparisons problem: by chance alone, about 1 in 20 lags will clear a 5% band even for a series with no real autocorrelation at any lag. A single isolated spike with no supporting pattern at nearby lags is much weaker evidence than a smooth, sustained decay (or a clean early cutoff) across several consecutive lags, which is far less likely to arise from chance alone. This is the same look-elsewhere logic the Multiple Testing lesson applies to scanning many trading strategies or many candidate pairs.
