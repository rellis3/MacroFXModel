Interest Rate Parity — Covered vs. Uncovered

One of these two theories is an accounting identity enforced by riskless arbitrage and holds to within a fraction of a basis point. The other one is a genuine economic hypothesis about what markets expect — and it has failed, on average, in almost every dataset anyone has tested it on for forty years. That failure is not a footnote; it is the empirical basis of the entire FX carry trade.

CONCEPT — the foundational FX theory this repo hasn't formalized yet ~17 min read Prereqs: Time Value of Money
TL;DR
  • Covered interest rate parity (CIP) is a no-arbitrage identity, not a forecast: \(F = S\cdot(1+r_d)/(1+r_f)\) is pinned down today by the spot rate and the two interest rates, and it holds in liquid G10 pairs to within a sliver of a basis point.
  • Uncovered interest rate parity (UIP) makes the same-looking claim without the forward hedge — that the market's expected future spot rate equals the forward rate, \(E_t[S_{t+T}] = F\) — which implies the higher-yielding currency should be expected to depreciate by roughly its rate advantage. Unlike CIP, nothing forces this to be true.
  • The worked example prices a 1-year USD/EUR forward at \(S=1.10\), \(r_d=5\%\), \(r_f=3\%\) as \(F^\ast \approx 1.1214\), then shows a hypothetical mispriced forward of 1.15 yielding a riskless $26,818.18 arbitrage on $1,000,000 — the exact trade that keeps CIP pinned in practice.
  • Fama (1984) tested UIP's one-for-one prediction and found the regression coefficient is typically negative (Froot & Thaler's 1990 survey reports roughly −0.8, not +1) — the forward premium puzzle: high-yielding currencies have tended to appreciate or hold flat rather than depreciate as UIP predicts, which is the empirical basis of the FX carry trade.
  • The JPY/AUD carry-trade scenario shows both sides at once: the interest differential (~4.2pp) was pocketed largely intact through calm 2023–mid-2024 markets, then AUD/JPY fell 10%+ in about two weeks after the BoJ's 31 July 2024 hike — the carry trade is short a fat left tail, not free money.
Section 01

Why this matters

Almost every serious conversation about currencies eventually touches interest rates: "the dollar is strong because the Fed is hiking," "the yen is weak because Japanese rates are pinned near zero," "carry traders are long AUD/NZD because the yield pickup is attractive." All of that language is really pointing at one underlying relationship — the link between the interest rate differential on two currencies and their exchange rate — and interest rate parity is the formal statement of that link, in two very different flavors that get confused with each other constantly.

Covered interest rate parity (CIP) is not really a theory about markets at all; it is closer to an accounting identity, enforced by the same riskless-arbitrage logic that ties a bond's price to its yield. It says the forward exchange rate is fully determined, today, by today's spot rate and today's two interest rates — full stop, no forecasting involved. Uncovered interest rate parity (UIP) looks almost identical on paper, but makes a completely different kind of claim: that the market's best guess of the future spot rate equals that same forward rate — i.e., that the higher-yielding currency is expected, on average, to depreciate by exactly enough to erase the extra interest an investor earns holding it. CIP holds almost by construction. UIP is a testable empirical prediction, and it is famously, repeatedly wrong. Understanding exactly why one holds and the other doesn't — and what that gap actually funds — is the entire point of this lesson, and it is the theoretical foundation any honest look at the carry trade has to start from.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Interest rates are the rental price of money

Think of an interest rate as the price you pay (or earn) to rent money for a period of time, and think of the forward exchange rate as nothing more than today's spot rate, adjusted for the fact that money rents at different prices in different currencies. If yen rents for almost nothing and Australian dollars rent for a much higher price, and you want to borrow yen, convert it to Australian dollars, park it for a year, and convert it back — the forward market has to charge you a correspondingly worse exchange rate on the way back, or you'd have discovered a machine that prints riskless money out of nothing. That "correspondingly worse" adjustment is the covered interest rate parity formula. It isn't a forecast of anything; it's the terms that make the round trip a wash, enforced by the fact that if the terms were ever meaningfully off, a trading desk would do exactly that round trip at size until the mispricing closed.

Uncovered interest rate parity asks a completely different question: if you don't lock in that forward rate at all — if you just borrow the cheap currency, buy the expensive one, and take your chances on whatever the exchange rate happens to be when you convert back — does the currency you bought drift down, on average, by about the same amount the locked-in forward rate implied? That's no longer an accounting identity anyone can force to be true. It's a claim about what actually tends to happen in the world, and the honest answer, repeatedly, is: not really.

Section 02

The math, step by step

This section builds up three ideas in order: the no-arbitrage argument that pins down covered interest rate parity exactly, the very different kind of claim uncovered interest rate parity makes once you remove the forward hedge, and the forward premium puzzle — the specific, replicated empirical finding that UIP gets the sign wrong on average.

\(S\)The spot exchange rate today, quoted as units of domestic currency per one unit of foreign currency (e.g. USD per EUR).
\(F\)The forward exchange rate agreed today for a transaction settling at time \(T\), same quote convention as \(S\).
\(r_d\)The domestic risk-free interest rate for the period \(T\) (e.g. the USD money-market rate).
\(r_f\)The foreign risk-free interest rate for the period \(T\) (e.g. the EUR money-market rate).
\(T\)Time to maturity of the forward contract and the two deposits, in years.
\(E_t[S_{t+T}]\)The market's expectation, formed today at time \(t\), of what the spot rate will actually be at time \(t+T\). Unlike \(F\), this is never directly observable — only its realizations are.
\(e\)Base of the natural logarithm, used in the continuous-compounding version of the formula below.

1. Covered interest rate parity — a no-arbitrage argument, not a forecast. Consider a domestic investor (say, based in USD, with EUR as the foreign currency) with two ways to turn one unit of domestic currency into a riskless payoff \(T\) years from now, using nothing but today's prices:

Route 1 — stay home. Deposit the money in domestic currency at the domestic rate. One unit today becomes \((1+r_d)\) units of domestic currency at \(T\), guaranteed, with no exposure to the exchange rate at all.

Route 2 — round-trip through the foreign currency, fully hedged. Convert the one unit of domestic currency to foreign currency at today's spot rate, getting \(1/S\) units of foreign currency. Deposit that at the foreign rate, growing to \((1/S)(1+r_f)\) units of foreign currency at \(T\). At the very same moment — today — also sell that known future foreign-currency amount forward, locking in today's forward rate \(F\) for the conversion back to domestic currency at \(T\). The final domestic-currency payoff, entirely determined today with zero exchange-rate risk in between, is \((1/S)(1+r_f)\,F\).

Both routes start with exactly one unit of domestic currency and end with a completely riskless, fully known payoff at \(T\) — no forecasting, no exposure, nothing left to chance in either leg. If the two riskless payoffs were not equal, there would be a genuine free-lunch arbitrage: borrow at zero net cost via the cheaper route, invest via the more expensive one, and pocket the difference with no capital and no risk. Real trading desks do exactly this the instant such a gap opens, and their trading closes it. No-arbitrage therefore requires the two routes to pay the same:

\[ 1 + r_d \;=\; \frac{F}{S}\,(1+r_f) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\dfrac{F}{S} = \dfrac{1+r_d}{1+r_f}} \]

Rearranged, \(F = S\cdot\dfrac{1+r_d}{1+r_f}\). Under continuous compounding this is usually written in its more compact exponential form, which converges to the same relationship as \(T\) shrinks:

\[ F = S\,e^{(r_d - r_f)T} \]
e^((r_d−r_f)T)continuous-compounding form of the same identity

Two things follow immediately and are worth internalizing before anything else in this lesson. First, this formula requires nothing about anyone's beliefs or expectations — \(F\) is completely pinned down today by \(S\), \(r_d\), and \(r_f\), which are all observable today. Second, notice which currency ends up "expensive" forward: when \(r_d > r_f\), \(F/S > 1\), meaning the foreign currency (the lower-yielding one) trades forward at a premium to spot, while the higher-yielding domestic currency trades at a forward discount. That higher-rate-currency-at-a-discount / lower-rate-currency-at-a-premium pattern is completely mechanical, baked into the arbitrage argument above — it says nothing yet about where the spot rate will actually be at \(T\). That's exactly the question the next section asks, and it's where things stop being an identity.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine two ways to turn $1 into more money a year from now: keep it in a US savings account, or swap it into euros, put it in a European savings account, and pre-book today the exchange rate you'll swap it back at. If those two routes ever paid different amounts, a bank could borrow on the cheap route and invest on the rich one, pocketing free money with zero risk — so banks do exactly that until the two routes pay the same. The forward rate isn't a guess about the future; it's just whatever number makes both routes tie.

In practice, CIP holds to within a sliver of a basis point in liquid G10 pairs during normal market conditions — deviations are bounded almost entirely by transaction costs (bid-ask spreads, funding costs, margin) so thin that only the largest bank trading desks can act on them at all, and even then only fleetingly. It is worth being precise, though, that "holds almost exactly" is not quite "holds with zero exception": Du, Tepper & Verdelhan (2018) documented small, persistent CIP deviations across major currencies after the 2008 financial crisis, driven by post-crisis bank balance-sheet and leverage-ratio regulatory constraints rather than a genuinely unexploited free-lunch — the arbitrage in the textbook derivation above assumes unlimited, cost-free balance sheet to execute it, and that assumption got measurably more expensive for banks to satisfy after 2008. The identity is still the right starting model; the real-world deviation is a story about who can fund the arbitrage, not evidence the no-arbitrage logic is wrong.

2. Uncovered interest rate parity — the same formula, with the forward hedge removed. UIP asks what happens if, instead of locking in \(F\) via a forward contract (Route 2 above), an investor simply converts to the foreign currency, earns \(r_f\), and converts back later at whatever the spot rate turns out to be — carrying the full exchange-rate risk uncovered. UIP is the hypothesis that, under rational expectations, the market-clearing condition for that uncovered bet to be a fair one (i.e., for neither currency to offer a systematically better expected return once risk is set aside) is exactly that the expected future spot rate equals today's forward rate:

\[ E_t[S_{t+T}] = F \]
E_t[S_t+T]the market's expectation of the future spot rate — never observable directly
= FUIP's claim — not enforced by any arbitrage

Combine this with the CIP identity above (substituting \(F\)) and UIP's real content becomes explicit as a statement purely about expectations:

\[ \frac{E_t[S_{t+T}]}{S} = \frac{1+r_d}{1+r_f} \]
>1if r_d > r_f, the market must expect S to rise
⇒ depreciatethe higher-yielding domestic currency is expected to weaken

Read this the way a carry trader has to: if the domestic currency's rate \(r_d\) is higher than the foreign rate \(r_f\), the right-hand side is greater than 1, which means the market must be expecting \(S\) itself to rise — the price of foreign currency, in domestic terms, is expected to go up. Concretely, that means the domestic, higher-yielding currency is expected to depreciate against the lower-yielding foreign one, by (approximately) the size of the interest differential. That is the whole claim of UIP in one sentence: the extra interest you earn holding the higher-yielding currency should be expected, on average, to be exactly offset by that currency's expected depreciation — so an uncovered bet on the high-yielder has the same expected return as just staying in the low-yielder. No free lunch, covered or uncovered. Unlike CIP, this is not enforced by any riskless arbitrage — nothing stops the market's actual expectation, let alone the outcome, from being something else entirely. It is a hypothesis about behavior, and it needs to be checked against data.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

UIP asks a totally different question: what if you don't lock in that exchange rate at all — you just hold the higher-paying currency and hope? The theory says the extra interest you earn should get canceled out, on average, by that currency losing value by about the same amount. Nobody can force this to be true the way a bank can force the forward rate to line up — it's a prediction about how currencies actually behave, and it can simply turn out wrong.

🧮
Worked example

Checking the identity, and forcing it back into line

Take USD as domestic and EUR as foreign, spot \(S = 1.10\) USD/EUR, a one-year domestic rate \(r_d = 5.00\%\) and a one-year foreign rate \(r_f = 3.00\%\). The fair one-year forward rate CIP implies is:

\[ F^\ast = 1.10 \times \frac{1.05}{1.03} = 1.10 \times 1.019417 \approx 1.1214 \ \text{USD/EUR} \]

Note EUR (the lower-yielding currency) is indeed priced forward at a premium to spot (1.1214 > 1.10), consistent with the sign established above. The roughly 1.94% forward premium is close to, but not exactly, the 2.00 percentage-point rate gap — the small difference is the compounding term, and for small rates \(F/S - 1 \approx r_d - r_f\) is a good rule-of-thumb approximation.

Now suppose the market forward were mispriced at \(F_{mkt} = 1.15\) instead of the fair 1.1214 — deliberately exaggerated here (a real G10 mispricing this size would never survive more than an instant) so the mechanics are easy to see. Borrow \$1,000,000 at 5% (owe \$1,050,000 in a year). Convert to EUR at spot: \$1,000,000 / 1.10 = €909,090.91. Invest at 3%: €909,090.91 × 1.03 = €936,363.64. Sell that forward at the mispriced 1.15: €936,363.64 × 1.15 = \$1,076,818.18. Repay the \$1,050,000 loan, and pocket \$26,818.18 — guaranteed, from zero net capital, before a single dollar of directional risk. That is precisely the trade real desks run the instant \(F\) drifts away from \(F^\ast\), in whatever direction, at whatever size their balance sheet allows — and it is exactly why the deviation never gets anywhere near this large in practice; it gets arbitraged back toward \(F^\ast\) continuously, bounded only by transaction costs and (per the Du-Tepper-Verdelhan point above) balance-sheet cost of capital.

3. The forward premium puzzle — Fama (1984). UIP makes a sharp, testable prediction: currencies with a higher interest rate should on average depreciate by roughly the size of their rate advantage, so that no systematic, riskless-in-expectation edge exists from simply holding the higher yielder unhedged. Eugene Fama's 1984 paper tested this directly by regressing the currency's subsequent realized change in the spot rate against the forward premium (which, by the CIP identity above, is essentially the interest differential). Under UIP with rational expectations, that regression should return a coefficient of 1 — the forward premium should predict subsequent depreciation one-for-one, no more and no less. Fama, and a large replicating literature since (surveyed by Froot & Thaler, 1990, who report an average estimated coefficient across published studies of roughly \(-0.8\) rather than \(+1\)), found instead that the coefficient is typically negative. In plain terms: high-interest-rate currencies have tended, on average, to appreciate or stay roughly flat rather than depreciate the way UIP predicts — the exact opposite sign from the theory's prediction, and remarkably consistent across currency pairs, decades, and studies. This is precisely the empirical basis of the FX carry trade: borrow the low-yielding currency, hold the high-yielding one unhedged, and — on average, historically, outside the episodes covered in the scenario box below — the interest-rate pickup has not been fully offset by depreciation, sometimes not offset at all.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Fama basically ran the experiment UIP predicts and checked the scorecard. If UIP were right, currencies paying higher interest should reliably lose value by about that much extra interest — a clean trade-off, no free lunch. Instead he found the opposite tendency: the higher-paying currency tended to hold its value or even gain. That's a real, repeated pattern in the data, not a one-off fluke — and it's the reason "borrow cheap, hold the higher-yielder" has historically been a profitable-looking trade rather than a wash.

🎯
Real-world trading scenario

The JPY/AUD carry trade — and the day it stopped working

A textbook carry trade: borrow Japanese yen, where policy rates sat at roughly 0.0%–0.1% through most of 2023 and into mid-2024, and use the proceeds to buy Australian dollars, where the RBA cash rate stood at 4.35% through the same period. Hold the AUD in short-dated bank bills or simply carry the position via the FX forward/swap points, which (per the CIP identity above) embed that roughly 4.2-percentage-point rate gap as a daily credit. On a notional \$10,000,000 position, unchanged exchange rate, that is on the order of \$420,000 a year, accruing day by day, for doing nothing but holding the position.

What UIP says about this trade. UIP's prediction is unambiguous: AUD, the higher-yielding currency, should be expected to depreciate against JPY by roughly that same 4.2 percentage points over the year, leaving the uncovered trade with no better expected return than simply holding yen. If UIP held exactly, this trade would be a wash on average — interesting only as a way to take on AUD/JPY volatility, not a source of edge.

What the forward premium puzzle says instead. The empirical literature on the yen carry trade specifically (and the carry factor across currencies generally) has found that AUD has not, on average, depreciated against JPY by anywhere near the size of the interest-rate gap over long stretches of calm markets — periods when global risk appetite is stable, this trade has tended to pocket the differential largely intact, sometimes alongside AUD holding steady or even appreciating. That is the forward premium puzzle showing up in a single, concrete cross: the "should depreciate" side of UIP simply hasn't shown up reliably.

What this trade is actually short. The honest other half of the story is early August 2024. The Bank of Japan raised its policy rate on 31 July 2024 — a hawkish surprise against a market that had spent years pricing Japan as a permanent zero-rate funding source — and a soft US payrolls report days later revived US recession fears at the same time. The result was a violent, fast unwind of yen-funded carry positioning across the board: the yen appreciated sharply and abruptly against nearly everything, AUD/JPY (one of the most heavily carry-traded crosses) fell on the order of 10%+ peak-to-trough in roughly two weeks, and volatility spiked across global markets (the episode is widely referenced as contributing to the early-August 2024 global equity selloff). A position that had been quietly earning something like 4.2% a year in carry gave back several years' worth of that accumulated edge in about ten trading days. Nothing about the interest-rate differential changed on the day of the crash — what changed was the market's tolerance for holding the funding-currency short, all at once.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Picture picking up spare change off the sidewalk every single day for two years, then one day stepping in front of a bus. The carry trade often looks exactly like that: small, steady gains that quietly add up for a long time, funded by taking on the risk of one sudden, sharp loss that can erase years of those small gains in about ten trading days. The daily interest pickup wasn't the whole story — it was the reward for being willing to stand in the road.

The trap: reading the calm-market carry pickup as free money. Nothing about the interest-rate differential changed on 31 July 2024 — what changed was the market's tolerance for holding the funding-currency short, all at once. The carry trade is short a fat, left-tail event, not a mispricing to arbitrage.

That is the honest shape of the trade in both directions: the forward premium puzzle describes a real, replicated empirical tendency for the yield pickup to survive uncorrected through calm periods, and it is a genuinely different empirical fact than the "no free lunch" logic of CIP. But the trade is not free money — it is short a fat, left tail event that arrives without warning and moves fast, structurally the same kind of risk this Theory Lab's Extreme Value Theory and Merton Jump-Diffusion lessons describe: most of the time nothing happens, and then, occasionally, everything happens at once. Reporting the average historical carry return without reporting the crash risk it is short is not a complete description of the trade.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

The FX carry trade — long a basket of high-yielding currencies, short a basket of low-yielders, typically funded in whichever currency has the lowest policy rate at the time (yen and, at various points, Swiss francs have been the classic funding legs) — is a long-standing, widely used systematic strategy built directly on the forward premium puzzle. Institutional versions size positions across many currency pairs rather than one, both to diversify idiosyncratic crash risk and because the average empirical edge is a cross-sectional phenomenon (higher-yielders as a group vs. lower-yielders as a group), not a reliable property of any single pair.

Crash-risk-aware carry. Because the strategy's return distribution is left-skewed (small, steady gains most of the time; sharp, infrequent losses), practitioners commonly overlay it with tail hedges (out-of-the-money FX options on the funding currency), volatility-based position sizing that de-risks automatically when realized or implied volatility rises, or momentum/trend filters that reduce exposure when a funding currency starts appreciating sharply — treating the crash risk as something to actively manage rather than simply accept.

Covered interest rate arbitrage is the domain of bank trading desks and cross-currency basis swap markets, where the actual (tiny) deviations from CIP documented by Du, Tepper & Verdelhan (2018) are traded as a distinct, much lower-risk strategy — closer to relative value on regulatory funding costs than a directional currency bet, and entirely separate from the carry trade's risk-bearing UIP violation.

Rate-differential signals more broadly underlie a huge amount of discretionary and systematic FX macro reasoning — central bank divergence trades, "dollar smile" framing, and this project's own empirical work on the US-Germany yield spread leading EUR/USD all start from the same interest-differential intuition IRP formalizes, whether or not the practitioner ever writes down the CIP/UIP equations explicitly.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where CIP and UIP get mixed up in practice

Treating a UIP "failure" as an arbitrage opportunity. It is not. CIP is enforced by riskless arbitrage; a UIP deviation is a risk-bearing bet with a real, sometimes brutal downside, not a free lunch. Financial media (and, occasionally, market commentary) blur this distinction by calling carry-trade returns "arbitrage" — they aren't; they're compensation for bearing a specific, well-documented tail risk.

The peso problem. A rare, large devaluation risk that hasn't yet occurred in a given sample will depress the currency's average realized appreciation relative to what a fully rational market was actually pricing in — the average return over any finite historical window can look like a clean violation of UIP purely because the sample didn't happen to include the crash the market was, correctly, pricing a probability of. This is a genuine confound for interpreting historical carry-trade profitability as proof UIP fails, rather than proof the sample was lucky; it does not mean the forward premium puzzle isn't real, but it means any specific historical average carry return should be read with real caution about what didn't happen to be in the window.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine a driver who buys expensive insurance every year for the small chance of a bad crash. If you only watch five years and no crash happens, they look like they wasted money on insurance — but they weren't wrong, the crash just didn't happen to occur in the years you were watching. Currency data has the same trap: a rare devaluation the market was rightly bracing for might just not show up in whatever historical window you happen to be studying, making the currency look like it "beat expectations" when really the sample got lucky.

Ignoring transaction costs and tenor mismatches when computing "the differential." A back-of-envelope carry calculation using headline policy rates, ignoring bid-ask spreads on the FX leg, funding costs, and margin requirements, and matching a policy rate of one tenor against a forward of a different tenor, routinely overstates the realizable edge. The differential that matters is the one embedded in the actual tradeable forward/swap points, at the actual tenor being traded, net of costs.

Treating the forward premium puzzle as evidence any single currency pair will keep paying carry forever. The documented empirical pattern is an average, across many currency pairs and long samples. It does not say every carry cross pays every year, and it says nothing at all about timing — the scenario box above is a real illustration of exactly this: a genuinely well-documented average tendency, and a genuinely brutal individual crash, both true at once.

Key Takeaways

✓UIP makes the same-looking claim without the hedge — \(E_t[S_{t+T}]=F\) — and nothing forces it to be true. Unlike CIP it's a behavioral hypothesis, testable against data.

Section 05

Self-test

Q1. Why does CIP hold almost exactly while UIP fails empirically — what's actually different about the two claims?
CIP is enforced by riskless arbitrage: both routes in the derivation (stay domestic, or convert-invest-and-sell-forward) produce a fully known, riskless payoff today, so if they weren't equal a trading desk could lock in free money with zero risk and zero net capital — and that trading itself closes any gap. UIP replaces the hedged forward sale with an unhedged bet on the future spot rate, which introduces real risk that no amount of trading can arbitrage away — the future spot rate simply isn't known today. UIP is therefore a claim about what the market's rational expectation of that unknown future rate actually is, which is a behavioral/expectations hypothesis, not an accounting identity, and there is no mechanical force compelling it to be true.
Q2. If the domestic interest rate is higher than the foreign interest rate, does the foreign currency trade forward at a premium or a discount, and why?
At a premium. From \(F/S = (1+r_d)/(1+r_f)\), if \(r_d > r_f\) then \(F/S > 1\), so the forward price of the foreign currency (in domestic terms) is higher than its spot price — it is more expensive to buy forward than today. Equivalently, the higher-yielding (domestic) currency trades at a forward discount. This falls straight out of the no-arbitrage derivation and is true regardless of what actually happens to the spot rate later — it says nothing yet about realized returns, only about today's forward price.
Q3. What is a carry trade actually short, in risk terms?
It is short a rare, large, and typically fast depreciation of the high-yielding currency against the funding currency — a left-tail "crash" risk that tends to arrive alongside broader risk-off shocks (a funding-currency rate surprise, a risk-sentiment shift, a liquidity squeeze) rather than gradually. Structurally this is the same shape of risk this Theory Lab's Extreme Value Theory and Merton Jump-Diffusion lessons describe: steady, modest gains most of the time, funded by bearing exposure to an infrequent, sharp, negative jump. The average historical carry return is, in effect, compensation for holding that tail risk, not a free-standing edge independent of it.
Q4. What does the forward premium puzzle say empirically, and what result from Fama (1984) established it?
Fama regressed a currency's subsequent realized change in the spot rate on its forward premium (which, via the CIP identity, closely tracks the interest-rate differential). Under UIP with rational expectations, that regression's coefficient should be +1 — the forward premium should predict subsequent depreciation one-for-one. Fama found the estimated coefficient was instead typically negative, a result replicated widely since (Froot & Thaler's 1990 survey reports an average across published studies of roughly −0.8). In plain terms: high-interest-rate currencies have tended, on average, to appreciate or stay flat rather than depreciate the way UIP predicts — the wrong sign from the theory's point of view, and the empirical basis of the FX carry trade.
Q5. What is a "peso problem," and why does it complicate reading a historical average carry-trade return as clean proof that UIP fails?
A peso problem is when a rare, large adverse event (e.g. a currency devaluation or crash) that the market was rationally pricing some probability of simply hasn't occurred yet within a given historical sample. If that event doesn't show up in the window being studied, the currency's average realized return over that window will look systematically better than what the market was actually, correctly, expecting — making UIP look like it failed by more than it really did, or making a carry trade's historical average look more reliably profitable than the underlying probabilities justify. It's a reminder that any specific historical carry-return average should be read alongside what tail event wasn't in the sample, not as a clean, sample-independent measurement of the true expected return.
Q6. Given that the forward premium puzzle is a well-replicated finding in the academic literature, why isn't that enough, on its own, for this repo to treat a carry-trade signal as validated?
Because "documented in the broader literature, on other datasets and time periods" is a different claim from "tested, with this repo's actual data and honest harness discipline, and found to hold out-of-sample here." Per Lego Principle 5 and the working agreement above, any signal — however well-supported elsewhere — only counts as validated once it clears realistic fills, real costs, a true in-sample/out-of-sample split, and a non-trivial OOS trade count (≥30) on this repo's own data. No such test has been run here yet; this lesson lays out the theory and the honest empirical backdrop, not a claim that a carry signal built in MacroFXModel would show an edge.
