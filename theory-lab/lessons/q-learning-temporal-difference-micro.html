Q-Learning, Learning Without a Model

The previous lesson solved a 3-state trading MDP by hand — but only because every transition probability and every reward was already written down in the diagram. No real market hands you that table. This lesson is the direct sequel: Q-learning, a way to learn the same kind of action-values straight from a stream of raw experience — state, action, reward, next state — one step at a time, without ever needing to know in advance how the environment will respond.

Section 1

The previous lesson's hidden assumption

Value iteration and policy iteration solved the Bellman equation exactly — but only because of what they were handed first.

What value / policy iteration need
  • Every transition probability P(s′|s,a), written down in full
  • Every reward R(s,a), known before a single action is taken
  • The entire MDP diagram given up front, as a table or a picture
  • The 3-state Flat/Long/Short example only worked because all of this was handed to us
What a real trading agent actually has
  • No one hands over the market's transition probabilities
  • Only a stream of lived experience: state, action, reward, next state
  • No way to "look ahead" through a model that doesn't exist
  • A need to learn values from exactly that stream, as it arrives

A trading agent doesn't know in advance exactly how the market will respond to its actions — it can't write down P(s′|s,a) any more than it can write down tomorrow's order flow. That's not a minor gap in the previous lesson's method; it's disqualifying for most real environments. Everything in this lesson exists to remove that one assumption.

Where the previous lesson was upfront about this

The Bellman-equation lesson itself flagged this as its biggest limitation: the clean solution only works when P and R are already known. This lesson is that caveat, turned into an actual method.

Section 2

Q-learning: model-free, action by action

Q

Q(s,a) is an action-value

Not "how good is this state" but "how good is taking this specific action a, in this specific state s, and acting well from then on." One number per (state, action) pair.

⊘

Model-free, by design

Q-learning never needs P(s′|s,a) or R(s,a) written down anywhere. It learns entirely from transitions it actually experiences, not from a model of the environment.

🎲

Learns from (s,a,r,s′) tuples

"I was Flat, I opened a position, it cost me 0.1, I ended up Long." That single sentence is exactly the unit of data Q-learning consumes, one at a time.

🎯

The best action just falls out

Once Q(s,a) is learned for every action available at s, the best action is whichever one has the highest Q — no model, no lookahead, just a table lookup.

The previous lesson solved for Q*(Long, Hold) = 10 by hand, using a fully known model. This lesson reaches that same number a different way — by sampling, never once looking at P or R directly.
Section 3

The temporal-difference update

After one real step, nudge the old estimate toward a target built from that one reward plus a discounted guess about what comes next.

The shape of the update
old Q(s,a)
+ α ×
TD error
=
new Q(s,a)
TD error = TD target − old Q(s,a), and the TD target itself is r + γ · maxa′ Q(s′,a′) — one real observed reward, plus the discounted value of the best action available from wherever you land next, judged by your current (possibly still wrong) estimates.

Nothing here waits for an episode to finish. The moment a single (s,a,r,s′) transition is observed, there's enough information to compute a target and take one small step toward it — learning incrementally, step by step, rather than only after a full trade or episode closes out.

Section 6

Getting close, never quite arriving

Keep sampling the same (Long, Hold, +1, Long) transition and Q(Long, Hold) climbs toward Q* = 10 — but with a fixed learning rate, never exactly onto it.

Q(Long, Hold) over 30 repeated updates
Each repeated sample closes about 5% of the remaining gap to Q* = 10 (that's α(1−γ) = 0.5 × 0.1 here) — fast early progress, then a long, slow approach that gets arbitrarily close but never exactly touches the asymptote in finite time.
Where this needs qualifying

A constant learning rate never fully converges — it only settles into a neighborhood of Q*. Formal convergence needs a decaying step size (the Robbins-Monro conditions). There's a second pitfall too: because the update always bootstraps off a max over noisy estimates, Q-learning tends to systematically overestimate action values, especially early in learning — a known bias, with Double Q-learning as the standard fix.

Section 7

Exploration vs. exploitation

Every one of those "not yet sampled" cells is a decision an agent has to make, over and over: try something new, or stick with what already looks best?

🧭

Exploitation

Take the current best-known action — whichever has the highest Q(s,a) right now. Good short-term, but it starves untried actions of any information at all.

🎲

Exploration

Deliberately try something else, even though it currently looks worse — purely to find out whether that impression is actually correct.

ε

ε-greedy — the simplest balance

With small probability ε, act completely at random. The rest of the time, act greedily. One knob, one number, and it's enough to keep the convergence guarantee alive.

♾

Why it isn't optional

Q-learning's convergence guarantee requires every (s,a) pair to be visited infinitely often, in the limit. A purely greedy agent can get permanently stuck never discovering a better alternative.

A trading system that only ever takes the setup it currently rates highest can end up structurally blind to whether a different setup is actually better — it will never generate the data needed to find out. Exploration is the cost of keeping that door open.

Push ε toward 0 and the agent almost never leaves its current best guess — fast to exploit what it already believes, but slow (or unable) to discover it might be wrong. Push ε toward 1 and it's exploring almost constantly — plenty of information, very little of it acted on. Most practical setups pick something small (often 0.05–0.2) and sometimes shrink it further as learning progresses.

Section 9

Q-learning's off-policy sibling: SARSA

Change exactly one term in the update, and the entire character of what's being learned changes with it.

Q-learning — off-policy

Bootstraps off maxa′ Q(s′,a′) — the best available next action, full stop — regardless of what the agent's real, exploring behavior actually did next. It learns the value of the greedy policy while behaving ε-greedily.

SARSA — on-policy

Bootstraps off Q(s′,a′) for whichever action a′ was actually taken next, exploration included. It learns the value of the policy genuinely being followed — noise, occasional bad detours, and all.

The name SARSA is literally the five things the update touches, in order: State, Action, Reward, next State, next Action. That's also the one substitution — maxa′Q(s′,a′) becomes Q(s′,a′) for the action actually taken — separating it from Q-learning.

Where this needs qualifying

Off-policy learning is what lets a system keep exploring while its learned Q-values still track what the fully greedy policy would be worth — useful for a strategy that's still being refined. But it can also end up favoring paths that are optimal only if you stop exploring off them entirely. SARSA's numbers are more conservative precisely because they price in the real, ongoing cost of the exploration actually happening.

Learning about the greedy policy's values while behaving under a different, exploring policy is exactly what "off-policy" means.

ε-greedy is the simplest practical mechanism for keeping exploration alive without abandoning exploitation entirely.

Section 11

What to carry forward

01

Value/policy iteration need P and R known up front. Q-learning learns Q(s,a) directly from sampled (s,a,r,s′) experience — no model required.

02

TD learning updates a value estimate toward a target built from one real reward plus a discounted guess about what's next, rather than waiting for a whole episode's actual outcome.

03

Q-learning's update — Q(s,a) ← Q(s,a) + α[r + γ·maxa′Q(s′,a′) − Q(s,a)] — always bootstraps off the best next action, which is exactly why it's off-policy.

04

Repeated sampling of one transition follows a clean geometric recursion toward Q* — but with a constant learning rate, it gets arbitrarily close without ever exactly arriving.

05

Exploration isn't optional — every (state, action) pair needs enough visits for its Q-value to be learned correctly. ε-greedy is the simplest practical mechanism.

06

SARSA changes one term — max becomes the action actually taken — to become on-policy, learning the value of the policy actually being followed, exploration included.

What this now lets you do

Estimate action-values directly from a stream of lived, trade-by-trade experience, without ever having to specify the market's transition probabilities up front. Update those estimates after every single step instead of waiting for a trade or episode to close. And treat the explore/exploit trade-off as an explicit, tunable knob (ε) rather than something left implicit or ignored.
