Hedging with Options: Puts, Collars and FX Cover

A hedge is insurance: a certain cost today for a better worst case later. Here is how the protective put, the collar and the put spread are built, how a business hedges a currency payment with a forward, an option or a seagull, what each costs, and when the premium is not worth paying.

CONCEPT — how hedges are built and priced; nothing here says a hedge will pay ~19 min read Prereqs: The Four Option Positions, Black-Scholes-Merton Option Pricing
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
Risk. This is education, not advice. Options can lose the whole premium, and options you sell can lose far more than you collect. Every price here comes from Black-Scholes with the inputs stated, not from a live market: real quotes add spreads, skew, fees and, for currency options, a dealer's margin. A hedge removes one risk and adds others (cost, basis, counterparty, complexity).
TL;DR
  • A hedge is insurance, not a profit line. You pay a certain premium now to improve the worst case later. On average that costs you something. The only question is whether the protection is worth its price.
  • Protective put. A six-month 95 put on a stock at 100 costs 2.68 (about 2.68%). Your worst case is then the 5 points to the strike plus the premium, 7.68%, whatever the market does, and you keep every gain above 102.68.
  • Cheaper hedges change the shape, not just the price. A 95/85 put spread costs 2.08 but protects nothing below 85. A 95/110 collar costs −0.08 (a small credit) but stops your gains at +10.08%. Always ask where the hole is.
  • Size by exposure, not by feel. Contracts = beta × portfolio value ÷ (index level × multiplier). A $2m portfolio at beta 1.2 against an index at 5,000 needs 4.8 contracts, so 5. Basis risk (your holdings are not the index) is left over.
  • Currency cover. A forward fixes 1.0881 with no premium (the 81.3 pips over spot are an interest difference, not a fee). An option at 1.10 costs 1.74% of the amount and keeps the upside. Collars, participating forwards and seagulls pay for protection by giving something up; the seagull gives protection back in the far tail.
  • Real-world episode: on 19 February 2020 the VIX closed at 14.38; on 16 March it closed at a record 82.69. On a flat-volatility illustration, the same three-month put, 10% out of the money, went from about 0.26% of the portfolio to 7.01%. Same insurance, 27 times the price.
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

I hold a hundred thousand dollars of an index fund and the next six months worry me. Someone said buy a put. What does that actually do?

A put is the right to sell at a fixed price. Say the fund is at one hundred and you buy a six-month put struck at ninety-five. If the fund ends at seventy, you still sell at ninety-five. So the most you can lose on the shares is five percent.

And what does that right cost?

About two dollars sixty-eight for every hundred of fund. Call it two point seven percent, paid today, gone if you never needed it. Add it to the five and your worst case is about seven point seven percent. That is the whole trade: you swap a possible big loss for a certain small one.

Two point seven percent for six months sounds like a lot.

It is, and that is the first lesson. Repeat it every six months and you pay over five percent a year, which is most of what you hope shares will earn. Insurance is not free, because the seller prices it for the risk.

Can I make it cheaper?

Only by giving something up. Sell a call at one hundred and ten and its premium pays for the put almost exactly, so the collar costs nothing. But your gains now stop at ten percent. Or buy a put spread: the ninety-five put, minus an eighty-five put. It costs about two dollars, but below eighty-five you are exposed again.

So a cheaper hedge is a different shape.

Always. When a hedge is cheap, ask where the hole is.

How many contracts for a big portfolio?

Size it by beta. Two million dollars, a beta of one point two, an index at five thousand, a hundred dollars a point. That works out at four point eight contracts, so you round to five. And the hedge only works as well as the index tracks what you own.

What about a company with a currency bill?

Same logic. An American firm owes ten million euros in six months. A forward fixes the rate at one point oh eight eight one, a little worse than today. That is not a fee, it is the gap between dollar and euro interest rates. An option costs a premium but lets you gain if the euro falls. The no-premium versions, the collar, the participating forward and the seagull, all sell some of that benefit, and the seagull hands the protection back if the euro soars.

When is an option hedge poor value?

After the shock. On the nineteenth of February twenty twenty the VIX closed at fourteen. On the sixteenth of March it closed at eighty-two point six nine, a record. On a simple flat-volatility illustration, a three-month put ten percent out of the money went from about a quarter of one percent of the portfolio to seven percent. Same insurance, twenty-seven times the price.

So the one-line version?

Decide the loss you cannot live with, price the ways to cap it, take the cheapest shape whose hole you can afford, and set the budget before the panic, not during it.

Section 01

Why this matters

Every earlier lesson in this series is about taking a view with options. This one is about the opposite job: you already own something, or owe something, and you want to be sure one bad outcome cannot hurt you more than you can bear. A portfolio manager with a drawdown limit, a company that will have to pay a supplier in euros, and a private investor holding one large share position are all in the same situation. None of them is forecasting. They are buying a better worst case.

Options are the natural tool because they pay only when the bad thing happens, and they leave the good outcome mostly open. A forward or a sale of the asset also removes the risk, but it removes the benefit too. That asymmetry is what you are paying for, and the price is set by the market's view of how far the asset might move, which is its implied volatility.

Hedging with options is easy to describe and easy to get wrong. The failures are rarely in the arithmetic. They come from paying too much, from a hole nobody looked for, from a hedge that does not match the exposure, or from running a hedge for years without asking what it costs. Each section below says what goes wrong as well as how the hedge works.

💡
Plain-English intuition

You do not insure the house because you expect a fire

You pay a known, small amount each year so that one ruinous outcome stops being ruinous. Most years the policy "wastes" your money, and nobody calls that a mistake. A hedge works the same way. The premium is the cost of sleeping at night, and it is higher when the insurer thinks fire is likelier.

The same analogy shows the traps. A policy with a large excess (a deep out-of-the-money put) is cheap but pays little in a small disaster. A policy that excludes flood (a put spread below its lower strike) is cheaper still but silent exactly when the worst happens. A policy you buy while the house is already burning costs the most. And if you insure the neighbour's house instead of your own (the wrong index), it may not pay when yours burns.

💸

Paying too much

Premium is a certain loss. Hedge every month at the money and the cost can take most of the return you hold the asset to earn.

🕳

A hole you did not look for

Cheaper structures give something up: a cap on gains, or no cover below a lower strike. The loss that hurts most can sit in the hole.

🎯

Hedging the wrong thing

An index put on a stock-specific portfolio leaves basis risk. A hedge that does not match the exposure is a separate bet.

⏳

Hedging after the shock

Protection costs most when everyone wants it. The premium rises with the volatility that just arrived.

Section 02

The math, step by step: protecting a share position

Watch the formula build
Where the floor, K − S₀ − P, comes from, one term at a time
\[ \Pi = \color{#fbbf24}{S_T - S_0} \]

Start with the shares alone. You bought at \(S_0\); at expiry they are worth \(S_T\). Profit is the difference, and it falls one-for-one with the market.

\[ \Pi = S_T - S_0 + \color{#fbbf24}{\max(K - S_T,\,0)} \]

Add the put. It pays \(K - S_T\) when the shares end below the strike \(K\), and nothing otherwise. Below \(K\), every point the shares lose, the put gives back.

\[ \Pi = S_T - S_0 + \max(K - S_T,\,0) - \color{#fbbf24}{P} \]

Subtract the premium \(P\) you paid today. It is the one cost you cannot avoid, and it shifts the whole line down by the same amount everywhere.

\[ \Pi \;\ge\; \color{#10b981}{K - S_0 - P} \quad\text{for every } S_T \]

Below \(K\) the share loss and the put payoff cancel, so the profit is stuck at \(K - S_0 - P\). That is the floor. With \(S_0 = 100\), \(K = 95\), \(P = 2.68\), the worst case is \(-7.68\).

\[ \Pi_{\text{collar}} = \Pi_{\text{put}} \;\color{#fbbf24}{-\,\max(S_T - K_c,\,0) + C} \]

Pay for the put by selling a call at a higher strike \(K_c\) and keeping its premium \(C\). Above \(K_c\) the call you sold takes back every extra point, so profit is capped at \(K_c - S_0 - (P - C)\). That is the price of cheap protection.

The protective put. You hold one share worth \(S_0\) and buy a European put with strike \(K\) and expiry \(T\), paying premium \(P\) now. At expiry the profit per share is the formula above. Three numbers describe the hedge:

\[ \text{floor} = K - S_0 - P, \qquad \text{breakeven} = S_0 + P, \qquad \text{cost} = \frac{P}{S_0} \]
K − S₀ − Pthe worst profit, a negative number: the gap to the strike plus the premium
S₀ + Pwhere the shares must end for the hedged position to break even
P / S₀the premium as a share of the position: what the insurance costs

Notice what is not in the formula: your view. The floor is fixed the day you buy, and it does not depend on what the market does next. The premium \(P\) does depend on the market's view. It comes from the Black-Scholes price at the volatility the market quotes, so it rises when implied volatility rises, and it is dearer for a strike close to the current price than for one far away.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

A protective put is a "you can't lose more than this" ticket for your shares. You pay a little for the ticket today. If the price drops a lot, you hand over the shares at the ticket price. If the price goes up, you keep the gain and the ticket just expires. The ticket costs more when everyone is scared.

Cheaper cousins. Two other ways to build protection are common, and each is the protective put with a leg added.

\[ \Pi_{\text{spread}} = S_T - S_0 + \max(K - S_T,0) - \max(K_\ell - S_T,0) - (P - P_\ell) \]
K_ℓthe lower strike: a put you sell to help pay for the one you buy
P − P_ℓnet premium of the put spread: cheaper than the plain put
−max(K_ℓ − S_T, 0)the hole: below K_ℓ the sold put cancels the bought one and the loss grows again

The put spread is cheaper because the put you sell is worth something. The price is a hole: protection stops at \(K_\ell\). The collar uses a sold call instead, so the hole is above the market rather than below it: you give up the best outcomes rather than insuring less of the worst ones. The zero-cost collar chooses \(K_c\) so that the two premiums match:

\[ C(K_c) = P(K_p) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{net premium} = P - C = 0 \]
C(K_c)premium received for the call you sell at the cap
P(K_p)premium paid for the put you buy at the floor
K_csolved for: the cap the market lets you sell at no net cost
🧩
The Lego view

Put-call parity says a hedged share is a call plus cash

With no dividends, put-call parity is \(C - P = S_0 - K e^{-rT}\). Rearranged, \(S_0 + P = C + K e^{-rT}\): a share plus a put at strike \(K\) has the same payoff as a call at strike \(K\) plus the cash that grows to \(K\). So a protective put is a long call in disguise, and a collar is a call spread plus cash. This is the same reshuffle that makes a covered call equal to a short put. The four positions in the first lesson of this group are the only bricks. Every hedge is a stack of them.

Worked example

1,000 shares at $100, six months, three ways to protect them

You hold 1,000 shares of a broad equity fund at \(S_0 = 100\), a $100,000 position (ten option contracts of 100 shares). Inputs for every price below: \(T = 0.5\), \(r = 4\%\), implied volatility 20% for every strike, no dividends.

1

Price the put and the call

Black-Scholes gives \(P(95) = 2.68\), \(P(85) = 0.60\) and \(C(110) = 2.76\) per share. On 1,000 shares the 95 put costs $2,679.

2

Protective put: floor and breakeven

\[ \text{floor} = 95 - 100 - 2.68 = -7.68, \qquad \text{breakeven} = 100 + 2.68 = 102.68 \]

The worst case is a loss of 7.68% ($7,679) however far the market falls. The shares must rise 2.68% before the hedged position is ahead.

3

Put spread: cheaper, with a hole

Net premium \(2.68 - 0.60 = 2.08\). Between 95 and 85 the loss is capped at \(5 + 2.08 = 7.08\), but a crash to 70 costs \(22.08\).

4

Collar: nearly free, capped

Net premium \(2.68 - 2.76 = −0.08\), a small credit. Worst case \(5 - 0.08 = 4.92\) lost, best case \(10 - (-0.08) = +10.08\) gained.

What it really costs to trade. The numbers above use the model price. In a real market you pay the ask and a commission. Say the 95 put quotes 2.66 bid, 2.70 ask and you pay 2.70, plus $0.65 a contract on ten contracts. The cost is 10 × 100 × 2.70 + 10 × 0.65 = $2,706.50, so the true floor is a loss of $7,706.50 (7.71%), about 3 cents a share worse than the model says. For a liquid index fund that is small. For a thinly traded option, the spread alone can eat the protection.

Section 03

Sizing the hedge and setting the budget

A hedge has two dials besides its shape: how much of the exposure it covers, and how often you pay for it. Both are easy to set by feel, and both turn a sensible hedge into a mismatch or a leak.

How many contracts: the hedge ratio

If you hold shares of the very thing the option is written on, the ratio is one for one: ten contracts of 100 shares cover 1,000 shares. If you hold a portfolio and hedge with an index option, you scale by how much the portfolio tends to move with the index, its beta:

\[ N = \frac{\beta \, V}{I \times m} \]
Nnumber of option contracts to buy, before rounding
βsensitivity of your portfolio to the index: 1.2 means it tends to move 1.2% for each 1% in the index
Vvalue of the portfolio you want to protect
I × mindex level times the contract multiplier: the notional of one contract

Where does beta come from? The hedge that minimises the variance of the hedged position uses \(h^* = \rho\,\sigma_s/\sigma_f\), the correlation between your holdings and the hedge instrument times the ratio of their volatilities, which is exactly the regression beta. The share of variance the hedge removes is \(\rho^2\).

\[ h^* = \rho\,\frac{\sigma_s}{\sigma_f}, \qquad \text{variance removed} = \rho^2 \]
ρcorrelation between your holdings and the index
σₛ, σ_fvolatility of your holdings and of the hedge instrument
ρ²the fraction of your variance a perfectly sized hedge can remove
🧮
Worked example

$2m of shares, beta 1.2, hedged with index puts

Your holdings have \(\rho = 0.8\) with the index, volatility 22.5% a year, against the index's 15%. So \(\beta = 0.8 \times 22.5 / 15 = 1.2\), and a perfect hedge would still leave \(1 - 0.8^2 = 36\%\) of your variance. The index trades at 5,000 (an illustrative level) with a multiplier of 100, so one contract covers $500,000.

\[ N = \frac{1.2 \times 2{,}000{,}000}{5{,}000 \times 100} = 4.8 \;\approx\; 5 \text{ contracts} \]

Rounding up to 5 covers $2.5m of index against $2.4m of beta-adjusted exposure, about 4% too much. Buy the 4,750 put (95%) for six months at 20% volatility: 133.9 index points, so $13,393 a contract, $66,964 for five, which is 3.35% of the portfolio. That is more than the 2.68% a one-for-one hedge costs, because you cover 1.2 times the notional and rounded up.

Case A, the index does what beta says. The index falls 20% to 4,000, so the portfolio falls \(1.2 \times 20\% = 24\%\), a loss of $480,000. The five puts pay \((4{,}750 - 4{,}000) \times 100 \times 5 = \$375{,}000\). Net of the $66,964 premium the loss is $171,964, or 8.60%.

Case B, basis risk. Your shares fall the same 24%, but the index falls only 10% to 4,500. The puts pay \((4{,}750 - 4{,}500) \times 500 = \$125{,}000\). The loss is $421,964, or 21.10%. The hedge worked as designed, and it covered about a quarter of the loss. The remaining 36% of variance is the price of hedging holdings with an instrument that is not them.

The trap: a beta from a regression is an average over a past window. Betas move with the market regime, and in a crash correlations tend to rise while idiosyncratic stocks still do their own thing. A hedge sized on last year's beta protects against last year's market.

How often you pay: the hedge budget

A one-off hedge for a known event is a decision. A hedge you roll every month is a running cost. The "cost if nothing happens" is the premium each time, scaled to a year:

\[ \text{annual cost} \;\approx\; \frac{12}{m}\cdot\frac{P(K, m)}{S_0} \]
mmonths to expiry of each put you roll
P(K, m)premium for a put of strike K and m months, in the same units as the share price
12 / mhow many times a year you buy it

The cost is also what is left of the premium's meaning: it is the decay you pay for the right to be protected, the theta you met in Theta Decay. At the worked example's inputs, the 95 put loses about 0.0097 per share per day if nothing moves, about $9.73 a day on 1,000 shares.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Paying for a hedge again and again is like renewing a phone-screen insurance plan every month. Each month's fee looks tiny, but twelve of them in a year add up to a lot, even if you never drop the phone. The closer the plan is to "fix any scratch", the more it costs.

🎯
Real-world trading scenario

"This position may not lose more than 8% in the next six months"

A risk limit says the 1,000-share position may not lose more than 8% from today over the next six months. Which hedge from the worked example keeps within it?

  • The 95 put: worst case 7.68%. Within the limit. A 94 put is cheaper, but its worst case is 8.37%, which breaks it, and a strike of about 94.5 is the lowest that works.
  • The 95/85 put spread: worst case 7.08% only while the price stays above 85. Below 85 the loss keeps growing, to 22.08% at 70. It looks like the cheap answer and it fails the limit in the very scenario the limit exists for.
  • The 95/110 collar: worst case 4.92%, inside the limit with room to spare, at the cost of gains above +10.08%.

The limit is a statement about the worst case, so the structure has to be judged on its worst case, not its premium. The collar and the put pass; the spread passes only on paper. Which of the two to choose depends on whether you would rather pay 2.68% or give up gains beyond +10%.

Section 04

Hedging a currency payment: forward, option, collar, participating forward, seagull

A company does not hold a portfolio. It has a cash flow in a foreign currency on a known date, and the exchange rate on that date is a risk it did not choose. The same tools apply, with a different vocabulary and one new ingredient: the forward, which removes the risk entirely at the cost of the benefit.

🧮
The setting for every FX number below (hypothetical inputs)

A US importer owes €10,000,000 in six months

EURUSD (dollars per euro) is 1.0800. Six-month rates, continuously compounded: dollar \(r_d = 4.5\%\), euro \(r_f = 3.0\%\). Implied volatility is a flat 8% for every strike, which real smiles are not. The importer is hurt if the euro rises, so it needs the right to buy euros at a fixed rate: a euro call (a dollar put). Currency options are traded over the counter, and the buyer's premium is quoted in several conventions; here it is in dollars per euro.

The forward: the price of certainty

\[ F = S\,e^{(r_d - r_f)T} \]
Fthe forward rate: dollars per euro for delivery at time T, fixed today
r_d − r_fthe interest gap between the currency you pay (USD) and the one you receive (EUR)
F − Sthe forward points: not a fee, the interest difference over the period

Here \(F = 1.0800 \times e^{0.015 \times 0.5} = 1.0881\), which is \(81.3\) pips above spot. Buying euros forward costs more than buying them now because dollar rates are higher than euro rates. If the importer bought euros today instead, it would earn 3% on them instead of 4.5% on dollars. The points pay for that gap, as in covered interest parity. They are interest, not a charge, and they are not a forecast. The cost of the hedge versus today's spot is \(0.0081 \times 10{,}000{,}000 = \$81,305\).

The forward costs nothing upfront and is firm: if the euro falls to 1.00 the importer still pays 1.0881, an opportunity cost of $881,305 against going unhedged. A forward also carries an obligation, so if the rate moves against it the bank may ask for collateral, depending on the credit terms. An option you buy has no such call: your loss is capped at the premium.

The option and the structures built from it

A currency option is priced by Black-Scholes with the foreign interest rate in the place of the dividend yield, the Garman-Kohlhagen form. The call on one euro, struck at \(K\), is:

\[ C = S\,e^{-r_f T} N(d_1) - K\,e^{-r_d T} N(d_2), \qquad d_{1,2} = \frac{\ln(S/K) + (r_d - r_f \pm \tfrac12\sigma^2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}} \]
S e^(−r_f T)today's rate, discounted at the euro rate: the euro behaves like a dividend-paying asset
K e^(−r_d T)the strike in dollars, discounted at the dollar rate
N(d₁), N(d₂)the normal probabilities from Black-Scholes; swap signs for the put

With these inputs a euro call struck at 1.10 costs \(0.0188\) dollars per euro, which is \(1.74\%\) of the amount, or $187,776 on €10m. The effective rate the importer pays at expiry, per euro, is the rate it ends up paying for euros after the structure has done its work:

1

Forward

Rate paid \(= F = 1.0881\) always. No premium. No benefit if the euro falls.

2

Vanilla call at \(K = 1.10\)

Rate paid \(= \min(S_T, K) + C = \min(S_T, 1.10) + 0.0188\). You pay market below 1.10 and 1.10 above it, plus the premium either way. The worst case, 1.1188, is worse than the forward's 1.0881: an out-of-the-money option is insurance with a deductible, and you keep the upside if the euro falls.

3

Zero-cost collar

Buy the 1.10 call, sell a euro put at \(K_p\) chosen so its premium equals the call's: \(K_p = 1.0769\). Rate paid \(= \text{clamp}(S_T,\ 1.0769,\ 1.10)\). You are protected above 1.10 and obliged to pay 1.0769 if the euro is lower, so almost all of the benefit of a falling euro is sold: only the 0.3% between spot and \(K_p\) is left.

4

Participating forward

Buy the 1.10 call on the full amount and sell the 1.10 put on only a fraction \(f = C/P\), which is \(0.0188 / 0.0304 = 61.8\%\). Above 1.10 you pay 1.10. Below it you pay \(f \times 1.10 + (1-f) \times S_T\), so you share in the benefit on the remaining 38.2%. At 1.00 that is 1.0618.

5

Seagull

Buy the 1.10 call, sell a 1.14 call and sell a put at \(1.0576\), so the premiums net to zero. You are protected between 1.10 and 1.14. Above 1.14, each extra cent costs you again: at 1.20 you pay 1.1600, not 1.20 and not 1.10. Below 1.0576 you pay 1.0576.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

You have to buy a toy in six months and you are scared it will get dearer. A forward is paying today's price for the toy later: safe, but if it gets cheaper you still pay the old price. An option is paying a small fee for the right to buy at a set price: if the toy gets cheaper you buy it cheap. The "free" versions pay the fee by promising to sell something back, so you give up some of the good news, or, with the seagull, you lose your protection if the price goes crazy high.

How much to hedge. The table is per euro, so it scales with the notional. Firms rarely hedge the whole of a forecast cash flow. A sale that may not close, or a payment that may be revised, is not a certain exposure, and a hedge on money that never arrives is a speculative position. A common answer is to hedge a share of the forecast and add to it in tranches as the date approaches. Accounting rules for hedges (IFRS 9, or ASC 815 in the US) also shape which structures a finance team prefers, so the finance team should be in the conversation, not told afterwards.

The seagull trap: a seagull is sold as "zero cost protection". It is protection between 1.10 and 1.14 and nothing beyond. The exposures that frighten a treasurer, a euro at 1.30 or 1.50, are exactly where a seagull stops covering. It is a fair structure if you know that and want it. It is a trap if you bought it to cover the tail.

Real currency quotes make collars and seagulls less symmetric than this flat-volatility example. Currency options are quoted by delta and by the market's risk reversal and butterfly, and the risk reversal is the premium one side of the market pays over the other. If euro calls are in demand, they trade at a higher volatility than the matching puts, and a "zero cost" collar needs a tighter floor or a small net payment. FX Options Quoting shows how that skew is quoted and read.

Section 05

It actually happened: the same insurance at two prices, 2020

The cleanest demonstration that a hedge's price moves with fear is the spring of 2020. Nothing about the insurance changed between February and March except what the market thought it was insuring against.

It actually happened

Cheap in February, dearest ever in March

On 19 February 2020 the S&P 500 closed at a record 3,386.15 and the VIX, the market's gauge of 30-day implied volatility on the index, closed at 14.38. Protection was cheap and, with the index at a record, few people wanted it.

Then the coronavirus shock hit markets. By 16 March the index had closed at 2,386.13, 29.5% below that peak, and the VIX closed at 82.69, the highest close in the series, which runs back to 1990 (the previous record was 80.86 on 20 November 2008). The index kept falling to a closing low of 2,237.40 on 23 March, 33.9% below its February peak.

Now price the same three-month put, struck 10% below the index, in each world, with Black-Scholes, a flat volatility and no interest. At 15%, close to the February VIX, it costs 0.26% of the portfolio. At 60% it costs 7.01%, about 27 times as much. The 60% is a deliberately round illustration: the VIX measures 30-day volatility, and longer-dated options were priced at lower volatilities because the volatility curve was inverted, so 60% is for three months, not 82.69.

Whoever bought that protection on 16 March paid close to the highest price on record for it. Three months later, on 16 June, the index closed at 3,124.74, 31.0% above the 16 March close, and it set a new record close of 3,389.78 on 18 August. A put bought at the panic price would have expired worthless against a recovery. That does not show that hedging in February was wise: in most months a hedge costs money and no crash comes. It shows that the price of cover moves with fear, and that you cannot buy it at the quiet price once the fear has arrived.

🧮
Worked example (illustrative prices, three months, no interest)

What a 90% put costs at two volatilities

For a spot of 100, strike 90, \(T = 0.25\), \(r = 0\), the Black-Scholes put is \(0.26\) at \(\sigma = 15\%\) and \(7.01\) at \(\sigma = 60\%\). The ratio is \(7.01 / 0.26 \approx 27\). Volatility rose four times, and the price of this particular put rose twenty-seven times, because a strike 10% out is a bet on a big move and a big move had just become likely.

The flip side is useful. The 120 call you could sell against it cost \(5.44\) at 60%, so a collar of the 90 put and the 120 call costs only \(1.57\) in net premium. When volatility is dear, the leg you sell pays well too.

⚠️
Where the textbook version breaks

The model gives you a price, not a good time to buy

Black-Scholes will price the put at any volatility you feed it, and it will not tell you the volatility is extreme. In stress, bid-ask spreads typically widen too, so the price you pay is above the model. The cheap moment is the moment nobody feels a need, which is why hedge programmes are set as policy, with a budget decided in calm, not as a reaction. The opposite error is as common: a hedger who stopped paying in the third quiet year and was unprotected in the fourth.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Umbrellas cost a pound in the sunshine and ten pounds the minute it starts pouring, and the seller knows it. If you wait until you are wet to buy one, you pay the rain price. Most people only think about umbrellas once the rain starts, so they pay the most for them.

Section 06

How practitioners actually use this, and when it is poor value

Corporate treasurers hedge currency payments mainly with forwards, and use options where the exposure itself is uncertain, such as a bid that may not be won, because an option lets you walk away for the cost of the premium. Fund managers use put spreads and collars to cap a drawdown for a known window, such as around an event or a reporting date. Private investors with one large holding use collars, because the capped upside is a price they can see and accept. Dealers sit on the other side and price all of it off the same volatility surface.

A hedge is a position with Greeks. A long put is long gamma and long vega and pays theta. So it earns on mark-to-market when volatility jumps and bleeds when markets are calm. That is the behaviour you bought, and it is why a hedge looks like a drag for years. A collar nets some of it: the short call gives back part of the vega and theta. A seagull and a put spread each leave a short option somewhere, so each has a tail where they behave like the thing you were hedging. Always list the legs you are short.

Options are good value when

  • The exposure is uncertain or contingent, so you want the right but not the obligation.
  • You need a hard worst case for a fixed window, with a premium budget set in advance.
  • Volatility is low relative to its recent range, so protection is cheap.
  • You can sell something you do not mind giving up (a cap, a far-out leg) to pay for it.

Options are poor value when

  • Volatility has just spiked: you pay the panic price. Consider a smaller size, a spread or a collar instead.
  • The exposure is certain and a forward gives the certainty for no premium.
  • You roll short-dated at-the-money puts all year: the cost can be most of the return you hold the asset for.
  • The hedge is the wrong instrument (a big basis gap), so you pay for cover that may not pay.
  • The position is small enough that selling a part of it does the same job without premium or spread.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where hedges quietly go wrong

Calling an unused hedge a waste. The premium bought a worst case for six months. Judge the hedge on what it insured and what it cost, not on whether the bad outcome happened. The honest question is whether the price was fair for the protection, and a fair price still loses money on average, which is what insurance is.

Hedging a different date or instrument from the exposure. A one-month put against a six-month exposure leaves five months open. A stock hedged with an index leaves basis risk. A currency payment hedged with the wrong pair leaves cross-rate risk.

Pricing a collar at flat volatility. Real puts and calls have different volatilities, so the "free" collar on the screen usually costs something or needs a lower cap. The flat-volatility toy in this lesson flatters it.

Hedging more than you have. If the exposure shrinks (the sale falls through, the share is sold) the hedge stays. An over-hedged position is a bet in the other direction.

Treating the strike as the loss limit. The floor is the strike minus the premium, not the strike. And with a put spread the floor stops at the lower strike. The limit you can promise is the worst case of the whole structure, including the legs you sold.

Scenario drill · 16 March 2020

The index is down 30%, the VIX is at a record, and the committee wants cover

You manage a $10m portfolio that tracks the S&P 500. It is unhedged. At the close on 16 March 2020 the index is 2,386.13, 29.5% below its peak of about four weeks earlier, and the VIX has just closed at a record 82.69. Your risk committee wants protection for the next three months. For the arithmetic, suppose three-month options are priced at a flat 60% volatility, with no interest (an illustration, not a quote). What do you do?

  1. Buy three-month at-the-money puts on the whole portfolio.
    At the illustrative price this costs about 11.9% of the portfolio, $1,192,354 on $10m. The index would need to fall more than that again just for you to break even, and you would have paid close to the highest price on record for the cover.
  2. Buy three-month puts 10% out of the money on the whole portfolio.
    Cheaper, at about 7.0%, and it does set a floor. But the worst case is then 10% plus 7.0%, about 17.0% of a portfolio that has already fallen by about 30%, and you are still paying a panic price for it.
  3. Buy the 90% put and sell a 120% call against it, a collar, so that the volatility you sell pays for most of the volatility you buy.
    At 60% the 120 call is worth about 5.4%, so the collar costs only about 1.6% net. The worst case is a further 11.6%, and the price is a cap on gains at +20% for three months. Real call volatilities sit below put volatilities (skew), so the real net cost is higher, which you should check before trading. The idea is the point: when volatility is dear, sell some of it too.
  4. Wait for volatility to fall before buying any protection.
    You stay fully exposed to the next leg down while you wait. A week later, on 23 March, the index closed at 2,237.40, another 6.2% lower, and the VIX was still 61.59.

What happened. The index closed at 2,237.40 on 23 March, its closing low and 33.9% below the February peak, then recovered: on 16 June it closed at 3,124.74, 31.0% above the 16 March close, and it set a new record close of 3,389.78 on 18 August. On those illustrative prices the plain 90% put would have cost 7.0% and expired worthless over the three months. The collar would have capped the recovery at +20% less its net cost, 18.4%, against the index's +31.0%. Hindsight favours the unhedged, and it proves nothing about the decision: in a different path the cap would have been irrelevant and the floor everything. The choice is graded on prices and mandate at the time. The option prices here are illustrative flat-volatility Black-Scholes values, not market quotes.

Section 07

Key Takeaways

✓A hedge buys a better worst case for a certain cost. The protective put's floor is \(K - S_0 - P\), set the day you buy, whatever the market does next. On average the hedge costs money. That is what insurance is.
✓Every cheaper hedge is a different shape. A put spread loses its cover below the lower strike; a collar gives up gains above the cap. Judge a structure by its worst case, not its premium.
✓Size by exposure. Contracts = beta × value ÷ (index × multiplier). A beta hedge removes only \(\rho^2\) of the variance, and the rest is basis risk.
✓Forward for certainty, option for contingency. A forward fixes the rate with no premium and gives up the benefit. Its points are the interest gap, not a fee. An option keeps the benefit and costs a premium, and an out-of-the-money option's worst case is worse than the forward's.
⚠Set the budget in calm, not in panic. A rolled 95% put costs roughly 4% to 6.4% of the position a year at 20% volatility if nothing happens, and the same put cost about 27 times as much after the March 2020 spike on illustrative prices.
⚠Know which legs you are short. A collar, a put spread and a seagull each carry a short option. Each has a tail where it behaves like the exposure you were hedging.
✗A seagull is not tail protection. Above its second call strike, and below its short put, it stops protecting and starts adding exposure. "Zero cost" means the premium was paid in something else.
Section 08

Self-test

Q1. You own a share at 100 and buy a 90 put for 3. What is the most you can lose, and where do you break even?
The put pays \(90 - S_T\) below 90, so below 90 the share loss and the put payoff cancel and the profit is stuck at \(90 - 100 - 3 = -13\). The worst case is a loss of 13, not 10: the premium sits on top of the gap to the strike. You break even where the share has risen enough to pay for the put, at \(100 + 3 = 103\).
Q2. A collar is advertised as "zero cost". What has been paid instead?
Gains above the cap. The call you sold returns its premium today but takes back every point above the call strike, so the profit is capped at the call strike minus the starting price, less the net premium. The premium has not vanished; it has been swapped for the upside. With skew the swap is worse still: puts trade at a higher volatility than calls, so the call funds less of the put than a flat-volatility model suggests, and a real collar needs a lower cap or a small net payment.
Q3. A $3m portfolio has beta 0.9. The index is at 4,000 and the multiplier is 100. How many put contracts size the hedge, and what is left unhedged even if you size it exactly?
\(N = 0.9 \times 3{,}000{,}000 / (4{,}000 \times 100) = 2{,}700{,}000 / 400{,}000 = 6.75\), so 7 contracts, a small over-hedge. What is left is basis risk: if the correlation between the portfolio and the index is \(\rho\), the best a perfectly sized hedge can remove is \(\rho^2\) of the variance, and a beta estimated on a past window may not hold in the next fall.
Q4. Why does a US company buying euros six months forward pay more than today's spot, when it does not expect the euro to rise?
The forward rate is \(F = S e^{(r_d - r_f)T}\), set by the interest gap, not by a forecast. Dollar rates are above euro rates here (4.5% against 3.0%), so the euro trades at a forward premium: about 81 pips over six months on a spot of 1.08. The points compensate for the fact that holding dollars earns more than holding euros. It is the interest difference, not a fee, and no expectation about the exchange rate is involved.
Q5. Where does a seagull stop protecting, and why might that matter more than its zero premium?
It protects between the bought call's strike and the sold call's strike (1.10 to 1.14 in the example). Above the higher strike, each extra cent costs the buyer again, so at 1.20 the importer pays 1.16 rather than 1.10. Below the sold put's strike the buyer pays that strike regardless of how cheap the euro becomes. The scenarios that worry a treasurer, a very large move in the hedged direction, sit exactly in the region where it stops working.
Q6. A treasurer says: "The option hedge cost us $188,000 and expired unused, so it was a waste." How would you reply?
The premium bought a defined worst case for six months, and the euro not rising is the outcome the hedge was designed to be wasted in. Judge it by two questions: what did it insure against, and was the price fair for that protection? Check the cost against the alternatives: a forward would have cost nothing upfront but given up the benefit, and a collar would have cost less but capped the gain. If the company needed a ceiling on the cost of the payment, the hedge did its job. A fair price still loses money on average. That is the nature of insurance.
