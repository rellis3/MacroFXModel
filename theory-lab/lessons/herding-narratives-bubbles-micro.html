Herding, Narratives and Bubbles

Following the crowd is often perfectly rational for each person. That's exactly why a whole market can end up confidently wrong. Add a good story and some leverage, and you get a bubble, along with the misery of being right too early.

The whole idea
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

When everyone piles into a trade, isn't that a sign they know something?

Sometimes. That's what makes herding so tricky. Copying the crowd is often perfectly rational for each person, and the whole crowd can still end up confidently wrong.

How can everyone be rational and the crowd be wrong?

Picture two restaurants side by side. Your guidebook mildly prefers the empty one, but the other has three tables of diners. You figure they know something, and you go in. The next couple's guidebook also prefers the empty one, but now there are four tables. In they go.

And after that, nobody's own guidebook matters.

Exactly. Once the first two or three choices line up, everyone after them copies, whatever they privately believe. Their choices tell the street nothing new. Economists call it an information cascade.

How often does it go wrong?

Say each person's private hunch is right seventy percent of the time. A copying crowd locks onto the wrong answer about fifteen percent of the time, usually settled by the first three or four people. The same twenty people voting on their own hunches would be wrong only about three percent of the time.

So copying makes the group about five times more likely to be wrong.

Right. The crowd ends up holding the information of about two people instead of twenty.

Where do bubbles come in?

Add a story and some leverage. A good story spreads from person to person, and a rising price is the most convincing evidence a story can have. Borrowing forces buying on the way up, and forces selling on the way down.

Give me the classic case.

In December 1996 the Fed chairman asked how we would know when irrational exuberance had pushed prices too high. The Nasdaq then nearly quadrupled. It peaked in March 2000 at just over five thousand, and by October 2002 it had fallen about seventy-eight percent.

So the sceptics made a fortune.

Many didn't last long enough. Julian Robertson's Tiger Management refused to buy the tech story. After heavy losses and client withdrawals, he announced at the end of March 2000 that he was closing the funds, within weeks of the top. Right on value, beaten by timing.

So what do I do with this?

Write down your own signal before you look at the crowd. Ask what you know that the last buyer didn't. And if you fade a crowd, size so the move against you can double without forcing you out.

So the one-line version?

A crowd is only as smart as the few people it is copying. Count your own evidence first, and size to survive being early.

Section 1 · The idea

Two restaurants, one busy, one empty

The example Banerjee (1992) used in his herding paper.

Early diners

Each follows their own guidebook. Each choice tells the street something.

Everyone after

Picks the busy one whatever their guidebook said. Their choice now tells the street nothing.

A busy restaurant can be full of people who each privately thought the other one was better. The first two or three diners decided for everyone, and they may just have been unlucky.
Section 2 · The rule

When copying becomes the rational choice

Private signal right with probability p. d = net Good-minus-Bad signals the crowd's actions have revealed. s = your own signal (±1).

odds of Good
=
(p / q)d + s
|d|≤1

Learning

Your own signal decides, so your action reveals it. d moves.

|d|=2

Cascade

Even an opposite signal can't flip the odds. You copy. Your action reveals nothing, so d stops moving for everyone after you.

Nobody behaved irrationally. Every person used Bayes' rule correctly, and the group still stopped learning.

Section 4 · The cost

Copying throws away the crowd's information

Before a cascade, d is a random walk stopped at ±2. Gambler's ruin gives the answer directly.

P(wrong cascade) = q² / (p² + q²)
Copying crowd, p = 0.7

Wrong about 15.5% of the time. Settled by the first ~3.4 people.

20 independent votes, p = 0.7

Wrong about 3.3% of the time. All twenty signals get counted.

Rational copying made the group about five times more likely to be wrong. It ends up holding the information of about two people instead of twenty.

Section 5 · Stories and stages

Narratives carry the cascade; leverage powers it

📖

Narrative economics (Shiller)

Stories spread person to person like an epidemic. A rising price is the most convincing evidence a story can have: price up → story more believable → more buyers.

①

Displacement → Boom

A real innovation creates real opportunities. Credit expands and the story spreads.

②

Euphoria

Buying because it's rising. "This time is different." Sceptics give up.

③

Profit-taking → Panic

Insiders sell and the market stalls. The leverage that forced buying now forces selling.

Minsky's stages, as Kindleberger set them out in Manias, Panics, and Crashes (1978).

Section 6 · It actually happened

1996–2002: right on value, wrong on time

It actually happened

5 Dec 1996: Greenspan asks how we'd know when "irrational exuberance" had unduly escalated asset values. The Nasdaq then nearly quadruples. Tiger Management, a value fund that wouldn't buy the tech story, closes at the end of March 2000 after losses and withdrawals, weeks from the top. The Nasdaq closes at 5,048.62 on 10 March 2000 and 1,114.11 on 9 October 2002: about −78%. It doesn't close above that peak again until 2015.

Limits of arbitrage (Shleifer & Vishny, 1997): a manager who fights a mispricing that gets worse loses clients exactly when the bet is best. Being early is the same as being wrong, for a while.

Section 7 · It actually happened

January 2021: GameStop and a narrative with a villain

It actually happened

A heavily shorted retailer, a Reddit forum, and a story anyone could follow: buy enough and the shorts must cover. From under $20 at the end of 2020 (pre-split) to an intraday high of about $483 on 28 January. Melvin Capital took $2.75bn from Citadel and Point72 on 25 January and closed in 2022. Brokers restricted buying on 28 January. Within weeks most of the spike was gone, and the late buyers held the losses.

Section 8 · What this desk found

Crowding warnings don't time the turn

The folk claim: "you can see the herd crowding in, and that tells you a break is coming."

Desk test: nothing found breadth-narrowing: 35 narrowing events in 23 years. Direction at 20 days −0.5% [−2.70, +1.57]; range +0.37 [−0.10, +0.85], and extreme broadening does the same (+0.28). A description of concentration, not a timing signal.
Desk test: nothing found dispersion-crowded-week: 68 crowded setups. Next week SPX500 +0.07 [−0.06, +0.23], NQ +0.07 [−0.03, +0.17]. Not a wild week.
Desk test: held up dispersion-reset, backwards from the story: a crowd that starts unwinding precedes a calmer week (SPX500 −0.122 [−0.190, −0.005]). Direction null. Thin sample, so not a trigger.
Section 9 · Do this now

The narrative check, before you join or fight a crowd

1

Thesis without the price. One sentence that doesn't mention it already going up.

2

My own signal. What did I see myself, before I knew what everyone else was doing?

3

The marginal buyer. Who buys from here? "People who haven't heard yet" means the cascade is near its end.

4

The forced seller. Who's on leverage, and what price forces them out?

5

Survival. If I'm right but six months early and the move against me doubles first, am I still in?

Question 1

The first two people both passed. Person 3's own signal says Good. What does Bayes' rule tell them?

Two revealed Bad signals outweigh one private Good one. Person 3 passes, which reveals nothing, so everyone after them is in the same position: a cascade.
Question 2

At p = 0.8, how often does a copying crowd lock onto the wrong answer?

q²/(p²+q²) = 0.04/0.68 ≈ 5.9%. The 0.2% is what 20 independent votes would give: copying costs most of the crowd's information.
Question 3

Why did Tiger Management close in March 2000, weeks before its view was proved right?

Clients judge managers on recent results. A mispricing that keeps widening drains capital exactly when the bet is most attractive.
Question 4

"Dispersion is high but now falling: the crowd is unwinding and the break has started." What did the desk find?

dispersion-reset was pre-registered with the story's expectation and came back with the opposite sign: SPX500 −0.122 over the next week.
Your call
Scenario drill · 5 December 1996

The Fed chairman has just said "irrational exuberance"

Tonight Alan Greenspan asked in a speech how we would know when "irrational exuberance" had unduly escalated asset values. You run a value-minded fund, and you already think technology stocks are priced far ahead of their earnings. Your clients judge you on recent results. What do you do?

  1. Short the Nasdaq heavily now. Even the Fed chairman agrees with you.
    Being right about value says nothing about when. A heavy short has to survive the crowd pushing prices further from value first, through margin calls and client withdrawals. A speech is one piece of public news, and this one moved markets for about a day.
  2. Write down your valuation case and what would have to be true to justify the price. Keep any fade small enough that the move against you can double without forcing you out, and measure how concentrated your own exposure is.
    This uses your own information without betting the fund on the timing. Most famous "right but early" losses were sizing failures, not analysis failures. Sizing to survive keeps you in the game until the evidence turns.
  3. Stay out of tech entirely and stick to your value book.
    It avoids both the short squeeze and the crash, which is sound. But years of lagging a booming index carry their own risk when clients judge you on recent results, and that risk can close a fund before the analysis is proved right.
  4. Buy the tech leaders on margin. The crowd cannot all be wrong.
    This joins a cascade with borrowed money. You add no information of your own, and the leverage that forces buying on the way up forces selling on the way down.

What happened. Markets wobbled for a day, and the Nasdaq Composite then nearly quadrupled over the next three-plus years. Julian Robertson's Tiger Management, a value investor that would not buy the technology story, suffered heavy losses and client withdrawals, and at the end of March 2000 Robertson announced he was closing the funds. The Nasdaq had closed at its peak, 5,048.62, on 10 March 2000. By 9 October 2002 it closed at 1,114.11, a fall of about 78%, and it did not close above its 2000 peak again until 2015. The analysis was right; the timing was what cost him.

Sources: Alan Greenspan, “The Challenge of Central Banking in a Democratic Society”, Federal Reserve, 5 Dec 1996; Julian Robertson, letter to Tiger Management limited partners, 30 Mar 2000; Nasdaq Composite daily closes (FRED, NASDAQCOM) (1,300.12 on 5 Dec 1996; 5,048.62 on 10 Mar 2000; 1,114.11 on 9 Oct 2002; first close above the peak on 23 Apr 2015).

What to carry forward

01

Herding can be rational for each person and wrong for the group: once revealed signals net to two, everyone copies and later information is thrown away.

02

03

Narratives carry the cascade; leverage powers Minsky's stages, with forced buying on the way up and forced selling on the way down.

04

What this now lets you do

Tell when your trade is adding information and when it's just copying. Separate a real displacement from the price put on it. Size a contrarian view so that being early can't force you out.
