Change-Point Detection: Did Something Just Change?

The two lessons before this one build a full model of every regime and every transition between them. This one asks a much narrower, more operational question: has something changed recently, and roughly when — answered live, as new data arrives, without ever modeling the full regime structure. Two classical tools answer it: CUSUM, a running tally that trips an alarm once evidence crosses a line, and Bayesian Online Changepoint Detection (BOCPD), which keeps a full probability distribution over how long it's been since the last change instead of a single yes/no.

Section 1

A narrower question than "model every regime"

The Hidden Markov Model and regime-switching lessons both build a generative model of the whole regime picture. This lesson deliberately doesn't.

The full regime picture

Infer a complete transition matrix between N discrete regimes, and produce a filtered or smoothed regime probability at every point in history. Rich, but heavy — exactly what the two prior lessons build.

Change-point detection

Just answer: has something changed recently, and roughly when? No transition matrix, no full regime characterization — a lighter, real-time tripwire a desk can run continuously and explain in one sentence.

A trading desk watching a strategy's rolling P&L, a risk system watching a spread's realized volatility, an execution algorithm watching fill quality — none of these usually need the full regime machinery. They need a tripwire, not a weather model. Because the question is narrower, the tools can be far lighter than a full HMM or a Kim's-filter fusion.

An ordinary in-control run can never bank a "credit" toward a false alarm. Only a genuine, sustained run of surprising values can climb high enough to cross the threshold.

μ₀ is the in-control (pre-shift) mean the process is expected to sit at; k is the allowance — how much deviation from μ₀ is forgiven each step before it starts accumulating; h is the decision threshold. Two knobs, and — as the next few slides show — they trade off directly against each other.

Section 3

Watching S_t cross the line

The same synthetic series as the full lesson's worked example: mean shifts from 0 to ~3.1 at t = 8. μ₀ = 0, k = 1.0, h = 3.5.

Read it
Through t = 7, every deviation is negative once k = 1.0 is subtracted, so S_t floors back to zero every step — CUSUM correctly ignoring ordinary pre-shift noise. At t = 8 the mean genuinely moves; S_8 = 2.2 starts accumulating but isn't yet enough. S_9 = 4.0 is the first value to exceed h = 3.5 — the change is flagged one step after it actually happened. That lag isn't a bug; it's the honest cost of not tripping on noise.

Section 5

Twitchy vs. conservative — the same two knobs

k and h always move together on the same trade-off curve. There's no setting that avoids it.

Small k, small h

Less allowance is forgiven per step and less evidence is needed to trip the alarm — genuine shifts, even small ones, are caught faster. The cost: ordinary noise accumulates more easily too, so false alarms happen more often. "Twitchy."

Large k, large h

More deviation is tolerated per step and more accumulated evidence is required — false alarms on ordinary noise are rare. The cost: a genuine shift takes longer to build enough evidence to cross the threshold. "Conservative."

No free lunch

Every choice of k and h sits somewhere on the same false-alarm-rate-versus-detection-delay curve. Picking a point on it is a real, unavoidable decision — not an implementation flaw waiting to be tuned away.

Section 7

BOCPD: a distribution, not a verdict

Adams & MacKay (2007) — instead of one running number, keep a full posterior over the run length r_t.

⏱

Run length r_t

How many steps it has been since the most recent changepoint. Not observed directly — BOCPD maintains a probability for every possible value of r_t at once.

🎲

Hazard rate H

The prior probability, at any single step, that a change happens right now: P(r_t=0)=H, P(r_t=r_{t-1}+1)=1−H. Held constant, it implies an exponential (geometric) — memoryless — prior on the gap between changepoints.

📈

Predictive likelihood

How well the new observation fits what's been seen since each hypothesized run began. Combined with the hazard, it reweights every run-length hypothesis after each new point.

🌫

Output: a full posterior

P(r_t | x_{1:t}) — the complete distribution over "when did the last change most likely happen," including its own uncertainty about that estimate.

A constant H is an assumption, not a law

Memorylessness means BOCPD assumes a change is exactly as likely tomorrow whether the current calm stretch has lasted 3 steps or 300 — no sense of "it's been quiet a while, so a change feels overdue." If real changes actually cluster, a constant-hazard BOCPD is quietly mis-specified.

Section 9

Neither tool tells you why — or what's next

A CUSUM crossing h says the accumulated evidence exceeded a chosen bar — not that there's a 99% chance a change occurred. BOCPD is more honest by construction, since it reports a posterior rather than a flag, but that posterior is still conditioned on an assumed hazard rate and predictive model that can both be wrong.
⚠

Detects, doesn't explain

Neither tool says why a shift happened or what the new regime looks like — that gap is exactly what the Hidden Markov Model and regime-switching lessons' fuller machinery exists to fill, once a monitor flags it's worth looking.

🎯

A monitor, not a diagnosis

A CUSUM on a strategy's daily P&L (or P&L over a rolling volatility estimate) is cheap, continuous, and immune to a single bad day by construction — but it can't say whether the market's regime genuinely changed or a run of correlated days was unlucky.

📐

Both stop short on purpose

Neither CUSUM nor BOCPD tries to build "the full model of every regime with a transition matrix between all of them" — that richer, heavier question belongs to the two lessons before this one.

Section 11

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

What does CUSUM's max(0, ·) floor actually do?

The floor prevents an ordinary in-control run from banking a "credit" toward a false alarm — only a sustained run of surprising values can climb high enough to cross the threshold.
Question 2

In the worked example (mean shifts at t=8, k=1.0, h=3.5), when is the change flagged?

S_8 = 2.2 starts accumulating but doesn't yet exceed h. S_9 = 4.0 is the first value past 3.5 — the honest one-step detection delay this (k,h) pair buys.
Question 3

How does BOCPD's output differ fundamentally from CUSUM's?

CUSUM is a binary crossed/not-crossed test on a single accumulated number. BOCPD maintains P(r_t | data) — a genuine probability distribution over every possible run length, updated after each observation.
Question 4

What does a constant hazard rate H imply about the time between changepoints?

A constant H means the chance of a change at any step doesn't depend on how long the current run has lasted — the same memorylessness that gives a constant-rate Poisson process exponential waiting times.
Question 5

Is BOCPD used anywhere in this repo's live trading code today?

A Gaussian BOCPD detector runs per pair every poll; its change_prob closes a position once it stays above bocpd_thresh (70%) for 2 consecutive polls (exit code X6). CUSUM, by contrast, has no implementation anywhere in the repo's trading code.
Section 12

What to carry forward

01

Change-point detection asks a narrower, operational question than the two prior lessons: has something changed, and roughly when — not "characterize every regime and every transition probability."

02

CUSUM is one accumulator and one threshold: S_t = max(0, S_{t−1} + (x_t − μ₀ − k)), flagged the moment S_t exceeds h.

03

k and h trade off directly — smaller values catch shifts faster but raise false alarms; larger values are calmer but slower. There is no parameter-free way out of that curve.

04

BOCPD replaces the single number with a full posterior over the run length r_t, updated via a hazard rate H and a predictive likelihood — richer, but only as honest as those assumptions.

05

A constant hazard rate implies a memoryless, exponential prior on the gap between changes — a real modeling assumption, not a law of how change actually arrives.

06

In this repo specifically: BOCPD (RegimeV2/bocpd.py) is live in regime_bot_v2.py's X6 exit gate; CUSUM remains conceptual only — verified while building this lesson.

What this now lets you do

Stand up a cheap, continuous tripwire on any single series — a strategy's P&L, a spread's realized volatility, a fill-quality metric — without building a full regime model first. Choose deliberately where you sit on the detection-speed-versus-false-alarm curve instead of treating it as a bug to eliminate. And read a BOCPD run-length posterior, or a CUSUM alarm, as a model output conditioned on real assumptions — not as a certainty about the world.
