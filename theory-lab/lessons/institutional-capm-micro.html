CAPM, One Beta to Price Them All

Markowitz proved there's a mathematically best portfolio once you know how everything covaries with everything else. CAPM asks the sharper question: if every investor actually holds that best portfolio, what does each individual asset's expected return have to be? The answer is one number — beta — and it's the founding idea this whole new category of the curriculum either builds on or was built to fix.

The whole idea
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

Every risk screen shows me a beta. Where does that number actually come from?

From the Capital Asset Pricing Model. William Sharpe in 1964, John Lintner in 1965, building on Markowitz.

What's the idea?

Suppose every investor has the same beliefs and optimizes the Markowitz way. Then everyone wants the same mix of risky assets. The cautious blend it with cash; the bold borrow to hold more of it.

And if everyone holds the same mix?

Every share still has to be owned by someone. So that mix has to be the whole market, every asset at its market weight. Then the only risk you get paid for is how much you move with the market. That sensitivity is beta.

How do I measure it?

It's just a regression slope: how much the asset moves with the market, divided by how much the market moves on its own. Same formula as the regression primer.

Numbers, please.

The lesson's toy market averages seven percent a year, with a risk-free rate of two. So the market premium is five points. Asset B has a beta of one and a half. CAPM says it should earn two, plus one and a half times five: nine and a half percent.

And what did it earn?

Thirteen and a half. Four points more than its beta justifies. That leftover is alpha.

So B's manager is a genius.

Maybe. Or B carries some other risk CAPM leaves out. Or five years is just noise. One regression cannot tell those apart.

Does CAPM hold up on real stocks?

Not well. Fama and French, in 1992, found beta explains far less of average returns than CAPM promises. Small companies and value stocks earned more than their betas said they should.

So why does every desk still use beta?

Because it's useful on its own terms. It sets the index hedge on an equity book, and Jensen's alpha from 1968 still splits a manager's return into market and skill.

One line?

Beta tells you how much market risk you carry; CAPM's claim that it explains all of your return is the baseline the better models were built to beat.

Section 1 · How it reached trading

One number for how much market risk you carry

CAPM · Sharpe 1964, Lintner 1965 → beta on every market-data screen

How it reached trading

Markowitz needed a covariance for every pair of assets and never said what return a stock should earn for the risk it adds. Sharpe (1964) and Lintner (1965) answered with one number per asset: beta.

Jensen (1968) used it to grade mutual funds, so alpha became the measure of skill; brokers published betas by the 1970s and index funds followed the market-portfolio logic. Beta still sets the index hedge on every equity book.

Section 2

A gap this curriculum has had until now

Markowitz, Black-Litterman, PCA, Random Matrix Theory — all covered. Never CAPM itself, until this lesson.

❓

Markowitz never says where returns come from

Given expected returns and a covariance matrix, Markowitz finds the best portfolio — but never says what those expected returns should be in the first place.

⚖️

CAPM answers the equilibrium version

If markets clear and every investor is a rational mean-variance optimizer with identical beliefs, what expected return must each asset offer? That's an asset-pricing question, not a portfolio-construction one.

🏛️

The founding factor model

One factor — the market — and one sensitivity number, beta. Every later lesson in this category either extends this idea or was built to fix a specific way it fails.

📆

1964, not new

William Sharpe (1964) and John Lintner (1965), building directly on Harry Markowitz's 1952 portfolio theory — CAPM is the historical starting point, chronologically as well as conceptually.

Section 3

Same beliefs, same portfolio, same market

Three moves take you from "everyone optimizes the Markowitz way" to "the market portfolio is everyone's portfolio."

The chain of reasoning

This is two-fund separation: every investor holds only a personal mix of the risk-free asset and that one common risky portfolio — cautious investors hold more risk-free, aggressive investors lever up the risky side. If literally everyone's risky holdings are a scaled version of the same portfolio, that portfolio has to be the market-value-weighted basket of every risky asset — otherwise some shares outstanding wouldn't be held by anyone, which can't be an equilibrium.

A short piece of algebra on top of that — the market portfolio being mean-variance-efficient — is what collapses directly into the CAPM pricing equation on the next slide.

Section 4

The formula itself

One equation. Every asset's required return, pinned down by one sensitivity number.

The CAPM equation
E[Rᵢ]
=
R_f
+
β × (E[Rₘ] − R_f)
Read it left to right: an asset's required return is the risk-free rate, plus its own beta times the market's risk premium. β = 0 → expect exactly the risk-free rate. β = 1 → expect exactly the market's own return. β = 2 → twice the market's risk premium on top of the risk-free rate.
R_f

Risk-free rate

What you'd earn taking on zero risk at all.

Rₘ

Market portfolio return

Every risky asset in existence, held at its market-value weight.

β

Beta

Asset i's sensitivity to market-wide moves — Cov(Rᵢ,Rₘ) ÷ Var(Rₘ).

Section 5

Beta isn't a new formula

It's the exact same OLS slope this curriculum already derived, applied to one specific choice of x.

The Correlation & Linear Regression primer derives β̂ = Cov(X,Y) / Var(X) as the slope that minimizes squared residuals for ANY two variables. CAPM's beta is that identical formula with Y = an asset's excess return and X = the market's excess return. Nothing about the arithmetic changes — CAPM only adds the economic claim that this particular slope determines how much expected return the asset is entitled to.
Regress it directly

Rᵢ − R_f = α + β(Rₘ − R_f) + ε — asset i's excess return regressed on the market's excess return. The fitted slope β̂ from that regression is CAPM's beta, full stop. Same covariance-over-variance ratio, same least-squares derivation, same formula — just one specific, economically meaningful choice of "x."

Section 6

Two lines, constantly confused

Both plot expected return on the vertical axis. The horizontal axis — and who's even allowed on the line — is completely different.

Compare
Axis: total risk, σ. Only efficient portfolios belong here — specifically, blends of the risk-free asset and the market portfolio, nothing else. E[Rₚ] = R_f + (Sharpe ratio of the market) × σₚ. An individual, badly-diversified stock does NOT belong on this line — it plots to the right of it, carrying risk it isn't paid for.
Axis: systematic risk only, β. Every correctly-priced asset belongs here — efficient portfolios AND single, poorly-diversified stocks alike. E[Rᵢ] = R_f + β(E[Rₘ] − R_f) — this is the CAPM equation itself, drawn as a line. Idiosyncratic risk plays no role in where an asset sits on this axis at all.

The clean way to keep them apart: the CML prices total risk and only rewards portfolios holding none of it uncompensated. The SML prices one slice of risk — the market-correlated slice — and applies to literally everything, diversified or not.

Section 9

Four assumptions, all known to be false

Every one is a deliberate simplification, not a claim about how markets actually behave.

⏱️

Single period

Choose a portfolio once, hold it — no rebalancing, no multi-period planning.

🧠

Identical expectations

Every investor agrees exactly on every asset's expected return and covariance — the assumption forcing everyone onto the same tangency portfolio.

💸

No taxes or costs

Trading is free and instant, prices identical for everyone.

🏦

Unlimited risk-free borrowing

Anyone can borrow or lend unlimited amounts at the single rate R_f.

Known to be false — that's the point

Investors disagree constantly, trading has real costs, almost nobody borrows at the risk-free rate. CAPM isn't offered as a photograph of real markets — it's the simplest model that still produces one specific, testable prediction for every asset's expected return. Being wrong in measurable ways is what makes the wrongness usable.

Section 10

Three assets, arithmetic by hand

Five years of toy returns, a 2% risk-free rate, one market index.

Asset A — β = 0.50
  • Predicted: 2 + 0.50 × 5 = 4.5%
  • Realized average: 4.5%
  • Alpha = 0.0% — sits exactly on the SML
Asset B — β = 1.50
  • Predicted: 2 + 1.50 × 5 = 9.5%
  • Realized average: 13.5%
  • Alpha = +4.0% — earned more than beta justified
Asset C — β = 0.80
  • Predicted: 2 + 0.80 × 5 = 6.0%
  • Realized average: 3.6%
  • Alpha = −2.4% — earned less than beta justified

Beta is computed the same way for all three: Cov(asset, market) ÷ Var(market), with the market's own variance at 34 (from a mean return of 7% across the five years). Only Asset A's returns were built as a pure linear function of the market — which is exactly why only its alpha comes out at zero.

Section 12

The empirical failure this category exists to fix

CAPM's own theory says alpha should be zero for every asset — beta is supposed to be a complete summary of the risk that matters. Fama & French (1992) sorted real stocks by beta and found a much weaker relationship with average returns than CAPM predicts — while firm size and book-to-market ratio ("value") both showed a much stronger one. Small-cap and value stocks earned systematically more than their beta alone justified, in sample after sample, for decades.
Where this needs qualifying

This is one of the most replicated results in empirical asset pricing, not a footnote. It's exactly why the toy Assets B and C carrying nonzero alpha earlier in this deck is a small, honest illustration of a real, large-scale, well-documented phenomenon — not just a constructed example.

This sets up the entire rest of this category directly: Fama-French & Carhart, next in this series, adds factors built specifically around the size and value anomalies this section describes — the direct empirical response to the exact gap CAPM's single factor leaves open.

Section 13

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

What does CAPM's beta actually equal, in formula terms?

Beta is exactly the Correlation & Linear Regression primer's OLS slope formula, β̂ = Cov(X,Y)/Var(X), with the market's excess return standing in as X.
Question 2

What common assumption turns Markowitz's individual-optimization framework into CAPM's market-wide pricing equation?

Identical beliefs force every investor onto the exact same tangency portfolio; market clearing then forces that one common portfolio to be the market-value-weighted market portfolio itself.
Question 3

An individual, poorly-diversified stock is plotted against total risk (σ). Should it sit on the Capital Market Line?

The CML only describes efficient portfolios. The line that prices an individual stock, diversified or not, is the Security Market Line, plotted against β alone.
Question 4

Asset B has β = 1.5, a CAPM-predicted return of 9.5%, and a realized average return of 13.5%. What is its alpha?

Alpha = realized − predicted = 13.5% − 9.5% = +4.0%, a positive residual sitting above the SML.
Question 5

What did Fama & French (1992) find that CAPM's beta alone fails to explain?

Scenario drill · an illustrative allocation meeting

A manager with four points of alpha

A manager's fund looks like the lesson's Asset B: over five years its beta to the market was 1.50 and it averaged 13.5%, against a market average of 7% and a risk-free rate of 2%. CAPM predicts 9.5%, so the pitch is "four points of alpha a year". The fund holds mostly small, cheap-looking companies. Do you pay for the alpha?

  1. Yes. Four points above the Security Market Line is skill.
    A single-factor alpha cannot separate skill from pay for a risk CAPM leaves out, or from luck in five annual observations. A tilt to small and value stocks is exactly the kind of exposure that showed up as "CAPM alpha" in the data.
  2. Re-measure the alpha after adding size and value factors, and ask for a much longer record before calling it skill.
    This is the lesson's own warning, and the next lesson's tool. If most of the four points disappears once size and value are accounted for, you would be paying active fees for a factor tilt you could buy cheaply.
  3. Hedge out the beta with index futures and keep the rest.
    Sensible portfolio engineering: beta is the right hedge ratio. But hedging the market does not answer whether the remaining return is skill, a size and value tilt, or noise.
  4. Reject it. CAPM's assumptions are false, so the alpha means nothing.
    False assumptions make CAPM a baseline, not a useless one. The alpha is a real measurement against that baseline; the question is what explains it, not whether to ignore it.

What this teaches. This is an illustrative case built on the lesson's toy Asset B, not a real fund. The real-world counterpart is well documented: Fama and French (1992) found that small and value stocks earned more than their betas predicted, so a small-value tilt shows up as positive CAPM alpha. That is why alpha is now judged against multi-factor models, not beta alone.

Section 14

What to carry forward

01

CAPM: E[Rᵢ] = R_f + β(E[Rₘ] − R_f), where β = Cov(Rᵢ,Rₘ)/Var(Rₘ) — exactly the OLS slope from the Correlation & Linear Regression primer, applied to the market as "x."

02

It's Markowitz's equilibrium consequence: identical investor beliefs force everyone onto the same tangency portfolio, and market clearing forces that portfolio to be the market portfolio.

03

The CML (return vs. total risk, efficient portfolios only) and the SML (return vs. beta, every priced asset) are different lines — confusing them is the most common CAPM mistake.

04

Every CAPM assumption — single period, identical beliefs, no frictions, unlimited risk-free borrowing — is known to be false. That's what makes it a clean baseline, not a finished answer.

05

Beta alone leaves a real, well-documented gap in the cross-section of stock returns (Fama-French, 1992) — small-cap and value stocks earned more than their beta predicted, for decades.

06

Beta is the first number a desk checks: the index hedge on an equity book, and the split of a manager's return into market exposure and Jensen's alpha.

What this now lets you do

Read a beta the same way whether it comes from a textbook or a Bloomberg terminal, or this repo's own factor-hedging code — it's always the identical OLS slope. Tell the CML and SML apart on sight. Recognize a nonzero alpha for what it honestly is: an unresolved question, not proof of skill. Understand exactly what specific, well-documented gap in CAPM the next lesson exists to close.
