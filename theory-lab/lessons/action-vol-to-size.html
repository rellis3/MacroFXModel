From a Volatility Forecast to a Position Size

A GARCH model says tomorrow's EUR/USD volatility is 0.40%. So what? This lesson turns that number into pips, a stop distance and a lot size, and shows the two standard ways to do it: per-trade risk and volatility targeting. It also covers where both go wrong.

CONCEPT — explained here; desk verdicts quoted where tested ~22 min read Prereqs: From a View to a Trade Plan; GARCH helpful
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide. It covers the same ground in short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • Size should shrink when volatility rises. The same lots in a market twice as volatile is twice the risk. Dividing by a volatility forecast keeps the risk constant.
  • Convert the forecast into price units first: σ in pips = σ% × price ÷ pip size. 0.40% on EUR/USD at 1.0850 is 43.4 pips. A random walk's expected daily high-to-low is about 1.6σ, roughly an ATR.
  • Route A, per-trade risk: stop = k·σ, lots = money at risk ÷ (stop pips × pip value). At k = 2 and $500 risk: 87 pips → 0.57 lots. Volatility doubles: 174 pips → 0.28 lots, same $500.
  • Route B, volatility targeting: notional = target vol × equity ÷ forecast annual vol. 5% target, 6.35% forecast, $100,000 → $78,743 → 0.72 lots.
  • Pick one route. A k·σ stop and a risk budget cut by volatility shrinks size with σ², not σ. And always cap leverage: a calm forecast produces the biggest position just before the next shock (February 2018).
  • The desk's verdicts: the pace of the tape persists (68 of 68 cells), so a volatility forecast is worth having. When implied sits far above realised, realised has caught up rather than calmed, so a forecast built only from trailing realised can understate. Whether volatility targeting itself adds return is not tested here.
Section 01

Why this matters

Earlier lessons spend a lot of effort forecasting volatility: GARCH, HAR-RV, range-based estimators like Garman-Klass and Yang-Zhang. Each ends with a number like "σ̂ = 0.62% tomorrow" or "realised variance 0.16". A forecast that never changes what you do is wasted work. The place it pays is position size.

In lesson 1 the stop came from the analysis and the size came from the stop. This lesson fills in the piece in between. It shows how a volatility forecast sets the noise buffer and the stop distance, and how it can set the size directly when there's no natural stop. It also links to Kelly, which says how much risk is worth taking, and to risk of ruin, which says what happens if you take too much.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Carry less water on a bumpy road

You're carrying a full tray of glasses across a room. On a smooth floor you can carry a lot. If the floor starts shaking, you carry fewer glasses, so that the number you're likely to drop stays the same. Volatility is how much the floor shakes. A volatility forecast tells you how bumpy the next stretch will be. Position size is how many glasses you pick up.

There are two ways to choose. You can decide how many glasses you could stand to drop on the worst bump you'll accept (per-trade risk), or you can aim for a steady amount of wobble overall and adjust the load as the shaking changes (volatility targeting).

How it reached trading

From the Turtles' "N" to volatility-targeted funds

Volatility-scaled sizing · Turtle rules (1983); RiskMetrics (J.P. Morgan, 1994); GARCH (Bollerslev, 1986) and HAR (Corsi, 2009) as forecasts; volatility targeting in managed futures and risk parity

Futures traders were scaling size by volatility long before the models existed. The Turtle rules of 1983 defined N, a moving average of the daily true range. Each position "unit" was sized so that a one-N move cost 1% of the account, and the stop sat at 2N. A quiet market meant more contracts and a wild one meant fewer, with the same dollars at risk either way.

Banks needed the same number for risk limits. J.P. Morgan's RiskMetrics (1994) published a daily exponentially weighted volatility (decay 0.94) for hundreds of instruments, and GARCH and later HAR models made the forecast sharper. Managed-futures funds and risk-parity portfolios built whole products on volatility targeting, running each position or the whole book at a fixed expected volatility. Moreira and Muir (2017) gave the academic case that scaling exposure down when volatility is high improved risk-adjusted returns for several equity factors. That result has since been debated, as the verdicts below note.

Section 02

The maths, step by step

1. Get the forecast into the right units

Volatility models usually forecast in percentage returns. GARCH gives a variance \(\hat\sigma^2_{t+1}\) (often in %², so take the square root). HAR-RV gives a forecast of realised variance, so take the square root of that too. Then convert to price, and to pips:

\[ \hat\sigma_{\text{price}} = \frac{\hat\sigma_{\%}}{100}\times P, \qquad \hat\sigma_{\text{pips}} = \frac{\hat\sigma_{\text{price}}}{\text{pip size}} \]
σ̂%forecast daily volatility, in percent
Pcurrent price
σ̂ priceone forecast standard deviation of the daily move, in price
σ̂ pipsthe same in pips, ready for a stop

Example: a HAR model forecasts tomorrow's realised variance on EUR/USD at 0.16 (%²). So \(\hat\sigma = \sqrt{0.16} = 0.40\%\), and at 1.0850 that's \(0.0040 \times 1.0850 = 0.004340\), or 43.4 pips.

2. Sanity-check it against ATR

Traders often think in ATR. For a random walk with no drift, the expected high-to-low range over a day is \(\sqrt{8/\pi}\,\sigma \approx 1.60\,\sigma\) (the relationship behind Parkinson's 1980 range estimator). So a 43.4-pip σ implies a typical day of about \(1.596 \times 43.4 = 69\) pips. If the pair's ATR is 65, the forecast is saying "slightly busier than recently". Real ATR includes gaps and fat tails, so treat 1.6 as a rough bridge, not an identity. It is still good enough to catch a units mistake.

\[ \mathbb{E}[\text{high} - \text{low}] \approx 1.6\,\sigma_{\text{daily}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{ATR} \approx 1.6\,\sigma, \qquad 2\sigma \approx 1.25\ \text{ATR} \]
1.6σexpected daily range of a driftless random walk, √(8/π) ≈ 1.596
ATRaverage true range: the measured version, gaps included

3. Match the forecast to the holding period

A one-day forecast fits a one-day trade. For a trade held \(h\) days, add up the daily variances, not the volatilities. In GARCH(1,1) the forecast decays toward the long-run level \(\bar\sigma^2\) at the rate of the persistence \(\phi = \alpha + \beta\):

\[ \hat\sigma^2_{t+i} = \bar\sigma^2 + \phi^{\,i-1}\left(\hat\sigma^2_{t+1} - \bar\sigma^2\right), \qquad \hat\sigma^2_{(h)} = \sum_{i=1}^{h} \hat\sigma^2_{t+i} \]
σ̄²long-run daily variance the model pulls back to
φ = α + βpersistence: how slowly a shock fades (0.97 is typical for daily FX)
σ̂²(h)variance over the whole holding period

With tomorrow's forecast at 0.80%, long-run 0.50% and \(\phi = 0.97\), the 10-day volatility is \(\sqrt{10 \times 0.25 + 0.39 \times 8.753} = \sqrt{5.913} = 2.43\%\). The lazy \(\sqrt{10} \times 0.80 = 2.53\%\) overstates it, because high volatility is expected to fade. Using the long-run \(\sqrt{10}\times 0.50 = 1.58\%\) badly understates it. Here \(8.753 = (1 - 0.97^{10})/(1-0.97)\).

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

After a big storm the sea stays rough for a few days, then calms down. If you're sailing for ten days, you plan for "rough at first, calmer later". Planning for ten days as rough as today is too careful, and planning for ten calm days is foolish.

4. Route A: per-trade risk, stop = k·σ

When the trade has a stop, use the forecast to set the stop's distance (or its noise buffer beyond the invalidation level, as in lesson 1). Then size from the stop:

\[ D_{\text{pips}} = k\,\hat\sigma_{\text{pips},(h)}, \qquad \text{lots} = \frac{r\,E}{D_{\text{pips}}\times v_{\text{pip}}} = \frac{r\,E}{k\,\hat\sigma_{\text{pips},(h)}\, v_{\text{pip}}} \]

5. Route B: volatility targeting

When there's no natural stop (a carry position, a macro sleeve held for weeks, a whole book), pick the volatility you want the position to contribute and scale the notional to hit it:

\[ \hat\sigma_{\text{ann}} = \hat\sigma_{\text{daily}}\sqrt{252}, \qquad N = \frac{\sigma^{*}\,E}{\hat\sigma_{\text{ann}}}, \qquad \text{leverage} = \frac{N}{E} = \frac{\sigma^{*}}{\hat\sigma_{\text{ann}}} \le L_{\max} \]
σ*target annual volatility for this position, e.g. 5%
√252trading days in a year: volatility scales with the square root of time
Nnotional in account currency; divide by the notional of one lot
L_maxa hard leverage cap: the forecast can be too low

Route A asks "how much do I lose if the stop is hit?" Route B asks "how much does this position typically swing per year?" Both give size proportional to \(1/\hat\sigma\). Use one, not both. If you set the stop at \(k\hat\sigma\) and also cut the risk budget \(r\) when volatility rises, size falls with \(\hat\sigma^2\). Volatility doubles and the position quarters. That is usually an accident, not a decision.

6. Where Kelly fits

The continuous-time Kelly leverage for an asset with expected excess return \(\mu\) and volatility \(\sigma\) is \(f^{*} = \mu/\sigma^2\). Write \(\mu = S\sigma\), where \(S\) is the Sharpe ratio, and the position's volatility at full Kelly is \(f^{*}\sigma = S\). In other words, full Kelly is a volatility target equal to your Sharpe ratio. A strategy with an honest annual Sharpe of 0.5 would run at 50% volatility at full Kelly, or 25% at half Kelly. A 5–10% target is a small fraction of Kelly, and that is deliberate: your Sharpe is an estimate, and over-estimating it pushes you past Kelly, where ruin rises and growth falls. If the Sharpe really is constant, Kelly sizing also scales with \(1/\sigma\), which is one theoretical reason volatility scaling is natural.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

If the swings get twice as big, you hold half as much. Then the swings in your pocket stay the same size. That's all volatility sizing is.

Worked example

EUR/USD: from a HAR forecast to 0.57 lots, and what a vol shock does

1

The forecast

HAR-RV forecasts tomorrow's realised variance at 0.16 (%²). \(\hat\sigma = \sqrt{0.16} = 0.40\%\). Price 1.0850, equity $100,000, risk 0.5% = $500, pip value $10 per lot.

2

Into pips, and the ATR check

\(0.0040 \times 1.0850 = 0.004340\) = 43.4 pips. Implied typical range \(1.596 \times 43.4 = 69\) pips, close to the 65-pip ATR. The units are right.

3

Route A: stop and size

Stop at 2σ: \(2 \times 43.4 = 86.8\), rounded to 87 pips. Lots \(= 500/(87 \times 10) = 0.575\), rounded down to 0.57 lots, risking \(0.57 \times 87 \times 10 = \$495.90\). (Lesson 1's structure stop was 86 pips. A good invalidation level usually sits a couple of σ away.)

4

Volatility doubles

After a shock the forecast is 0.80%: σ = 86.8 pips, stop \(2 \times 86.8 = 173.6\), rounded to 174 pips. Lots \(= 500/1{,}740 = 0.287\), rounded down to 0.28 lots, risking $487.20. Same budget, half the size.

5

Route B: a 5% volatility target instead

\(\hat\sigma_{\text{ann}} = 0.40\% \times \sqrt{252} = 6.35\%\). Notional \(= 0.05 \times 100{,}000 / 0.0635 = \$78{,}743\). One lot is €100,000 = $108,500, so 0.72 lots (0.7257, rounded down), leverage 0.79×. At 0.80% volatility: 12.70% annual, $39,371, 0.36 lots.

6

Why A and B differ

They answer different questions. A caps the loss if the stop is hit at $500. B makes the position's typical yearly swing about $5,000 (5% of equity). Pick the one that matches the trade: a stop-and-target trade → A; a held exposure with no natural stop → B.

Real-world trading scenario

The calm-market trap, in numbers

A sleeve runs a 10% volatility target on $1,000,000 in a stock index. In a calm year the trailing forecast falls to 8% annual, so the sleeve holds \(0.10/0.08 = 1.25\times\), $1.25m. The forecast keeps falling, to 5%, and the sleeve holds 2×, $2m. Then volatility jumps to 30% in a week. The forecast catches up only after the move. The sleeve took the first leg of the shock at 2× leverage, and only then cut to \(0.10/0.30 = 0.33\times\), selling into the fall.

Three fixes, all used in practice: a leverage cap (say 1.5×); a floor on the forecast (never assume volatility below, say, half its long-run level); and a check against implied volatility, which looks forward where trailing realised looks back. The desk's own data on that last point are in Section 04.

Section 03

It actually happened: when calm was the risk

It actually happened

5 February 2018: "Volmageddon"

2017 was one of the calmest years for US stocks on record. The VIX spent much of the year at historically low levels, and selling volatility looked like steady income. Exchange-traded products that paid the inverse of short-term VIX futures had grown large, and many investors and funds had positions sized from a long run of quiet, trailing volatility.

In late January and early February 2018 stocks fell. On Monday 5 February the VIX more than doubled in a single session, its largest one-day percentage rise on record. The biggest inverse-VIX note, Credit Suisse's XIV, lost almost all its value, much of it in trading after the close as the product rebalanced. Credit Suisse then moved to terminate it. Many post-mortems also pointed to the selling done by strategies that size by recent volatility, which had to cut exposure after the jump. How much each group contributed is still debated.

For this lesson the mechanism is what matters. A position sized by trailing volatility is largest when the recent past is calmest, and a forecast built only from the recent past learns about the shock after it arrives. Volatility sizing is still right. What it needs is a cap on leverage, a floor under the forecast, and a check against implied volatility.

Section 04

What this desk found when it tested the claims

Desk test: held up Pace persists, which is why a volatility forecast is worth having. Volatility sizing only helps if tomorrow's volatility looks like today's more than chance would allow. At the 15-minute-to-next-hour scale the desk found exactly that: a slow approach is followed by a slow hour, a fast one by a fast hour, with next-hour dwell "monotonic in 68 of 68 cells across 17 pairs". The effect was the same at random prices, so it's a property of the tape, not of any level. It says nothing about direction.
Desk test: held up When implied sits far above realised, realised has caught up, not calmed down. With VIX ÷ 20-session realised SPX volatility in its top decile (88 first days, 2010→): NAS100 next-5 range +0.38 ATR [+0.11, +0.62], next-20 +0.56; SPX500 next-20 +0.67 [+0.13, +1.21] (next-5 +0.17, null). For sizing: a forecast built only from trailing realised volatility can understate what's coming when the options market disagrees. Check the implied-to-realised ratio before letting a calm trailing number set a large size.
Not tested here "Volatility targeting improves risk-adjusted returns." Not tested here. The desk sizes by volatility for risk control, but hasn't tested whether scaling by a forecast adds return on its own instruments. The published evidence is mixed: Moreira and Muir (2017) found gains for several equity factors, and later work, such as Cederburg and co-authors (2020), found many of those gains hard to capture out of sample. The risk-control case (steadier dollar swings, smaller worst days) doesn't depend on that answer.
The trap: the lower the forecast, the bigger the position, with no limit unless you add one. A sizing rule without a leverage cap and a forecast floor is quietly a bet that the calm will continue.
Section 05

How practitioners actually use this

📐

Equal risk per position

A book of FX, gold and index trades is sized so each one's expected daily swing is the same in dollars. Otherwise the gold trade quietly dominates (see Risk Parity).

🎚️

Target vol at book level

Managed-futures and risk-parity funds commonly run the whole portfolio at a stated target, often around 10%, re-sizing as forecasts change, with leverage limits.

🔁

Re-size on a schedule, with a band

Re-sizing daily on every forecast twitch costs spread. Many re-size weekly, or only when the target size moves by more than, say, 10–20%.

⚡

A separate line for jumps

σ-based stops describe ordinary movement. Around events, desks add a gap scenario (see bipower variation and jump tests for separating jumps from diffusion).

Before you size: eight checks

1

Which forecast, which horizon?

GARCH, HAR, ATR or implied. Matched to the holding period: sum variances over \(h\) days, don't multiply today's σ by \(\sqrt{h}\) if a shock is fading.

2

Units

Variance → square root. Percent → price (× P) → pips (÷ pip size). Daily → annual (× √252, never × 252).

3

ATR cross-check

1.6 × σ in pips should be near the ATR. If it's off by 10×, a unit is wrong.

4

One route only

Per-trade risk (stop = k·σ) or volatility target. Not both, or size scales with 1/σ².

5

Compute and round down

Lots to the broker's increment, rounded down so actual risk ≤ planned.

6

Floors and caps

Forecast floor (e.g. ≥ half the long-run level), leverage cap, and the implied-vs-realised check.

7

Gap line

Loss if price jumps past the stop before the next event or weekend. Size down if it's unacceptable.

8

Kelly ceiling

The implied position volatility should be a small fraction of your honest Sharpe (full Kelly = Sharpe).

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where vol-based sizing goes wrong

Using variance as volatility. A HAR forecast of 0.25 (%²) is σ = 0.50%, not 0.25%. Read it as σ and the stop is half as wide and the size twice as big.

Annualising with 252 instead of √252. 0.8% daily is 12.7% a year, not 202%.

Double scaling. A k·σ stop plus a risk budget that also falls with σ gives size ∝ 1/σ², a much steeper rule than intended.

No cap, no floor. A calm trailing forecast makes the largest position just before the shock (February 2018).

Horizon mismatch. A one-day σ for a three-week trade puts the stop inside the noise of the holding period.

Wrong pip value. USD/JPY's pip value moves with the price and differs by account currency. Recompute it, don't remember it.

Section 07

Self-test

Q1. GARCH forecasts USD/JPY daily volatility at 0.60% with the price at 150.00. Equity $100,000, risk 0.5%, stop at 2σ, USD account. How many lots?
σ in price \(= 0.006 \times 150 = 0.90\) yen = 90 pips (pip 0.01). Stop \(2 \times 90 = 180\) pips. Pip value per 100,000-dollar lot \(= 1{,}000/150 = \$6.67\). Lots \(= 500/(180 \times 6.667) = 500/1{,}200 = 0.417\), rounded down to 0.41.
Q2. A HAR model outputs 0.25 for tomorrow's realised variance (in %²). A colleague puts "0.25%" into the sizer as σ. What happens?
The true σ is \(\sqrt{0.25} = 0.50\%\). Using 0.25% halves the stop distance and doubles the size. The trade risks twice the plan, with a stop inside the noise.
Q3. Annualise a 0.80% daily volatility.
\(0.80\% \times \sqrt{252} = 0.80 \times 15.87 = 12.70\%\). Volatility scales with the square root of time because variance adds across independent days.
Q4. A 10% volatility target on $200,000 with an 8% annual forecast. What notional? What if the forecast falls to 2%?
\(0.10 \times 200{,}000 / 0.08 = \$250{,}000\) (1.25×). At 2%: \(\$1{,}000{,}000\) (5×). That's why the leverage cap and the forecast floor exist. A 2% forecast for an asset that usually runs at 8% is more likely an unusually calm stretch than a permanent change.
Q5. A trader sets the stop at 2σ and also halves risk per trade whenever volatility doubles. Volatility doubles. By how much does the position shrink?
The stop doubles (size halves) and the risk budget halves (size halves again): a quarter of the original size. Size ∝ 1/σ². Choose one adjustment deliberately.
Q6. Your strategy's honest annual Sharpe is 0.4. What volatility does full Kelly imply, and what fraction of Kelly is a 10% target?
Full-Kelly position volatility = Sharpe = 40%. A 10% target is a quarter Kelly. That's a sensible margin, given that a Sharpe estimated from a backtest is usually too high.
