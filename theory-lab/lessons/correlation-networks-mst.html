Correlation Networks & Minimum Spanning Trees

A correlation matrix for even a modest basket is a wall of numbers nobody can eyeball. Turn it into a distance graph and prune it down to its minimum spanning tree, and the basket's clustering structure — who's really close to whom — becomes a picture instead of a table.

CONCEPT — background theory, not built or tested anywhere in this repo ~16 min read Prereqs: Graph Theory Fundamentals & Centrality Measures
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • A correlation coefficient isn't a distance — high correlation should mean "close," but correlation runs the wrong way for that (1 = identical, -1 = opposite), so it needs a transform before it can be used as an edge weight in a network.
  • Mantegna's (1999) transform \(d_{ij}=\sqrt{2(1-\rho_{ij})}\) fixes this: perfectly correlated pairs get distance 0, uncorrelated pairs get distance \(\sqrt{2}\), perfectly anti-correlated pairs get the maximum distance 2.
  • The Minimum Spanning Tree (MST) is the subgraph that connects every node using the least total edge weight, with no cycles — built conceptually via Kruskal's algorithm (sort edges by distance, add the shortest one that doesn't close a loop, repeat) or Prim's (grow one connected tree outward, always adding the nearest unconnected node).
  • A worked 5-node example computes all 10 pairwise distances from a plausible correlation matrix, runs Kruskal's algorithm by hand, and produces a 4-edge backbone tree that reveals a hub-and-outlier structure a raw correlation table doesn't show at a glance.
  • Mantegna's original 1999 application was a basket of Dow Jones equities; applying the same transform to FX/cross-asset baskets is a natural extension, not one this repo has tested or validated — that's stated plainly throughout this lesson.
  • PCA and the MST are different lenses on the exact same correlation matrix: PCA collapses it into a handful of ranked, orthogonal factors; the MST keeps every pairwise relationship intact as a sparse graph — the two aren't competitors, they show different things.
Section 01

Why this matters

The PCA lesson in this curriculum summarizes a whole correlation matrix at once — one number, \(\text{EVR}_1\), for "how much of the basket is really one trade." The cointegration lesson tests exactly one pair at a time for whether their spread has an actual equilibrium. Between those two — "the whole basket" and "one specific pair" — sits a genuinely useful third view: the whole basket's pairwise structure, all at once, but kept as individual relationships rather than compressed into factors. A correlation heatmap technically contains this information already, but a table of \(N(N-1)/2\) numbers is not something a human reads and immediately understands. Turning that same matrix into a graph — and then pruning the graph down to its essential backbone — is the standard fix, and it's exactly what this lesson builds.

This is the second of three lessons in this new category. The previous lesson, Graph Theory Fundamentals & Centrality Measures, built the vocabulary (graphs, adjacency matrices, "which node matters") this lesson needs. This lesson applies that vocabulary to a correlation matrix specifically, and needs one new idea on top: how to turn a correlation (a similarity) into a distance, and how to extract a graph's "skeleton" — the Minimum Spanning Tree.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Building the cheapest road network that still reaches every town

Imagine a country planner given a map of towns and the cost of building a road directly between any two of them, and told: connect every town to the network, somehow, for the least total construction cost. You don't need every possible road — that would be enormously wasteful, most towns only need to reach the network once, not by every possible route. What you actually want is the cheapest possible set of roads that still lets you get from any town to any other (possibly via a few hops), with absolutely no redundant loops, since any loop is, by definition, at least one road too many. That specific object — the lowest-total-weight way to connect every node with no cycles — is a Minimum Spanning Tree.

Now replace "towns" with financial instruments and "road cost" with "how far apart two instruments' price behavior is" (built from their correlation), and the same construction answers a market-structure question: which pairs are the tightest, most direct links in the whole basket, and how do smaller clusters connect to the rest of the basket through the fewest, cheapest bridges? The MST doesn't throw away information carelessly — it keeps exactly the \(N-1\) edges (for \(N\) instruments) that are individually most essential, in the sense that no cheaper way exists to keep the whole basket connected.

Section 02

The math, step by step

Step 1 — turning a correlation into a distance

A correlation coefficient \(\rho_{ij} \in [-1, 1]\) measures similarity, not distance — and it runs backwards from what a graph edge weight needs. In a graph, a small edge weight should mean "close" or "strongly linked"; but the most similar pair (perfectly correlated, \(\rho=1\)) has the largest possible correlation value, and the least similar, most oppositely-behaving pair (\(\rho=-1\)) has the smallest. Using raw correlation directly as a graph distance would have highly correlated pairs registering as "far apart" — backwards.

Rosario Mantegna's widely used fix (1999), developed for a basket of equities, is a specific transform turning correlation into a proper metric distance:

\[ d_{ij} = \sqrt{2(1-\rho_{ij})} \]
ρ_ij
Correlation
How similarly instruments i and j move, from -1 to 1
1 − ρ_ij
Dissimilarity
Flips the direction — 0 when perfectly correlated, 2 when perfectly anti-correlated
d_ij
The distance
A proper metric: 0 (identical) to 2 (perfectly opposite), √2 when uncorrelated

Read this in three checkpoints. When \(\rho_{ij}=1\) (perfectly correlated), \(d_{ij}=\sqrt{2(1-1)}=0\) — identical behavior means zero distance, exactly as a distance metric should behave. When \(\rho_{ij}=0\) (uncorrelated), \(d_{ij}=\sqrt{2}\approx1.414\) — the natural "neutral" distance. When \(\rho_{ij}=-1\) (perfectly anti-correlated), \(d_{ij}=\sqrt{2(1-(-1))}=\sqrt{4}=2\) — the maximum possible distance, since moving in perfect opposition is, in this metric, as "far apart" as two series can be. The \(\sqrt{2(1-\rho)}\) form specifically is a proper metric (it satisfies the triangle inequality, unlike some simpler transforms such as \(1-\rho\) alone) — a technical requirement for the shortest-path and spanning-tree machinery below to behave the way graph theory expects.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Correlation says "how alike," but a distance needs to say "how far apart" — and those point in opposite directions. Two instruments moving in perfect lockstep should be zero distance apart, not the maximum. Mantegna's formula just flips the direction: identical behavior becomes distance zero, and the more oppositely two things move, the further apart the formula puts them.

Step 2 — the distance matrix becomes a complete graph

Compute \(d_{ij}\) for every pair of \(N\) instruments and the result is a complete graph: every node connects to every other node (there's always some distance between any two correlations), with \(N(N-1)/2\) total edges. This is the same adjacency-matrix idea from the previous lesson, just built from distances instead of raw connection strengths — a fully-connected graph on which "closeness" is well-defined but which is still too dense, at this point, to read as a picture. A 20-instrument basket has 190 edges; nobody looks at that and sees a clean clustering structure. The next step's job is pruning it down to what actually matters.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Once you've computed how far apart every single pair is, you technically have a map connecting everyone to everyone — but a map with every possible road drawn on it is just a mess of crossed lines, not something a person can actually read. The next step is figuring out which handful of roads you truly need.

Step 3 — the Minimum Spanning Tree

A spanning tree of a connected graph with \(N\) nodes is any subgraph that (a) touches every node, (b) contains no cycles, and (c) is therefore built from exactly \(N-1\) edges — the minimum number that can still connect \(N\) nodes at all. Many different spanning trees exist for a given graph (many different choices of which \(N-1\) edges to keep); the Minimum Spanning Tree is specifically the one whose edges sum to the lowest total weight.

Two classical algorithms build it, both provably optimal, differing only in how they search:

Kruskal's algorithm

Sort every edge in the graph by weight, ascending. Walk down the sorted list and add each edge to the growing tree unless it would close a cycle (i.e., unless both its endpoints are already connected to each other through edges already added) — in which case skip it and move to the next. Stop once \(N-1\) edges have been added. Simple to run by hand on a small graph, and the algorithm this lesson's worked example uses.

Prim's algorithm

Start from any single node as a one-node "tree." Repeatedly find the shortest edge connecting a node already in the tree to any node not yet in it, and add that node and edge. Stop once every node has joined. Grows outward from a single seed rather than sorting the whole edge list up front — often preferred computationally for dense graphs.

Both are greedy algorithms — at each step they make the locally cheapest choice available, never revisiting or reconsidering a prior choice — and both are mathematically guaranteed to find the true global minimum-weight spanning tree, not merely a good one. That guarantee (formalized by the "cut property" of matroid theory) is what makes MST construction one of the cleanest results in combinatorial optimization: unlike most greedy heuristics, greedy is provably optimal here.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

A minimum spanning tree is the cheapest possible set of roads that still lets you drive from any town to any other, with zero wasted loops. Kruskal's way of building it is simple: always grab the cheapest road left on the list, unless it would just create a loop between towns you can already reach each other from — in which case skip it and move to the next cheapest.

\(d_{ij}\)The Mantegna distance between \(i\) and \(j\): \(\sqrt{2(1-\rho_{ij})}\), ranging from 0 (identical) to 2 (perfectly opposite).

Worked example

Five FX pairs, ten distances, one 4-edge backbone

Take five instruments — EUR, GBP, CHF, JPY, AUD — with a plausible (not measured from live data) pairwise correlation matrix:

\[ \begin{array}{c|ccccc} & \text{EUR} & \text{GBP} & \text{CHF} & \text{JPY} & \text{AUD} \\\hline \text{EUR} & 1.00 & 0.80 & 0.70 & 0.30 & 0.50 \\ \text{GBP} & 0.80 & 1.00 & 0.60 & 0.25 & 0.45 \\ \text{CHF} & 0.70 & 0.60 & 1.00 & 0.20 & 0.35 \\ \text{JPY} & 0.30 & 0.25 & 0.20 & 1.00 & 0.40 \\ \text{AUD} & 0.50 & 0.45 & 0.35 & 0.40 & 1.00 \end{array} \]

Applying \(d_{ij}=\sqrt{2(1-\rho_{ij})}\) to each off-diagonal entry gives the ten pairwise distances, sorted ascending for Kruskal's algorithm:

\[ \begin{array}{lc} \text{EUR–GBP} & 0.63 \\ \text{EUR–CHF} & 0.77 \\ \text{GBP–CHF} & 0.89 \\ \text{EUR–AUD} & 1.00 \\ \text{GBP–AUD} & 1.05 \\ \text{JPY–AUD} & 1.10 \\ \text{CHF–AUD} & 1.14 \\ \text{EUR–JPY} & 1.18 \\ \text{GBP–JPY} & 1.22 \\ \text{CHF–JPY} & 1.26 \end{array} \]

Running Kruskal's algorithm: add EUR–GBP (0.63) — the shortest edge, no cycle risk yet. Add EUR–CHF (0.77) — next shortest, connects CHF into the growing {EUR, GBP} component. GBP–CHF (0.89) is next in sorted order but both endpoints are already in the same component (adding it would close the EUR-GBP-CHF triangle into a cycle) — skip it. Add EUR–AUD (1.00) — connects AUD into the main component. GBP–AUD (1.05) is skipped for the same reason as GBP–CHF (cycle). Add JPY–AUD (1.10) — connects the last remaining node, JPY, into the tree. That's 4 edges for 5 nodes — done.

The resulting MST: EUR–GBP, EUR–CHF, EUR–AUD, and JPY–AUD, total weight \(0.63+0.77+1.00+1.10=3.50\). Read as a picture rather than a list, this says something the correlation table alone didn't make obvious at a glance: EUR is the structural hub of this basket (three of the four MST edges touch it), and JPY — the instrument correlated most weakly with everything else — doesn't connect to the hub directly at all; it attaches through its single closest relationship, AUD, which happens to be its least-weak link into the rest of the basket. The backbone reveals a hub-and-outlier shape that a same-sized table of ten numbers doesn't visually deliver on its own.

🎯
A named scenario

Spotting redundant risk before adding a sixth pair

Suppose a discretionary desk already holds positions sized around EUR, GBP, CHF, and AUD, and is considering adding JPY as a fifth, "diversifying" instrument. The MST gives a fast, honest read: JPY's only path into this basket's backbone is a single, comparatively long edge through AUD (distance 1.10 — the second-longest edge kept in the whole tree) — meaning JPY is indeed the basket's most genuinely distinct instrument, its closest relationship to anything else in the book is still fairly weak. That's a legitimate diversification argument, and exactly the kind of structural read an MST is built to surface quickly. It is not, on its own, evidence that adding JPY improves risk-adjusted returns — that still requires testing the actual combined book's performance, with costs, the same as any other change to a strategy in this curriculum's house discipline. A backbone picture tells you about structure; it does not replace a backtest.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this — and where it comes from

Mantegna introduced this exact distance transform and MST construction in 1999, applied to a basket of stocks in the Dow Jones Industrial Average, to show that the resulting tree recovers economically sensible industry clusters (technology stocks clustering together, financials clustering together) purely from price-correlation structure, with no industry labels fed in as input — the tree "discovers" sector structure. That result, and the broader "econophysics" literature that followed it, is specifically an equities result. Applying the same machinery to an FX or cross-asset basket — as this lesson's worked example does — is a natural, structurally sound extension (currencies correlate, correlation can be turned into a distance, a distance graph can be pruned to an MST regardless of asset class), but it is a different application than the one Mantegna tested, and this repo has not validated whether an FX/cross-asset MST recovers anything as clean or as tradeable as the equity-sector result. Treat the FX framing here as a plausible hypothesis worth building carefully, not as a proven technique simply because the underlying math is sound.

Network stability monitoring

Some studies track how MST topology itself changes across market regimes (the tree "flattens" toward one dominant hub in stressed markets) as a structural, not just numerical, crisis indicator.

🧮

PCA vs. MST — different lenses, same matrix

PCA (previous category lesson) collapses the whole correlation matrix into a handful of ranked, mutually orthogonal factors — good for "how many independent bets do I really have." The MST keeps every individual pairwise relationship intact as a sparse graph — good for "which specific pairs are closest, and how does the whole basket connect together." Neither replaces the other; a full cross-asset structure read typically uses both.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where correlation-network analysis goes wrong in practice

Treating the MST as fixed. Exactly like the correlation matrix it's built from, an MST is estimated over a specific historical window and can change shape — sometimes substantially — as correlations shift across regimes. A tree built on calm-market data and one built on crisis data can genuinely disagree about which instrument is the hub.

Confusing "a natural extension" with "a validated one." As stated plainly above, Mantegna's original demonstration is an equities result; extending the same transform to FX/cross-asset data is mathematically valid but has not been tested for whether it produces anything economically meaningful or tradeable in that setting — that's a separate empirical question from whether the algorithm runs correctly.

Reading tree distance as literal "how many hops away." An instrument two edges away from the hub in the tree isn't necessarily "half as correlated" as one directly connected to it — the MST discards every non-tree edge's exact value, keeping only which edges survived the pruning. For the precise pairwise number, go back to the correlation matrix; use the tree for structure, not for reading off exact distances between non-adjacent nodes.

Small-basket instability. Just as with PCA's eigenvectors on a small \(N\), an MST built from only a handful of instruments and a short history can be sensitive to which exact days are included — two very close distances (like GBP–CHF at 0.89 versus EUR–AUD at 1.00 in the worked example) can swap which one the tree keeps with only modest changes to the underlying correlation estimate.

Section 04

Key Takeaways

✓Correlation needs a transform before it's a distance. Mantegna's \(d_{ij}=\sqrt{2(1-\rho_{ij})}\) turns "1 = identical" into "0 = zero distance," fixing correlation's backwards direction and satisfying the triangle inequality a proper metric needs.
✓The MST is the cheapest way to connect every node with no cycles. Built via Kruskal's (sort edges, add if no cycle) or Prim's (grow outward, always nearest) — both greedy, both provably optimal.
✓The worked example's backbone revealed a hub-and-outlier shape — EUR as the structural hub, JPY reached only through its single closest relationship — that the raw ten-number correlation table didn't show at a glance.
✓PCA and the MST are complementary, not competing. PCA collapses a correlation matrix into ranked factors; the MST keeps every pairwise relationship as a sparse graph — different questions, same underlying matrix.
⚠Mantegna's original result is an equities result. The FX/cross-asset framing in this lesson is a natural, structurally sound extension — it is not a technique this repo has tested or validated in that setting.
⚠A backbone picture is not a backtest. Seeing which instrument is structurally the outlier is a genuine diversification read, but it says nothing on its own about whether adding that instrument improves risk-adjusted returns.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. Why can't a raw correlation coefficient be used directly as a graph edge weight representing "distance" or "closeness"?
Because correlation measures similarity, and its scale runs in the opposite direction a distance needs: the most similar pair (\(\rho=1\)) has the largest correlation value, while a distance should be smallest for the closest pair. Using raw correlation directly would make highly correlated (truly close) pairs register as far apart in the graph. A transform like Mantegna's \(d=\sqrt{2(1-\rho)}\) is needed to flip the direction and produce a proper distance, where 0 means identical and larger values mean further apart.
Q2. In the worked example, why was the edge GBP–CHF (distance 0.89) skipped by Kruskal's algorithm even though it's shorter than several edges that were kept?
By the time Kruskal's algorithm reached GBP–CHF in the sorted edge list, both GBP and CHF were already connected to each other indirectly through the two edges added just before it (EUR–GBP and EUR–CHF) — they were already in the same connected component. Adding GBP–CHF on top would have closed a cycle (the EUR-GBP-CHF triangle) without connecting any new node to the tree, which Kruskal's algorithm always rejects regardless of how short the edge is. Being shorter than a later edge doesn't matter if the edge would be redundant.
Q3. What specific reason does Mantegna's \(d_{ij}=\sqrt{2(1-\rho_{ij})}\) transform have for its exact functional form, rather than something simpler like \(1-\rho_{ij}\)?
The \(\sqrt{2(1-\rho)}\) form is chosen specifically because it satisfies the triangle inequality (a requirement for a proper metric: the direct distance between two points can never exceed the sum of distances through a third point), which a simpler transform like \(1-\rho\) alone does not reliably satisfy for correlation matrices in general. Since shortest-path algorithms and spanning-tree construction rely on distances behaving like genuine metric distances, using a transform that isn't guaranteed to satisfy the triangle inequality can produce a graph where the usual intuitions about "shortest path" and "closest neighbor" break down.
Q4. What does the MST in the worked example reveal about JPY that the raw correlation matrix doesn't make immediately obvious?
The correlation matrix shows JPY has the lowest correlation with every other instrument in the basket (0.20–0.40, versus 0.35–0.80 among the other four), but that fact is buried among ten numbers and isn't visually obvious. The MST makes it structural and immediate: JPY isn't connected to the hub (EUR) directly at all — it only joins the tree through its single least-weak relationship, to AUD, and that edge (1.10) is the second-longest kept in the whole tree. The tree turns "JPY has uniformly weak correlations" into a visible shape — an outlier hanging off the main cluster by one thin thread — rather than a pattern a reader has to notice by scanning a row of numbers.
Q5. Why is Mantegna's original 1999 result specifically an equities result, and what should that caution you about applying the same MST construction to an FX basket?
Mantegna built and validated the transform-and-MST approach on a basket of Dow Jones equities, where the resulting tree was shown to recover recognizable industry clusters purely from price correlation. That empirical validation — "this specific technique produces economically sensible, useful structure" — was done for stocks, not currencies. Running the identical math on FX pairs is algorithmically valid (correlation-to-distance and MST construction don't care what asset class the data comes from), but whether the resulting FX tree is similarly meaningful or useful for anything practical is a separate empirical question this repo has not tested. The caution is to treat the FX application as a reasonable hypothesis to test carefully, not as something already proven simply because the original equities version was.
