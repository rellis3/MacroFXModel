CoVaR: Conditional Value-at-Risk for Systemic Risk

Plain VaR asks how bad institution j's own losses can get. CoVaR asks a genuinely different question: how bad can j's losses get given that institution i is already in distress — a measure of spillover and co-movement, not standalone risk. Despite the near-identical name, this is not the same thing as CVaR (Conditional VaR / Expected Shortfall) from the coherent-risk-measures lesson, and confusing the two is one of the easiest mistakes to make in this literature.

The only "systemic risk" hits at all are two unrelated, descriptive comment/message strings — RegimeV2/macro_overlay.py's docstring "True when VIX > 25 — elevated systemic risk" and a formatter string in RegimeV2/formatter.py reading "All FX vol indices elevated simultaneously — systemic risk-off, not pair-specific" — both of which label a VIX threshold and a display message, not any co-movement or spillover calculation between two series. TL;DR
  • CoVaRj|i (Adrian & Brunnermeier) is the VaR of institution/asset j's returns conditional on institution/asset i being in a distressed state — typically i sitting at its own VaR level — rather than j's plain, unconditional VaR computed alone.
  • The real systemic-risk-ranking tool is ΔCoVaR = CoVaRj|i in distress − CoVaRj|i at median — the marginal contribution of i's distress to j's risk, isolating the spillover effect from j's own baseline risk level.
  • The standard estimation approach is a quantile regression of j's returns on i's returns: fit the conditional τ-quantile line, then read it off at i's own distress level (i's VaR) versus i's median — the exact same pinball-loss machinery the sibling Quantile Regression lesson derives from scratch, reused here rather than re-derived.
  • CoVaR and CVaR are not the same thing, despite the near-identical three letters. CVaR (from Coherent Risk Measures) is the average loss within one distribution's own tail. CoVaR is a VaR of one variable conditioned on a different variable's distress — a co-movement statistic across two series, not a tail-averaging statistic within one.
  • The original 2016 paper's setting is banking-sector systemic risk. Applying the same machinery to "how does EM currency risk (j) respond to elevated USD funding stress (i)" is a practically useful extension — but it's an application by analogy to a different domain, not something the original paper itself studied.
  • A small toy dataset and a live calculator below compute CoVaR and ΔCoVaR by hand from a fitted conditional-quantile line, and the Pitfalls section is explicit about threshold arbitrariness, small-sample noise, and why CoVaR is a pairwise statistic, not a full network map of systemic risk.
Section 01

Why this matters

Every risk measure covered elsewhere in this Theory Lab — VaR, CVaR, a factor-model volatility estimate — answers a question about one portfolio or institution, taken in isolation: how bad can its own losses get? That question is necessary, but it quietly assumes something a financial system almost never delivers: that one institution's distress doesn't change the risk facing everyone else. In reality, one large institution failing, or one currency's funding markets seizing up, can make losses elsewhere more likely and more severe at exactly the same moment — a systemic, co-moving component of risk that a standalone risk number, computed on each institution separately, has no way to see at all.

Tobias Adrian and Markus Brunnermeier's CoVaR, introduced in their paper "CoVaR" (Adrian & Brunnermeier's CoVaR paper, published in the American Economic Review in 2016), was built specifically to answer this different question: not "how risky is institution j," but "how much riskier does j become when institution i is in trouble" — turning a standalone risk number into a genuinely relational one.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Not "how sick is this patient," but "how sick does this patient get when their roommate is sick"

Imagine tracking two roommates' health independently — each one's own baseline risk of getting sick this month. That's the equivalent of plain VaR: a standalone number for each person, computed without any reference to the other. Now ask a different question: given that roommate i has already come down with something contagious, how much worse does roommate j's own risk of getting sick become? That's CoVaR — it's not a claim about j's baseline health at all, it's a claim about how j's risk moves in response to i's condition. And the genuinely useful number for ranking "how dangerous is roommate i to be around" isn't roommate j's sick-day risk in absolute terms — some people are just more prone to catching things regardless — it's the difference between j's risk when i is contagious and j's risk when i is healthy. That difference is ΔCoVaR: the marginal, spillover contribution i's distress makes to j's risk, stripped of j's own baseline susceptibility.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Regular VaR is like asking "how bad could your day get?" all by itself. CoVaR is asking "how bad could your day get, GIVEN that your best friend is having a terrible day?" It's not about you on your own — it's about how much your bad-day risk changes because of someone else's trouble. And the really useful number isn't "how bad is your day when your friend is having a bad day" on its own — it's how much worse that is compared to a totally normal day for your friend. That gap is the real measure of how much your friend's mood actually spills over onto you.

Section 02

The math, step by step

1. Plain VaR, as a reminder

Following the same loss-random-variable convention as the Coherent Risk Measures lesson, let \(L_j\) be institution/asset j's loss. \(\text{VaR}_q(L_j)\) is the smallest loss threshold exceeded with probability at most \(1-q\) — a single number describing j's own tail risk, computed with no reference to anything else in the system. CoVaR's entire departure point is replacing "computed alone" with "computed conditional on something else."

A necessary sign-convention note before going further. The Coherent Risk Measures lesson works in loss units, where a bigger positive number is worse. The CoVaR literature, by contrast, is typically stated directly on returns, where a large negative number is the bad outcome and "distress" means a low (very negative) return, not a large positive one. This lesson follows the CoVaR literature's own convention — returns, not losses — throughout, and flags this explicitly so nobody carries the "bigger is worse" reading from the CVaR lesson over into this one by habit, where it would flip every sign.

2. CoVaR: a VaR that's conditional on someone else's distress

Let \(X_i\) and \(X_j\) be the returns of institution/asset i and institution/asset j. Fix a quantile level \(q\) (e.g. \(q=0.05\), the same 5% tail probability used throughout this Theory Lab). Adrian & Brunnermeier define \(\text{CoVaR}_{j|i}(q)\) as the \(q\)-quantile of \(X_j\), conditional on \(X_i\) equaling some specified state — most commonly, i's own VaR level:

\[ P\Big(X_j \le \text{CoVaR}_{j|i}(q) \;\Big|\; X_i = \text{VaR}_q(X_i) \Big) = q \]
X_i, X_jreturns of the conditioning institution i and the target institution j
VaR_q(X_i)i's own q-quantile return — i's "distress" level, e.g. i's own 5th-percentile worst return
qthe tail probability, e.g. 0.05 — the same q used for both i's own VaR and the conditional quantile of j

Read this exactly as written: \(\text{CoVaR}_{j|i}(q)\) is the value such that, given i is sitting at its own distress level, j's return falls below that value with probability \(q\). It is a full VaR-style quantile of \(X_j\) — the only thing that changed versus plain \(\text{VaR}_q(X_j)\) is that the whole calculation is now conditioned on a specific state of a different variable, \(X_i\), instead of being computed on \(X_j\)'s own unconditional distribution.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Plain VaR for j asks: "on a normal day, what's j's worst-case-ish outcome?" CoVaR asks a completely different day: "on the specific kind of day where i is already having a really bad one — what's j's worst-case-ish outcome THEN?" Same style of question (a worst-case percentile), asked about a different, conditioned-on world.

3. ΔCoVaR: the marginal, spillover part — the number practitioners actually rank institutions by

\(\text{CoVaR}_{j|i}(q)\) alone mixes two things together: whatever baseline risk j already has, plus whatever extra risk comes specifically from i's distress. To isolate the second piece — the actual systemic contribution — Adrian & Brunnermeier define \(\Delta\text{CoVaR}\) as the difference between CoVaR computed with i in distress and CoVaR computed with i at its normal, "nothing going on" state (typically i's own median):

\[ \Delta\text{CoVaR}_{j|i} = \text{CoVaR}_{j|i \text{ in distress}} - \text{CoVaR}_{j|i \text{ at median}} \]
CoVaR_{j|i in distress}j's conditional VaR when i sits at i's own VaR_q(X_i) — i's distress level
CoVaR_{j|i at median}j's conditional VaR when i sits at i's own median return — an ordinary, unremarkable day for i

This is the number actually used in practice to rank institutions by systemic importance — not \(\text{CoVaR}_{j|i}(q)\) alone, which still carries j's own baseline risk level bundled in, but \(\Delta\text{CoVaR}_{j|i}\), which nets that baseline out and isolates specifically how much worse j's risk gets because i is in distress rather than at a normal level. A large, negative \(\Delta\text{CoVaR}\) (in the return convention above) means i's distress genuinely drags j's tail risk down further; a \(\Delta\text{CoVaR}\) near zero means i's condition barely moves j's risk at all, regardless of how risky either institution looks on its own.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Some people are just naturally more likely to catch a cold, contagious roommate or not — that's their baseline risk. ΔCoVaR isn't interested in that baseline at all. It only asks: "how much MORE likely are you to get sick specifically because your roommate is sick right now, compared to a normal day where your roommate is fine?" That "extra, because-of-them" amount is the actual spillover — and it's what you'd use to decide which roommate is genuinely dangerous to live with, not just which roommate happens to get sick a lot on their own.

4. Estimating it: quantile regression, read off at two states of i

The standard way to estimate both quantities in practice is a single quantile regression of j's returns on i's returns (and, in richer specifications, additional state variables) at the target level \(q\). If quantile regression is new territory, the sibling Quantile Regression lesson derives the whole machinery from scratch — the pinball loss, why minimizing its expectation recovers a specific conditional quantile rather than the conditional mean OLS targets, and a full worked toy example. This lesson won't re-derive any of that; it only needs the headline result, in one sentence: quantile regression fits a line that directly targets a chosen percentile of \(Y\) given \(X\), the same way OLS targets the conditional mean — just with an asymmetric loss function instead of squared error.

Fitting that regression at level \(q\) gives a linear conditional quantile function:

\[ q_q(X_j \mid X_i = x) = \alpha_q + \beta_q\, x \]
α_q, β_qthe fitted quantile-regression intercept and slope at level q — β_q is how strongly j's tail co-moves with i
xthe specific value of i's return being conditioned on

CoVaR and ΔCoVaR then fall out of this one fitted line by simply plugging in two different values of \(x\) — i's own VaR (distress) and i's own median (normal):

\[ \text{CoVaR}_{j|i \text{ in distress}} = \alpha_q + \beta_q\,\text{VaR}_q(X_i), \qquad \text{CoVaR}_{j|i \text{ at median}} = \alpha_q + \beta_q\,\text{Median}(X_i) \]
\[ \Delta\text{CoVaR}_{j|i} = \beta_q\,\big[\text{VaR}_q(X_i) - \text{Median}(X_i)\big] \]

That last line is worth sitting with: \(\Delta\text{CoVaR}\) collapses to just \(\beta_q\) — the fitted quantile regression's slope — multiplied by how far i's own distress level sits from i's own normal level. The whole systemic-risk-ranking number reduces to one regression coefficient times one distance, once the quantile regression is fit. Everything genuinely new in this lesson beyond "fit a quantile regression" is contained in that one substitution.

βq is doing all the systemic-risk work here. If \(\beta_q = 0\) — j's conditional quantile doesn't move with i's return at all — then \(\Delta\text{CoVaR}_{j|i} = 0\) regardless of how extreme i's own distress level is. A large \(|\beta_q|\) is what makes i's condition actually matter for j; a large VaR gap for i alone, with a near-zero \(\beta_q\), produces almost no systemic spillover onto j at all.
\(X_i, X_j\)Returns of the conditioning institution/asset i and the target institution/asset j.
\(q\)The tail probability defining "distress," e.g. 0.05 (a 5% quantile) — the same q used for i's own VaR and for the conditional quantile of j.
\(\text{VaR}_q(X_i)\)i's own unconditional q-quantile return — i's distress level.
\(\text{Median}(X_i)\)i's own median return — an ordinary, unremarkable day for i.
\(\text{CoVaR}_{j|i}(q)\)The q-quantile of j's return, conditional on i being in a specified state (distress or median).
\(\Delta\text{CoVaR}_{j|i}\)CoVaRj|i in distress minus CoVaRj|i at median — the marginal, spillover-only contribution of i's distress to j's risk.
\(\alpha_q, \beta_q\)Fitted quantile-regression intercept and slope of j's returns on i's returns, at level q.

\[ q_{0.05}(X_j \mid X_i = x) = -0.4 + 0.8x \]

Checking this line against the ten points: exactly 1 of the 10 points (10%) falls below it (the \(i=-4.5\) point, where \(j=-4.3\) sits below the line's \(-4.0\)) — the closest a sample of only 10 integer-count observations can get to a true 5% exceedance rate, and a small, honest illustration of the small-sample noise the Pitfalls section discusses below.

1

i's own unconditional median and VaR

These are calibrated separately — typically from a longer or different historical sample than the small toy dataset above, exactly as a real desk would do. Take, for clean arithmetic, \(\text{Median}(X_i) = 0\%\) and \(\text{VaR}_{0.05}(X_i) = -3\%\) (i's own 5th-percentile worst daily return).

2

CoVaR with i at its median (the baseline)

\(\text{CoVaR}_{j|i\text{ at median}} = -0.4 + 0.8\times0 = \mathbf{-0.4\%}\). On an ordinary day for i, j's own conditional 5th-percentile return sits at \(-0.4\%\).

3

CoVaR with i in distress

\(\text{CoVaR}_{j|i\text{ in distress}} = -0.4 + 0.8\times(-3) = -0.4 - 2.4 = \mathbf{-2.8\%}\). When i sits at its own 5%-VaR level, j's conditional 5th-percentile return is a full 2.4 percentage points worse than the baseline case above.

4

ΔCoVaR — the actual systemic-risk number

\(\Delta\text{CoVaR}_{j|i} = -2.8 - (-0.4) = \mathbf{-2.4\%}\) — equivalently, \(\beta_{0.05}\times[\text{VaR}_{0.05}(X_i) - \text{Median}(X_i)] = 0.8\times(-3-0) = -2.4\%\). i's distress, on its own, drags j's conditional 5th-percentile return down by 2.4 additional percentage points versus a normal day for i.

Section 03

CoVaR vs. CVaR — a naming collision worth getting exactly right

"CoVaR" and "CVaR" differ by one letter and are, despite that, completely different concepts answering completely different questions. Mixing them up is one of the single easiest mistakes to make in this literature, and it's worth a dedicated section rather than a footnote.

CVaR — Conditional Value-at-Risk / Expected Shortfall

Covered in Coherent Risk Measures: \(\text{CVaR}_p(L) = E[L \mid L > \text{VaR}_p(L)]\) — the average loss within one variable's own tail. It is "conditional" only in the sense of conditioning on already being past that same variable's own VaR threshold. There is exactly one random variable involved throughout.

CoVaR — Conditional VaR of j, given i's state

\(\text{CoVaR}_{j|i}(q)\) is a VaR-style quantile of one variable (j), conditioned on the state of a different variable (i). It is "conditional" in the sense of conditioning on someone else's distress, not on being past its own threshold. There are always (at least) two distinct random variables involved: the target and the conditioning institution.

The distinction in one sentence: CVaR averages over a tail; CoVaR conditions on a different variable's distress. They happen to share letters and a superficial "extends VaR" framing, and nothing else. A number labeled "CVaR" in a risk report and a number labeled "CoVaR" in a systemic-risk report are not interchangeable, not approximately equal, and not answering variations of the same question.
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

CVaR is like asking "on your worst days, on average, how bad do THEY get?" — still just about you. CoVaR is asking "how bad do YOUR days get because of someone ELSE'S bad day?" One looks deeper into a single person's own worst outcomes; the other looks across two different people entirely. Same-sounding names, genuinely different questions — mixing them up is an easy, common mistake.

Section 04

From banks to FX and macro: an application by analogy

Adrian & Brunnermeier's original setting is banking-sector systemic risk: i and j are financial institutions, and the question is which banks' distress spills over most into the rest of the financial system — the paper's own motivating use case, and the one its empirical results actually speak to directly. Nothing in the mathematics above, though, actually requires i and j to be banks. The same machinery — fit a conditional quantile of j's returns on i's returns, read it off at i's distress level versus i's median — applies equally to any two return series with a plausible spillover relationship, including currency pairs and macro risk factors.

A natural FX/macro reframing of the exact toy numbers above: let \(i\) be a proxy for USD funding stress (for instance, a cross-currency basis spread or a short-term USD funding-cost indicator, standardized so a large negative move represents tightening funding conditions) and let \(j\) be the return on a basket of emerging-market currencies against the dollar. The same fitted line, \(q_{0.05}(X_j\mid X_i=x) = -0.4 + 0.8x\), would then say: on an ordinary day for USD funding conditions, the EM basket's conditional 5th-percentile return sits at \(-0.4\%\); on a day where USD funding stress is itself at its own worst 5% level, the EM basket's conditional 5th-percentile return worsens to \(-2.8\%\) — a \(\Delta\text{CoVaR}\) of \(-2.4\%\) attributable specifically to the funding-stress spillover, not to the EM basket's own baseline volatility.

Be honest about what this reframing is and isn't. This is an application by analogy — reusing a technique built and validated for banking-sector systemic risk in a different domain, because the underlying statistical machinery (a conditional quantile of one series given another) doesn't actually care what the two series represent. It is not a claim that Adrian & Brunnermeier's paper itself studied FX or macro risk factors, and it is not a claim that a USD-funding-stress-to-EM-FX spillover of this specific size has been empirically estimated anywhere — the numbers above are the same illustrative toy figures from Section 02, relabeled, not a fresh empirical result.
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

The original CoVaR idea was invented to study "which banks are dangerous to the rest of the banking system." The same math doesn't actually know or care that it's looking at banks — it just needs two number series that might move together. So it's perfectly reasonable to point the same tool at "USD funding stress" and "emerging-market currencies" instead. Just be upfront that you're borrowing a tool built for one job and pointing it at a different one, not quoting a result the original inventors actually produced themselves.

Section 05

How practitioners actually use this

📊

Ranking institutions by systemic contribution

Computing ΔCoVaRsystem|i for many institutions i against a system-wide index j, and ranking by the size of ΔCoVaR, is the paper's own headline application — identifying which institutions contribute most to system-wide tail risk, as distinct from which are individually largest or most volatile.

🏦

A complement to size-based "too big to fail" measures

An institution can be individually small but have a large ΔCoVaR (highly interconnected, moves the system a lot when distressed) or individually enormous but contribute little marginal spillover — the two rankings genuinely diverge in practice, which is part of the point of computing ΔCoVaR at all rather than relying on size alone.

📐

Extending the conditioning set with state variables

Richer specifications add macro state variables (a VIX-style volatility index, a credit-spread proxy, a yield-curve slope) into the quantile regression alongside i's own return, so ΔCoVaR can be computed conditional on the broader macro backdrop, not i's return in isolation.

🌐

A pairwise building block, not a finished network map

In practice CoVaR/ΔCoVaR is usually computed for many (i, j) pairs and read as a set of individual spillover strengths — a genuinely different, and simpler, object than a full systemic-risk network model, discussed in the pitfall below.

🎯
Real-world trading scenario

Two EM currencies that look equally risky in isolation, ranked very differently by ΔCoVaR against USD funding stress

Suppose two EM currencies, X and Y, have nearly identical plain \(\text{VaR}_{0.05}\) computed on their own historical returns — by a standalone-risk read, they look about equally dangerous to hold. Fit a quantile regression of each currency's return against a USD funding-stress indicator, and suppose currency X's fitted \(\beta_{0.05}\) comes out large (its tail return co-moves strongly with funding stress) while currency Y's comes out close to zero (its tail losses are driven mostly by idiosyncratic, country-specific factors, largely independent of USD funding conditions). \(\Delta\text{CoVaR}\) for X against funding stress would then be large and negative, while Y's would be near zero — even though the two currencies' own plain VaR numbers looked essentially identical. A risk desk relying on standalone VaR alone would treat X and Y as equally risky positions; one that also computes \(\Delta\text{CoVaR}\) against a common stress factor would correctly flag X as carrying meaningfully more systemic, co-moving exposure to a funding-stress event than Y does, information the standalone VaR numbers alone cannot provide.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where CoVaR quietly gets over-trusted

Quantile regression on short or non-stationary samples is noisy. The worked example's own ten-point toy dataset could only land 1 of 10 points (10%) below its fitted 5%-quantile line, not exactly 5% — a small, honest illustration of a real problem: fitting a tail quantile is inherently a small-effective-sample-size exercise (you're pinning down a line mostly using the handful of points near that specific tail), and financial return series that shift regime over time make "the fitted relationship is stable" an assumption that needs checking, not a given.

The choice of distress threshold is somewhat arbitrary, and results can be sensitive to it. Nothing in the theory pins down whether "distress" should mean i's 5%, 1%, or 10% quantile — a different choice of \(q\) can produce a meaningfully different fitted \(\beta_q\) and therefore a different \(\Delta\text{CoVaR}\), especially if the relationship between i and j isn't stable across the tail (the same quantile-crossing-adjacent concern the sibling Quantile Regression lesson raises for separately-fit quantile lines in general).

CoVaR captures one pairwise conditional relationship, not a full network map of systemic risk. \(\Delta\text{CoVaR}_{j|i}\) says something specific and useful about how i's distress relates to j's tail risk — it does not, on its own, describe how distress might propagate through multiple institutions in sequence, feed back on itself, or interact with several institutions' distress occurring together. More elaborate network-based systemic risk measures exist in the broader literature to address exactly that gap; this lesson won't invent specifics of any particular one it isn't certain of, beyond noting honestly that CoVaR is a pairwise building block, not a finished systemic map.

Causal language should be avoided. A large \(\Delta\text{CoVaR}_{j|i}\) is evidence that j's tail risk and i's distress co-move strongly — it is not, by itself, proof that i's distress causes j's losses. Both could be driven by a shared third factor (a common macro shock hitting both at once) without either institution's distress mechanically causing the other's. CoVaR is a conditional/co-movement statistic; treating a large ΔCoVaR as established proof of a causal spillover channel overstates what the number itself demonstrates.

Section 06

Key Takeaways

✓CoVaRj|i is a VaR of j conditional on i's distress — not j's plain, unconditional VaR. The conditioning on a second, distinct variable is the entire departure from standard VaR.
✓ΔCoVaR = CoVaRj|i in distress − CoVaRj|i at median is the actual systemic-risk-ranking tool used in practice — it isolates the marginal spillover effect from j's own baseline risk level.
✓Estimation is a quantile regression of j on i, read off at i's own VaR level versus its median: \(\Delta\text{CoVaR}_{j|i} = \beta_q[\text{VaR}_q(X_i)-\text{Median}(X_i)]\) — the fitted slope times the distance between i's two states.
✗CoVaR is not CVaR. CVaR averages a loss within one variable's own tail; CoVaR conditions a quantile of one variable on a different variable's distress. Sharing letters is the only thing they share.
⚠Applying CoVaR to FX/macro pairs is an application by analogy, not something the original 2016 banking-sector paper itself studied — a genuinely useful extension, presented honestly as one.
✗ΔCoVaR is a co-movement statistic, not proof of causation, and not a full network map. Threshold choice, small-sample noise, and the pairwise (not systemic-network) scope of the measure all need to be stated plainly alongside any ΔCoVaR number.
Section 07

Self-test

Q1. In your own words, what does CoVaRj|i(q) measure, and how does that differ from plain VaRq(X_j)?
CoVaRj|i(q) is the q-quantile of j's returns, computed conditional on i being in a specified state (usually i's own VaR level, i.e. distress). Plain VaRq(X_j) is j's q-quantile computed on its own, unconditional distribution, with no reference to any other institution or asset at all. The conditioning on a second, distinct variable's state is the entire difference.
Q2. Define ΔCoVaR, and explain why it — not CoVaR alone — is the number actually used to rank institutions by systemic importance.
ΔCoVaRj|i = CoVaRj|i in distress − CoVaRj|i at median. CoVaRj|i alone still bundles together j's own baseline risk level with whatever extra risk comes specifically from i's distress. Subtracting the median-state CoVaR nets out j's baseline and isolates only the marginal, spillover-attributable part — which is the genuinely useful quantity for asking "how much does i's distress specifically add to j's risk," as opposed to "how risky does j look overall."
Q3. What is the standard estimation approach for CoVaR, and what does the fitted slope β_q represent?
A quantile regression of j's returns on i's returns (and possibly additional state variables) at the target level q, giving a fitted conditional quantile line q_q(X_j|X_i=x) = α_q + β_q·x. CoVaR and ΔCoVaR are then read off this one fitted line by plugging in i's distress level and i's median. β_q represents how strongly j's tail quantile co-moves with i's return — ΔCoVaR reduces to β_q times the distance between i's distress level and i's median, so a near-zero β_q produces near-zero ΔCoVaR regardless of how extreme i's own distress is.
Q4. A colleague says "CoVaR and CVaR are basically the same thing, just spelled slightly differently." What's the precise correction?
They are not the same thing. CVaR (Conditional VaR / Expected Shortfall, from the Coherent Risk Measures lesson) is the average loss within one variable's own tail — E[L | L > VaR(L)], involving exactly one random variable. CoVaR is a VaR-style quantile of one variable (j) conditioned on a different variable's (i's) distress — it always involves at least two distinct random variables and answers a co-movement/spillover question, not a within-tail-averaging question. The near-identical name is a coincidence of terminology, not a sign the two concepts are related variations of the same idea.
Q5. Is applying CoVaR to a currency pair or a macro risk factor something Adrian & Brunnermeier's original paper studied directly? How should that application be described honestly?
No — the original paper's setting and empirical results are about banking-sector systemic risk, with i and j as financial institutions. Applying the identical quantile-regression machinery to, say, USD funding stress (i) and an EM currency basket (j) is an application by analogy: the underlying statistics don't require i and j to be banks, but doing so borrows a technique validated in one domain and points it at a different one. It should be presented as a plausible, useful extension — not as a result the original paper itself produced or validated in an FX/macro setting.
Q6. Name two of the pitfalls this lesson raises about interpreting a computed ΔCoVaR number, beyond "the arithmetic might be wrong."
Any two of: (1) the choice of distress threshold q is somewhat arbitrary and ΔCoVaR can be sensitive to it; (2) quantile regression on short or non-stationary samples is noisy, since a tail quantile is pinned down mostly by the few observations near that tail; (3) CoVaR captures one pairwise conditional relationship, not a full network map of how distress might propagate through many institutions at once; (4) a large ΔCoVaR is evidence of co-movement, not proof of a causal spillover — a shared third factor could drive both i's distress and j's tail risk without either causing the other.
