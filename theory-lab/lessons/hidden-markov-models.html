Hidden Markov Models & Regime Detection

Markets don't announce whether they're trending or chopping — an HMM is a formal way to guess the unobserved state from what you can actually see, and to be honest about how confident that guess is.

CONCEPT — not yet built in this repo ~17 min read Prereqs: GARCH & Volatility Clustering, Bayesian Inference
TL;DR
  • An HMM treats the market's regime (e.g. trending vs. choppy) as a hidden state that must be inferred, with each state emitting observations (returns) via its own probability distribution — a Gaussian with its own \(\mu_k,\sigma_k^2\) per state is the standard choice.
  • Every row of the \(N \times N\) transition matrix \(A\) sums to 1; the diagonal entries \(A_{ii}\) capture regime persistence, and expected regime duration is \(1/(1-A_{ii})\).
  • The worked example uses \(A_{11}=0.70\) (stay trending) and \(A_{22}=0.85\) (stay choppy), implying trending stretches last about 3.3 days on average and choppy stretches about 6.7 days — choppy is both stickier and, at a long-run share of ~66.7% vs. ~33.3%, more common.
  • All practical HMM use reduces to three problems: evaluation (forward algorithm — how likely is this data under the model), decoding (Viterbi — the single most likely hidden state path), and learning (Baum-Welch, an EM variant — estimate \(A\) and the emission parameters from data).
  • This is a concept lesson only — nothing here is built in this repo; it's an illustrative alternative to the existing hand-set classifyRegime and dayTypeScore classifiers, not a validated improvement over either.
Section 01

Why this matters

A huge amount of trading logic — in this repo and everywhere else — is secretly a regime bet. A trend-following rule only makes sense while the market is trending; the same rule bleeds money in a choppy, mean-reverting stretch. A mean-reversion rule is the mirror image. The GARCH lesson established that volatility itself clusters — calm periods and turbulent periods each persist for a while before switching — and this repo's classifyRegime and dayTypeScore already act on that idea with hand-set thresholds. This lesson introduces a different, more general tool for the same underlying problem: the Hidden Markov Model (HMM). An HMM treats "which regime are we in" as an unobserved variable that has to be inferred from data, rather than read directly off a rule like "if realized vol crossed above 1.5× its 20-day average, call it turbulent." It gives you a principled, probabilistic way to answer three questions that come up constantly in systematic trading: how likely is the data I'm seeing under my model of "the market alternates between these regimes" (evaluation); given the data so far, what's my best guess of the current and past hidden states (decoding); and how do I even estimate the regime dynamics — how sticky is each regime, how different do returns look in each one — directly from historical data instead of guessing constants by eye (learning)? Understanding HMMs is valuable even if you never ship one, because the concepts — latent state, transition probability, emission distribution, the Markov property itself — are the vocabulary underneath a large fraction of quantitative regime-switching literature, including the econometrics-native cousin covered later in this lesson (Hamilton's Markov-switching models).

💡
Plain-English intuition

You can't see the weather in another city, only what people are wearing

Imagine a friend in another city texts you a photo every day of a stranger on the street, but never tells you the weather directly. Some days the stranger is bundled in a heavy coat; other days they're in a t-shirt. You don't observe the weather (the hidden state) — you only observe clothing choices (the emission, i.e. what the hidden state produces that you can actually see). Two things make this problem tractable rather than hopeless. First, weather is sticky: if it was cold yesterday, it's probably still cold today — weather doesn't teleport between states independently each day, it transitions with some persistence. Second, clothing choice is informative but noisy: a heavy coat is much more likely on a cold day than a warm one, but it's not a certainty — someone might overdress, or be cold-blooded, or be walking straight from an air-conditioned building. An HMM formalizes exactly this setup: a hidden sequence of states that evolves with some persistence (the transition structure), each state producing an observable signal according to some probability distribution (the emission structure), and your job is to infer the hidden sequence — or the model's parameters — from the observable sequence alone. Swap "weather" for "trending vs. choppy market regime" and "clothing" for "today's return and realized volatility," and you have the trading application this lesson builds toward.

Section 02

The math, step by step

We'll build the model in three layers: first the plain Markov chain (a chain of observed states with no hidden part at all), then add the "hidden" layer on top, then walk through — at a conceptual level — the three classic questions people ask of a fitted HMM.

Layer 1 — the Markov chain and the Markov property

A Markov chain is a sequence of states \(S_1, S_2, S_3, \ldots\) that evolves over discrete time steps, where each state is drawn from a finite set (in our trading example: "trending" or "choppy" — two states, though there could be more). The defining assumption, the Markov property, is that the probability of moving to the next state depends only on the current state — not on the full history of how you got there:

\[ P(S_t \mid S_{t-1}, S_{t-2}, \ldots, S_1) = P(S_t \mid S_{t-1}) \]
S_tthe hidden state at time t
= P(S_t∣S_{t-1})only yesterday's state matters — the Markov property

Read this in words: "the probability of tomorrow's state, given the entire history of states up to today, is exactly the same as the probability of tomorrow's state given only today's state." Everything before today is, formally, irrelevant once you know today. This is sometimes called "memorylessness," though that phrase is a bit misleading — the chain isn't literally forgetful, it's that today's state is assumed to already summarize everything about the past that matters for predicting tomorrow. This is a strong simplifying assumption (real markets plausibly have longer memory than "just today" — more on this in the pitfalls section), but it's the assumption that makes the whole machinery below computationally tractable, and it's the same first-order-Markov assumption used throughout time-series regime modeling, including Hamilton's econometric Markov-switching models referenced at the end of this lesson.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

The Markov property just means "only today matters for guessing tomorrow — you don't need to know the whole backstory." It's like guessing tomorrow's weather from today's weather alone, without needing to know what the weather was doing last week. It's not perfectly true of real markets, but it's a simplification that makes the math solvable, the same trade-off every model in this curriculum makes somewhere.

Layer 2 — the transition matrix

If there are \(N\) possible states, the full set of one-step transition probabilities is captured by an \(N \times N\) transition matrix \(A\), where each entry is:

\[ A_{ij} = P(S_t = j \mid S_{t-1} = i) \]
A_ijprobability of moving from state i to state j in one step

Read \(A_{ij}\) as "the probability that, given you're in state \(i\) today, you'll be in state \(j\) tomorrow." Each row of \(A\) is a full probability distribution over "where do I go next, given I'm currently in row-state \(i\)" — and because it's a probability distribution (you have to go somewhere, possibly back to the same state), every row must sum to exactly 1:

\[ \sum_{j=1}^{N} A_{ij} = 1 \quad \text{for every state } i \]
Σⱼ A_ijevery row is a full probability distribution — sums to 1

For our 2-state trending/choppy example, \(A\) is a 2×2 matrix. The diagonal entries \(A_{11}\) (stay trending) and \(A_{22}\) (stay choppy) capture persistence — how sticky each regime is. The off-diagonal entries \(A_{12}\) and \(A_{21}\) capture how often the regime actually switches. A diagonal close to 1 means a very persistent, slow-switching system; a diagonal close to 0.5 (for a 2-state chain) means the state is nearly random from one step to the next, with almost no persistence at all.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

The transition matrix is just a table of odds: "if I'm in this mood today, how likely am I to still be in it tomorrow, versus switching to the other mood?" The numbers on the diagonal (stay-put odds) tell you how sticky each mood is — close to 1 means once you're in that mood, you tend to stay there a while; close to 50/50 means the mood is basically a coin flip every single day.

Layer 3 — hidden states and emission distributions

Everything above described an observed Markov chain — you'd know \(S_t\) directly. An HMM adds exactly one twist: you do not observe \(S_t\) directly. Instead, at each time step, the hidden state \(S_t\) produces (emits) an observation \(O_t\) — in our case, that day's return, or some feature computed from it — according to a state-specific probability distribution called the emission distribution:

\[ O_t \sim P(O_t \mid S_t) \]

The natural, standard choice for continuous data like returns is a Gaussian (normal) emission per state, each with its own mean and variance:

\[ P(O_t \mid S_t = k) = \mathcal{N}(O_t;\ \mu_k,\ \sigma_k^2) \]

\(N\)The number of possible hidden states in the model — a choice you make when you design the HMM (e.g. \(N=2\) for trending/choppy, or \(N=3\) to add "high-vol crisis").
\(A\)The \(N \times N\) transition matrix. \(A_{ij}=P(S_t=j\mid S_{t-1}=i)\) is the probability of moving from state \(i\) to state \(j\) in one step. Every row sums to 1.
\(O_t\)The observation at time \(t\) — the thing you actually see and can compute, e.g. that day's return or realized volatility. Read as "oh-sub-t."
\(\mu_k, \sigma_k^2\)The mean and variance of the emission distribution for state \(k\) — e.g. "trending" days might have \(\mu_1 = +0.04\%\) drift with lower variance, "choppy" days \(\mu_2 \approx 0\%\) with higher variance. These are exactly the kind of per-regime mean/vol parameters the GARCH lesson's "different regimes, different volatility" intuition motivates.
\(\mathcal{N}(x; \mu, \sigma^2)\)The Gaussian (normal) probability density evaluated at \(x\), with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\) — the familiar bell curve. It's the standard (though not the only possible) choice of emission distribution for continuous returns.
\(\pi\)The initial-state distribution — \(\pi_k = P(S_1 = k)\), the probability the chain starts in state \(k\) before any data is observed. Needed to fully specify the model, along with \(A\) and the emission parameters.
\(\theta\)Shorthand for "the whole set of model parameters" — \(A\), \(\pi\), and every state's emission parameters \((\mu_k, \sigma_k^2)\) — used when talking about fitting or evaluating "the model" as a single object.
\(O_{1:T}\)Shorthand for the entire observed sequence \(O_1, O_2, \ldots, O_T\) — "all the data from time 1 through time \(T\)."

Put the three pieces together and an HMM is fully specified by \(\theta = (A, \pi, \{\mu_k, \sigma_k^2\})\): how the hidden state moves (\(A\)), where it starts (\(\pi\)), and what each state looks like once you can observe it (the emission parameters). Everything the model can tell you — and everything you'll fit from data — flows from these three pieces.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

You can't see the hidden state directly — you only see the clues it leaves behind. The emission distribution just says "here's what a typical day looks like when the market is secretly in this regime" — trending days might typically drift up gently with low noise, choppy days might typically sit flat with more noise. Seeing a particular day's return is like seeing a stranger's coat: it's a hint about the hidden weather, not proof of it.

The three canonical HMM problems

Given this setup, essentially all practical use of HMMs reduces to one of three questions, each with a named classical algorithm. None of these need to be derived line-by-line to use an HMM productively — what matters is knowing which question you're asking and which tool answers it.

  • 1. Evaluation — "how likely is this data, under my model?" Formally: compute \(P(O_{1:T} \mid \theta)\), the probability of the entire observed sequence given a specific set of model parameters. This matters because the hidden state could have followed any of exponentially many possible paths to produce the same observed sequence, and naively summing over all of them is computationally infeasible for anything but a toy example. The forward algorithm solves this efficiently by building up the calculation recursively: at each time step, it tracks the probability of "being in state \(k\) at time \(t\) and having produced everything observed so far," reusing the previous step's work instead of recomputing from scratch. This turns an exponential problem into one that's linear in the length of the sequence. Evaluation is the tool for comparing models — e.g. "does a 2-state HMM or a 3-state HMM explain this return series better?" — and for scoring how well-fit a candidate model is to new data.
  • 2. Decoding — "what was the most likely hidden path?" Formally: find the single state sequence \(S_1, \ldots, S_T\) that maximizes \(P(S_{1:T} \mid O_{1:T}, \theta)\) — the most probable explanation for the data you actually saw, given a fixed model. This is the question you ask when you want an actual regime label for each historical day: "was March 14th more likely trending or choppy?" The Viterbi algorithm solves this, again by dynamic programming — instead of tracking the probability of every path (there are exponentially many), it tracks only the best path ending in each state at each time step, and the second-best possibilities are simply discarded, because they can never become part of the overall best path later. The result is the single most likely regime label at every point in history — as opposed to the forward algorithm, which sums over all possible paths rather than picking the best one. This distinction matters: evaluation answers "how plausible is this data overall," decoding answers "what specifically happened."
  • 3. Learning — "what are \(A\) and the emission parameters, given only the observed data?" This is the hardest and most important problem in practice, because in real use you almost never know the true transition probabilities or the true per-regime mean/variance in advance — you have to estimate them from historical returns. The standard method is the Baum-Welch algorithm, a special case of the more general Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm. Conceptually, without deriving the full update equations, Baum-Welch works by iterating two steps until the parameters stop changing much:
    1. E-step ("guess the states"): given the current guess of \(A\) and the emission parameters, use the forward algorithm (and a companion "backward" pass) to compute, for every time step, the probability of being in each hidden state — a soft, probabilistic labeling of the whole sequence, not a hard Viterbi-style single best path.
    2. M-step ("refit the parameters"): given those soft state-probability labels, re-estimate \(A\) and each state's \((\mu_k, \sigma_k^2)\) the way you'd estimate any weighted mean/variance/transition-frequency, treating the E-step's probabilities as weights.
    Repeat E-step, then M-step, then E-step again, and so on. Each full cycle is guaranteed to never decrease the likelihood of the observed data under the model (it converges to at least a local maximum, not necessarily the global one — a genuine practical caveat, which is why real implementations run the whole procedure from several random starting points and keep the best result). This "guess the labels, refit to the guessed labels, repeat" pattern is the same basic idea behind K-means clustering and a wide family of EM-based estimation methods — it's worth recognizing the pattern beyond just HMMs.
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Three different questions, three different tools. "How believable is this whole story overall?" — that's evaluation. "What's the single most likely thing that actually happened, day by day?" — that's decoding. "I don't even know the rules of this game yet — can you figure them out just by watching?" — that's learning, and it works by alternating best guesses: guess the hidden days, then refit the rules to match those guesses, then guess again with the better rules, over and over until it stops improving.

Worked example

A 2-state trending/choppy transition matrix, by hand

Suppose — purely as an illustrative, made-up-but-sensible starting point, not a fitted result — you set up a 2-state HMM with states "trending" (state 1) and "choppy" (state 2), and you assume (or have fitted) this transition matrix:

\[ A = \begin{pmatrix} A_{11} & A_{12} \\ A_{21} & A_{22} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.70 & 0.30 \\ 0.15 & 0.85 \end{pmatrix} \]

Read row 1 as: "given today is trending, there's a 70% chance tomorrow is still trending, and a 30% chance it flips to choppy." Read row 2 as: "given today is choppy, there's an 85% chance tomorrow is still choppy, and a 15% chance it flips to trending." Both rows sum to 1, as required. Notice the asymmetry: choppy is the stickier regime here (0.85 vs. 0.70) — a reasonable prior for FX, where range-bound, low-conviction trading is often the more common baseline state and genuine trends are the rarer, harder-to-sustain condition.

Expected regime duration. A useful, easy-to-compute quantity: if a regime has self-transition probability \(p = A_{ii}\), the expected number of consecutive periods you stay in that regime (a standard property of a geometric distribution — each period you either "stay" with probability \(p\) or "leave" with probability \(1-p\), independently, so the expected run length is \(1/(1-p)\)) is:

\[ \text{Expected duration}_i = \frac{1}{1 - A_{ii}} \]
A_iiself-transition probability — how sticky the regime is

For trending: \(1/(1-0.70) = 1/0.30 \approx 3.33\) periods. For choppy: \(1/(1-0.85) = 1/0.15 \approx 6.67\) periods. If each period is one trading day, this transition matrix implies trending stretches typically last about 3–4 days before breaking, while choppy stretches typically run 6–7 days — the model's own numbers are already telling you something concrete and checkable about regime persistence, before you've looked at a single emission distribution.

Long-run (stationary) probability of each state. If you let this chain run for a very long time, what fraction of periods will it spend in each state, regardless of where it started? This is the stationary distribution \(\pi^*\), the fixed point where applying one more transition step leaves the distribution unchanged: \(\pi^* A = \pi^*\). For a 2-state chain, there's a direct formula. Writing \(a = A_{12}\) (probability of leaving trending) and \(b = A_{21}\) (probability of leaving choppy):

\[ \pi^*_{\text{trending}} = \frac{b}{a+b}, \qquad \pi^*_{\text{choppy}} = \frac{a}{a+b} \]

Plugging in \(a = 0.30\), \(b = 0.15\): \(\pi^*_{\text{trending}} = 0.15/0.45 \approx 0.333\), and \(\pi^*_{\text{choppy}} = 0.30/0.45 \approx 0.667\). So this transition matrix implies that, over the long run, the market spends about a third of its time trending and two-thirds of its time choppy — consistent with the intuition that choppy is both the more common and the more persistent state in this made-up example. Note the intuitive check: the state with the higher escape probability (trending, which leaves 30% of the time) ends up with the lower long-run share, and vice versa — a state you leave more often, you spend less total time in.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

In real quantitative research, HMMs (and their econometric cousin, Markov-switching regression) show up in a fairly consistent set of roles:

📊

Regime-conditional sizing/selection

Fit an HMM to identify high-vol vs. low-vol or trending vs. mean-reverting regimes, and switch strategy or size based on the current smoothed state probability, not a hard Viterbi label.

🔍

Post-hoc labeling, not live decisioning

Viterbi decoding uses the entire sequence — including the future — so a Viterbi-labeled history has lookahead baked in. A live system needs the filtered probability \(P(S_t\mid O_{1:t})\), not the smoothed one.

⚖️

Model comparison via likelihood

The forward algorithm gives a principled way to compare a 2-state vs. 3-state model, penalized for parameter count via AIC/BIC.

🧭

One voice among several

Practitioners rarely bet size purely on an HMM's call — it's blended with other regime signals, mirroring this repo's own scores/selectors philosophy.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Ways HMMs get misused, in trading and elsewhere

  • Using smoothed (Viterbi/forward-backward) labels in a live signal. As noted above, the standard decoding output uses the entire sequence, including the future relative to any given point, to find the best overall labeling. A backtest that labels history with Viterbi and then "trades" based on those labels is quietly using information from the future. The live- valid quantity is the filtered probability, computed using only data available up to that point in time.
  • Assuming markets are genuinely, stably Markov. The Markov property assumes today's state fully summarizes everything relevant about the past. Real markets plausibly have longer memory than that (a slow-building macro trend, a multi-week liquidity drought) and regime definitions themselves can drift over years — a "trending" regime in 2015 and a "trending" regime in 2023 may not share the same statistical character. Treating a fitted 2- or 3-state HMM as a literal, stable description of market mechanics rather than a useful simplification is a common overreach.
  • Overfitting the number of states. Adding more hidden states will essentially always increase the fitted likelihood (more free parameters, more flexibility) — that alone is not evidence a richer state structure is real. Model-selection criteria that penalize parameter count (AIC/BIC), and — more importantly per this repo's own standard — genuine out-of-sample validation, are needed before trusting a bigger model over a simpler one.
  • Baum-Welch converging to a poor local optimum, or a degenerate one. EM-based fitting is only guaranteed to reach a local maximum of the likelihood, not the global one, and different random parameter initializations can converge to meaningfully different fitted models. A degenerate case worth watching for specifically: one state's emission variance collapsing toward a handful of nearly-identical observations, which can spuriously inflate that state's likelihood contribution. Running multiple restarts and sanity-checking the fitted emission parameters (do the per-state means/variances actually look like a "trending" regime and a "choppy" regime, or like statistical noise?) is standard practice, not optional polish.
  • Treating the hard state label as certain. Even the best HMM produces a probability distribution over states at each time, not a certainty. A day the model calls "72% trending" is meaningfully different from one at "99% trending," and collapsing both to a single binary flag throws away real information — exactly the kind of information a position-sizing rule could use, but only if it's carried through rather than discarded at the labeling step.

Section 05

Self-test

Q1. State the Markov property in your own words, and explain what it assumes away.
The Markov property says the probability of the next state depends only on the current state, not on the full history of states before it: \(P(S_t \mid S_{t-1}, \ldots, S_1) = P(S_t \mid S_{t-1})\). It assumes away any "longer memory" — any influence of how you arrived at the current state (e.g. having been trending for 10 days vs. 1 day shouldn't matter to a first-order Markov model, only that you're currently trending at all). This is a real simplification of anything with genuine path-dependence or slow-building momentum.
Q2. Why must every row of the transition matrix \(A\) sum to 1, but not necessarily every column?
Each row of \(A\) represents the full probability distribution of "where do I go next, given I'm currently in this row's state" — and since the system has to transition to some state (possibly staying in the same one), those probabilities must exhaust all possibilities and sum to 1. A column, by contrast, represents "probability of arriving at this state, from each possible starting state" — there's no requirement those sum to anything in particular, since how likely you are to arrive somewhere depends on how likely you were to be in each starting state in the first place (which isn't uniform).
Q3. What question does the forward algorithm answer, and what question does Viterbi decoding answer? Why are these different?
The forward algorithm answers the evaluation question: "how likely is this entire observed sequence, under this model?" — it sums the probability over every possible hidden state path consistent with the data. Viterbi decoding answers the decoding question: "what is the single most likely hidden state sequence that produced this data?" — it finds the one best path rather than summing over all of them. They're different because "the data is plausible overall" (many so-so paths adding up) is a different claim from "here specifically is the most likely explanation" (one path standing out) — a model can assign high total probability to a sequence without any single path through it being especially dominant.
Q4. In plain terms, what do the E-step and M-step of Baum-Welch each do, and why do you have to repeat them?
The E-step takes the model's current parameter guesses and computes, for every time step, the probability of being in each hidden state given the data (a soft labeling of the whole sequence). The M-step takes those soft labels and re-estimates the transition matrix and emission parameters as if the labels were the truth, weighted by their probabilities. You repeat because better parameters produce better state-probability estimates in the next E-step, which in turn produce better parameter estimates in the next M-step — the two refine each other, and the process is repeated until the parameters stop changing meaningfully (convergence to at least a local optimum of the likelihood).
Q5. In the worked example (\(A_{11}=0.70\), \(A_{22}=0.85\)), which regime is more persistent, and what does that number mean concretely?
Choppy is more persistent: \(A_{22}=0.85 > A_{11}=0.70\). Concretely, expected duration is \(1/(1-A_{ii})\): trending regimes last about \(1/0.30 \approx 3.3\) periods on average before switching, while choppy regimes last about \(1/0.15 \approx 6.7\) periods on average — roughly twice as long. A diagonal transition probability near 1 always implies a regime that, once entered, tends to persist for a long stretch before flipping; a diagonal near the "flip a coin" value (0.5 for a 2-state chain) implies almost no persistence at all.
Q6. Why might a 2-state HMM be too simple a model for real market regimes?
Real markets plausibly cycle through more than two qualitatively distinct conditions (e.g. calm trending, volatile trending, calm chop, crisis/panic chop), and regime character itself likely drifts over years rather than repeating two fixed, stable statistical profiles indefinitely. A 2-state model forces every observation into one of only two buckets, which can blur genuinely different conditions together, and the first-order Markov assumption (today fully summarizes the relevant past) may be too strong for slow-building macro trends or multi-week liquidity regimes that plausibly have memory beyond just "yesterday." More states can capture richer structure, but only trade this off against overfitting risk and reduced data per state — not automatically resolve the problem.

Hamilton, "A New Approach to the Economic Analysis of Nonstationary Time Series and the Business Cycle," Econometrica, 1989 — the econometrics-native regime-switching cousin of the HMM, applying Markov-switching dynamics directly to macroeconomic and financial time series (originally to model U.S. GNP growth switching between expansion and recession regimes).
  • Robert J. Elliott, Lakhdar Aggoun, and John B. Moore, Hidden Markov Models: Estimation and Control — a more mathematically complete treatment of HMM theory, including continuous-time and filtering extensions, for readers who want the full derivations this lesson deliberately stayed above.
