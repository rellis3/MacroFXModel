Local Volatility, A Third Way to Model σ

Heston and SABR make volatility stochastic — its own random process, its own source of noise. Dupire's 1994 result takes a third route entirely: keep volatility deterministic, but let it depend on the spot's own level and time. One random source instead of two, and a formula that reads the whole thing straight off the market's own option prices.

The whole idea
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

My vanilla smile is marked. A client wants a barrier on the same pair. Which volatility do I use?

Not one flat number. Price the barrier at the at-the-money vol and it disagrees with the vanillas on your own book. Someone could hedge one against the other and pick you off.

So I need a model that matches every vanilla at once.

That's what Bruno Dupire gave us in 1994, in a paper called Pricing with a Smile. Emanuel Derman and Iraj Kani built the same idea as a tree that same year.

How is it different from Heston?

Heston makes volatility random, with its own shock on top of the price shock. Two sources of randomness. Dupire keeps one. Volatility becomes a fixed map: tell me the spot and the date, and I'll read you the vol.

And where does the map come from?

Straight from option prices. Take how fast a call gains value as you add time, adjust for interest, and divide by how curved the call price is across strikes. That curvature is the market's probability of ending near that strike.

Does it actually work?

The lesson tests it. Five call prices from a world where volatility is a flat twenty percent, around a strike of a hundred and a one-year expiry. Run the formula and you get twenty point oh six percent. The tiny gap is just the coarse grid.

So it fits perfectly.

Today, by construction. Like tracing paper over a drawing. That's its strength and its catch. It's a calibration device, not a claim about how the market behaves.

What's the catch exactly?

Ask it what the smile looks like next month and its answer is typically too flat compared with what markets go on to show. For a barrier, where the path matters, that weakness bites. Many desks blend it with stochastic volatility for that reason.

Anything practical to watch?

Never feed it raw quotes. The curvature is a second difference, and it blows up noise. In the lesson's example, moving one quote by five cents pushes the answer from about twenty to about twenty-two and a half percent. Fit a smooth surface first.

One line?

Dupire turns today's whole option surface into a volatility map that reprices every vanilla exactly, so use it for consistency, smooth before you differentiate, and don't trust its view of tomorrow.

Section 1 · How it reached trading

Pricing an exotic that agrees with every vanilla

Local volatility · Dupire; Derman & Kani, 1994 — equity exotics desks

How it reached trading

After 1987 an exotic priced with one flat vol was inconsistent with the vanillas hedging it. In 1994 Dupire ("Pricing with a Smile") and Derman & Kani at Goldman Sachs ("Riding on a Smile") gave a model that reprices every vanilla by construction.

It became the default calibration engine on equity exotics desks. Its too-flat forward smile (Hagan et al., 2002) is why many desks now wrap it in local-stochastic-vol hybrids.

Section 2

One random source, or two?

The distinction to keep above everything else in this deck.

Local vol (Dupire) — one dW
  • dS_t = μS_t dt + σ_local(S_t, t)·S_t dW_t
  • σ_local is a deterministic function — plug in (S,t), get a number
  • No second SDE, no vol-of-vol, no ρ — nothing else is random
Stochastic vol (Heston/SABR) — two dW's
  • dS_t = ... dW_t¹  and  dv_t = ... dW_t², Corr = ρ
  • Even knowing S_t and t exactly, v_t is still uncertain
  • A genuinely separate source of randomness, not a lookup table
Once you know the spot and the time, local vol's σ is a known number, not a random draw. Stochastic vol's σ has its own private history of shocks on top of that.
Section 3

Dupire's formula

Given a complete, arbitrage-free grid of call prices C(K,T), extract the unique local-vol surface consistent with all of it.

The formula
σ²_local(K,T)
=
[ ∂C/∂T + rK·∂C/∂K ] / [ ½K²·∂²C/∂K² ]
Numerator: how the price changes with maturity, corrected for the strike's own carry. Denominator: strike-convexity — up to a constant, the risk-neutral density of S_T at K (Breeden-Litzenberger, next lesson in this category).

This is the zero-dividend-yield form of the formula. A production version carries an extra term for a dividend/foreign rate q — exact conventions belong to Dupire's original 1994 paper and standard references like Gatheral's The Volatility Surface, not to any one restated version.

Section 7

A tracing, not a painting

📐

Local vol — a calibration device

Built by reading derivatives straight off today's surface. It cannot fail to match today's prices — that's what it's for, the way tracing paper can't fail to match the picture underneath.

🧬

Heston/SABR — parametric behavioral models

A handful of free parameters governing an assumed dynamic, fit to today's surface with a real residual. Whether that dynamic matches how the market actually evolves is a separate, testable question.

Asking "does local vol fit today's smile?" is a category error — of course it does, that's the mechanism. The genuinely testable question is whether its implied dynamics for tomorrow's smile look like reality.
Section 8

Right today, unrealistic tomorrow

The standard critique — and the industry's practical answer.

📉

Sticky strike vs. sticky delta

Does the smile stay pinned to fixed strikes as spot moves, or shift bodily with spot in moneyness terms? Local vol's own implied answer tends not to match either convention cleanly.

📏

Forward smile, too flat

A calibrated local-vol model's implied smile for a future date is widely considered flatter than what markets actually go on to show — a qualitative, well-known pattern, not one precise universal number.

🔀

SLV hybrids — the practical answer

Stochastic-local-vol models pair a Heston-like stochastic core (for realistic dynamics) with a local-vol-style correction (for an exact fit today) — conceptually, not a specific formula asserted here.

Section 9

Smooth the surface first

Why the worked example's clean numbers aren't how real market data looks.

Raw market quotes
  • Discrete, sparse, bid-ask-noisy
  • Finite differences amplify that noise
  • ∂²C/∂K² (a second derivative, in the denominator) amplifies it worse
  • Can produce unstable or negative "local vol" — meaningless
Fit a smooth surface first
  • A spline, or a parametric smile fit like SABR (this category's own smoothing tool)
  • Differentiate the smooth fitted curve, never the raw quotes
  • This step is close to mandatory for any real-data application
Section 10

Pitfalls and what's in this repo

⚠️

Extrapolation into illiquid corners

Deep OTM strikes or long maturities often have no real quotes at all — a local vol read off there is a modeling artifact, not a market fact.

🌐

FX surface symmetries

FX desks quote in delta, and known EUR/USD-vs-USD/EUR symmetries exist. Converting to a strike/maturity C(K,T) grid carelessly can break those symmetries.

✅

No Dupire code in this repo

A grep for "Dupire" across every .js/.py file returns zero hits.

🚫

"local vol" hits are unrelated

The only 4 "local vol" phrase hits are an ATR-bucketing label in bot/backtest.py and the Lee-Mykland jump detector's rolling realized-vol window — a different, older sense of "local," not Dupire's σ(S,t).

Section 11

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

What is the key structural distinction between local vol and stochastic vol?

σ_local(S_t,t) rides on the same single dW_t driving price. Heston/SABR add a genuinely separate dW_t², correlated by ρ but not determined by dW_t¹.
Question 2

What does Dupire's formula's denominator, ∂²C/∂K², actually represent?

This is the Breeden-Litzenberger result, covered in full in this category's next lesson — a non-positive value here means the input prices imply an impossible negative density.
Question 3

Why is local vol called a "calibration device" rather than a "behavioral model"?

Reading derivatives off the observed surface guarantees an exact match — the same way tracing paper can't fail to match the picture underneath. Heston/SABR fit assumed dynamics, with a real, testable residual.
Question 4

What is the standard critique of local volatility models?

A calibrated local-vol model fits today's snapshot perfectly, but its own implied smile for a future date tends to be flatter than what markets actually show later — the sticky-strike/sticky-delta discussion.
Question 5

Why can't Dupire's formula be applied directly to raw, noisy market quotes?

The standard fix is to fit a smooth surface first (a spline, or a parametric smile model like SABR) and differentiate that fitted surface — never the raw discrete quotes.
Question 6

C(K−h,T)=12.00, C(K,T)=10.00, C(K+h,T)=8.40, C(K,T−Δt)=9.00, C(K,T+Δt)=11.50, K=100, h=5, Δt=0.25, r=3%. σ_local ≈ ?

∂C/∂K=−0.36, ∂²C/∂K²=0.016, ∂C/∂T=5.00. Numerator = 5.00−1.08 = 3.92. Denominator = 80. σ²_local = 0.049, so σ_local ≈ √0.049 ≈ 22.1%.
Your call
Scenario drill · an illustrative client request

A down-and-out barrier on a pair whose smile you have already marked

You run an FX exotics book with a full, liquid USD/JPY vanilla smile marked at one expiry, as in the lesson's scenario. A client asks for a price on a down-and-out barrier option on the same pair and expiry. Its value depends heavily on the path spot takes towards the barrier. How do you price it?

  1. Use the at-the-money implied vol for the whole thing.
    One flat vol is inconsistent with your own marked smile. A client could hedge the barrier with your vanillas and trade against the mispricing.
  2. Build local vol straight from the raw vanilla quotes with finite differences.
    The strike curvature amplifies noise. In the lesson's own numbers, a 5-cent change in one quote moves local vol from about 20.06% to 22.59%. Unsmoothed quotes can even give negative local variance.
  3. Fit a smooth surface, build local vol from it, price the barrier, and stop there.
    The price is now consistent with every vanilla on the book. But a barrier is sensitive to how the smile moves, and local vol's forward smile is typically too flat, so this inherits that weakness unexamined.
  4. Fit a smooth surface, price with local vol for vanilla consistency, and check the price against a stochastic-vol or SLV model before quoting.
    Local vol gives the exact match to today's vanillas. A model with tested forward dynamics shows how much the barrier depends on the smile's future path, which is the part local vol gets least right.

What the lesson says. This is an illustrative case built on the lesson's own trading scenario, not a real trade. The point: local vol reprices every vanilla exactly by construction, which removes the static-hedge inconsistency, but its implied forward smile is widely considered too flat. For a path-dependent exotic like a barrier, that is exactly the risk to check, and it is why larger desks often use stochastic-local-vol hybrids for barriers.

Section 12

What to carry forward

01

Local vol (Dupire, 1994) makes volatility a deterministic function of spot and time, σ_local(S,t) — one source of randomness, the same dW_t that drives price. Heston/SABR use two.

02

Given a complete, arbitrage-free option-price surface, Dupire's formula extracts the unique local-vol function consistent with it — no calibration search, and the worked example recovered a known 20% flat vol to within 0.06 points.

03

Local vol is a calibration device (guaranteed exact fit by construction); Heston/SABR are parametric behavioral models (an assumed, testable dynamic with a real fitting residual).

04

The standard critique: local vol's own implied forward-smile dynamics are widely considered too flat — the main reason practitioners lean on stochastic-vol models or SLV hybrids.

05

Raw market quotes can't be differentiated directly — a smoothing/fitting step (a spline, or SABR) has to come first, or the formula's own second derivative amplifies noise into instability.

06

On a desk: Dupire and Derman & Kani (1994) made exotics consistent with the vanilla surface; local vol is still the calibration anchor, often inside SLV hybrids.

What this now lets you do

Tell apart three genuinely different ways of handling non-constant volatility — reading the surface, making vol stochastic, and making vol local — and know which question each one is actually answering: what the market quotes today, how volatility might evolve, or how to fit today's surface exactly.
