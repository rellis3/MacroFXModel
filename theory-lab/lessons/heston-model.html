The Heston Model

Black-Scholes prices every option off one fixed \(\sigma\) forever — Heston's 1993 fix is to stop treating volatility as a constant at all and instead let it be its own random process, mean-reverting and correlated with price, the same way GARCH already treats it in discrete time.

CONCEPT — not yet built in this repo ~17 min read Prereqs: GARCH & Volatility Clustering, Black-Scholes-Merton Option Pricing
TL;DR
  • Heston (1993) replaces Black-Scholes' one constant \(\sigma\) with a second stochastic process \(v_t\) for the instantaneous variance, mean-reverting toward a long-run level \(\theta\) at speed \(\kappa\) with its own noise \(\xi\sqrt{v_t}\,dW_t^2\) — a CIR-type process.
  • The two Brownian motions driving price and variance are allowed to correlate (\(\rho\)); empirically \(\rho\) is usually negative, the "leverage effect" where volatility spikes when price falls.
  • That negative \(\rho\) is exactly what generates a realistic volatility smile/skew — it makes large down-moves disproportionately likely to coincide with a vol spike, so low-strike puts price with higher implied vol than at-the-money, something flat-\(\sigma\) Black-Scholes structurally cannot produce.
  • The variance SDE is the same OU "rubber band" mean-reversion this Theory Lab already covers, just pointed at variance instead of price — informally the continuous-time cousin of GARCH(1,1)'s discrete-time "variance depends on yesterday's shock and yesterday's forecast" recursion, already running in this repo's js/volBacktestEngine.js.
  • Prices come from a semi-closed-form solution via the model's characteristic function, not a simple algebraic formula — this repo has no options-pricing module of any kind, so this lesson is pure theory background.
Section 01

Why this matters

The Black-Scholes-Merton model prices an option by assuming the underlying follows geometric Brownian motion with one fixed volatility parameter, \(\sigma\), held constant for the life of the option. The GARCH lesson in this Theory Lab already spent an entire lesson establishing, empirically, that this is not how real markets behave: volatility clusters, it has its own memory, calm regimes and turbulent regimes persist and decay over time rather than sitting at one flat level. A GARCH(1,1) forecast for tomorrow's variance depends on yesterday's shock and yesterday's variance forecast — volatility is itself a process with dynamics, not a fixed input you look up once.

Black-Scholes, taken literally, has no room for that idea at all. Every application of the formula bakes in a single number and treats it as known and unchanging from now until expiry. That assumption is directly falsified by the same evidence the GARCH lesson covered — realized volatility visibly rises and falls over any option's life, sometimes dramatically, and options markets price this in: two options on the same underlying with different expiries or struck at different levels routinely trade at meaningfully different implied volatilities, something a single true constant \(\sigma\) could never produce.

Steven Heston's 1993 paper is, informally, GARCH's core insight — "variance is itself a stochastic process with its own mean-reverting dynamics" — carried across from discrete-time econometrics into the continuous-time stochastic calculus that option pricing runs on. Instead of one fixed \(\sigma\), Heston's model describes the underlying's instantaneous variance as a second random process running alongside the price, with its own drift, its own noise term, and — critically — a noise term that is allowed to be correlated with the noise driving price itself. That single added correlation parameter turns out to be exactly what's needed to generate a realistic volatility smile, something flat-\(\sigma\) Black-Scholes structurally cannot produce.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Weather that has its own weather

Black-Scholes treats volatility the way a very naive forecaster treats tomorrow's weather: "the average temperature this month is 20°C, so I'll just assume every single day is exactly 20°C." That's obviously wrong in a specific, structured way — some days are much hotter, some much colder, and crucially, hot spells and cold spells tend to cluster: a hot day is more likely to be followed by another hot day than by a random draw from the whole month's temperature distribution. That clustering is exactly the GARCH lesson's "volatility has memory" idea, applied to temperature instead of returns.

Heston's model says: fine, let's stop pretending the temperature is fixed. Let's build a second little model, running alongside the main one, whose entire job is to describe how "today's temperature" itself drifts and wiggles and reverts back toward a seasonal average over time — hot spells fade, cold spells fade, but there's real random weather-of-the-weather in between. Now bolt one more realistic detail on: on days the temperature is unusually low, storms (turbulence) tend to be more likely too — the two processes are not independent, they're correlated. That's the whole model: price has its usual random wiggle, but the size of that wiggle is itself randomly wiggling and mean-reverting, and it tends to get bigger exactly when price falls.

Section 02

The math, step by step

Recall plain Black-Scholes describes the underlying with one stochastic differential equation and a single constant \(\sigma\):

\[ dS_t = \mu S_t\,dt + \sigma S_t\,dW_t \]

Heston's model keeps the same basic shape for the price equation but replaces the constant \(\sigma\) with the square root of a second, time-varying, randomly evolving quantity \(v_t\) — the instantaneous variance:

\[ dS_t = \mu S_t\,dt + \sqrt{v_t}\,S_t\,dW_t^{1} \]
√v_treplaces the constant σ — now itself a random process
dW_t¹the Brownian shock driving price

That one change of notation — \(\sigma \to \sqrt{v_t}\) — is the entire conceptual leap. Volatility is no longer a parameter you plug in once; it is the square root of a variable that itself moves randomly through time and needs its own equation of motion. Heston supplies exactly that second equation, describing how \(v_t\) itself evolves:

\[ dv_t = \kappa(\theta - v_t)\,dt + \xi\sqrt{v_t}\,dW_t^{2} \]

Every symbol, spelled out:

\(S_t\)The underlying's price at time \(t\) — same role as in plain Black-Scholes.
\(\mu\)(mu) The price process's drift rate — same role as in plain Black-Scholes; under risk-neutral pricing this becomes the risk-free rate, exactly as in the Black-Scholes lesson.
\(v_t\)The instantaneous variance at time \(t\) — a second stochastic process, not a constant. \(\sqrt{v_t}\) plays the role plain \(\sigma\) played in Black-Scholes, except it is now itself random and time-varying rather than fixed for the life of the option.
\(dW_t^{1}\)The Wiener increment driving the price process — the same object from the OU and Black-Scholes lessons: mean zero, variance \(dt\).
\(dW_t^{2}\)A second, separate Wiener increment driving the variance process. Separate from \(dW_t^1\) in the sense of being its own source of randomness, but — see \(\rho\) below — not independent of it.
\(\kappa\)(kappa) The speed of mean reversion of variance — how fast \(v_t\) gets pulled back toward its long-run level after a shock. Plays exactly the role \(\theta\) (the reversion-speed parameter, sometimes written \(\kappa\) or \(\alpha\) depending on notation) played in the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck lesson, just applied to variance instead of price.
\(\theta\)(theta) The long-run (unconditional) variance that \(v_t\) reverts toward — the variance-process equivalent of \(\mu\), the long-run mean level, in the OU lesson. Note this \(\theta\) is a long-run-variance level, unrelated to the option-Greek \(\theta\) (time decay) from the Black-Scholes lesson — an unfortunate but standard notation collision in the literature.
\(\xi\)(xi, sometimes written \(\sigma_v\) in other sources) The volatility of variance ("vol of vol") — how large the random wiggles in \(v_t\) itself are. A larger \(\xi\) means variance itself swings around more violently rather than reverting smoothly toward \(\theta\).
\(\sqrt{v_t}\) (in the variance SDE)The variance process's own diffusion term is scaled by \(\sqrt{v_t}\), not a constant — this is a square-root (CIR-type) diffusion, which keeps \(v_t\) non-negative (variance can't legitimately go negative) and makes the noise shrink toward zero as \(v_t\) approaches zero, unlike a plain OU process whose noise term is a flat constant regardless of the current level.
\(\rho\)(rho) The correlation between the price shock \(dW_t^1\) and the variance shock \(dW_t^2\). This is the parameter with no Black-Scholes or plain-OU analogue at all, and it is what makes the model capable of generating a realistic smile/skew — see below.

The variance process is an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process — applied to variance instead of price

It is worth being explicit about how directly this connects to the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck lesson already in this Theory Lab. Strip the square-root-scaled noise term off the variance SDE for a moment and look at the drift alone:

\[ dv_t = \kappa(\theta - v_t)\,dt + (\text{noise}) \]

Compare this to the OU process from that lesson, written with its own reversion-speed and long-run-mean parameters:

\[ dX_t = \kappa(\mu - X_t)\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t \]
κ(μ−X_t)same "rubber band" pull toward a long-run mean μ
X_there a generic price/spread — variance is just a different X_t

The structure is identical: a term that pulls the process back toward a long-run level, at a rate proportional to how far away it currently is. When \(v_t > \theta\) (variance is currently elevated), the drift term \(\kappa(\theta - v_t)\) is negative, pulling \(v_t\) back down; when \(v_t < \theta\), the drift is positive, pulling it back up. This is exactly the "rubber band" intuition the OU lesson built for mean-reverting price spreads, applied here to a completely different quantity — variance instead of price or a price spread. Heston's only structural addition on top of plain OU is the \(\sqrt{v_t}\) scaling on the noise term (so variance can never diffuse into negative territory, unlike an unconstrained OU process, which technically can wander arbitrarily low) — this specific variant of a mean-reverting square-root diffusion is called a CIR process (Cox-Ingersoll-Ross, originally built for interest rates, another quantity that shouldn't go negative). The headline takeaway: Heston did not invent a new kind of randomness for variance — it reuses the OU-style mean-reversion story this Theory Lab already covers, just pointed at a different variable, with one technical tweak to keep that variable non-negative.

Why the correlation \(\rho\) is the whole story behind the smile

In equity and FX markets, empirically estimated \(\rho\) is very often negative. This has a well-known name: the leverage effect — the tendency for volatility to rise precisely when prices fall (originally explained, for equities, by a falling stock price mechanically raising a firm's leverage ratio and therefore its equity-return volatility; the empirical pattern is observed far more broadly than that original story, including in FX, where there is no literal corporate leverage mechanism, but the same "down move, vol spike" co-movement shows up in the data regardless).

A negative \(\rho\) means the shock that pushes price down and the shock that pushes variance up are correlated — when \(dW_t^1\) draws a large negative value (price falls), \(dW_t^2\) tends to also draw a positive value (variance rises) more often than chance alone would produce. The practical consequence for the shape of the return distribution: large down-moves are disproportionately likely to be accompanied by a simultaneous vol spike, which fattens the left tail of the return distribution specifically — a different distributional distortion, from a different mechanism, than the Merton jump-diffusion lesson's fat tails (there, a discontinuous jump process injects rare large moves directly; here, a continuous but correlated stochastic-variance process makes ordinary diffusion itself wider exactly when price is falling) — but the observable symptom, a heavier left tail than a symmetric normal distribution would predict, rhymes with that earlier lesson's discussion from a different angle entirely.

This is the mechanism that lets Heston generate a genuine volatility smile, or more precisely in equity/FX markets a skew (asymmetric smile): because down-moves and vol-spikes tend to co-occur under negative \(\rho\), the model assigns more probability to a large downside move than a symmetric, zero-correlation model would — which is exactly the scenario that makes low-strike (out-of-the-money put) options worth more than flat-\(\sigma\) Black-Scholes would say. Back out the implied volatility that would make Black-Scholes match Heston's (correlation-aware) price at each strike, and low strikes come out with higher implied volatility than at-the-money — a skew, generated purely from model specification, with no jump term anywhere in the equations. Set \(\rho = 0\) and \(\xi \to 0\) (no correlation, no vol-of-vol) and the model collapses back toward flat Black-Scholes, with no smile at all — the smile is not free, it is earned specifically by \(\xi \neq 0\) (variance actually moves) combined with \(\rho \neq 0\) (that movement is linked to price direction).

Black-Scholes assumes one flat \(\sigma\) for every strike — the dashed line, by construction perfectly horizontal, because the model has no parameter that could make it slope. The solid curve is the shape typically implied when the true underlying process is Heston with a negative \(\rho\): implied volatility is higher for low strikes (out-of-the-money puts) than for at-the-money or high-strike options, because the negative correlation between price and variance shocks makes a large down-move disproportionately likely to coincide with a vol spike, fattening the left side of the return distribution relative to the right. This is a stylized illustration of the shape, not a fitted curve from real market data or real Heston parameters.

The pricing formula — semi-closed-form, not derived here

Having two correlated stochastic processes instead of one constant makes the option-pricing problem considerably harder than plain Black-Scholes. Heston's genuinely important 1993 contribution was proving that, despite this added complexity, European option prices under this model still admit a semi-closed-form solution — not a simple algebraic formula like Black-Scholes, but an expression built from the model's characteristic function (the Fourier transform of the underlying's log-price distribution), which can be evaluated by numerically integrating that characteristic function rather than by Monte Carlo simulation or a full partial-differential-equation solve. This is what "semi-closed-form" means here: there is a precise formula, but it requires a numerical Fourier/characteristic-function step to evaluate, unlike Black-Scholes' fully closed-form cumulative-normal expression.

That derivation — solving the associated pricing PDE via a characteristic-function ansatz, handling the branch-cut numerical subtleties that later research found in naive implementations — is genuinely technical and is not derived here. It is not needed to understand what the model is, why it exists, or why it can generate a smile. The point of this lesson is the model specification — two correlated SDEs, mean-reverting variance, the mechanism connecting \(\rho\) to skew — not reproducing the closed-form pricing machinery, which is a substantial piece of applied mathematics in its own right and belongs in a dedicated numerical-methods treatment, not a conceptual model-specification lesson.

🎯
Real-world trading scenario

Why out-of-the-money puts are structurally more expensive than a flat-σ model says they should be

An options desk pulls up an implied-volatility curve for a given expiry and notices what shows up on almost every equity index and most major FX pairs, almost all of the time: out-of-the-money puts (low strikes) are trading at a meaningfully higher implied volatility than at-the-money options. A trader reasoning purely from flat-\(\sigma\) Black-Scholes has no way to make sense of this at all — that model has exactly one \(\sigma\) per underlying and expiry, and it is supposed to price every strike consistently off that same number. A flat-\(\sigma\) world literally cannot produce a sloped implied-vol curve; if the market shows one, either the model is misspecified or there is something the flat-\(\sigma\) story is missing.

A Heston-consistent explanation is available, and it does not require assuming the market is "wrong" or mispriced: volatility itself is stochastic, and it tends to spike exactly when prices fall (negative \(\rho\), the leverage effect). That means a big down-move is more likely to arrive together with a volatility explosion than a symmetric up-move of the same size is — the joint event "price falls a lot AND volatility spikes" is more probable under Heston's correlated dynamics than the joint event "price rises a lot AND volatility spikes." An out-of-the-money put only pays off in exactly that first, more likely scenario. Priced honestly under a process where that scenario has elevated probability, the put is worth more than a symmetric, zero-correlation (or flat-\(\sigma\)) model would say — and that is precisely what shows up as a persistently higher implied volatility at low strikes.

The desk's takeaway is not "puts are overpriced, sell them" — it's the opposite: this is a structural feature of how price and volatility actually co-move, not a mispricing to be arbitraged away. Downside protection commanding a persistent premium (sometimes called the "volatility risk premium" when discussed in the context of systematically selling that protection) is a market correctly reflecting a real statistical asymmetry, not a free lunch — and any strategy built to harvest that premium is taking on exactly the tail risk the premium compensates for, which is a separate question from whether the premium exists.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

Heston is one of the most widely implemented stochastic-volatility models in production option-pricing systems, precisely because the semi-closed-form solution makes calibration to an observed implied-volatility surface fast enough to run repeatedly through a trading day. Desks fit \((\kappa, \theta, \xi, \rho, v_0)\) to match observed option prices across strikes and expiries, then use the calibrated model to price and hedge options that aren't directly quoted in the market, and to compute model-consistent Greeks that account for the fact that volatility itself moves (an additional "vega-like" sensitivity to changes in \(v_t\), on top of the Black-Scholes Greeks).

Heston is not the last word, and real desks are honest that no single stochastic-volatility model perfectly reproduces every observed smile at every expiry simultaneously. Two directions extend it further, and this lesson names them without deriving either: stochastic-vol-plus-jumps models (sometimes called Bates' model, which is literally Heston's two SDEs with a Merton-style jump term from the previous lesson added on top of the price process) combine both smile-generating mechanisms — continuous stochastic volatility for the "shape" of the smile and discontinuous jumps for extra short-dated tail risk that pure diffusion, however correlated, still can't fully capture at very short expiries. And local-volatility and local-stochastic-volatility models take a more purely empirical route, calibrating a volatility surface directly to match observed option prices exactly at a point in time, at the cost of less clean forward-looking dynamics than Heston's parametric SDEs. Which of these a given desk reaches for is a practical trade-off between fit quality, calibration speed, and how the model needs to behave for hedging purposes — not a claim that any one of them is universally "correct."

Continuous-time cousin of the GARCH sigma this repo already runs — and no options module to connect it to

Say this plainly up front: this repo has no options-pricing module of any kind — no Black-Scholes implementation, no Heston implementation, no implied-volatility surface, nothing that prices a derivative contract. This lesson is pure theory background, exactly like the Black-Scholes and Merton jump-diffusion lessons before it. Nothing here describes existing code, and nothing here proposes building an options desk inside this repo.

What the repo does already have, built and running in production, is the discrete-time cousin of exactly the insight Heston's variance SDE formalizes. Per CLAUDE.md's core-modules table, js/volBacktestEngine.js implements GARCH(1,1) as one of three sigma estimators (alongside HV20 and Yang-Zhang) feeding the live forecaster's bands, with the recursion \(v \leftarrow \omega + \alpha\,r_{i-1}^2 + \beta\,v,\ \sigma_i=\sqrt v\) and fitted constants \(\alpha=0.06,\ \beta=0.91\) (a persistence of 0.97, roughly a 23-trading-day half-life). That recursion is discrete-time variance with its own memory — today's variance forecast depends on yesterday's shock and yesterday's own forecast. Heston's \(dv_t=\kappa(\theta-v_t)dt+\xi\sqrt{v_t}\,dW_t^2\) is, informally, the same idea — variance reverting toward a long-run level with its own source of randomness — expressed as a continuous-time SDE instead of a discrete daily recursion. Neither engine copies the other's code, and they are not claimed to be mathematically equivalent (GARCH is discrete and this repo's GARCH has no explicit correlation-with-price term the way Heston's \(\rho\) does); the connection is conceptual — "let variance itself be a process with memory and dynamics" is the shared insight, one lesson's discrete-time home and the other's continuous-time home.

The Merton jump-diffusion lesson is the other half of this repo's theory coverage of the smile: that lesson covers discontinuous jumps as one way Black-Scholes gets extended to explain a real smile; this lesson covers correlated stochastic volatility as a different mechanism producing the same observable symptom. Real practitioner models often combine both (the "stochastic-vol-plus-jumps" family mentioned above) rather than picking one mechanism as the single true explanation — this lesson does not take a position on which mechanism, or which combination, would best describe any specific FX pair's real smile, because that would require fitting real option data this repo does not currently source or store.

Building an actual Heston calibration engine here would be new, unvalidated work requiring a characteristic-function pricer, a calibration routine against a real implied-vol surface this repo does not currently have a data source for, and — per the Lego Principle — a concrete reason at least two callers would use it. Nothing here proposes that; this is theory background connecting an existing, tested brick (GARCH in volBacktestEngine.js) to the wider stochastic- volatility literature it belongs to.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where Heston-style thinking is misused or overextended

Treating calibrated parameters as physically meaningful, time-invariant constants. \((\kappa,\theta,\xi,\rho)\) fitted to today's option surface are a snapshot, not physical laws — refitting the same model a week later, especially after a regime shift, routinely produces materially different parameter values. Don't treat one calibration as a permanent description of "how this underlying's volatility works."

The Feller condition and parameter admissibility. The CIR-type variance process technically requires \(2\kappa\theta \geq \xi^2\) (the Feller condition) to guarantee \(v_t\) never hits exactly zero; many real calibrations to option data land outside this region in practice, which has real numerical and interpretive consequences that a careless implementation can miss entirely.

A single correlation \(\rho\) is a simplification, not a complete description of the price-vol relationship. Real markets can show a more complex, regime-dependent relationship between price moves and volatility than one constant correlation coefficient captures — the leverage effect's strength itself is not obviously constant across calm and crisis regimes.

Semi-closed-form is not the same as fast in every implementation. Naive numerical implementations of the Heston characteristic-function integral are known to hit branch-cut discontinuities in the complex logarithm that produce silently wrong prices for some parameter combinations — a well-documented practical trap in the numerical-methods literature, and exactly the kind of subtlety this lesson flags but does not attempt to resolve, since deriving or debugging that integral is out of scope here.

A good smile fit does not validate a trading edge. Calibrating Heston to match today's observed option prices is a curve-fitting exercise by construction — it will fit reasonably well almost by definition, since that is what calibration optimizes for. It says nothing, by itself, about whether a strategy built on top of that calibration (e.g. trading realized vs. implied variance, or a volatility-risk-premium harvesting strategy) would show a real edge out-of-sample. Per this repo's validation discipline, that would need to be tested on data the calibration didn't see, not assumed from a tight in-sample fit.

Section 05

Self-test

Q1. Why can constant-σ Black-Scholes never generate a volatility smile at all, structurally, regardless of what σ value is chosen?
Because the model has exactly one free parameter, \(\sigma\), and it is applied identically to every strike and every expiry for a given underlying — there is no mechanism in the model by which the "correct" \(\sigma\) could differ by strike. Backing out an implied volatility from market prices at different strikes and finding they differ is, by construction, evidence that the true underlying process is not flat-\(\sigma\) geometric Brownian motion; it is not something any choice of a single \(\sigma\) could reproduce, no matter how it's chosen.
Q2. What roles do κ and θ play in Heston's variance SDE, and how do they map onto the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck lesson's parameters?
\(\kappa\) is the speed of mean reversion — how quickly \(v_t\) gets pulled back toward its long-run level after a shock, the same role played by the reversion-speed parameter in the OU lesson. \(\theta\) is the long-run (unconditional) variance level that \(v_t\) reverts toward — the variance-process analogue of the OU lesson's long-run mean \(\mu\). The drift term \(\kappa(\theta - v_t)\) is structurally identical to the OU process's \(\kappa(\mu - X_t)\) drift, just applied to variance instead of price: when variance is above its long-run level the drift pulls it down, and vice versa.
Q3. What does a negative correlation ρ between the price process's Brownian motion and the variance process's Brownian motion actually produce, and what is this phenomenon usually called?
A negative \(\rho\) means the random shock pushing price down and the random shock pushing variance up tend to occur together more often than chance alone would predict. This is called the leverage effect — the empirical tendency for volatility to rise when prices fall. The consequence for the return distribution is a fatter left tail than a symmetric (zero-correlation) model would produce, because large down-moves are disproportionately likely to coincide with a volatility spike.
Q4. Why does negative ρ specifically make out-of-the-money puts command higher implied volatility than at-the-money options, rather than just making the whole curve shift up uniformly?
An out-of-the-money put only pays off in the specific scenario of a large downside move. Negative \(\rho\) raises the probability of exactly that scenario — a large downside move co-occurring with a volatility spike — relative to a symmetric, zero-correlation model, without proportionally raising the probability of a large upside move co-occurring with a vol spike. Because the effect is concentrated in the downside-move scenario specifically, it raises the fair value (and hence the backed-out implied volatility) of low-strike puts more than it does at-the-money or high-strike options, producing a sloped skew rather than a uniform upward shift of the whole curve.
Q5. Heston's actual option-pricing formula is described as "semi-closed-form." What does that mean, and why doesn't this lesson derive it?
It means there is a precise pricing expression, but evaluating it requires numerically integrating the model's characteristic function (its Fourier transform), rather than plugging into a simple algebraic formula the way Black-Scholes' cumulative-normal expression works. Deriving that characteristic-function solution and handling its known numerical subtleties (branch-cut issues in the complex logarithm) is substantial applied mathematics in its own right. This lesson's goal is understanding the model specification — the two correlated SDEs, the mean-reverting variance, why ρ produces a smile — not reproducing the closed-form pricing machinery, which is explicitly out of scope here.
Q6. Does this repo have an options-pricing module or a Heston implementation? What does it have instead that connects to this lesson's theory, and how exactly are the two related?
No — this repo has no options-pricing module of any kind, and this lesson is pure theory background, not a description of existing code. What it does have is GARCH(1,1) as one of three sigma estimators in js/volBacktestEngine.js, which treats variance as a process with its own memory (today's forecast depends on yesterday's shock and yesterday's forecast) in discrete time. Heston's variance SDE formalizes essentially the same insight — mean-reverting variance with its own source of randomness — in continuous time, with the added feature (not present in this repo's plain GARCH(1,1)) of an explicit correlation between the variance shocks and the price shocks. The two are conceptual cousins sharing the same core idea, not the same model and not code that has been ported between them.
