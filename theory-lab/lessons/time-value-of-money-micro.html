Deeper Foundations 4

Why a Dollar Today Beats a Dollar Tomorrow

Every discount factor, every present-value calculation, and the mysterious r sitting inside Black-Scholes's Ke^(−rT) term all trace back to one four-hundred-year-old idea: money has a price, and moving it across time changes its value in a way you can compute exactly. This is where that idea gets built from nothing — compounding forward, discounting backward, and the two places a risk-free rate quietly reappears everywhere else in this curriculum.

Section 1

A dollar today can go to work. A dollar next year can't — yet

Someone offers you $100 now, or a guaranteed $100 exactly one year from today. Take the $100 now.

Not out of impatience — because of what you can do with it in the meantime. Deposit it at 5% and in a year it's become $105. Whoever waited for their $100 is now $5 poorer than you, for making an economically identical promise a year earlier. "A dollar today is worth more than a dollar tomorrow" is just this: today's dollar can be lent, invested, or deposited, and interest is the price the world pays you for the use of it while you wait.

⏱

Opportunity cost, not psychology

The gap isn't "a bird in hand" folk wisdom — it's a specific, computable amount: whatever the money could have earned elsewhere while you waited.

r

A risk-free rate must exist

The moment "wait or invest" is a real choice, there has to be some rate r that prices the waiting. Every later formula's r is this same rate, wearing different clothes.

↺

Run it backward, get discounting

If $100 today grows to $105 in a year, then $105 arriving in a year must be worth exactly $100 today. That flip is the entire rest of this lesson.

This isn't a saying — it's an arithmetic fact with a precise formula. That formula is where the risk-free rate in Black-Scholes comes from, and it's the entire foundation forward FX rates are built on.
Section 2

Future value: interest earning interest

Watch what happens to a balance of PV over two periods at rate r.

The compounding chain

Period 1: the balance grows by r — the original PV, plus its interest.

PV(1+r)²
Period 2: the whole balance from period 1 gets multiplied again — including the interest period 1 already added. That's the entire meaning of "compounding": interest earns interest.
Repeat this t times and each period multiplies the running balance by another factor of (1+r): FV = PV(1+r)ᵗ. Exponential in t, not linear — that's what separates compound interest from simple interest PV(1+rt).

$1,000 growing at a fixed 6% a year doesn't just add $60 every year — it curves upward, because each year's interest is bigger than the last. That curve, not a straight line, is the visual signature of compounding.

Section 3

Present value: running the same formula backward

Divide both sides of FV = PV(1+r)ᵗ by (1+r)ᵗ and PV falls out — discounting is nothing new.

FV
÷ (1+r)ᵗ
PV

The multiplier FV gets multiplied by here, DF(r,t) = 1/(1+r)ᵗ, is the discount factor — the answer to "what is one dollar, arriving t periods from now, worth today?" Multiply any future cash flow by the discount factor for its arrival date and you get its present value. That's the entire operation underneath every bond price and every Black-Scholes Ke^(−rT) term.

Low rate (r = 3%) High rate (r = 10%)
Always strictly between 0 and 1

If r>0, then (1+r)>1, and raising a number bigger than 1 to a positive power keeps it bigger than 1 — so dividing 1 by it always lands strictly between 0 and 1. As t→0, DF→1 (a dollar due right now needs no discounting). As t→∞, DF→0 (a dollar arbitrarily far away is worth arbitrarily little today, however large it nominally is) — the higher r is, the faster that decay happens.

The limit every row above is creeping toward. This is why textbook formulas — Black-Scholes included — default to continuous compounding: it's the clean, closed-form case that real-world compounding already approximates closely at any ordinary frequency.

Taking the compounding frequency n to infinity invokes the same limit that defines the constant e itself: lim(1+r/n)ⁿ = e^r as n→∞, so FV = PV·e^(rt).

Section 5

A continuously-compounded rate is a log return in disguise

Take logs of both sides of FV = PV·e^(rt).

FV = PV·e^(rt)
take ln( )
ln(FV/PV) = rt

A log return over any horizon is defined as ln(P_end / P_start). The left side above, ln(FV/PV), is exactly that, applied to a risk-free deposit growing from PV to FV. The right side, rt, says that log return accrues at a constant rate r per unit time.

This is not an analogy — a continuously-compounded interest rate is a log return per unit time, the identical mathematical object. That's also why continuously-compounded rates are additive across time with no cross term: two years at a continuous 5% gives exactly the same growth as year one at 5% plus year two at 5%, the same log-additivity that comes from ln(ab) = ln a + ln b.
Worked check

$1,000 at 6% for 5 years: annually compounded, FV = 1000×(1.06)⁵ ≈ $1,338.23. Continuously compounded, FV = 1000×e^(0.06×5) = 1000×e^0.3 ≈ $1,349.86. An $11.63 gap from the identical stated rate — purely because continuous compounding lets interest start earning interest immediately, with no full-year wait between compounding dates.

Section 7

Where r comes from inside Black-Scholes

The Black-Scholes formula contains a term Ke^(−rT). This lesson is the entire reason it's there.

What the term computes

Ke^(−rT) is the present value of the strike price K, potentially payable T years from now, discounted using the continuous-compounding discount factor at the risk-free rate r. Same PV = FV × DF(r,t) from Section 6, with FV = K and t = T.

Why some version of it must appear

Comparing a dollar amount due in the future to a price quoted today, without first converting them to the same point in time, is comparing two different kinds of quantities as if they were equal — the exact "$100 now vs. $100 in a year" mistake from Section 1, embedded inside a longer formula.

None of this requires forecasting where markets are going. Like the rest of risk-neutral pricing, discounting is a mechanical, no-forecast operation on a known future amount and a quoted rate. r shows up in Black-Scholes because you cannot price a future payoff correctly without first moving it to today's terms — not because the model has an opinion about direction.
Section 8

Where r comes from inside an FX forward

Two currencies, two interest rates — the forward rate has to reflect the gap, or a riskless trade prints free money.

USD/JPY spot is 150.00. USD deposits pay 5%, JPY deposits pay 0.1%. A trader with zero directional view still needs a fair 3-month forward. It isn't 150.00: borrow JPY (cheap), convert to USD at spot, invest at 5%, convert back at a forward equal to spot, and you'd pocket riskless profit funded purely by the rate gap. The market closes that gap by pricing the forward below spot instead — covered interest rate parity.

Section 9

Not a forecast — and not optional either

💵

Bond pricing

Sum of coupon and principal cash flows, each individually discounted back to today by the discount factor for its payment date.

🏗

Capital project NPV

Projected future cash flows discounted the same way, minus the upfront cost.

📈

The yield curve

Nothing more than a table of discount factors — or the rates that produce them — at different maturities, extracted from observed bond prices.

The trap

A forward trading below spot is not a market call that a currency will weaken. It's the mechanical PV/FV adjustment that keeps borrowing the low-rate currency to fund the high-rate one from being free money — nothing more, nothing less.

Small rate gaps compound into large gaps over long horizons: at 3 years, 3% and 10% discount curves are still fairly close (0.914 vs. 0.741); by 20 years they've diverged to 0.549 vs. 0.135 — a 4x gap that started as 7 percentage points. Long-dated cash flows — a 30-year bond, a pension liability, a long-dated FX forward — are far more sensitive to the discount rate than short-dated ones, purely because (1+r)ᵗ is exponential in t.

Section 10

Quick check

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Why is FV = PV(1+r)ᵗ, rather than the straight-line PV(1+rt)?

PV(1+r)ᵗ lets each period's interest join the balance and earn interest itself. PV(1+rt) — simple interest — only ever pays r on the original PV, so it grows linearly and understates true growth for t > 1 period.
Question 2

A discount factor DF(r,t) = 1/(1+r)ᵗ, with r > 0 and t > 0, must be:

r>0 makes (1+r)>1, and raising it to a positive power keeps it >1 — so its reciprocal must lie strictly between 0 and 1.
Question 3

ln(FV/PV) = rt, from FV = PV·e^(rt). What does this say about a continuously-compounded rate r?

Taking logs of FV=PV·e^(rt) gives ln(FV/PV)=rt exactly. A log return is defined as ln(end/start), so rt is precisely that log return — meaning r is a log return per unit time, not merely similar to one.
Question 4

In Black-Scholes, the term Ke^(−rT) computes:

Ke^(−rT) is PV = FV × DF(r,t) with FV=K and t=T — the exact discounting machinery from Section 6, applied to a strike price.
Question 5

USD pays 5%, JPY pays 0.1%, spot is 150.00. Why does the 3-month forward trade below 150.00?

Covered interest rate parity, not a forecast: pricing the forward equal to spot would let a trader borrow cheap JPY, invest in USD at 5%, and convert back for a riskless profit. The forward moves below spot exactly far enough to close that gap.
Section 11

What to carry forward

01

FV = PV(1+r)ᵗ is compounding — interest earning interest — which is why growth is exponential in t, not the linear PV(1+rt) of simple interest.

02

Present value just runs future value backward. The discount factor DF(r,t) = 1/(1+r)ᵗ is always strictly between 0 and 1 whenever r,t > 0.

03

Continuous compounding, FV = PV·e^(rt), is the clean limit as compounding frequency goes to infinity — and daily compounding already sits within a thousandth of it.

04

Taking logs shows ln(FV/PV) = rt — a continuously-compounded rate is a log return per unit time, not an analogy to one.

05

The risk-free rate shows up in Black-Scholes (Ke^(−rT), discounting a strike) and in FX forward points (covered interest rate parity, discounting two currencies against each other) for the exact same reason: a future amount and a today amount can't be compared until one is moved to the other's point in time.

What this now lets you do

Compute a present or future value by hand, at any compounding frequency. Recognise Ke^(−rT) on sight as "discount the strike," not a mysterious model term. Read a forward discount or premium as mechanical no-arbitrage pricing, never as a directional market view.
