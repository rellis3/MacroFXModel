Lesson 4 · Time Series & Regimes

Mean Reversion, Given an Actual Speed

Every trader has a gut feeling about whether a price that's strayed from "normal" tends to come back. The Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process turns that gut feeling into an equation — one with a speed parameter you can fit from real data and convert into a half-life measured in days, not a vibe you'd hesitate to say out loud to a non-quant.

Section 1

A rubber band attached to a moving average

The picture

Picture a ball tied by a rubber band to a fixed post, sitting in a tub of water that's constantly jostled by random currents. Two things act on the ball every instant: the band pulls it back toward the post, harder the further away it currently is, while the jostling shoves it in an unpredictable direction by an unpredictable amount. The ball never sits still — the jostling keeps knocking it away — but it never wanders off to infinity either, because the band always drags it back.

⚑

The post — μ

The long-run mean: the level a price, spread, or z-score is being pulled toward.

↺

The band's stiffness — θ

The mean-reversion speed. A bigger θ is a stiffer band — the price snaps back faster.

≈

The jostling — σ

Random noise, the same volatility concept used everywhere else in this curriculum, just jostling the ball instead of the price.

A plain random walk — the model behind "prices have no memory" — is what's left if you cut the rubber band entirely: θ = 0, nothing pulls the ball back, and it really can wander off forever.

The equation: drift plus diffusion

dXₜ = θ(μ − Xₜ)dt + σdWₜ. Read left to right: the next tiny move is a predictable nudge back toward μ, plus an unpredictable random shock.

Drift (amber) is the predictable restoring force. Diffusion (blue) is pure random noise. Add them and you get the next instant's move (green).
Xₜ

The process's value at time t

"Today's reading" — a price, a spread, or a z-scored deviation from a moving average. OU wants something with a plausible stable center, not a raw trending price.

μ−Xₜ

The current deviation, signed

Above μ, this term is negative and pulls down; below μ, it's positive and pulls up. Structurally identical to Hooke's law for a spring.

θ

Mean-reversion speed

A positive number with units of "1/time." Larger θ = faster reversion. θ = 0 removes the pull entirely.

σdWₜ

The diffusion term

Wₜ is a Wiener process — pure random-walk noise, mean zero, no memory. σ scales how large the shocks are.

Section 3

Cut the band and you get a random walk

A random walk isn't a different model — it's this exact equation with θ set to zero.

OU process — θ > 0

Drift pulls back toward μ, diffusion adds noise. Variance stays bounded over time — the process settles into a stable, stationary distribution around μ.

Random walk — θ = 0

Diffusion only, no drift — nothing "knows" where μ is. Variance grows without bound as t→∞; the process can wander arbitrarily far from any starting point.

The resting spread — σ²/2θ

Given long enough, an OU process settles into a stable normal distribution centered at μ with variance σ²/2θ. More noise (σ) widens that resting spread; a stiffer pull-back (θ) tightens it — so "loud" (high σ) and "fast-reverting" (high θ) are not opposites. A process can be both, and the two combine into how wide its typical range actually is.

Section 5

Turning θ into a number you can say out loud

Ignore the noise for a moment — in expectation it averages to zero — and solve the drift-only decay for how long a deviation takes to halve.

The derivation
Expected deviation decays exponentially: E[Xₜ − μ] = (X₀ − μ)·e−θt
Set the decay factor to one half: e−θ·t½ = 1/2
Take logs of both sides: −θ·t½ = ln(1/2) = −ln(2)
Divide both sides by −θ: t½ = ln(2) / θ

ln(2) ≈ 0.693 is just a constant that falls out of the algebra — the same constant that shows up in radioactive-decay half-life formulas, applied here to a price gap instead of atoms. A half-life isn't "the time until the deviation is fully gone" — under continuous exponential decay it's never exactly zero, only asymptotically closer. It's "the time until you'd expect today's gap to μ to be cut in half."

A short half-life (say, 1 day) is a snappy series where a deviation today is mostly gone within a couple of days. A long half-life (say, 60 days) is technically still mean-reverting, but far too slow to trade on a multi-day horizon — by the time it pulls back, the world has moved on. As θ→0, t½→∞, consistent with the random-walk limit having no reversion at all.
Section 6

Fitting this from daily bars: the AR(1) twin

You almost never fit the continuous-time SDE directly. You fit its discrete-time analogue, AR(1), then convert.

Xₜ = μ(1−φ) + φXₜ₋₁ + εₜ
where
φ = e^(−θΔt)

φ is the ordinary "regress today's value on yesterday's value" coefficient from a linear regression — the discrete-time stand-in for the drift/diffusion split above. Inverting the relationship gives θ from a fitted φ:

θ = −ln(φ) / Δt
→1

φ close to 1

Today is almost entirely explained by yesterday — weak pull-back, small θ, long half-life.

→0

φ close to 0

Almost no persistence — the series snaps back to μ almost immediately, large θ, short half-life.

⚠

φ ≥ 1

Not mean-reverting at all — φ = 1 is exactly the random walk, φ > 1 is explosive. ln(φ) is undefined or non-negative here, which is the algebra's way of saying the OU model doesn't apply.

Section 8

Worked example: two coefficients, two very different trades

Same regression, same daily data, two hypothetical fits.

φ = 0.90

θ = −ln(0.90)/1 ≈ 0.1054/day. t½ = ln(2)/0.1054 ≈ 6.58 days. A spread 40 pips from its mean would be expected near 20 pips after ~6–7 days, ~10 pips after another ~6–7 days.

φ = 0.98

θ = −ln(0.98)/1 ≈ 0.0202/day. t½ = ln(2)/0.0202 ≈ 34.3 days. Still technically mean-reverting — but far too sluggish to be the basis of a multi-day fade trade.

Both series pass a basic "is φ < 1" screen. Only one of them reverts on a timescale worth trading. This is exactly why the half-life, not the raw coefficient, is the number worth carrying into a sizing or holding-period decision — 0.90 and 0.98 look close as coefficients but describe holding periods roughly five times apart.

Section 9

What a half-life is actually used for

↔

Pairs trading / stat-arb entry & exit

Fit OU to a spread between two related instruments. The stationary standard deviation √(σ²/2θ) gives entry/exit thresholds the same way a z-score does — enter a few stationary-sds from μ, exit near μ.

⏱

Half-life as a horizon filter

If a signal's half-life is 2 days, holding for 3 weeks fights the wrong timescale, and exiting in 10 minutes never lets the edge play out. Half-life is a principled way to pick a holding period instead of guessing it.

🔎

A diagnostic, not just a target

Fitting OU and getting φ ≥ 1 is a useful, humbling null result — evidence the series behaves like a random walk rather than something to explain away.

Where this stands in this repo, honestly

This project's own same-day trend-day-ness score, T = drift ÷ diffusion (blended from rolling efficiency-ratio, variance-ratio, Hurst, and similar estimators), already answers a version of "does this tend to revert" heuristically — but it doesn't produce a number you can say out loud as "reverts halfway every N days." OU is the textbook model that does. It is a research concept here, not yet fit or tested against real data in this codebase — any real use would need the same in-sample/out-of-sample validation and minimum trade count this repo already requires elsewhere, plus a bias-corrected φ estimate rather than a single point fit, since small, noisy samples bias φ toward 1.

Section 10

Quick check

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

As θ → 0, what happens to the OU process and its half-life?

The drift term θ(μ−Xₜ)dt shrinks toward zero regardless of the current deviation — the "band" goes slack, leaving dXₜ = σdWₜ, pure noise. And t½ = ln(2)/θ blows up as θ→0: dividing by a number approaching zero.
Question 2

An AR(1) fit on daily data returns φ = 1.02. What should a careful analyst conclude?

φ > 1 makes ln(φ) positive, so θ = −ln(φ)/Δt is negative — nonsensical in the OU model (it would mean the "restoring force" pushes away from μ). The formula breaking down is the algebra telling you to stop, not a cue to force a number.
Question 3

A daily AR(1) fit gives φ = 0.90. Closest half-life?

θ = −ln(0.90)/1 ≈ 0.1054/day, and t½ = ln(2)/0.1054 ≈ 6.58 days. (≈34 days is what φ = 0.98 gives instead.)
Question 4

A spread has an estimated half-life of 3 days. What does that NOT tell a trader?

Half-life describes the expected path, not a guarantee. The diffusion term σdWₜ means any individual path can widen before it narrows, or fail to revert within a reasonable window at all — and it says nothing about μ itself possibly drifting during the trade.
Question 5

Why does a bigger θ mean a SMALLER stationary variance, σ²/2θ?

θ sits in the denominator: a stronger restoring force corrects deviations faster, so the process doesn't wander as far from μ before being pulled back in, even with the same-sized noise hitting it every instant.
Section 11

What to carry forward

01

OU is drift plus diffusion: dXₜ = θ(μ−Xₜ)dt + σdWₜ — a predictable pull toward μ at speed θ, plus noise scaled by σ.

02

A random walk is the special case θ = 0 — cut the rubber band and nothing pulls the process back toward any mean.

03

Half-life turns θ into a number you can say out loud: t½ = ln(2)/θ, the time for an expected deviation to shrink by half.

04

Fit it via AR(1), not the raw SDE — regress Xₜ on Xₜ₋₁ to get φ, then convert with θ = −ln(φ)/Δt.

05

φ ≥ 1 means the model doesn't apply, not "very slow reversion" — the half-life formula breaks down on purpose.

06

A half-life describes the expected path, not a promise for any one trade — real θ, μ, and σ drift, and small samples bias φ toward 1.

What this now lets you do

Read a fitted AR(1) coefficient and immediately state a half-life in days, not just "it's mean-reverting." Recognise the moment φ ≥ 1 disqualifies a series from OU-style reversion trades, instead of forcing a number out of it. Size a holding period or an entry/exit threshold around a real speed estimate rather than a hunch — and know that estimate needs a confidence interval, not blind trust.
