When an Edge Has Stopped Working

Every edge has losing stretches, and some edges really do die. Tell the two apart with rules set in advance — streak odds, a record test, a CUSUM alarm, and an honest count of how many trades it takes.

The whole idea
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

My system just lost seven trades in a row. It tested at fifty-five percent wins. I think it's broken.

Maybe. But the streak is the weakest evidence you have. At fifty-five percent, a run of seven losses turns up somewhere in a hundred and eighty trades about three times in ten. Edge or no edge, streaks look much the same.

So what should I look at instead?

The whole record. How many trades so far, and how many wins?

A hundred and eighty trades, eighty-eight wins.

At fifty-five percent you would expect ninety-nine wins. Luck alone typically moves that by about seven either way. You are eleven short, so about one and two thirds of those typical swings below.

Is that bad?

It is amber. Not nothing, not proof. The rule you write in advance says amber means halve the size and look for a cause: wider spreads, worse fills, a data change, a market the backtest never saw.

Why not just stop?

Because a hundred and eighty trades can barely tell. To separate a fifty-five percent edge from a coin flip reliably, you need around six hundred trades. At a hundred trades a year, that is six years.

Six years? Then how do I ever know?

You set an alarm that watches every trade. Each loss adds a little to a running total, each win takes a little off, and it never goes below zero. When the total crosses a level you chose in advance, you stop and re-test.

Like a smoke alarm.

Exactly like one. Set it low and toast sets it off. Set it high and the real fire takes longer to catch. You can't have fast and quiet together, so you pick the trade-off before you start.

Has this gone wrong for real money?

August two thousand and seven. Many quant equity funds took sudden, heavy losses in the same few days while the broad market moved relatively little. Much of it reversed soon after, but some funds had already cut near the worst point. And underneath, the same kind of strategy had been earning less for years as more money chased it.

So a sharp shock that wasn't decay, and a slow decay nobody saw.

Both in one episode. A rule that only watched the last week would have read them exactly backwards.

The one-line version?

Write down what broken means before you go live, judge the whole record against the tested edge, and never re-fit the system on its losing stretch.

How it reached trading

Allocators have always asked: a bad year — fire the manager or hold? A year is too short, and checking every month quietly raises the false-alarm rate.

The tools came from factory quality control: Page's CUSUM chart, built on Wald's sequential test. Philips, Yashchin and Stein set out a CUSUM for active managers in 2003. Any trading rule with a tested record can be watched the same way.

CUSUM: an alarm on every trade

S = max(0, S + score) · alarm at S ≥ h

Section 9 · It actually happened

Slow decay, sudden shocks

August 2007 · quant quake

Many quant equity funds lost heavily in the same few days while the market barely moved; much of it reversed soon after. Khandani and Lo blamed crowded positions being unwound — and found the stand-in strategy's returns had been falling for years as capital piled in. Slow erosion was real decay; the week itself was a shock.

2016 · publication

McLean and Pontiff studied 97 published return predictors: returns were 26% lower out of sample and 58% lower after publication. Decay has a nameable cause — someone else found the edge — and is usually partial.

Section 12

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Tested at 60%, live 105 wins of 200. Which band?

Expected 120 ± 6.93 → z = −15/6.93 = −2.17. Between −1.28 and −2.33: half size, review.
Question 2

Roughly how many trades to tell 55% from 50% (5% / 80%)?

A five-point fall is small against the noise of single trades. At 100 trades a year that's six years.
Question 3

You drop the CUSUM threshold from 4 to 2. What happens?

False alarms in 300 healthy trades go from ~0.5% to ~21%; median time to catch a real decay falls from ~475 to ~194 trades.
Question 4

Backtest 80% wins; live 42% from the first trade. Most likely?

A gap that size from day one points at look-ahead, survivorship or impossible fills. Replay the live period through the backtest before any decay test.
Your call
Scenario drill · the week of 6 August 2007

Your quant strategy has its worst few days ever, and the market has moved relatively little

You run a quantitative long/short equity strategy with a long tested record. In the space of a few days this week it has suffered sudden, severe losses, far outside anything in its history. The broad stock market has hardly moved. Other quant funds, running different models, are reporting the same thing. What do you do?

  1. The edge is dead. Liquidate everything today.
    A few days of losses is the most vivid part of the record and the least informative about the edge. When many funds hold the same positions, selling into the unwind means selling near the worst prices.
  2. Cut to a size you can survive under a pre-set rule, change nothing in the model, and judge decay separately on the long record.
    This keeps the two questions apart. The sudden loss looks like a crowding shock, which calls for survival, not a verdict. Whether the edge itself has been eroding is a slower question that only the long record, tested against the tested edge, can answer.
  3. Hold full size. It is only noise and it will come back.
    It might come back, but holding full size through a crowded unwind assumes you will survive the path, and nothing guarantees it. It also skips the slower question of whether the edge has been shrinking.
  4. Re-fit the model on this week's data so it stops losing.
    That is in-sample optimisation on the worst sample you have. It fits the shock, not the edge, and resets nothing.

What happened. In the second week of August 2007, many quant long/short equity funds took sudden, severe losses in the same few days. They were hit hardest on 7 and 8 August, while equity and bond markets moved relatively little, and the losses continued on the 9th when the S&P 500 fell nearly 3%. Much of the loss reversed on Friday 10 August, but some funds had already cut positions near the worst point. Khandani and Lo later explained the losses as a crowded unwind: many funds held similar positions, and the rapid selling of one or more large portfolios pushed prices against all of them. They also found a slower decay: their stand-in strategy's returns had been falling for years as more capital chased similar trades. The week was a shock, not the decay; the decay was the quiet trend underneath.

Sources: Khandani and Lo, "What Happened to the Quants in August 2007?", 2007 (Federal Reserve Bank of New York conference copy); Khandani and Lo, NBER Working Paper 14465, November 2008.

Section 13

What to carry forward

01

Streaks are not verdicts: at 55%, 7 in a row appears in 180 trades about 31% of the time.

02

Test the whole record against the tested edge (z), and let LR move your odds — no more.

03

Know the trades needed before launch: 55→50 needs ~615, 63→50 ~88.

04

CUSUM watches every trade with a known false-alarm rate. Choose h from the table, not your mood.

05

Amber halves size and looks for a cause; red stops and re-tests. Never refit on the losing stretch.
