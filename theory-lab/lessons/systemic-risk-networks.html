Systemic Risk & Financial Contagion Networks

One institution's failure isn't a single event — it's a shock that propagates through a web of exposures, and DebtRank is the algorithm central banks use to measure exactly how far that propagation reaches before it stops.

CONCEPT — regulatory/systemic-risk research tool, not a component of this repo's trading strategies ~16 min read Prereqs: Graph Theory Fundamentals & Centrality Measures
TL;DR
  • Systemic risk is naturally a network problem: nodes are institutions or instruments, edges are exposures (counterparty, correlation, or funding linkages), and the question is how far a single node's distress spreads once it starts.
  • DebtRank (Battiston et al., 2012) measures exactly this: it propagates a "distress level" iteratively through the exposure network, capital-weighted, and sums the total distress induced across the whole system relative to its size.
  • DebtRank is conceptually related to eigenvector centrality — both are recursive, iterative computations over a network — but propagates a one-time shock forward through time rather than describing a fixed, static importance.
  • A worked 4-institution example shows one default (Bank C) propagating through capital-weighted exposures to leave the other three banks at roughly 24%, 48%, and 32% distress after the cascade settles — and the total induced distress (≈1.05) slightly exceeds the size of the initiating shock (1.00), the amplification effect DebtRank is built to capture.
  • This is stated plainly: DebtRank and tools like it are used by central banks, the Federal Reserve, and regulators for financial-stability stress testing — it is a market-structure research tool, not a component of any trading strategy in this repo.
Section 01

Why this matters

The first lesson in this category asked which node in a network is structurally most important, using three different static measures of importance. The second turned a correlation matrix into a graph and pruned it to its backbone. This lesson asks a different kind of question, one that only makes sense once you already have a network in hand: if something goes wrong at one node, how far does the damage actually travel, and how much worse does it get by the time it stops spreading? That's not a question about static structure anymore — it's a question about dynamics on a network, and it's the exact question regulators ask after every financial crisis: which institution's failure would do the most damage to the system as a whole, not just to its own direct counterparties?

The 2008 financial crisis made this question urgent in a very specific way: some institutions that looked individually unremarkable by size turned out to be deeply, systemically important once their web of interconnections was traced through — "too interconnected to fail" rather than simply "too big to fail." DebtRank, developed in the years after that crisis specifically to formalize this idea, is the best-known academic and regulatory answer, and it builds directly on the eigenvector-centrality machinery from this category's first lesson — just applied to a shock propagating forward in time instead of a fixed importance score.

💡
Plain-English intuition

A financial network is a row of dominoes wired together, not in a line

A single row of dominoes is easy to reason about: knock one over, and exactly the ones after it fall, one at a time, in a predictable chain. A real financial network isn't a line — it's a tangle, where each institution is propped up partly by its own capital and partly by the expectation that its counterparties will make good on what they owe it. Knock one institution over (it defaults, or takes a large loss) and every institution exposed to it absorbs some fraction of that loss — not the whole thing, usually, but a real fraction, proportional to how large that specific exposure was relative to their own capital cushion. Those institutions are now somewhat weaker, which means their counterparties absorb a smaller, second-round hit, and so on, rippling outward until the shock is either fully absorbed by capital cushions along the way, or it isn't, and the cascade keeps going.

DebtRank is the formal version of tracing exactly that ripple: not just "who is directly exposed to the failing institution" (that's just reading one row of a matrix), but "how much total distress does this one failure eventually cause once every round of knock-on weakening has played out and settled." The word "rank" in the name is deliberate — it produces a comparable score for every institution, answering "if this one had failed instead, would the total damage have been bigger or smaller?"

Section 02

The math, step by step

Step 1 — the exposure network

Model the system as a directed, weighted graph: nodes are institutions, and each edge carries an impact factor \(W_{ij}\) — the fraction of institution \(i\)'s own capital that would be wiped out if institution \(j\) defaulted completely on what it owes \(i\). This is a directed edge (\(i\)'s exposure to \(j\) is generally not the same size as \(j\)'s exposure to \(i\)), and it's naturally capital-weighted rather than raw-dollar-weighted, since a $10 million exposure means something very different to a bank with $50 million of capital than to one with $5 billion.

Every node also carries a distress level \(h_i \in [0,1]\): 0 means fully healthy, 1 means fully defaulted, and values in between represent partial distress (a large mark-to-market loss, a credit downgrade, a funding squeeze — anything short of outright default). The whole exercise starts by setting one or more nodes' \(h_i(0)\) to reflect an initiating shock (most simply, one institution set fully to \(h=1\)) and asking how \(h\) evolves across the rest of the network from there.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Think of "distress" as a dial from 0 (perfectly fine) to 1 (completely failed) sitting on every bank. The impact factor between two banks is like asking "if my neighbor's house burned down completely, how much of my own house would catch fire too?" A bank heavily exposed to another gets a high number here; one with barely any exposure gets a low one.

Step 2 — the iterative distress-propagation rule

At each round \(t\), every node updates its own distress level based on how much its neighbors' distress changed since the last round, weighted by the impact factors:

\[ h_i(t+1) = \min\!\Big(1,\; h_i(t) + \sum_j W_{ij}\,\big[h_j(t) - h_j(t-1)\big]\Big) \]
h_i(t)
Node i's distress so far
0 = healthy, 1 = fully defaulted, carried forward from the previous round
W_ij
The impact factor
Fraction of i's capital exposed to j — how hard j's distress hits i
h_j(t) − h_j(t−1)
j's newest distress
Only the change since last round propagates onward — not the whole history again

Read this as: each institution's distress next round equals its current distress, plus a capital-weighted sum of how much worse each of its counterparties just got — capped at 1 (fully defaulted can't get "more" defaulted). The reason the rule uses the change in each neighbor's distress, \(h_j(t)-h_j(t-1)\), rather than their raw current level \(h_j(t)\) every round, is to avoid the same distress being counted over and over as it echoes back and forth between two connected nodes — the published DebtRank algorithm formalizes this with explicit node states (undistressed / distressed-and-transmitting / distressed-and-already-processed) so that each unit of distress is only ever transmitted once along a given edge. The version above is a simplified, linearized illustration of that same core mechanic for teaching purposes — it captures the essential idea (iterative, capital-weighted, change-based propagation) without the full bookkeeping the original paper uses to guarantee no distress double-counts around a cycle.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Picture dominoes wired together in a tangle instead of a line. Knock one over, and everything directly touching it wobbles a bit — not all the way over, just weakened in proportion to how exposed it was. Those wobbling dominoes then pass a smaller wobble on to whatever they touch, and so on, getting weaker each round, until eventually nothing new is wobbling and the whole thing settles down.

Iterate this update until nothing changes any further (the network reaches a steady state) or every node has hit either 0 or 1. The DebtRank of the originally-shocked node is then defined as the total distress this process induced across the rest of the system, typically weighted by each node's share of total system economic value:

\[ \text{DebtRank} = \sum_{i} \big[h_i(T) - h_i(0)\big]\cdot v_i \]
h_i(T) − h_i(0)how much node i's distress rose from start to the settled end-state
v_inode i's relative economic value (e.g. share of total system capital) — equal weights, 1/N each, in the simplest illustrative version

A single number, comparable across every institution in the system: "if this node had been the one to fail, this is how much total distress the system would have absorbed by the time the cascade settled." Note the family resemblance to eigenvector centrality from the first lesson in this category — both are iterative, recursive computations over a network's adjacency structure — but DebtRank propagates an actual event forward through simulated time, rather than describing a fixed, timeless importance score.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

DebtRank is a single score that answers "if this one bank had been the one to fail, how much total damage would the whole system end up absorbing once every knock-on wobble finally stopped?" It lets regulators compare institutions on a like-for-like basis — not just "how big is this bank," but "how much trouble would this bank's failure actually cause everyone else."

\(h_i(t)\)Node \(i\)'s distress level at round \(t\), from 0 (healthy) to 1 (fully defaulted).
\(W_{ij}\)The impact factor: the fraction of node \(i\)'s own capital that would be lost if node \(j\) fully defaulted — a directed, capital-weighted exposure.
\(T\)The round at which the cascade settles — distress stops changing further, or every node has reached 0 or 1.

Worked example

Four banks, one default, a cascade that settles above the size of the initial shock

Four banks — A, B, C, D — with a capital-weighted impact-factor matrix (plausible, illustrative numbers, not drawn from any real institutions), row \(i\) = affected bank, column \(j\) = source of distress:

\[ \begin{array}{c|cccc} W_{ij} & j{=}A & j{=}B & j{=}C & j{=}D \\\hline i{=}A & - & 0.3 & 0.1 & 0.0 \\ i{=}B & 0.2 & - & 0.4 & 0.1 \\ i{=}C & 0.0 & 0.2 & - & 0.5 \\ i{=}D & 0.1 & 0.0 & 0.3 & - \end{array} \]

Read \(W_{BC}=0.4\), for example, as: "if C fully defaulted, B would lose 40% of its own capital on that exposure alone" — B is heavily exposed to C. Now suppose Bank C fails outright: \(h_C(0)=1\), every other bank starts healthy, \(h_A(0)=h_B(0)=h_D(0)=0\).

Round 1. Each bank absorbs \(W_{i,C}\) times C's full distress: \(h_A(1) = 0.1\times1 = 0.10\); \(h_B(1) = 0.4\times1 = 0.40\); \(h_D(1) = 0.3\times1 = 0.30\) (C stays at 1, the absorbing default state).

Round 2. Now each bank also absorbs its exposure to whichever other banks just got newly distressed in round 1: \(h_A(2) = 0.10 + W_{AB}\times(h_B(1){-}0) = 0.10 + 0.3\times0.40 = 0.22\); \(h_B(2) = 0.40 + W_{BA}\times0.10 = 0.40+0.2\times0.10=0.42\); \(h_D(2) = 0.30 + W_{DA}\times0.10 = 0.30+0.1\times0.10=0.31\).

Rounds 3 onward. The same rule keeps running — each bank picking up progressively smaller increments from its neighbors' most recent changes — until the increments become negligible. Iterating this out numerically, the system settles (after roughly 7-8 rounds) at approximately: \(h_A^{*}\approx0.24\), \(h_B^{*}\approx0.48\), \(h_D^{*}\approx0.32\) (and \(h_C=1.00\), unchanged, since C's default was the initiating event).

Using equal weights (\(v_i=1/4\) for illustration), the DebtRank of Bank C's default is the total distress induced in the rest of the system: \(0.24+0.48+0.32 = 1.04\). That number is worth sitting with: the total distress the system ends up absorbing (1.04) is slightly larger than the size of the single shock that started it (1.00, since only C itself defaulted). This is exactly the amplification DebtRank is built to reveal — network propagation adding to the damage beyond the initiating event itself, not just redistributing an equivalent amount of pain across more institutions. A larger, more densely connected network with higher impact factors can amplify a single default far more dramatically than this small four-node illustration shows.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

You might expect one bank failing to just spread its own trouble around, like slicing a pie into smaller pieces for everyone else — the total pain staying the same size, just shared out. But the worked example shows the total pain (1.04) actually ends up bigger than the original failure (1.00). Each round of wobbling adds a little extra damage on top, rather than simply redistributing what was already there — that "extra on top" is exactly what makes network contagion dangerous.

A named scenario

A regulator deciding which bank's stress test to run first

Imagine a financial-stability unit at a central bank with limited time before a reporting deadline and four candidate institutions it could run a full contagion stress test on. Simple exposure totals (how much the rest of the system is owed by each bank) might rank the institutions one way; DebtRank, run on the network in the worked example above, would specifically flag whichever node's failure produces the largest downstream distress once the whole cascade settles — in that example, C's default (weighted at 1.04 total induced distress) versus what a similar run starting from A, B, or D failing instead would produce. This is precisely the kind of exercise the Federal Reserve, the Bank of England, and other systemic-risk regulators run using DebtRank-style algorithms on real interbank exposure data reported to them — a genuine, real-world use of this exact machinery, in a domain (financial-stability regulation) that is deliberately separate from position-taking or trading. Nothing about this scenario, or DebtRank generally, is a component of any strategy in this repo — it's presented here purely as background theory on how regulators reason about network contagion.

Section 03

How this is actually used — and how it isn't

DebtRank was developed specifically in response to the 2008 crisis' clearest lesson: that measuring an institution's systemic importance by its size alone ("too big to fail") missed institutions that were critically important because of who they were connected to, not how large they were on their own ("too interconnected to fail"). Battiston et al.'s original 2012 paper applied the method to Federal Reserve data on emergency lending during the crisis and found that a handful of institutions were far more systemically central by DebtRank than their balance-sheet size alone would have suggested. Since then, variants of DebtRank and related network-contagion algorithms have become a standard part of the financial-stability toolkit at central banks and regulators worldwide, feeding into stress tests, systemic-risk surcharge calculations for globally important banks, and interbank network monitoring.

Said plainly: DebtRank and the network-contagion framing in this lesson are a market-structure and financial-stability research tool — the kind of analysis central banks and regulators run on interbank exposure networks to stress-test the financial system. It is not, and is not presented here as, a component of any trading strategy in this repo. Explaining how the technique works is background knowledge, not a claim that it produces trading edge.
⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where contagion-network analysis goes wrong in practice

Confusing correlation-based and exposure-based networks. The previous lesson built a network from correlation (a statistical relationship); this lesson builds one from actual counterparty exposures (a contractual/economic relationship). Both are legitimate network constructions, but they answer different questions and are built from fundamentally different data — a correlation network says "these two tend to move together"; an exposure network says "if one fails, the other specifically loses money." Conflating the two — e.g. running a DebtRank-style cascade over a correlation-derived network as if correlation implied an actual capital-loss transmission mechanism — is a category error.

Real exposure data is exactly the hard part. This lesson's worked example uses an invented impact-factor matrix for teaching purposes. In practice, the single biggest obstacle to running this kind of analysis for real is that detailed bilateral counterparty exposure data is generally confidential, held only by regulators (via mandatory reporting) or not comprehensively available to any single party at all — a private researcher or a trading desk essentially cannot replicate a regulator's DebtRank exercise without that non-public exposure data.

The simplified propagation rule shown here isn't the full, published algorithm. As noted in the math walkthrough, the published DebtRank paper tracks explicit node states to guarantee distress is never re-transmitted around a cycle more than once; the simplified iterative rule in this lesson captures the core mechanic for teaching purposes but should not be treated as a literal, publication-accurate implementation.

A cascade model's results are only as good as its impact factors. The whole propagation depends entirely on the \(W_{ij}\) matrix — how exposures translate into fractional capital losses — which itself depends on assumptions (loss-given-default rates, which exposures are even counted, whether second-order effects like fire-sale price impact are modeled). Different reasonable assumptions can produce materially different DebtRank rankings for the same underlying network.

Section 05

Self-test

Q1. In the worked example, why did the total induced distress across banks A, B, and D (≈1.04) come out larger than the size of the initiating shock (1.00, Bank C's default alone)?
Because the shock didn't just transfer once from C to its direct counterparties and stop — it propagated in further rounds as each newly-distressed bank's own worsening then hit its own counterparties (e.g. Bank B's round-1 distress from C then fed into Bank A's round-2 update via \(W_{AB}\)). Each round of propagation adds further, smaller increments on top of what came before, so the system's total settled distress reflects the shock plus every knock-on round of secondary transmission, not merely a one-to-one handoff of the original 1.00 unit of distress. This compounding effect — total damage exceeding the initiating event — is exactly the amplification DebtRank is designed to reveal.
Q2. Why does Bank B end up the most distressed of the three surviving banks in the worked example, rather than Bank A or Bank D?
Bank B has the largest direct impact factor from the failing bank, \(W_{BC}=0.4\) — meaning 40% of B's own capital was exposed to C, versus only 10% for A (\(W_{AC}=0.1\)) and C has no direct exposure listed to D in the reverse direction relevant here. B's first-round distress (0.40) starts far higher than A's (0.10) or D's (0.30) purely because of that direct exposure size, and subsequent rounds only add smaller increments on top — the initial, direct exposure to the failing node dominates where the system ultimately settles.
Q3. How is DebtRank conceptually related to eigenvector centrality from the first lesson in this category, and how is it fundamentally different?
Both are iterative, recursive computations defined over a network's adjacency/exposure structure — eigenvector centrality's \(Ax=\lambda x\) and DebtRank's round-by-round update rule both work by repeatedly combining a node's neighbors' values, similar to how power iteration converges to a leading eigenvector. The fundamental difference is what each computation represents: eigenvector centrality describes a static, timeless property of the network's structure (which nodes are connected to important nodes, right now, with no shock involved). DebtRank instead simulates an actual event unfolding forward through simulated time — a real shock at one node, propagating in rounds until it settles — producing a score about a hypothetical cascade, not a permanent structural property of the network.
Q4. What's the practical, real-world obstacle to anyone outside a regulator running a genuine DebtRank analysis on an actual banking system?
The impact-factor matrix \(W_{ij}\) requires detailed bilateral counterparty exposure data — exactly how much each institution owes to, and is owed by, every other specific institution, and how that compares to each institution's own capital. This kind of granular, institution-by-institution exposure data is generally confidential and is typically available only to regulators through mandatory reporting requirements, not publicly. A private researcher or trading desk lacks the underlying data needed to build a real (rather than illustrative/toy) version of the exposure network in the first place, regardless of how well they understand the propagation algorithm itself.
Q5. Why does this lesson state so explicitly that DebtRank is not a component of any trading strategy in this repo — what's the risk of not saying that plainly?
Explaining a technique thoroughly and with genuine appreciation for its elegance (which this lesson does) can easily be read, if not stated otherwise, as an implicit claim that the technique is used here or produces some trading edge. DebtRank's actual domain — financial-stability regulation and contagion stress testing on confidential interbank exposure data — has essentially nothing to do with generating a trading signal on tradeable instruments, and this repo has no exposure-network infrastructure or interbank data of any kind. Being explicit prevents the lesson's evident enthusiasm for the method from being mistaken for a claim about its use, or usefulness, here — exactly the "built ≠ works ≠ has edge" and "don't get excited about a method" discipline this curriculum tries to hold consistently across every lesson.
