The Human Side · Lesson 2 of 6

Selling Winners, Holding Losers

The disposition effect: why you cash in what's working and keep what isn't, how it was measured in 10,000 real brokerage accounts, and how to measure it in your own trades.

The whole idea
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

Last month I needed cash, so I sold my two best positions. It felt good. Was that a mistake?

Maybe. It's one of the most common patterns in investing: selling winners too early and holding losers too long. Shefrin and Statman named it the disposition effect in 1985.

Why would anyone do that on purpose?

It's the loss-aversion curve acted out on a portfolio. Selling a winner closes a happy story. Selling a loser makes the regret real. Holding it keeps the story open. Maybe it comes back.

Has anyone measured it, or is it just a theory?

Terrance Odean got the records of ten thousand accounts at a US discount broker, from 1987 to 1993. Real people, trading their own money.

How do you measure it fairly? In a rising market everyone sells more winners.

That's the clever part. On each day someone sold, he looked at everything they could have sold. Of the winners available, what share did they sell? Of the losers available, what share? He called them the proportion of gains realised and the proportion of losses realised.

And the numbers?

Fourteen point eight percent of gains realised, against nine point eight percent of losses. On any sale day, a winner was about one and a half times as likely to be sold as a loser.

Maybe the losers were due a bounce.

He tested that. Over the next year, the winners they sold beat the losers they kept by about three point four percentage points. On average, the bounce never came.

And tax?

That's the second cost. Realised gains get taxed, and realised losses can offset them. So tax logic says the opposite: take losses, defer gains. The only month the pattern reversed was December, when people sell losers for tax.

So they knew losses were worth taking.

Exactly. They just needed a deadline.

How do I check myself?

Take your last twenty exits. If you hold several positions, count the same two proportions. If you hold one at a time, compare how long you held winners and losers. Losers held clearly longer is the fingerprint.

So the one-line version?

The market doesn't know your entry price, so don't let it vote. Ask whether you would buy each holding today, and sell the ones you wouldn't.

Section 1

The S-curve, acted out on a portfolio

Shefrin and Statman (1985) named it: the disposition to sell winners too early and ride losers too long. Four ingredients:

S

Prospect theory

Concave for gains: bank them. Convex for losses: hold and hope.

£

Mental accounting

Each position is its own little account you want to close in the black.

↺

Regret aversion

Selling a loser makes "I was wrong" real. Holding keeps the story open.

✋

Self-control

Knowing you should take the loss and doing it are different things.

The market does not know what you paid. Whether to hold tomorrow depends on the stock's future, not your entry.
Section 2

How to measure it: PGR and PLR

Counting sales fails in a rising market (most positions are winners). Odean compared what was sold with what could have been sold, on each sale day.

PGR = RG ÷ (RG + PG)
PLR = RL ÷ (RL + PL)

Section 3

What 10,000 accounts showed

Odean (1998): a large US discount broker, 1987–1993, every sale day.

PGR 0.148

Of the gains investors could have realised on a sale day, they realised 14.8%.

PLR 0.098

Of the losses they could have realised, only 9.8%. A winner was about 1.5× as likely to be sold.

And it cost them

Over the next year, the winners they sold beat the losers they kept by about 3.4 percentage points (returns above the market). The "it's due a bounce" defence did not hold up.

Section 5

It's tax-backwards, too

Realised gains are taxed; realised losses can offset them. Tax logic says defer gains and take losses. The bias does the opposite.

Instinct

Sell Alder + Cedar: $11,400 raised, +$2,400 realised, $480 tax at 20%. PGR 0.67, PLR 0.

Tax-aware

Sell Birch + Delta: $9,800 raised, −$3,200 realised, up to $640 tax saved. PGR 0, PLR 0.67.

In Odean's data the pattern reversed in exactly one month: December, the tax-loss-selling month. With a deadline, people took losses readily. It is a choice.

Section 6 · It actually happened

10,000 accounts, seven years

It actually happened

Terrance Odean took the records of 10,000 randomly chosen accounts at a US discount broker, 1987–1993: real people, their own money, no advice. He found PGR 0.148 vs PLR 0.098, checked rebalancing and trading costs as innocent explanations (the pattern survived), saw it reverse only in December, and showed the sold winners beat the kept losers by ~3.4 pp the next year.

Prices

Frazzini (2006): prices under-react to news when holders sit on gains or losses.

Where the textbook version breaks

Trimming an oversized winner is risk management; a target set before entry is a plan. PGR/PLR measure a tendency, not one sale. If you hold one position at a time, there are no paper gains or losses to count: compare days held for losers vs winners instead.

Section 8

What this desk has, and hasn't, tested

The claim: "cut your losers and let your winners run" beats the reverse.

Not tested here No ledger entry measures how the desk's own trades are closed. The evidence on this deck is from investors in published studies. A desk test would need the closed-trade log with the open positions at each exit, and a pre-registered threshold for what gap counts as a finding.
What professionals do

Exits written at entry. The fresh-eyes test: if this were cash, would I buy it today? Tax-loss harvesting on purpose. Audit exits quarterly, not just entries.

Section 10

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Why isn't "I sold more winners than losers" evidence of the disposition effect?

PGR and PLR divide by the winners and losers available on each sale day, so they compare like with like.
Question 2

You hold 5 winners and 3 losers and sell 2 winners. PGR and PLR?

RG = 2, PG = 3 → 2/5. RL = 0, PL = 3 → 0/3.
Question 3

In Odean's data, how did the winners sold compare with the losers kept over the next year?

The instinct was not just tax-inefficient; it kept the worse stocks.
Question 4

You hold one position at a time. What's your disposition fingerprint?

With no other open positions, PGR and PLR can't be computed. Holding time (and average loss vs average win) carries the signal.
Your call
Scenario drill · an illustrative sale day

You need $10,000 by Friday. Which one goes?

You need $10,000 in cash this week. Two holdings are each worth about $10,000 today. One is up 40% and you feel it has run its course. The other is down 30% and you feel it is due a bounce. Realised gains are taxed at 20%, and realised losses can offset gains elsewhere. What do you sell?

  1. Sell the winner. It has done its job, and the loser just needs time.
    This is the disposition effect in one decision. Your entry prices are choosing, not the stocks' futures. You also realise a taxable gain of about $2,857, around $571 of tax, while a usable loss sits idle.
  2. Ask of each: if I had $10,000 in cash, would I buy this today? Sell the one you would not buy. If you would buy both equally, sell the loser for the tax offset.
    The fresh-eyes test takes the entry price out of the decision. Only the future matters. When the two are a toss-up, tax breaks the tie: the loser's realised loss of about $4,286 can offset up to about $857 of tax at 20%.
  3. Sell half of each, to be fair to both.
    Better than selling only the winner, and it banks some of the tax offset. But splitting the difference still lets the entry prices vote instead of asking which holding you actually want to own.
  4. Wait for the loser to get back to even, then sell it, and borrow for now.
    This is the break-even effect from the previous lesson. "Back to even" is a reference point the market does not know about, and borrowing to wait for it adds cost and risk to a decision you are avoiding.

What tends to happen. This sale day is illustrative, not a real account. In Odean's 10,000 real accounts, the winners investors sold beat the losers they kept by about 3.4 percentage points over the following year, and the habit also paid tax that selling losers would have saved. The only month the pattern reversed was December, when a tax deadline made taking losses feel worthwhile.

Section 11

What to carry forward

01

The disposition effect is the S-curve on a portfolio: bank gains, avoid booking losses (Shefrin and Statman, 1985).

02

Measure it with PGR and PLR. Odean's 10,000 accounts: 0.148 vs 0.098.

03

It costs twice: sold winners beat kept losers by ~3.4 pp, and selling gains first pays tax early.

04

December's reversal shows people can take losses when a deadline makes it salient.

05

Audit your exits: PGR vs PLR, or days held for losers vs winners.

06

Desk verdict: untested. No ledger entry measures how the desk's own trades are closed.
