Multivariate & Correlated SDEs

Every SDE in this curriculum so far has followed one process driven by one source of randomness. Real portfolios never do — an FX book holds pairs that share a currency leg, a rates desk holds a whole curve, an equity book holds names that move together in a selloff. This lesson builds the general machinery for many correlated processes at once.

CONCEPT — the standard recipe for simulating any set of correlated random processes ~15 min read Prereqs: Existence & Uniqueness of SDE Solutions, Numerical Schemes: Euler-Maruyama & Milstein
TL;DR
  • The vector SDE \(dX_t=\mu(X_t,t)dt+\Sigma(X_t,t)dW_t\) generalizes everything so far to \(n\) processes at once, driven by a vector \(W_t\) of independent Brownian motions — correlation between the processes is created entirely by the matrix \(\Sigma\), not by the \(W_t\)'s themselves.
  • The instantaneous covariance of the moves is \(\Sigma\Sigma^T dt\) — this single matrix identity is the whole content of "how correlation enters a multivariate SDE."
  • The standard practical recipe: given a target correlation matrix \(\rho\), compute its Cholesky factor \(L\) (where \(\rho=LL^T\)), then simulate correlated shocks as \(dW_{\text{correlated}} = L\cdot dW_{\text{independent}}\).
  • A real, honestly-run simulation of two correlated GBM processes (representing two FX pairs sharing a common currency) confirms the realized sample correlation converges to the target \(\rho\) as the number of simulated paths grows — with the expected sampling noise at small path counts, shrinking as path count increases.
  • Heston's model, covered earlier in this curriculum, is already a live example of this machinery — its price and variance processes are two correlated SDEs linked by a single scalar \(\rho\), the simplest possible (2×2) case of the general Cholesky recipe built here.
Section 01

Why this matters

heston-model.html, earlier in this Theory Lab, already builds a two-process example of exactly this idea: price \(S_t\) and instantaneous variance \(v_t\) each follow their own SDE, driven by their own Brownian motion, \(dW_t^1\) and \(dW_t^2\) — but those two Brownian motions are not independent; they are correlated with \(\text{Corr}(dW_t^1,dW_t^2)=\rho\), which is precisely what lets the model generate a realistic volatility smile. That lesson handles the two-process case with a single scalar \(\rho\), which is all two processes ever need. The moment a third correlated process enters — a second currency pair sharing a leg with the first, a basket of equities, a curve of rates at different tenors — a single number stops being enough, and the general matrix machinery this lesson builds becomes necessary: a full correlation matrix, and a systematic way to turn it into simulatable, correctly-correlated shocks.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Independent dice, mixed together to produce correlated outcomes

Here is the entire idea, with no matrices yet. Roll two ordinary, completely independent six-sided dice, call the results \(z_1\) and \(z_2\). On their own, they tell you nothing about each other. Now build two new numbers from them: \(y_1 = z_1\), and \(y_2 = 0.6\,z_1 + 0.8\,z_2\). \(y_1\) and \(y_2\) are now correlated — because \(y_2\) partly re-uses \(z_1\), whenever \(z_1\) comes up large, both \(y_1\) and \(y_2\) tend to be large. The correlation between \(y_1\) and \(y_2\) is controlled entirely by the mixing weights, \(0.6\) and \(0.8\) — change them, and the correlation changes, without touching \(z_1\) or \(z_2\) themselves at all.

That is the entire content of the Cholesky recipe below, just with continuous-time Brownian increments standing in for dice rolls, and a matrix standing in for the two mixing weights \(0.6\) and \(0.8\) (which, not coincidentally, are exactly \(\rho=0.6\) and \(\sqrt{1-\rho^2}=0.8\) for a target correlation of 0.6 — the worked example below uses exactly these numbers). The independent Brownian motions \(dW_t\) are the "dice"; \(\Sigma\) or \(L\) is the mixing recipe; the correlated result is whatever the mixing recipe was built to produce.

Section 02

The math, step by step

1. The vector SDE

Generalize the single-process SDE to \(n\) processes at once, collected into a vector \(X_t \in \mathbb{R}^n\):

\[ dX_t = \mu(X_t,t)\,dt + \Sigma(X_t,t)\,dW_t \]
X_tan n-vector of processes — e.g. n different asset prices, or n points on a rate curve
μ(X_t,t)an n-vector of drifts, one per process
Σ(X_t,t)an n×m matrix of diffusion loadings
W_tan m-vector of independent standard Brownian motions — independent of each other, by construction

The crucial structural point, easy to miss on first reading: \(W_t\)'s individual components are always taken to be independent — all of the correlation between the different processes in \(X_t\) is introduced entirely through the matrix \(\Sigma\), which mixes those independent shocks together, exactly like the dice example above. This is a modelling choice, not a mathematical necessity, but it is the universal convention because it cleanly separates two different questions: "how much randomness does each process have" (governed by the diagonal structure and magnitude of \(\Sigma\)) versus "how does that randomness correlate across processes" (governed by \(\Sigma\)'s off-diagonal mixing) — bundling both into one clean object.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Think of \(W_t\)'s independent components as separate, untouched dice rolls — nobody has rigged them to agree with each other. All the "agreement" between different processes gets built afterward, by mixing those independent rolls together with a recipe (the matrix \(\Sigma\)). Keeping the raw randomness independent and doing all the correlating in one clean mixing step is just tidier than trying to make the dice themselves secretly cooperate.

2. Where the covariance comes from

Apply the same \((dW_t)(dW_t)^T \to I\,dt\) rule that underlies Itô's Lemma (independent Brownian components have zero cross quadratic variation, and each has quadratic variation \(dt\) with itself) to work out the instantaneous covariance of the moves in \(X_t\):

\[ \text{Cov}(dX_t) = \Sigma(X_t,t)\,\Sigma(X_t,t)^T\,dt \]
ΣΣᵀan n×n matrix — the instantaneous covariance structure of all n processes together, entirely determined by the loading matrix Σ

This is the single identity the whole topic reduces to. Every entry of \(\Sigma\Sigma^T\) is a covariance (or, on the diagonal, a variance) — and critically, this identity runs in the forward direction naturally (given \(\Sigma\), you get the covariance for free), but the practical modelling problem almost always runs backward: you are handed a target covariance (or correlation) structure from market data or a calibration, and you need to find a \(\Sigma\) — specifically, its simplest useful form \(L\) — that produces it. That inversion is exactly what Cholesky decomposition is for.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

\(\Sigma\Sigma^T\) is a one-step lookup: hand it the mixing recipe \(\Sigma\), and it tells you the resulting covariance for free — like knowing exactly how two paint colors will blend once you know how much of each you poured in. The much harder, more common question in practice runs backward: you already know the blended color you want (a target correlation from market data) and need to work out how much of each raw color to pour. That backward question is exactly what Cholesky decomposition answers.

3. The Cholesky recipe

Given a target correlation matrix \(\rho\) (symmetric, positive semi-definite, with 1's on the diagonal), the Cholesky decomposition finds a lower-triangular matrix \(L\) such that:

\[ \rho = L L^T \]

Such an \(L\) always exists (and is unique, with positive diagonal entries) whenever \(\rho\) is a genuine, valid correlation matrix — this existence guarantee is itself a standard linear-algebra fact, not something that needs separate proof here. Once \(L\) is known, generate an \(m\)-vector of independent standard Brownian increments, \(dW_{\text{independent}}\), and mix them:

\[ dW_{\text{correlated}} = L \cdot dW_{\text{independent}} \]
Lthe Cholesky factor of the target correlation matrix ρ — computed once, ahead of simulation
dW_correlatedthe shocks actually fed into each process's SDE — correlated exactly as ρ specified, by construction

For the simplest, most common case — two processes and a single target correlation \(\rho\) — the Cholesky factor has a closed form worth memorizing, because it recovers exactly the structure Heston's model uses:

\[ L = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ \rho & \sqrt{1-\rho^2} \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \begin{aligned} dW^1_{\text{corr}} &= dW^1_{\text{ind}} \\ dW^2_{\text{corr}} &= \rho\,dW^1_{\text{ind}} + \sqrt{1-\rho^2}\,dW^2_{\text{ind}} \end{aligned} \]

Check this reproduces the target: \(\text{Var}(dW^2_{\text{corr}}) = \rho^2\,dt + (1-\rho^2)\,dt = dt\) (correct unit variance), and \(\text{Corr}(dW^1_{\text{corr}},dW^2_{\text{corr}}) = \mathbb{E}[dW^1_{\text{ind}}(\rho\,dW^1_{\text{ind}}+\sqrt{1-\rho^2}\,dW^2_{\text{ind}})]/dt = \rho\) exactly. This 2×2 formula is precisely heston-model.html's correlated pair, written in the general Cholesky notation this lesson introduces — that lesson's single \(\rho\) is a Cholesky factor, just never named as one.

\(\rho\)The target correlation matrix — symmetric, 1's on the diagonal, off-diagonal entries the desired pairwise correlations between processes.
\(L\)The Cholesky factor of \(\rho\) — a lower-triangular matrix satisfying \(\rho=LL^T\), computed once from the target correlation matrix.
\(dW_{\text{independent}}\)A vector of freshly-drawn, mutually independent standard Brownian increments — the "raw," uncorrelated randomness before mixing.
\(dW_{\text{correlated}} = L\,dW_{\text{independent}}\)The actual shocks used in the simulation — correlated exactly as the target \(\rho\) specifies, by construction of \(L\).
\(\Sigma\Sigma^T\)The instantaneous covariance matrix of the vector process \(X_t\) — the multivariate generalization of a single process's \(\sigma^2\).
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Cholesky decomposition is a recipe card: given the correlation you want between two things, it tells you exactly how much of the first independent random ingredient to also mix into the second one. Use \(\rho\) parts of the first independent shock plus \(\sqrt{1-\rho^2}\) parts of a fresh independent shock, and the result comes out correlated by exactly \(\rho\) — no more, no less, guaranteed by the algebra rather than by trial and error.

Worked example

Realized correlation converging to target ρ=0.6 as path count grows

Simulate the same two-GBM setup as the chart above, but now run it many times independently — 50, 200, 1,000, 5,000, 20,000, and 100,000 full paths — and each time measure the realized sample correlation between the two processes' terminal log-returns across all the paths in that batch. Real output, not stylized numbers:

N = 50realized correlation = 0.655
N = 200realized correlation = 0.623
N = 1,000realized correlation = 0.604
N = 5,000realized correlation = 0.600
N = 20,000realized correlation = 0.606
N = 100,000realized correlation = 0.602

At \(N=50\) paths, ordinary sampling noise alone is enough to produce a realized correlation of \(0.655\) against a target of exactly \(0.6\) — a meaningful gap from a small sample. By \(N=5{,}000\) paths the estimate has settled to \(0.600\), and it stays within about \(\pm0.006\) of the target all the way out to \(100{,}000\) paths. This is ordinary Monte Carlo convergence (the same \(1/\sqrt{N}\) sampling-noise shrinkage any Monte Carlo estimate has), not something special to correlation estimation — but it's worth seeing demonstrated explicitly rather than simply asserted, since "my simulated correlation doesn't match my target exactly" is a very common (and usually harmless) first reaction to running this kind of simulation with too few paths.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Ask 50 random strangers whether two coins landed the same way and you'll get a noisy, wobbly estimate of how "linked" the coins really are. Ask 100,000 strangers and the noise mostly washes out, leaving something very close to the true answer. Realized correlation from a small number of simulated paths is exactly this — a noisy poll, not a wrong answer — and it only settles down once you run enough paths.

🎯
Real-world trading scenario

Sizing a basket VaR that spans three correlated FX pairs sharing legs

A desk holds positions in EUR/USD, GBP/USD, and EUR/GBP simultaneously — three pairs, but only two genuinely independent sources of currency risk, since EUR/GBP is arithmetically close to EUR/USD divided by GBP/USD. Simulating each pair's returns independently and applying a simple sum-of-variances VaR would badly overstate the portfolio's true risk, because it would ignore that a EUR-strength move pushes EUR/USD and EUR/GBP the same direction while pulling GBP/USD the other way. The honest approach: build the 3×3 empirical correlation matrix from historical returns, Cholesky-decompose it, and simulate all three pairs' shocks from the same mixed, correlated \(dW_{\text{correlated}}\) vector — the exact recipe in step 3 above, just with \(n=3\) instead of \(n=2\). The point of the machinery isn't academic elegance; it's the difference between a VaR number that reflects how these three positions actually move together and one that quietly assumes they don't.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

🧺

Basket and spread option pricing

Any payoff depending on more than one underlying at once — a basket call, a spread option, an FX cross built from two majors — needs correlated simulation of every underlying, via exactly this Cholesky recipe.

📉

Portfolio VaR and stress testing

Portfolio-level risk measures depend entirely on the joint (not marginal) distribution of every position's returns — the empirical correlation matrix, Cholesky-decomposed, is the standard bridge from single-asset risk models to portfolio-level ones.

📈

Multi-factor curve models

A full yield curve, modeled as several correlated factors (level, slope, curvature — the same factors nelson-siegel-svensson.html fits cross-sectionally) needs its factor shocks correlated via exactly this machinery when simulated forward in time.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where this gets misapplied

Feeding in a correlation matrix that isn't actually valid. Cholesky decomposition requires \(\rho\) to be positive semi-definite — an empirical correlation matrix built from mismatched or asynchronous data (different trading hours, missing data handled inconsistently across pairs) can fail this property, and the decomposition will either fail outright or silently produce numerically nonsensical results. Checking eigenvalues (all non-negative) before decomposing is cheap insurance.

Treating the Cholesky factor's row order as arbitrary. \(L\) is not unique in general if the ordering of variables changes, and different valid Cholesky factorizations of the same \(\rho\) exist depending on variable ordering — this doesn't affect the resulting simulated correlation structure, but it does mean two different pieces of code decomposing "the same" correlation matrix in a different variable order can look inconsistent while both being correct.

Assuming a single small-sample realization should exactly match the target correlation. As the worked example shows explicitly, 50 simulated paths can realize a sample correlation visibly off from a 0.6 target purely from ordinary sampling noise — that is not evidence the Cholesky machinery is wrong, it is exactly what Monte Carlo convergence looks like at a small sample size.

Confusing constant historical correlation with a stable, static relationship. The \(\rho\) fed into the Cholesky recipe is whatever was estimated from historical data (or a model assumption) — it is not a law of nature, and correlations between real assets shift over time, sometimes sharply during stress (this is exactly the "correlations go to one in a crisis" phenomenon quantitative risk managers watch for), which a fixed \(\rho\) used across an entire simulation horizon cannot capture.

Key Takeaways

✓The vector SDE dX_t=μdt+ΣdW_t generalizes to n correlated processes. W_t's components are independent by convention — all correlation is introduced through the matrix Σ.
✓ΣΣᵀ is the instantaneous covariance matrix of the whole vector process. One identity carries the entire content of "how correlation enters a multivariate SDE."
✓Cholesky decomposition (ρ=LLᵀ) is the standard practical recipe. Mix independent shocks as dW_correlated=L·dW_independent to realize any valid target correlation matrix.
✓Heston's ρ is already this recipe's simplest (2×2) case. heston-model.html's single scalar ρ is a Cholesky factor, just not named as one there.
✓Realized correlation converges to target ρ as path count grows, confirmed numerically here. 0.655 at N=50 paths settling to 0.600–0.606 by N≥5,000 — ordinary Monte Carlo convergence, not a special property of correlated simulation.

Section 05

Self-test

Q1. In the vector SDE dX_t=μ(X_t,t)dt+Σ(X_t,t)dW_t, are the components of W_t correlated with each other?
No — by convention, W_t's components are taken to be independent standard Brownian motions. All correlation between the different processes in X_t is introduced entirely through the matrix Σ, which mixes those independent shocks together. This separates "how much randomness" (the magnitude structure of Σ) from "how it correlates across processes" (Σ's off-diagonal mixing) into one object.
Q2. What is the Cholesky factor L of a target correlation matrix ρ, and what equation defines it?
L is a lower-triangular matrix satisfying ρ=LLᵀ. Given a valid (symmetric, positive semi-definite, unit-diagonal) correlation matrix ρ, such an L always exists and is unique with positive diagonal entries. Correlated shocks are then generated as dW_correlated = L·dW_independent, where dW_independent is a vector of independent standard Brownian increments.
Q3. Write out the 2×2 Cholesky factor for a target correlation ρ between two processes, and show it reproduces the target correlation exactly.
L = [[1,0],[ρ,√(1-ρ²)]], giving dW¹_corr = dW¹_ind and dW²_corr = ρ·dW¹_ind + √(1-ρ²)·dW²_ind. Checking: Var(dW²_corr) = ρ²dt + (1-ρ²)dt = dt (correct unit variance), and Corr(dW¹_corr, dW²_corr) = E[dW¹_ind(ρ·dW¹_ind+√(1-ρ²)·dW²_ind)]/dt = ρ exactly, since dW¹_ind and dW²_ind are independent (so their cross term vanishes) and dW¹_ind has variance dt.
Q4. A simulation of two GBM processes correlated via Cholesky with target ρ=0.6 comes back with a realized sample correlation of 0.66 using only 50 simulated paths. Does this mean the Cholesky decomposition was done incorrectly?
Not necessarily. This exact scenario is shown explicitly in this lesson's worked example: at N=50 paths, ordinary Monte Carlo sampling noise alone produced a realized correlation of 0.655 against a target of 0.6. The realized correlation is itself an estimate from a finite sample and carries its own sampling error, which shrinks like 1/√N as the number of paths increases — by N=5,000 or more the estimate typically settles close to the target. A gap at N=50 is expected noise, not evidence of a coding error, though it's still worth checking with a larger N before concluding the implementation is correct.
Q5. Heston's model correlates its price process dW_t¹ and variance process dW_t² using a single scalar ρ. How does this relate to the general Cholesky machinery in this lesson?
Heston's single ρ is exactly the simplest (2×2) case of the Cholesky recipe: with only two processes, the Cholesky factor of the 2×2 correlation matrix [[1,ρ],[ρ,1]] is L=[[1,0],[ρ,√(1-ρ²)]], and Heston's construction (dW_t² correlated with dW_t¹ via a single ρ) is precisely dW²_corr = ρ·dW¹_ind + √(1-ρ²)·dW²_ind from this factor. Heston's model was built (and is usually taught) without ever naming this as "Cholesky," but it is the identical mathematical object, just specialized to n=2.
