Hierarchical Bayesian Models & Partial Pooling

Five strategies, five trade counts, five noisy Sharpe estimates — should the one with 6 lucky-looking trades really be trusted as much as the one with 400? Partial pooling is the principled answer between "trust every estimate fully" and "ignore the differences entirely."

CONCEPT — explained here, not a claim this repo has built or validated it ~17 min read Prereqs: Conjugate Priors & Bayesian Updating in Practice
TL;DR
  • There are three ways to estimate several related group means at once: complete pooling (ignore group differences, one shared number), no pooling (estimate every group in total isolation), and partial pooling (the hierarchical compromise between them).
  • The hierarchical model states this precisely: \(y_{ij} \sim N(\theta_j, \sigma^2)\) at the observation level, with group parameters themselves drawn from a shared hyper-distribution, \(\theta_j \sim N(\mu, \tau^2)\).
  • The resulting shrinkage estimator is the same precision-weighted average from the Normal-Normal conjugacy lesson, applied per group: \(\hat\theta_j = B_j\,\bar{y}_j + (1-B_j)\,\mu\), where \(B_j\) grows toward 1 as that group's own sample size grows.
  • Worked example: five strategies with wildly different trade counts (5 to 50 trades) and raw Sharpe estimates get pulled toward a common grand mean by very different amounts — the smallest samples move the most.
  • This is the same idea behind the James-Stein estimator's famous, provable result: shrinking several noisy estimates toward each other, even when the underlying things being estimated have nothing to do with one another, reduces total estimation error.
Section 01

Why this matters

The Conjugate Priors & Bayesian Updating in Practice lesson derived the Normal-Normal posterior for a single parameter, combining one prior with one batch of data. Real portfolios and research programs almost never involve just one parameter — they involve several related ones at once: five currency pairs' true expected returns, ten strategies' true Sharpe ratios, a dozen analysts' true forecast skill. Each of these has its own noisy sample estimate, and — critically — each sample is a wildly different size. A strategy traded for two years has hundreds of trades behind its Sharpe estimate; one launched last month might have six.

The naive approach — trust every group's own raw sample average and treat all of them as equally reliable — badly overstates confidence in the small-sample groups. The other naive approach — pool everything into one number and ignore the fact that groups differ at all — throws away real, informative differences the larger samples have earned the right to show. Hierarchical Bayesian models formalize the principled middle ground: let each group have its own parameter, but assume those parameters are themselves drawn from a shared distribution, so that a noisy estimate borrows strength from the rest of the group. The Sharpe, Sortino, Calmar & the Deflated Sharpe Ratio lesson already flagged that a single strategy's reported Sharpe needs a minimum track-record length before it's trustworthy in isolation; this lesson is the natural next question — what do you do about several strategies' Sharpe ratios at once, especially when some have barely any history at all?

💡
Plain-English intuition

A rookie's batting average shouldn't be taken at face value

A baseball player who goes 3-for-4 in their first four at-bats of the season has a .750 batting average — but nobody believes they're actually a .750 hitter; the number is nearly all noise from a tiny sample. A player who's gone 120-for-400 over a full season, hitting .300, has earned much more trust in that number reflecting something real. If you had to guess both players' true underlying ability today, the honest move isn't to take either number at face value or to ignore both and guess the league average for everyone — it's to pull the rookie's wild .750 most of the way back toward a sensible league-average expectation (because four at-bats tell you almost nothing), while barely adjusting the established player's .300 (because 400 at-bats is real evidence). That "pull toward the average, by an amount that depends on how little data you have" is exactly what partial pooling formalizes — not as a vague gut instinct, but as an exact, computable weighted average.

Section 02

The math, step by step

Suppose there are \(J\) groups (strategies, pairs, analysts), and group \(j\) has \(n_j\) individual observations \(y_{1j},\dots,y_{n_j j}\) (per-trade returns, say). Three distinct modeling choices are available for estimating each group's true underlying parameter \(\theta_j\) (its true mean return, or true Sharpe):

Complete pooling

Assume every group shares exactly the same true parameter — \(\theta_1=\theta_2=\dots=\theta_J=\mu\). Pool all observations from every group into one giant sample and estimate a single grand mean. Group differences are treated as pure noise and discarded entirely — a strategy with 400 trades and one with 6 trades contribute to (and receive) the exact same estimate.

No pooling

Assume every group's parameter is entirely unrelated to the others — estimate each \(\theta_j\) using only group \(j\)'s own data, as if the other groups didn't exist. A group with 6 trades gets exactly as much (mis)placed confidence in its raw sample average as one with 400 — the small-sample noise is never corrected for.

Partial pooling — the hierarchical model — sits between these two extremes by treating the group parameters themselves as random draws from a shared distribution, rather than fixing them to be either identical or unrelated. Stated explicitly, at two levels:

\[ y_{ij} \sim N(\theta_j,\ \sigma^2) \qquad\qquad \theta_j \sim N(\mu,\ \tau^2) \]
y_ijobservation i within group j (e.g. a per-trade return), scattered around that group's own true mean θⱼ with noise σ²
θⱼ ~ N(μ,τ²)the hierarchical layer — each group's true parameter is itself drawn from a shared distribution centered at grand mean μ, with between-group spread τ²

This is why it's called hierarchical: there are two levels of randomness stacked on top of each other — individual observations vary around their group's mean, and group means themselves vary around a shared grand mean. Crucially, \(\mu\) (the grand mean) and \(\tau^2\) (how much true group means actually differ from each other) are not fixed in advance — they are estimated from the full collection of groups (commonly by method-of-moments or maximum likelihood on the observed group means, an estimation step this lesson names but doesn't derive in full — the hyperparameters used below are stated as given, illustrative values). Once \(\mu\) and \(\tau^2\) are in hand, group \(j\)'s posterior mean, given its own sample mean \(\bar{y}_j\) (with sampling variance \(\sigma^2/n_j\)), is exactly the Normal-Normal precision-weighted average from the previous lesson — except the "prior" is now the shared hyper-distribution instead of a hand-picked belief:

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

"Hierarchical" just means there are two layers of randomness stacked on top of each other, like a company's org chart. At the bottom, each employee's daily output wobbles around their own personal average — that's the observation level. One level up, those personal averages themselves aren't totally random either; they cluster around a company-wide average, because everyone works under similar conditions. Estimating a group's true number well means using information from both layers — its own track record, and what "typical" looks like across the whole group.

\[ \hat\theta_j = B_j\,\bar{y}_j + (1-B_j)\,\mu, \qquad B_j = \frac{\tau^2}{\tau^2 + \sigma^2/n_j} \]

Notice \(B_j\) recovers both extremes as limiting cases, which is the unifying insight of this whole lesson: as \(\tau^2 \to 0\) (assuming groups don't really differ at all), \(B_j\to 0\) for every group and \(\hat\theta_j\to\mu\) for all \(j\) — complete pooling. As \(\tau^2\to\infty\) (assuming groups could differ arbitrarily, so the shared structure carries no information), \(B_j\to 1\) and \(\hat\theta_j\to\bar{y}_j\) — no pooling. Partial pooling is what happens for any finite, positive \(\tau^2\) estimated honestly from the data — the two "pure" strategies are just its endpoints, not separate, unrelated ideas.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

\(B_j\) is a trust dial between 0 and 1, one per group. Turn it all the way to 0 and you're saying "ignore this group's own data completely, just use the overall average" (complete pooling). Turn it all the way to 1 and you're saying "trust this group's own number completely, the overall average tells me nothing extra" (no pooling). The formula sets that dial automatically based on how much real data the group actually has — a small sample gets a dial stuck near 0, a big sample gets a dial that swings toward 1.

The James-Stein connection: James & Stein (1961) proved something genuinely surprising — when estimating three or more group means simultaneously under total squared-error loss, shrinking every raw estimate toward a common target by a factor like \(B_j\) above achieves strictly lower total expected error than using each group's own raw average, even if the groups' true parameters have nothing to do with each other. This isn't an assumption that the groups are related in reality — it's a mathematical fact about how squared-error loss behaves in three-or-more dimensions at once, popularized by Efron & Morris's "Stein's Paradox" using baseball batting averages.
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

The strange part of James-Stein is this: even if you're simultaneously guessing three totally unrelated things — say a friend's height, a stranger's favorite number, and the temperature in a city you've never visited — you can still get a better combined score, on average, by nudging all three of your noisy guesses slightly toward each other's overall average, instead of trusting each individual guess fully on its own. It sounds like it shouldn't work because the three things have nothing to do with each other, but it's a proven mathematical fact once you're scored on total error across three or more estimates at once, not each one separately.

Worked example

Five strategies, five trade counts, one shared shrinkage rule

Assume (for illustration) an estimated grand mean \(\mu=0.70\) and between-group variance \(\tau^2=0.25\) across a pool of strategies, with each group's own sampling variance approximated simply as \(\sigma^2/n_j\) using \(\sigma^2=1\) (a simplifying stand-in for the true, more involved standard error of a Sharpe ratio estimate — stated plainly as a simplification, not the exact formula). Five strategies, each with a raw ("no pooling") Sharpe estimate \(\bar{y}_j\) and a trade count \(n_j\):

  • Strategy A — \(n=5\) trades, raw Sharpe \(\bar{y}=2.10\). \(B_A=\frac{0.25}{0.25+1/5}=\frac{0.25}{0.45}=0.556\). Shrunk: \(0.556(2.10)+0.444(0.70)=1.167+0.311=\mathbf{1.478}\).
  • Strategy B — \(n=50\) trades, raw Sharpe \(\bar{y}=0.85\). \(B_B=\frac{0.25}{0.25+1/50}=\frac{0.25}{0.27}=0.926\). Shrunk: \(0.926(0.85)+0.074(0.70)=0.787+0.052=\mathbf{0.839}\).
  • Strategy C — \(n=8\) trades, raw Sharpe \(\bar{y}=-0.60\). \(B_C=\frac{0.25}{0.25+1/8}=\frac{0.25}{0.375}=0.667\). Shrunk: \(0.667(-0.60)+0.333(0.70)=-0.400+0.233=\mathbf{-0.167}\).
  • Strategy D — \(n=40\) trades, raw Sharpe \(\bar{y}=0.75\). \(B_D=\frac{0.25}{0.25+1/40}=\frac{0.25}{0.275}=0.909\). Shrunk: \(0.909(0.75)+0.091(0.70)=0.682+0.064=\mathbf{0.745}\).
  • Strategy E — \(n=6\) trades, raw Sharpe \(\bar{y}=1.90\). \(B_E=\frac{0.25}{0.25+1/6}=\frac{0.25}{0.4167}=0.600\). Shrunk: \(0.600(1.90)+0.400(0.70)=1.140+0.280=\mathbf{1.420}\).

The pattern is exactly what the shrinkage weight formula predicts: the two small-sample strategies with the flashiest raw numbers — A (2.10 → 1.478) and E (1.90 → 1.420) — get pulled down hardest, because their \(n_j\) is small and \(B_j\) is furthest from 1. Strategy C's ugly-looking \(-0.60\) gets pulled up toward positive territory (to \(-0.167\)), for the same reason — a small, unlucky-looking sample is untrustworthy in either direction. Meanwhile B and D, with real trade histories (50 and 40), barely move at all (0.85→0.839, 0.75→0.745) — their raw estimates had already earned most of the trust the grand mean would otherwise contribute. No pooling would have taken A's 2.10 and E's 1.90 completely at face value; complete pooling would have thrown away B and D's hard-earned, largely-correct differences from the pack entirely. Partial pooling did neither.

🎯
Real-world scenario

Sizing a multi-strategy book without over-trusting the newest strategy

A systematic allocator runs five sub-strategies side by side and wants to size positions roughly in proportion to each one's expected Sharpe ratio — more capital to the strategies that look better risk-adjusted. Using raw, no-pooling Sharpe estimates directly (as in the worked example above) would send an outsized allocation to Strategy A purely because its first 5 trades happened to look spectacular — precisely the kind of small-sample noise the Deflated Sharpe Ratio lesson already warns inflates a reported Sharpe when many variations get tried. Using the partially pooled estimates instead means Strategy A only receives an allocation appropriate to its real, much more modest 1.478 shrunk estimate until more trades accumulate and its own data can (if it holds up) earn back the higher allocation on its own merit. This describes the logic of the approach, not a claim that this specific allocation rule has been built, backtested, or is running anywhere in this repo — it would still need to prove itself out of sample before being trusted with real capital.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

Hierarchical models and empirical-Bayes shrinkage are a standard tool anywhere many related, unequally-sized samples need to be compared fairly:

⚾

Sports analytics

The original James-Stein/empirical-Bayes showcase: shrinking early-season batting averages toward the league mean predicts end-of-season performance far better than trusting small-sample averages at face value.

🗺️

Small-area estimation

Public-health and survey statisticians routinely need a rate (disease incidence, unemployment) for every small region, even when some regions were barely sampled — partial pooling across regions is the standard fix.

🧪

Many-variant A/B testing

Testing dozens of website variants or ad creatives at once means most variants only get modest traffic — hierarchical shrinkage keeps a lucky-looking, low-traffic variant from being declared a winner prematurely.

The common theme: whenever there are many groups, unequal sample sizes, and a real (if unproven) reason to believe the groups are at least somewhat related, partial pooling produces estimates with lower total error than either extreme. It costs something too — a hierarchical model needs \(\mu\) and \(\tau^2\) themselves estimated (adding its own layer of uncertainty, and typically requiring the simulation-based tools from MCMC: Metropolis-Hastings & Gibbs Sampling once the model is more complex than the simple Normal case shown here), and it assumes the groups share enough real structure for the "shared hyper-distribution" framing to be meaningful at all — a handful of strategies built on genuinely unrelated market mechanisms might not belong in the same hierarchy in the first place.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where partial pooling quietly goes wrong

Pooling groups that shouldn't be pooled. The entire benefit of partial pooling depends on the groups genuinely being drawn from a common shared distribution. Shrinking a carry-trade strategy's Sharpe estimate toward a grand mean computed from four unrelated momentum strategies assumes a shared structure that may not exist — the wrong grouping can make estimates worse, not better, than no pooling at all.

Treating the empirical-Bayes hyperparameters as free of uncertainty. \(\mu\) and \(\tau^2\) are themselves estimated from a finite number of groups (often frustratingly few — five or ten strategies is a small sample for estimating a between-group variance). Point-estimating them and plugging them in, as this lesson's worked example does for clarity, understates the true uncertainty compared to a fully Bayesian treatment that puts its own prior on \(\mu\) and \(\tau^2\) and integrates over them.

Confusing shrinkage with validation. A partially pooled Sharpe estimate is a more honest point estimate given the data collected so far — it is not evidence the strategy will perform this way going forward. The same in-sample/out-of-sample discipline this curriculum applies everywhere else still applies to whatever number comes out of a hierarchical model.

Forgetting that τ²=0 and τ²=∞ are themselves modeling choices, not neutral defaults. Complete pooling and no pooling both quietly assume something extreme about the world (identical groups, or totally unrelated groups); the estimated, finite \(\tau^2\) in a real hierarchical fit is doing real modeling work, not avoiding it.

Section 04

Key Takeaways

✓Complete pooling, no pooling, and partial pooling are one spectrum, not three unrelated ideas — they're the τ²→0, τ²→∞, and finite-τ² cases of the same hierarchical model.
✓The shrinkage formula is the Normal-Normal precision-weighted average again, just applied per group with the shared hyper-distribution standing in for a hand-picked prior.
✓Small samples get shrunk hardest, in either direction. A flashy small-n estimate and an ugly-looking small-n estimate both get pulled toward the group mean by roughly the same mechanism.
⚠Pooling assumes the groups actually share structure. Shrinking unrelated strategies toward each other's mean can make estimates worse, not better — the grouping choice is itself a modeling assumption.
⚠Point-estimating μ and τ² understates uncertainty. A fully Bayesian treatment puts its own prior on the hyperparameters rather than plugging in a single estimated value, as this lesson's worked example simplifies to for clarity.
✗A shrunk estimate is a better point estimate, not a validated forecast. It still needs the same true OOS proof this curriculum demands everywhere else before it justifies real capital allocation.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. What distinguishes complete pooling, no pooling, and partial pooling?
Complete pooling assumes every group shares exactly one true parameter and estimates a single number from all the data combined, discarding group differences entirely. No pooling assumes every group's parameter is unrelated to the others and estimates each one using only that group's own data, ignoring any shared structure. Partial pooling (the hierarchical model) treats group parameters as random draws from a shared distribution, producing a shrinkage estimate that blends each group's own data with the overall pattern across groups — with complete pooling and no pooling as its two limiting cases.
Q2. In the hierarchical model y_ij ~ N(θⱼ,σ²), θⱼ ~ N(μ,τ²), what does each level of randomness represent?
The first level, y_ij ~ N(θⱼ,σ²), says individual observations within group j scatter around that group's own true mean θⱼ with variance σ². The second level, θⱼ ~ N(μ,τ²), says the group means themselves aren't fixed, unrelated numbers — they're drawn from a shared distribution centered at grand mean μ with between-group variance τ², which is what lets information flow between groups during estimation.
Q3. Why does a group with a small sample size get shrunk more heavily toward the grand mean than a group with a large sample size?
Because the shrinkage weight Bⱼ = τ²/(τ²+σ²/nⱼ) depends on nⱼ through the group's own sampling variance σ²/nⱼ. A small nⱼ makes σ²/nⱼ large, so it dominates the denominator and pushes Bⱼ toward 0 — meaning most of the weight in the blended estimate falls on the grand mean μ rather than the noisy raw estimate ȳⱼ. A large nⱼ makes σ²/nⱼ small, pushing Bⱼ toward 1 and putting most of the weight on that group's own, better-supported raw average.
Q4. What is the "surprising" part of the James-Stein result?
That shrinking several group estimates toward a common target reduces total expected squared error even when the groups' true underlying parameters have nothing to do with each other in reality — it isn't a claim that the groups are secretly related, but a mathematical fact about how squared-error loss behaves once three or more parameters are being estimated simultaneously. This defied statisticians' initial intuition that each parameter should simply be estimated on its own terms, independent of the others.
Q5. Why isn't a partially pooled Sharpe estimate the same thing as evidence that a strategy will keep performing that way?
Because shrinkage only improves the honesty of a point estimate given the data already collected — it corrects for small-sample noise in what's been observed, it does not add any information about whether the future will resemble the past. A strategy's true edge, shrunk or not, still has to be demonstrated on genuinely new, out-of-sample data before it earns trust, exactly as the house discipline in this curriculum requires for every other kind of result.
