Market Microstructure Invariance, One Ruler for Every Market

A $2 billion mega-cap and a $200 million micro-cap trade at wildly different sizes and costs. Kyle & Obizhaeva's claim: that's an illusion of the wrong ruler. Section 2

The wrong ruler makes every market look unique

A bowling ball in a bathtub, a pebble in a swimming pool — splash height alone tells you almost nothing.

The intuition

Comparing raw share counts, or even raw dollar sizes, across a mega-cap and a micro-cap is comparing splash heights without accounting for the size of the pool. Kyle & Obizhaeva's fix: measure a trade in dollars of risk actually transferred — size × price × volatility — and measure the market's own capacity to absorb risk the same way. Once both sides share that currency, the bowling ball and the pebble stop looking so different.

Kyle's Lambda (already covered)

One insider, one market, one linear impact coefficient λ=σ_v/(2σ_u), with σ_v and σ_u given for that specific asset. Answers: "for this market, how much does trading more cost?"

Invariance (this deck)

A cross-sectional claim spanning every asset at once: once trade size is measured in risk-transfer units and rescaled by an asset's own trading activity W, the distribution of standardized sizes is the same everywhere.

Bet size, in dollars of risk

This lesson's central object — read it as the dollar standard deviation of value the bet exposes someone to.

Q

Quantity

The bet's size in shares, contracts, or units.

P

Price

Converts shares into dollars — the obvious first fix, but not the whole story.

σ

Volatility

Daily volatility throughout — folds in how much that dollar notional actually moves.

$10,000 in a sleepy savings account and $10,000 on a single spin of roulette are the same size in dollars — not remotely the same size in risk. Two bets with identical L put an identical amount of genuine price risk on the table, whatever their share counts or underlying instruments.

V is average daily share (or contract) volume — the same P and σ used to build one bet's L above, applied to the whole market instead.
Plain-English

W is a single number for "how much of a fire hose is this market" — how much dollar risk flows through it in an ordinary day, purely from everyone's regular buying and selling. A firehose market swallows a huge bet without fuss; a garden-hose market chokes on the same bet. Every quantity this lesson discusses — typical bet size, bets placed per day, cost of trading a given bet — is hypothesized to be a predictable function of this one number.

Section 5

Bets per day scale as W^(2/3); typical bet size only as W^(1/3)

Both sub-linear and concave, the same qualitative shape as the square-root law of impact, applied to different quantities. Most extra activity is more bets, not bigger ones.

Try it
A market with 10× the trading activity W does not have 10× the typical bet size. Its bets are only 101/3 ≈ 2.15× bigger, and there are 102/3 ≈ 4.64× more of them per day.
A hundred-fold jump in W makes typical bets only 1001/3 ≈ 4.64× bigger, with 1002/3 ≈ 21.5× more of them per day.
A thousand-fold jump in W makes typical bets exactly 1,0001/3 = 10× bigger and bets per day 1,0002/3 = 100× more numerous: 1,000 = 10³, and 10 × 100 = 1,000.
Visualize it
All three lines start from the same point by construction — the comparison is entirely in the slopes, and the two invariance slopes add up to the 1-to-1 line. Axes are illustrative and unitless, not calibrated to real dollar figures.
Section 6

Worked example: 1,042× apart in activity, 10× apart in typical bet size, 103× in bets per day

Asset A — mega-cap: P=$150, V=50M shares, 25% annualized vol. Asset B — small-cap: P=$20, V=200K shares, 45% annualized vol.

Visualize it
Bar heights are on a shared log scale (illustrative indices, not literal dollars or bet counts). The activity gap splits in two: 1,042^(1/3) ≈ 10.1 for typical bet size and 1,042^(2/3) ≈ 102.8 for bets per day, and 10.1 × 102.8 ≈ 1,042.

Even at the same percentage of ADV (1%), the square-root law alone says the small-cap costs roughly 1.8× more than the mega-cap (28.3 bps vs. 15.7 bps). Invariance adds that 1% of ADV is not the same-sized bet in the two names. A typical bet's share of volume scales as W^(−2/3), so B's typical bet is about 103× larger a share of B's volume than A's is of A's. The same 1%-of-ADV order is a far more ordinary bet in the thin name than in the busy one.

Section 7

The full hypothesis: one shared distribution, not just one center

z = L / W^(1/3) = (Q/V) · W^(2/3) — a specific bet's size, standardized by its own market's activity.

Just the scaling law
  • Only pins down the typical bet size, L* ∝ W^(1/3), and bets per day, γ ∝ W^(2/3)
  • Says nothing about spread or tail behavior
  • A much weaker claim than invariance proper
The full invariance hypothesis
  • The entire distribution of z, not just its center
  • Shape, spread, and tails claimed shared across stocks, venues, eras
  • W is the only asset-specific ingredient needed to translate it
Where cost fits in

The square-root law from the Almgren-Chriss lesson, I ≈ Yσ√(Q/ADV), already gives expected cost for one asset. Invariance adds the cross-sectional layer: because z is meant to sit at the same statistical position in every market's own distribution, a bet with a given z should carry a broadly comparable expected cost, in risk-normalized terms, no matter which asset it's placed in. That layer — not the square-root law itself — is the new claim here.

Section 9

How practitioners use this — and where it breaks

🔀

Cross-asset cost prediction

Given only price, volume, and volatility, invariance implies an expected order-size distribution and cost profile with no name-specific trading history required.

✂️

Sizing order slices sensibly

A typical bet's share of volume scales as W^(−2/3): larger in thin markets, smaller in busy ones. The same %ADV is routine in one name and outsized in another, so compare orders by z, not one fixed ratio.

🌍

Extending Kyle's Lambda across a universe

Where Kyle treats σ_v and σ_u as separately given per asset, invariance is a falsifiable theory of how those quantities — and the bet sizes they support — compare across a whole universe of assets.

Common pitfalls

Not an exact physical law — an empirical regularity with real scatter around it in Kyle & Obizhaeva's own data. Not the same claim as the square-root law — that's about one asset's own cost scaling with trade size; invariance is the cross-sectional claim about how typical sizes compare across assets. Says nothing about transient dynamics — how impact decays moment to moment once a bet stops trading is a separate question, picked up by the next lesson in this category.

Section 10

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Why is bet size defined as L = Q·P·σ instead of stopping at dollar notional Q·P?

Two bets with identical L carry an identical amount of genuine price risk, even if their share counts, dollar notionals, and underlying instruments look nothing alike — that's the whole point of folding volatility in directly.
Question 2

What is W = P·V·σ, and why does invariance need only this one number per asset?

W is the "how much of a firehose is this market" number. Given a market's own W, invariance claims the shape of its standardized bet-size distribution follows a distribution shared across every market — no separate calibration of that shape needed.
Question 3

Two assets differ in trading activity W by 1,000×. By roughly how much should their typical bet sizes (in risk units) differ, per the invariance scaling law?

W^(2/3) is the number of bets per day, not their size. Typical bet size grows only as W^(1/3), so a 1,000× activity gap splits into bets about 10× bigger and about 100× more of them. This deck's own worked example: ~1,042× in W, ~10.1× in L*, ~103× in bets per day.
Question 4

How does invariance relate to Kyle's (1985) single-asset linear price-impact model, λ = σ_v / (2σ_u)?

Neither a replacement nor a contradiction. Kyle's model is single-asset and single-market; invariance answers a question Kyle's model was never built to address — how those parameters compare across a whole universe of instruments.
Question 5

What does invariance say nothing about, and which lesson in this category picks up exactly that gap?

Invariance sets the statistical scale of a typical bet and its average cost; it's silent on the time-path of impact within one trade. That's precisely the subject of the next lesson in this Liquidity category.
Section 11

What to carry forward

01

Measuring in the wrong unit makes markets look falsely unique — raw share counts and dollar sizes hide a shared pattern that shows up once bet size is L = Q·P·σ.

02

W = P·V·σ is the one number summarizing a market's trading activity — the sole asset-specific input the invariance hypothesis needs.

03

Bets per day scale as W^(2/3) and typical bet size only as W^(1/3) — a 1,000× activity gap means about 100× more bets but only about 10× bigger ones, so in thin names each bet is a larger share of volume.

04

The full hypothesis is stronger than the scaling laws alone: the entire distribution of standardized size z = L/W^(1/3) is claimed to be shared across assets, venues, and eras.

05

This extends Kyle's (1985) single-asset Lambda into a cross-sectional theory — and it's an empirical regularity with real scatter, silent on transient impact dynamics.

06

What this now lets you do

Compute a new, unfamiliar instrument's own W from nothing but its price, volume, and volatility, and read off a principled starting estimate of its typical bet size and cost — instead of guessing, or waiting for a history of its own trades to accumulate.
