Base Rates First

Before you ask what is special about this setup, ask how often this kind of thing happens at all. This lesson shows how to pick a reference class, start from its frequency, and then move only as far as the evidence earns. Done properly, it turns "this looks like a turn" into a probability you can price.

CONCEPT — explained here; desk verdicts quoted where tested ~20 min read Prereqs: Bayesian inference (helpful); Thinking in Expected Value (helpful)
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide. It covers the same ground as short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • Start from the base rate: how often the outcome happens across a reference class of similar situations, before anything about this case.
  • Then adjust with Bayes, in odds: posterior odds = prior odds × likelihood ratio. The likelihood ratio is how much more often you'd see this evidence if the outcome is coming than if it isn't.
  • A hit rate means nothing without its base rate. "Followed by a rally 54% of the time" is no edge at all if rallies follow 54% of all days.
  • Rare events stay rare. A pattern that shows up before 60% of turns and on 20% of ordinary days moves a 5% base rate to only 13.6%.
  • Pick the reference class with care. Too broad and it isn't relevant. Too narrow and it's a handful of cases, so shrink those cases toward the broader rate.
  • Desk verdicts: the desk's own "base rate" results are exactly this method. A rates-pivot signal hit 54.1% against a 54.1% base rate. A post-FOMC dollar drift held 65% against 49% and survived a placebo. "Dr Copper" failed because it had a hit rate with no false-alarm rate.
Section 01

Why this matters

Expected value needs a probability, and the honest way to get one starts with a base rate. Scoring your calls shows how people's probabilities go wrong in practice. The most common reason is that they started from the story instead of the frequency. Bayesian inference gives the formal machinery. This lesson is the working habit: name the reference class, look up its rate, write it down, and only then read the chart.

It is also the habit behind every honest backtest. A signal's hit rate, a pattern's success rate, or an event's typical reaction is only meaningful next to what happens without the signal. Much of the desk's research comes down to computing that comparison, which is why its ledger has a whole verdict type called "base rate".

💡
Plain-English intuition

The outside view first, then the inside view

There are two ways to estimate how likely something is. The inside view looks at the case in front of you: the chart, the story, the reasons it should work. The outside view ignores the details and asks how often things like this have worked before. The inside view feels like analysis. The outside view feels like giving up on the details.

Research on forecasting keeps finding that the outside view is the better place to start, and that people skip it. A vivid case crowds out the boring frequency. The fix is mechanical: write the base rate down first, before you look at the specifics. Then let the specifics move you, by an amount you can justify.

How it reached trading

Line up the events and count

Base rates · into finance via the event study (Fama, Fisher, Jensen & Roll, 1969); named as the "outside view" by Kahneman & Lovallo (1993)

Before 1969, claims about how prices react to news were mostly anecdote. Fama, Fisher, Jensen and Roll changed that with a study of stock splits. They took many split announcements, lined them up on the announcement date, and measured what the average stock did before and after compared with what it would normally have done. That is a base-rate method: define a class of events, count what usually happens, and compare it with what happens anyway. The event study became one of the standard tools of empirical finance and of desk research.

Psychology supplied the warning that people skip this step. Kahneman and Tversky's work in the 1970s on "base-rate neglect" showed that people judge probability by how well a case fits a story and largely ignore how common the outcome is. Kahneman and Lovallo (1993) named the remedy the outside view. Superforecasters in Tetlock's forecasting tournaments were later described as starting from it routinely.

On a desk, base rates show up as "what usually happens after X" tables: post-event drifts, seasonal profiles, gap-fill rates, the hit rate a random entry would get. They are the yardstick every signal is measured against, and the starting point for every probability that goes into expected value.

Section 02

The math, step by step

1. A base rate is a frequency in a reference class

Choose a reference class: a set of past situations similar enough to today that their outcomes are informative. The base rate is the share where the outcome happened:

\[ p_0 = \frac{\#\{\text{cases in the class where the outcome happened}\}}{\#\{\text{cases in the class}\}} \]
p₀the base rate, or prior: your probability before today's specifics
classwhich past cases count as "like this one"; the choice matters most

Examples: the share of weeks EUR/USD closed higher; the share of Monday gaps that filled the same day; the hit rate a random entry gets with your stop and target, which is \(S/(S+T)\) from the EV lesson.

2. Bayes in words, then in odds

New evidence \(E\) (a pattern, a print, a signal) should move you from \(p_0\) to a posterior. Bayes' rule says by how much:

\[ P(H \mid E) = \frac{P(E \mid H)\,P(H)}{P(E \mid H)\,P(H) + P(E \mid \neg H)\,P(\neg H)} \]
P(H)the base rate p₀: how often the outcome happens at all
P(E|H)hit rate: how often you see this evidence before the outcome
P(E|¬H)false-alarm rate: how often you see it when the outcome doesn't follow
P(H|E)the posterior: the probability once you've seen the evidence

The same rule is far easier to use in odds, where it becomes one multiplication:

\[ \underbrace{\frac{P(H\mid E)}{P(\neg H\mid E)}}_{\text{posterior odds}} \;=\; \underbrace{\frac{P(H)}{P(\neg H)}}_{\text{prior odds}} \;\times\; \underbrace{\frac{P(E\mid H)}{P(E\mid \neg H)}}_{\text{likelihood ratio, LR}} \]
prior odds
Where you start
The base rate as odds: 20% is 1 to 4
LR
How strong the evidence is
Hit rate ÷ false-alarm rate. LR = 1 tells you nothing
posterior odds
Where you end
Convert back: odds a:b → probability a/(a+b)

The odds form makes three things obvious. Evidence with \(\text{LR} = 1\) doesn't move you, however impressive its hit rate looks. Evidence with \(\text{LR} = 3\) triples your odds, which is a big move from 1:1 and a small one from 1:19. And a hit rate alone is never enough, because the false-alarm rate is half of the ratio.

3. Two worked updates

The cab problem (Tversky and Kahneman's classic). 15% of a city's cabs are Blue and 85% Green. A witness says the cab in an accident was Blue, and witnesses identify colours correctly 80% of the time. Most people answer 80%. The base rate says otherwise. Prior odds are 15:85, the LR is 0.8/0.2 = 4, so posterior odds are 60:85 and the probability is \(60/145 = 41\%\). It is still more likely Green.

A trading version. Define a "turn" as a day that starts a move of a set size. In your history, 5% of days are turns. A reversal pattern appeared before 60% of turns, and also on 20% of ordinary days. Prior odds 5:95 = 1:19. LR = 0.60/0.20 = 3. Posterior odds 3:19, so \(P = 3/22 = 13.6\%\). The pattern nearly tripled the probability, and the day is still about six times more likely not to be a turn. In natural frequencies: of 1,000 days, 50 are turns and 30 of them show the pattern; 950 are not, and 190 of those show it too. Of the 220 days with the pattern, 30 are turns.

4. Lift, and the hit rate that is just the base rate

Research write-ups often report a hit rate \(P(H \mid E)\) and sometimes a lift:

\[ \text{lift} = \frac{P(H \mid E)}{P(H)} \]
P(H|E)what happened after the signal
P(H)what happens anyway
lift1.0 means the signal adds nothing

A hit rate of 54% with a base rate of 54% has a lift of 1.0, and so does a hit rate of 90% when the base rate is 90%. Always ask for the second number before you react to the first.

5. Small reference classes: shrink toward a broader one

The more specific the class ("post-FOMC weeks with a hawkish surprise and the long end already up"), the more relevant it is and the fewer cases it holds. A narrow class with \(k\) hits in \(n\) cases is blended with a broader base rate \(p_0\) worth \(m\) pretend cases:

\[ \hat p = \frac{k + m\,p_0}{n + m}, \qquad \text{uncertainty} \approx \sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n + m + 1}} \]
k / nthe narrow class's own record
p₀the broader class's rate
mhow much you trust the broader class, in cases
p̂the estimate you carry forward, with its standard deviation

This is the Beta-prior posterior from Conjugate Priors, and the same shrinkage used in the EV lesson. It is also the reasoning behind hierarchical models: small groups borrow strength from the population they belong to.

\(\neg H\)The outcome doesn't happen.
\(E\)The evidence: a signal, pattern, print or analysis reading.

\(P(E \mid H)\)Hit rate (sensitivity): how often the evidence appears before the outcome.

Section 03

Choosing the reference class

Most of the judgement is here. The arithmetic afterwards is easy.

🔭

Too broad

"All trading days" is large and stable but may not resemble today. Use it as the outer anchor, the rate any narrower class has to beat.

🔬

Too narrow

"Hawkish surprise, curve steepening, oil up, a Tuesday" may hold seven cases. Its rate is mostly noise, so shrink it hard toward the broader class.

🪜

Use a ladder of classes

Compute the rate for two or three nested classes. If they agree, you're on firm ground. If the narrowest jumps far away on few cases, believe the broader ones.

🧪

Check the class is stable

Split it in halves (first half and second half of the sample). A base rate that differs between halves is describing a regime, not a constant.

Section 04

Worked example: the dollar after a Fed meeting

🧮
Worked example

"The Fed just surprised hawkish. Will the dollar be higher in five sessions?"

The inside view writes itself: hawkish surprise, higher yields, stronger dollar, so call it 80%. Here is the base-rate route, using the desk's own measured frequencies.

1

The question, exactly

The dollar basket (against the major pairs) higher over the next five sessions after an FOMC decision. A yes/no outcome with a fixed horizon.

2

Broad class: any five-session window

On non-FOMC windows the dollar basket rose 49% of the time. That is \(p_0\), close to a coin.

3

Narrow class: five sessions after any FOMC meeting

Over 81 meetings, the dollar rose 65% of the time, about 53 of 81 (65.4%). The raw 95% interval is wide: 54.6% to 74.9%. The desk checked it against 10,000 placebo dates and in both halves of the sample, and it held.

4

Shrink toward the broad rate

With \(m = 20\): \(\hat p = (53 + 20 \times 0.49)/(81 + 20) = 62.8/101 = 62.2\%\), with a 95% band of about 52.8% to 71.6%.

5

Adjust for today's specifics: "hawkish surprise"

This is where the inside view wants to add 20 points. The evidence says don't. The desk found the post-meeting drift doesn't depend on the meeting's outcome. Its table of hawkish-surprise meetings shows the dollar up 64% five sessions later, with a 95% interval of 43% to 86% on few meetings. That is the same rate as all meetings, so the evidence has an LR of about 1. Odds stay at \(0.622/0.378 = 1.65\), and the probability stays at 62%.

6

Round to what the data supports

About 60–65%, not 80%. That is a lean, not a conviction, and it is a calendar base rate, not a forecast for this particular meeting.

The usable output:

Fed into the EV card, 62% is a modest edge on a five-day hold, and its size has to be weighed against costs and the width of that band. The surprise itself, the thing the story leaned on, added nothing.

Section 05

It actually happened: the outside view, ignored

It actually happened

The textbook that took eight years

Daniel Kahneman tells this story about himself in Thinking, Fast and Slow (2011). In the 1970s he was part of a team writing a curriculum and textbook on judgement and decision-making for Israeli high schools. About a year in, he asked each member to estimate privately how long it would take to finish. The estimates clustered around two years.

He then asked the team's curriculum expert, Seymour Fox, how long similar teams had taken. Fox's answer, as Kahneman recounts it, was that about 40% of such teams never finished, and that none of those that did finished in less than about seven years. He also judged their own team to be somewhat below average. That was the outside view, and it contradicted everyone's inside view, including Fox's own.

The team carried on as if it hadn't heard. The book was finished about eight years later, by which time the ministry's interest had faded and it was never used. Kahneman's point was that the base rate was available and was asked for and given, and still nobody used it, because the specifics of their own case felt more real than other teams' statistics.

It actually happened

1973: "Tom W." and the stereotype

In "On the Psychology of Prediction" (1973), Kahneman and Tversky gave participants a short personality sketch of a graduate student, "Tom W.", written to fit the stereotype of a computer-science student, and asked them to rank how likely he was to be in each of several fields. Another group was simply asked what share of graduate students were in each field. The likelihood rankings followed how well Tom fitted the stereotype almost exactly, and the size of the fields made little difference. A sketch of uncertain reliability outweighed the base rates. The trading equivalent is a chart that "looks exactly like" a famous reversal, judged on resemblance rather than on how often that resemblance has been followed by a reversal.

Section 06

What this desk found when it tested the claims

The desk's research is largely base-rate work. Four results show the method from four angles.

Desk test: nothing found A 54% hit rate that was exactly the base rate. The claim: a confirmed turn in short-term rates leads the Nasdaq intraday, so you can confirm the pivot and pre-position. The desk tested 2,010 de-clustered events at one hour and found a signed return of −0.005% against a control interval of [−0.021, +0.012]. The test was well powered, so anything tradeable would have shown. The number that makes this lesson's point: a confirmed rates low was followed by an up-move 54.1% of the time, and the base rate is 54.1%. The lift is 1.0. On its own, 54% would have looked like an edge.
Desk test: held up A base rate that beat its base rate. The dollar basket over the five sessions after an FOMC meeting: N = 81 meetings, mean +26bp, 65% positive, against non-FOMC windows at −3bp and 49% positive. The event mean sat at the 99.8th percentile of 10,000 placebos, and both halves (2016–20, 2021–26) came in at +26bp. The ledger calls it the one directional base rate here that survived a placebo and two halves. It is to be quoted as a base rate, never as a call on a single meeting, and the meeting's outcome doesn't change it. Not forward-proven.
Desk test: nothing found A hit rate with no false-alarm rate. "Copper falls before every recession" is a claim about \(P(E\mid H)\) alone. Tested: 12 of the 14 negative US quarters since 1990 had no 15% copper fall in front of them, so the universal claim fails. At a looser 10% gate the signal fired 22 times with an 82% false-alarm rate and a lift of 1.77. With only 5 signals at the pre-registered gate, whether any weaker lift is real couldn't be tested. Without the denominator, "every single time" says almost nothing.
Desk data: base rate Choosing the right comparison class. The story: after a Fed decision, the first move is later unwound. Compared with non-FOMC days that had a day-0 move of the same size, the share giving back at least half within 20 sessions was the same (2-year 38% vs 39%, 10-year 50% vs 45%, dollar 48% vs 40%, S&P 500 45% vs 51%). A decision-day move has about even odds of being half-undone within a month, the same as any big day. The reference class that mattered was "big days", not "Fed days".
The trap: quoting a hit rate on its own. "Followed by a rally 54% of the time", "preceded every recession", "worked 8 of the last 10": each is one half of a likelihood ratio, or a rate with no base to compare against. The desk's rule is that no hit rate gets quoted without its base rate beside it.
Section 07

How practitioners actually use this

📊

Matched controls

Every event study compares "after the signal" with "after comparable non-signal moments", matched on volatility, time of day or move size. The control is the base rate.

🎲

Placebos

Run the same test on thousands of random dates. If the real result doesn't beat most placebos, the "edge" is the base rate (see multiple testing).

🗂️

A base-rate library

Gap fills, post-event drifts, day-of-week ranges, month-start widening. Kept as tables, quoted as frequencies, used as the starting point for every scenario.

✍️

Prior first, in writing

Write \(p_0\) in the journal before the analysis. It stops the story from quietly replacing the frequency, and it makes the later calibration score mean something.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where base-rate thinking goes wrong

Base-rate neglect. Judging by how well the case fits the story. The cure is to write \(p_0\) first.

Hit rate without false alarms. \(P(E\mid H)\) is half of the evidence. Without \(P(E\mid\neg H)\) you can't compute an LR.

Multiplying correlated evidence. Multiplying LRs assumes the pieces of evidence are independent. Three momentum indicators are one piece of evidence, not three (Combining Signals).

A class chosen after seeing the outcome. Picking the reference class that gives the answer you want is a form of p-hacking. Fix the class before you count.

A base rate from a different regime. A rate that differs between halves of the sample describes a period, not a constant. Check stability before you lean on it.

Key Takeaways

✓Write the base rate down first, from a named reference class, before you look at the specifics.
✓Posterior odds = prior odds × LR, with LR = hit rate ÷ false-alarm rate. LR = 1 means don't move.
✓Rare stays rare: LR 3 on a 5% base rate gives 13.6%. A 15% base rate and an 80% witness give 41%.
✓Shrink narrow classes toward broader ones with \((k + m\,p_0)/(n+m)\), and check the class is stable across halves.
✗The desk's rates-pivot test found nothing: a 54.1% hit rate against a 54.1% base rate. A hit rate without its base rate can look like an edge and be exactly nothing.
⚠The post-FOMC dollar drift (65% vs 49%) held up in the desk's test, and it is quoted as a base rate, not a call on any single meeting, and is not forward-proven.
Section 09

Self-test

Q1. 10% of days are "trend days". A filter flags 90% of trend days and 10% of other days. A day is flagged. How likely is it to be a trend day?
Prior odds 1:9. LR = 0.9/0.1 = 9. Posterior odds 9:9 = 1:1, so 50%. In natural frequencies: of 1,000 days, 100 trend days with 90 flagged; 900 others with 90 flagged. Half of the 180 flags are right.
Q2. A research note says "after this signal the index rallied 54% of the time." What do you need before you react?
The base rate: how often the index rallies over the same horizon without the signal. If that is also about 54%, the lift is 1 and the signal is worthless. This is exactly what the desk found in its rates-pivot test, 54.1% against 54.1%. You'd also want the sample size and an interval.
Q3. Your prior that a breakout holds is 20%. A volume condition has LR = 3. What is the posterior?
Prior odds 1:4. × 3 = 3:4. Probability \(3/7 \approx 42.9\%\). A strong piece of evidence, and the breakout is still more likely to fail than hold.
Q4. Two independent pieces of evidence have LRs of 2 and 3, and your prior is 10%. What is the posterior, and what is the catch?
Prior odds 1:9. × 2 × 3 = 6:9. Probability \(6/15 = 40\%\). The catch is "independent". If the two signals are driven by the same underlying move, multiplying double-counts it, and the true combined LR can be much closer to one of them alone than to their product.
Q5. A narrow reference class has 7 hits in 8 cases. The broad class rate is 45%. With m = 10, what probability do you carry forward?
\(\hat p = (7 + 10 \times 0.45)/(8 + 10) = 11.5/18 \approx 63.9\%\), not 87.5%. Eight cases can't support a near-certain rate. The shrunk value is still well above the broad rate.
Q6. "Copper has fallen before every recession." What one number is missing, and why does it matter?
The false-alarm rate: how often copper fell without a recession following. "Before every recession" is a hit rate, \(P(E\mid H)\). The likelihood ratio needs \(P(E\mid\neg H)\) too, and a signal that also fires often without a recession has a low LR. In the desk's test the universal claim also failed outright: 12 of 14 negative quarters since 1990 had no 15% copper fall before them.
