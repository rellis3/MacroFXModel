Glosten-Milgrom, One Trade at a Time

A market maker who sees orders arrive one at a time, each either a buy or a sell, can derive a bid-ask spread from nothing but Bayes' rule — no inventory cost, no order-processing fee, just the plain fact that some of the traders showing up actually know something the market maker doesn't. This deck walks through the same model already touched on elsewhere in this Theory Lab, taken all the way to its most useful form: quotes that requote after every single trade.

Section 1

Same year, same "adverse selection" family, completely different machinery. Pick one.

One insider continuously chooses order size to hide inside noise volume. The market maker sets one linear price rule; price impact scales smoothly with how much is traded. Answers: "how much does trading more cost?"
buy / sell?
→
Bayes' rule → Ask, Bid
Traders arrive one at a time, each simply informed or noise — no notion of size at all. The market maker posts a bid and ask via Bayesian updating, then requotes after every arrival. Answers: "why is there a gap between bid and ask at all?"
Both are 1985 results, both are "adverse selection" — neither is a special case of the other, and this deck is entirely about the second one.
Section 2

Who's arriving? Two kinds of trader, one at a time

🎯

Informed trader — probability μ

Knows the true value V exactly. Buys with certainty if V is really high (V_H), sells with certainty if V is really low (V_L). Never trades the "wrong" way.

🎲

Noise trader — probability 1−μ

Trades for reasons unrelated to V entirely — a rebalance, a cashflow conversion. Buys or sells with equal 50/50 probability, regardless of the truth.

🙈

The market maker sees neither type

Only the side of the trade — buy or sell — is visible. Which type just arrived is never revealed directly.

δ

δ — the market maker's belief

The probability the market maker currently assigns to V being the high value, V_H. Starts at a prior δ₀ and updates after every single trade — the variable this whole model runs on.

Because informed traders always push in the direction that's true and noise traders push randomly, any given buy or sell is slightly more likely to have come from an informed trader pointed the "right" way — which is exactly why watching nothing but the direction of each trade is enough to learn something.
Section 3

A trade is a clue — exactly the way this curriculum already defines a clue

Same Bayes' rule as the Bayesian Inference lesson: P(H|E) = P(E|H)P(H) / P(E). Here H = "V is high," E = "a buy just arrived."

The two likelihoods
P(buy | V_H) = μ + (1−μ)/2
vs
P(buy | V_L) = (1−μ)/2
If V is really high, a buy comes from an informed trader (who always buys here) or a noise trader who happened to buy. If V is really low, a buy can only come from a noise trader — an informed trader never buys what they know is overvalued. That gap between the two likelihoods is entirely caused by μ, and it's the whole engine of the model.
Plug the two likelihoods and the current δ into Bayes' rule exactly as derived in the Bayesian Inference lesson, and a buy always pushes the posterior above δ; a sell always pushes it below.
Section 4

From belief to price: Ask above, Bid below

Zero-expected-profit pricing pins the ask to E[V | buy] and the bid to E[V | sell] — never the other way round.

Hand-drawn
A buy is evidence for V_H, so it pins the ask above the current midpoint. A sell is evidence for V_L, so it pins the bid below it. The gap between them isn't decoration — it's exactly wide enough to keep the market maker's expected profit at zero.
Section 5

The edge case that proves the point

Set μ = 0

With no informed traders at all, P(buy | V_H) = P(buy | V_L) = ½ exactly — a buy tells the market maker nothing whatsoever. The posterior always equals the prior, Ask = Bid = E[V], and the spread is exactly zero.

Every dollar of spread this model predicts exists because μ > 0 — pure adverse selection, with no inventory-holding cost and no order-processing fee anywhere in the derivation. That's the headline result: a spread doesn't need any other justification to exist at all.

If literally nobody you trade against ever knows more than you do, one flat price is fine. The instant even a few of them might, quoting one price becomes a losing bet — and the gap you need widens exactly with how many of them you think there are.

Section 9

A genuine, testable prediction

📅

Wider spreads before scheduled events

Ahead of a central bank decision or a jobs report, more of the flow is believed to be trading on a private read of the outcome — a spike in perceived μ, which this model says should widen quotes mechanically.

⚡

Fast compression right after

The first wave of post-announcement trades is heavily one-sided — powerful evidence that collapses residual uncertainty about the new fair value fast, and spreads compress accordingly, often within minutes.

📊

Real, documented — not just theory

This pre/post-event breathing of the quoted spread is a well-established empirical pattern in real markets, not a theoretical curiosity invented for this lesson.

See Central Bank Policy, elsewhere in this Theory Lab, for what actually drives the outcome uncertainty behind a rate decision — this model is the formal reason that uncertainty shows up in the spread at all.
Section 10

Where this gets misread

Common mistakes
  • Confusing μ (fixed informed share) with δ (the belief that actually updates)
  • Treating this and Kyle's Lambda as rival explanations, instead of two different questions
  • Assuming the spread only ever narrows — a contrarian trade widens it right back up
  • Reading the whole real-world spread as pure adverse selection, ignoring inventory/order-processing costs
The honest version
  • μ is a parameter; δ is the variable, updated by Bayes' rule after every trade
  • Kyle explains cost-by-size; Glosten-Milgrom explains the gap's existence — both can be true at once
  • Narrowing happens as evidence accumulates in one direction, not as a one-way ratchet
  • This model isolates one real, provable component of the spread — not the entire observed number
Section 11

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

What's the core structural difference between Kyle's Lambda and Glosten-Milgrom?

Kyle's insider chooses order size continuously, producing a linear price-impact coefficient. Glosten-Milgrom has no size at all — traders simply arrive one at a time, and the market maker sets a discrete bid and ask via Bayes' rule after each one.
Question 2

What happens to the spread in this model if μ = 0 (no informed traders at all)?

With μ = 0, P(buy | V_H) = P(buy | V_L) = ½ exactly, so a buy or sell is completely uninformative — the posterior always equals the prior, and the ask and bid collapse to the same point.
Question 3

Starting at δ₀ = 0.5, μ = 0.3, V_H = 105, V_L = 95, what's the opening ask?

P(buy|V_H)=0.65, P(buy|V_L)=0.35, so with δ₀=0.5, P(V_H|buy)=0.65. Ask = 0.65(105)+0.35(95) = 101.50.
Question 4

In the worked sequence (buy, buy, sell), why doesn't the sell reset δ all the way back to 0.50?

Each buy multiplies the current odds δ/(1−δ) by a fixed factor; each sell multiplies by the exact reciprocal. Two buys and a sell leave exactly one buy's worth of net evidence, landing δ back at 0.6500 — the same value it took after the very first buy.
Question 5

What's the genuine, testable real-world prediction this model makes about scheduled events like a central bank decision?

Ahead of the event, more flow is believed informed, raising the effective μ and widening quotes. The initial post-announcement trades are strongly one-sided evidence, so a fast sequence of Bayesian updates collapses residual uncertainty and the spread compresses — a real, documented pattern.
Section 12

What to carry forward

01

Glosten-Milgrom is discrete and sequential — traders arrive one at a time, and the market maker requotes bid/ask via Bayes' rule after every one, no order-size dimension at all.

02

A trade is Bayesian evidence: informed traders correlate their side with the truth, noise traders don't, so every buy or sell moves the market maker's belief δ.

03

Ask = E[V | buy], Bid = E[V | sell] — zero-expected-profit pricing, with the spread collapsing to exactly zero when μ = 0. Pure adverse selection is enough to create a spread on its own.

04

Sequential updating means quotes move: consistent evidence in one direction narrows the spread, and a single contrarian trade widens it back — narrowing is not a one-way ratchet.

05

The testable, real-world prediction: spreads widen ahead of scheduled information events and compress fast afterward — a genuine empirical pattern, not just a theoretical artifact.

What this now lets you do

Tell Kyle's Lambda and Glosten-Milgrom apart on sight, and know which question each one actually answers. Recognize a widening spread ahead of a scheduled release as a rational, calculable response to perceived informed-trading risk, not noise. Read a market maker's requoting behavior as literal, mechanical Bayesian updating — one trade, one clue, one revised quote at a time.
