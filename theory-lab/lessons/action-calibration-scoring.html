Scoring Your Own Calls

"I'm usually right" is a feeling. "My 70% calls come true 55% of the time" is a measurement you can size from. This lesson turns a decision journal into a score: calibration, the Brier score and its three parts, and a reliability chart you fill in yourself.

CONCEPT — explained here; desk verdicts quoted where tested ~22 min read Prereqs: Judge the Decision, Not the Result; Probability (helpful)
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • Calibrated means your 70% calls come true about 70% of the time. Group your calls by the confidence you wrote down and compare with what happened — that is a reliability diagram.
  • The Brier score \(\frac{1}{N}\sum (f_i - o_i)^2\) grades probability calls: 0 is perfect, always saying 50% scores 0.25, and always quoting the base rate \(\bar o\) scores \(\bar o(1-\bar o)\). That last one is the bar to beat.
  • It splits into three parts: Brier = reliability (how far your stated numbers are from your hit rates) − resolution (how much your calls separate outcomes from the base rate) + uncertainty (how hard the questions were).
  • Worked example: 40 calls at an average stated 66.5% came true 55% of the time. Brier 0.2775 against a base-rate score of 0.2475 — worse than saying "55%" every time. The high-confidence calls are 2.4 standard errors short.
  • Why it matters for money: sizing off your stated 66.5% at even odds gives a Kelly fraction of 33%; your record supports 10%. Uncalibrated confidence is over-betting.
  • The desk's record: a conviction score that was never scored turned out anti-predictive; and a 54.1% hit rate that looked like an edge matched a 54.1% base rate exactly — zero resolution.
Section 01

Why this matters

Judge the Decision, Not the Result asked you to write a confidence on every journal entry before the outcome is known. This lesson is what you do with those numbers once a few dozen outcomes are in. Without scoring, a journal is a diary. With scoring, it answers the one question every other step depends on: when you say 70%, is it really 70%?

Everything downstream uses that number. Expected value multiplies by it. Kelly sizing is built from it. Combining signals produces it. If it is systematically too high — and for most people it is, as Overconfidence and Overtrading showed — every one of those steps is quietly wrong in the same direction.

💡
Plain-English intuition

A weather forecaster's homework

A forecaster says "70% chance of rain" on many different days. You cannot grade any one of those days: rain does not prove her right and sunshine does not prove her wrong. But collect every day she said 70%, and count. If it rained on about seven in ten of them, her 70% means 70%. If it rained on half of them, her 70% is really 50% with extra confidence painted on.

Do the same for her 30% days, her 90% days and so on, and you get a report card for her numbers, not her luck. There is a second question too: does she ever say anything other than the usual? A forecaster who says "30%" every day in a town where it rains 30% of days is perfectly calibrated and completely useless. Good forecasting is calibrated and willing to say 10% or 90% when the evidence is there.

How it reached trading

A way to grade a call that said "70%"

Brier score and calibration · onto trading desks through forecasting tournaments and probability-output models, 2000s–2010s

Traders have always been told to keep a journal, and the journal has always had the same flaw: a call that said "probably" cannot be marked right or wrong from one outcome. So it was graded by outcome — the error the previous lessons warned about — or not at all. What a desk lacked was a score for a probability that rewards honesty and punishes overconfidence over many calls.

The score existed already. Glenn Brier proposed it in 1950 to grade weather forecasts, and Allan Murphy showed in 1973 how it splits into reliability, resolution and uncertainty. It reached judgement-driven finance through forecasting research: Philip Tetlock's studies of expert prediction (2005) and the Good Judgment Project's results in a US intelligence forecasting tournament (from 2011), both scored with Brier, made "keep score in probabilities" a standard discipline for macro and discretionary analysts. On the systematic side, models that output a probability of an up move or a range expansion are checked with the same reliability diagram and Brier skill score.

Section 02

The math, step by step

1. Write calls that can be scored

A scorable call has four parts: a yes/no event ("EUR/USD trades above 1.0920"), a deadline ("within 5 sessions"), a probability \(f\) written before the outcome, and a rule for what counts as the event happening that leaves no room for argument afterwards. "Bullish euro" cannot be scored. "62% that EUR/USD closes Friday above today's close" can.

Phrase each call as the thing you think more likely, so \(f\) runs from 50% to 100%, and round to the nearest 10%. Rounding is not laziness: it puts your calls into a handful of buckets (50%, 60%, 70%, 80%, 90%), and buckets are what make calibration measurable. Never write 100%; one wrong call at 100% is an infinitely bad log score and a very bad Brier score.

2. Calibration and the reliability diagram

For each bucket \(k\), count the calls \(n_k\) and the ones that came true \(h_k\). The observed frequency is \(\bar o_k = h_k/n_k\). You are calibrated if \(\bar o_k \approx f_k\) in every bucket. Plot \(\bar o_k\) against \(f_k\): calibrated points sit on the diagonal, points below it are overconfidence (said 80%, happened 60%), points above it underconfidence.

How close is close enough? If you really are calibrated at \(f_k\), the hit count in a bucket is binomial, so the observed frequency has standard error \(\sqrt{f_k(1-f_k)/n_k}\). Ten calls at 70% give a standard error of 14.5 percentage points: anything from about 42% to 98% is consistent with being calibrated. Calibration needs many calls, and a bucket with five calls in it is a rumour.

A stronger test pools buckets. If you are calibrated, the total hits should be close to the sum of your stated probabilities:

\[ z = \frac{\sum_i o_i - \sum_i f_i}{\sqrt{\sum_i f_i(1-f_i)}} \]
Σ oᵢhow many of the calls came true
Σ fᵢhow many you said would — the sum of your probabilities
Σ fᵢ(1−fᵢ)the variance of the hit count if your numbers were honest
zbelow about −2: you are overconfident, beyond reasonable luck
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

If you say "I'm pretty sure" ten times and you're right only five times, then "pretty sure" really means "50-50" when you say it. Counting lets you find out what your own words mean.

3. The Brier score

\[ \text{BS} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} (f_i - o_i)^2 \]
fᵢ
What you said
The probability written before the outcome
oᵢ
What happened
1 if the event happened, 0 if not
BS
The score
Average squared miss: 0 is perfect, lower is better

A 70% call that comes true costs \((0.7-1)^2 = 0.09\); one that fails costs \(0.7^2 = 0.49\). Confident misses hurt far more than timid hits help. Two benchmarks matter. Saying 50% on everything scores exactly 0.25. Saying the base rate \(\bar o\) — the overall fraction of your calls that come true — on everything scores \(\bar o(1-\bar o)\). That second benchmark is the honest one: it is what you would get with no skill at telling one call from another. The Brier skill score measures you against it: \(\text{BSS} = 1 - \text{BS}/(\bar o(1-\bar o))\). Positive means you add something; negative means quoting the base rate every time would have done better.

The Brier score is proper: if your true chance of being right is \(p\), your expected score \(p(1-f)^2 + (1-p)f^2\) is lowest when you write \(f = p\). The penalty for writing anything else is exactly \((f-p)^2\). You cannot game it by shading your numbers; the best strategy is honesty.

What you write f70%
Expected Brier \(= p(1-f)^2 + (1-p)f^2\), lowest at \(f = p\). The extra cost of writing \(f\) instead of the truth is \((f-p)^2\) per call — this is the "reliability" part of the score below.

4. Three parts: reliability, resolution, uncertainty

When calls are grouped into buckets that each use one probability, the Brier score splits exactly into three parts (Murphy, 1973):

\[ \text{BS} = \underbrace{\frac{1}{N}\sum_k n_k (f_k - \bar o_k)^2}_{\text{reliability}} \;-\; \underbrace{\frac{1}{N}\sum_k n_k (\bar o_k - \bar o)^2}_{\text{resolution}} \;+\; \underbrace{\bar o\,(1-\bar o)}_{\text{uncertainty}} \]
reliabilityhow far each bucket's stated number is from its hit rate — want small; it is the calibration error
resolutionhow much your buckets' hit rates differ from the overall rate — want big; it is discrimination
uncertaintyhow hard the questions were; depends only on the outcomes, not on you

The decomposition tells you what kind of bad you are. High reliability error with decent resolution: your ranking is useful but your numbers are inflated — fix it by mapping stated to observed. Low resolution: your 80% calls do no better than your 60% calls — your confidence carries no information and should not drive size, however well calibrated it looks. Since the base-rate forecaster scores exactly the uncertainty term, you beat it only when resolution exceeds the reliability error.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Two ways to be a bad guesser: saying "super sure" when you're only a bit sure, and saying "a bit sure" about everything so your guesses never tell anyone anything. The score catches both.

A last alternative is the log score, \(-\ln f\) if right and \(-\ln(1-f)\) if wrong, introduced by I. J. Good in 1952. It is also proper and punishes confident misses much harder: a wrong 99% call costs 4.6 against 0.69 for a wrong 50% call. It connects to the information measures in Entropy & KL Divergence. Brier is the friendlier choice for a personal journal because one bad call cannot dominate it.

40 calls on FX, gold and indices, January to March

1

Brier score and the benchmark

\(\text{BS} = 11.10/40 = 0.2775\). Base rate \(\bar o = 22/40 = 0.55\), so quoting 55% on every call would have scored \(0.55 \times 0.45 = 0.2475\). Skill: \(1 - 0.2775/0.2475 = -12.1\%\). Worse than the base rate — and worse than saying 50% on everything (0.25).

2

Reliability (calibration error)

\(\frac{1}{40}[8(0)^2 + 12(0.6-0.583)^2 + 10(0.1)^2 + 6(0.3)^2 + 4(0.4)^2] = \frac{1}{40}[0 + 0.0033 + 0.10 + 0.54 + 0.64] = 1.2833/40 = 0.0321\).

3

Resolution (discrimination)

\(\frac{1}{40}[8(0.05)^2 + 12(0.0333)^2 + 10(0.05)^2 + 6(0.05)^2 + 4(0.05)^2] = \frac{1}{40}[0.02 + 0.0133 + 0.025 + 0.015 + 0.01] = 0.0833/40 = 0.0021\).

Check: \(0.0321 - 0.0021 + 0.2475 = 0.2775\) ✓. Resolution is tiny: the 90% calls did no better than the 50% calls.

4

Is it more than bad luck?

All 40 calls: expected hits \(\sum f = 26.6\), variance \(\sum f(1-f) = 8.30\), so \(z = (22 - 26.6)/2.88 = -1.60\) — suggestive, not conclusive. The 70–90% calls pooled: expected 15.4, observed 11, variance 3.42, \(z = -4.4/1.85 = -2.38\). The high-confidence calls are overconfident beyond reasonable luck.

5

What it costs in size

At even payoff (\(b = 1\)), Kelly is \(f^{*} = 2p - 1\). Sizing off the average stated 66.5% gives 33%; the record's 55% gives 10%. Betting 3.3× Kelly is past the point where, as Drawdowns and Risk of Ruin showed, growth turns negative.

6

The output: a correction table and a rule

For next quarter, replace each stated number with the bucket's hit rate shrunk toward the 55% base rate by 10 pseudo-calls, \((h_k + 10\,\bar o)/(n_k + 10)\): 50% → 52.8%, 60% → 56.8%, 70% → 57.5%, 80% → 53.1%, 90% → 53.6%. And the rule: "Until my 80–90% calls beat my 60% calls, they get the same size."

Section 04

It actually happened: people who keep score

It actually happened

The weather forecasters who mean what they say

Probability forecasting grew up in meteorology, and so did the habit of scoring it. Glenn Brier was a meteorologist at the US Weather Bureau when he proposed his score in 1950. In the 1970s Allan Murphy and Robert Winkler examined the probability-of-precipitation forecasts issued by US National Weather Service forecasters and found them remarkably well calibrated: across the range of stated probabilities, it rained on close to the stated fraction of days.

Part of the reason is that weather forecasters get exactly what this lesson asks for — many similar calls, quick and unambiguous outcomes, and a score kept on every one. The contrast is instructive. Nate Silver's The Signal and the Noise (2012) describes a "wet bias" in some commercial television forecasts, which deliberately overstated the chance of rain because viewers forgive an unneeded umbrella more readily than a soaking. Same science, different incentive, worse calibration.

It actually happened

Tetlock's forecasters and the Good Judgment Project

Over roughly two decades, Philip Tetlock collected probability forecasts from political and economic experts and scored them. His book Expert Political Judgment (2005) reported that, on average, the experts did little better than simple rules of thumb, and that the better forecasters tended to be the ones who drew on many ideas and adjusted rather than those committed to one big theory.

From 2011, Tetlock and Barbara Mellers ran the Good Judgment Project in a forecasting tournament sponsored by IARPA, the US intelligence community's research agency. Thousands of volunteers made probability forecasts on world events, scored with the Brier score. The project's best performers — the "superforecasters" of Tetlock and Dan Gardner's 2015 book — kept outperforming year after year, which is what skill looks like and luck does not. The habits they shared were the ones this lesson builds: precise probabilities, small updates, and keeping score.

Section 05

What this desk found when it tested the claims

Not tested here "The confidence on our calls matches our hit rate." Not tested here. The desk's ledger records whether a signal had an edge; it does not yet hold a Brier score or reliability diagram for the probabilities written on calls. The two results below are the reason to start one.
Desk test: nothing found "A higher conviction score on an entry means a better trade." A backtest system's conviction vote, used to grade its own entries, turned out to be anti-predictive of outcome; the system was a net loser (−£38k) on a 30% win rate. Nobody had checked whether high-conviction entries came true more often than low ones until the test was run. That check is a reliability diagram with two buckets. The desk's ruling: never scale size by a score that has not been scored.
Desk test: nothing found A 54% hit rate that was exactly nothing. Testing whether a confirmed turn in short-term rates leads the Nasdaq, the desk found that a confirmed rates low is followed by an up-move 54.1% of the time — and the base rate is 54.1%. In this lesson's terms: perfectly calibrated, zero resolution. A call that is right as often as the base rate earns a Brier skill of zero, however good "54%" sounds. Never quote a hit rate without its base rate.
The trap: a hit rate on its own tells you nothing. 60% right is skill if the base rate is 45%, and nothing if the base rate is 60%. Score every call against the base rate, and treat any confidence number — yours or a model's — as unproven until a reliability diagram says otherwise.
Section 06

How practitioners actually use this

📓

Probability on every call

Macro and discretionary desks that keep score write a number and a deadline on each idea, then review Brier and calibration per analyst and per type of call. The numbers make "I had a feeling" discussable.

🤖

Calibrate the models too

Any model that outputs a probability — up/down, range expansion, regime — gets a reliability diagram on out-of-sample data and, if needed, a recalibration map, before its output is allowed to set size.

📏

Skill against the base rate

Good desks quote Brier skill, never raw Brier or raw hit rate. Real edges in markets are small: a skill score of a few per cent out of sample is a meaningful result.

⚖️

Calibrated p into sizing

Kelly and every expected-value calculation take the calibrated probability, not the felt one — and fractional Kelly on top, because even a calibrated p is estimated (see Kelly Criterion).

🧩
Where this connects to the desk

Brier skill is already the yardstick for model research

The desk scores some probability models this way. In its research on forecasting FX range expansion, a composite of three market-state measures reached a Brier skill of about +1% out of sample against the base rate — described there as real but modest, and used for sizing and target width rather than direction. That is the scale of a genuine edge. The same scoring has not yet been applied to the probabilities written on the desk's own calls.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where scoring goes wrong

Scoring the P&L, not the call. A call is ✓ or ✗ by its written rule. A winning trade on a failed call is luck; a losing trade on a correct call is execution or sizing.

Judging a bucket of five. Five calls at 80% that went 3/5 are consistent with being calibrated. Wait for numbers, or pool buckets.

Hiding at 50%. Saying 50–60% on everything keeps calibration perfect and resolution zero. The score rewards the courage to say 80% when you can back it.

Editing calls after the fact. The deadline moved, the level "basically" got hit. The journal must be written before and marked by the rule, or the score measures your memory.

Ignoring the base rate. "I'm right 60% of the time" means nothing until you know how often the event happens anyway.

Key Takeaways

✓Calibration is countable. Bucket calls by stated probability; compare each bucket's hit rate with its number. Points below the diagonal are overconfidence.
✓Brier = reliability − resolution + uncertainty. Beat the base-rate score \(\bar o(1-\bar o)\), not 0.25.
✓Honesty is the best policy, mathematically: the Brier score is proper; writing \(f\) instead of your true \(p\) costs \((f-p)^2\) per call.
✓Size from the measured number. Stated 66.5% vs actual 55% is a Kelly fraction of 33% vs 10% at even odds.
⚠It needs many calls. Ten calls at 70% have a ±28-point luck band. Pool buckets and wait.
✗Desk tests, nothing found: an unscored conviction vote turned out anti-predictive; a 54.1% hit rate matched a 54.1% base rate. Those were the desk's tests of those signals — and the reason confidence must be scored before it sets size.
Section 08

Self-test

Q1. You make three calls at 80%: two come true, one fails. What is your Brier score?
Two hits cost \((0.8-1)^2 = 0.04\) each; the miss costs \(0.8^2 = 0.64\). Total 0.72, divided by 3: \(\text{BS} = 0.24\). Just better than always saying 50% (0.25) — and three calls tell you almost nothing about calibration.
Q2. 60% of your calls come true. What Brier score would quoting 60% on every call earn, and why is that the benchmark?
\(0.6 \times 0.4 = 0.24\). It is what a forecaster with no ability to tell one call from another, but who knows the overall rate, would score. Your skill is how far below 0.24 you get.
Q3. Your true hit rate on a type of call is 60%, but you write 75%. How much does that cost per call in expected Brier score?
\((f - p)^2 = (0.75 - 0.60)^2 = 0.0225\). Expected score at 75% is \(0.6(0.25)^2 + 0.4(0.75)^2 = 0.2625\) against \(0.6(0.4)^2 + 0.4(0.6)^2 = 0.24\) at the honest 60%. The difference is exactly 0.0225.
Q4. A forecaster's reliability term is 0.001 and resolution is 0.000. Is she good?
She is calibrated but useless: her calls do not separate outcomes at all. Her score equals the base-rate score plus a little, so her skill is slightly negative. Calibration is necessary, not sufficient; resolution is what makes calls worth acting on.
Q5. Twenty calls at 90%: fifteen came true. Is that evidence of overconfidence?
Expected 18, variance \(20 \times 0.9 \times 0.1 = 1.8\), standard error 1.34. \(z = (15-18)/1.34 = -2.24\). Yes — beyond what luck usually produces. The calls behave more like 75% calls.
Q6. The desk found a 54.1% hit rate after rates lows against a 54.1% base rate. What is that signal's resolution, and what should its Brier skill be?
Zero resolution: the signal's hit rate is the base rate, so it separates nothing. Quoting 54.1% when it fires scores exactly what quoting 54.1% always would, so skill is zero (or negative once any miscalibration is added). That is what the desk found in its test of that signal; it says nothing about rates in general.
