Hasbrouck Information Share & Price Discovery

A VECM tells you which of two prices does more of the correcting when they drift apart. Hasbrouck's Information Share asks the sharper question underneath that: when brand-new information hits, how much of the resulting price-variance actually showed up first in venue A versus venue B? The answer turns out to depend, uncomfortably, on a modeling choice nobody's economics forces you to make.

TL;DR
  • When the "same" asset trades in more than one place — spot vs. futures, or a pair vs. a tightly correlated proxy — and the two prices are cointegrated around one common efficient price, the question is which venue's price moves first when new information arrives. The sibling VECM lesson gave an informal answer (compare \(|\alpha_Y|\) and \(|\alpha_X|\)); Hasbrouck's Information Share (IS) makes it rigorous by decomposing variance, not just adjustment speed.
  • The pipeline: fit the same two-equation VECM, invert it into its Wold/vector-moving-average representation, extract the long-run impact matrix \(\Psi(1)\) — whose rows come out identical here, because both venues track one common efficient price by construction — and read off a shared common-factor loading vector \(\psi=(\psi_Y,\psi_X)\), derivable directly from the VECM's own \(\alpha_Y,\alpha_X\).
  • Hasbrouck's actual trick: the two venues' shocks are correlated, so their contribution to the common factor's variance can't just be split additively. Cholesky-decompose the residual covariance matrix \(\Omega\) to manufacture orthogonal shocks, then attribute variance venue by venue. The venue ordered first in the Cholesky decomposition claims the entire shared, correlated portion of the variance for itself; the venue ordered second only gets what's left over.
  • That ordering is arbitrary — nothing in the economics says which venue goes first — and the split can swing dramatically depending on it. This lesson's own worked numeric example: the same fitted system gives venue Y a 57.1% information share when Y is ordered first, and only 10.7% when X is ordered first instead. This is the standard critique of Hasbrouck IS.
  • Two standard responses exist: Hasbrouck's own suggested fix — compute IS both ways and report the midpoint or the range — and alternative measures, Gonzalo & Granger's (1995) Component Share and Putniņš' (2013) Information Leadership Share, that don't hinge on an arbitrary ordering the same way. All three are covered below, with a live calculator that computes both Cholesky orderings from any inputs you enter.
Section 01

Why this matters

The VECM lesson built the two-equation error-correction system \(\Delta Y_t = \alpha_Y Z_{t-1} + \dots + \epsilon_{Y,t}\), \(\Delta X_t = \alpha_X Z_{t-1} + \dots + \epsilon_{X,t}\) and pointed out, in passing, that comparing \(|\alpha_Y|\) and \(|\alpha_X|\) is an informal first pass at "which price is leading." A large \(|\alpha_Y|\) paired with an \(\alpha_X\) near zero says \(Y\) absorbs almost all of the correction when the spread drifts — \(X\) behaves like the anchor, \(Y\) like the follower. That comparison is real and useful, but it's a statement about adjustment speed, not about where new information actually shows up first. Those two questions turn out to be closely related but genuinely distinct, and Joel Hasbrouck's 1995 "One Security, Many Markets" paper is the paper that made the distinction precise and gave it a rigorous answer.

The setting is deliberately narrow, and the narrowness is the point: the "same" underlying asset trades in two (or more) venues or forms — spot FX vs. FX futures, a currency pair vs. a tightly cointegrated proxy built from interest-rate differentials, a stock's price on two competing exchanges. Both quoted prices bounce around a single, unobserved common efficient price — the "true" value the asset would trade at if markets processed information instantly and perfectly — and each venue's observed price is that common price plus its own transitory, venue-specific noise. Cointegration is exactly what makes this framework valid at all: the two observed prices being cointegrated is the formal statement that they share one common stochastic trend rather than wandering off independently forever. Hasbrouck's Information Share asks: of the variance in the shock that moves the common efficient price each period, how much of that variance is attributable to information that first showed up in venue \(Y\) versus venue \(X\)?

💡
Plain-English intuition

Two clocks, one true time, and a question about which one you'd trust

Imagine two clocks in the same room, both supposedly showing the same true time, but each running slightly fast or slow at any given moment and each getting corrected back toward the true time periodically. The VECM's \(\alpha_Y\) and \(\alpha_X\) tell you how hard each clock gets yanked back toward agreement whenever they drift apart — useful, but it doesn't tell you which clock's readings you'd actually want to consult right now if you needed to know the true time this instant.

Hasbrouck's Information Share answers that sharper question instead: if the true time just changed (new information arrived), which clock's reading moved first, and by how much of the total movement? If Clock A almost always changes first and Clock B just follows along a beat later, most of the informational "news" is showing up in Clock A — it has a high information share, and it's functionally the reference clock everyone else is chasing, whether or not it's the one doing more of the eventual self-correcting.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

The VECM lesson asked "when the two prices drift apart, which one snaps back harder?" This lesson asks a different question: "when something genuinely new happens in the world, which price hears about it and reacts first?" A price can be a slow, lazy corrector (small α) and still be the one that always reacts to news first — being quick to react to news and being quick to self-correct afterward are not automatically the same trait, and Hasbrouck's Information Share is built specifically to measure the first one.

Section 02

The math, step by step

Four steps get from the VECM's own residuals to a number for each venue's Information Share. The first two steps are mechanical bookkeeping; the third step — pinning down the common-factor loading vector \(\psi\) — is where the VECM's own \(\alpha_Y, \alpha_X\) come back in explicitly; the fourth step is Hasbrouck's actual contribution and the source of the ordering problem covered right after it.

\(P_t=(Y_t,X_t)'\)The vector of the two venues' prices for the same underlying asset — the VECM lesson's own \(Y_t,X_t\).
\(\epsilon_t=(\epsilon_{Y,t},\epsilon_{X,t})'\)The VECM's own reduced-form residual vector — the two equations' own shocks, exactly as defined in the VECM lesson.
\(\Omega\)The residual covariance matrix, \(\Omega=\text{Var}(\epsilon_t)=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_Y^2 & \sigma_{YX}\\ \sigma_{YX} & \sigma_X^2\end{pmatrix}\).
\(\Psi(L),\ \Psi(1)\)The Wold/vector-moving-average lag polynomial, and its sum at \(L=1\) — the long-run impact matrix: how much a shock permanently moves each price once all its effects have played out.
\(\psi=(\psi_Y,\psi_X)\)The shared row of \(\Psi(1)\) — the common-factor loading vector: how much of each venue's own shock feeds into the one permanent common-price innovation.
\(\alpha=(\alpha_Y,\alpha_X)',\ \alpha_\perp\)The VECM's adjustment vector and its orthogonal complement (any vector with \(\alpha'\alpha_\perp=0\)) — the input that pins \(\psi\) down.
\(F\)The lower-triangular Cholesky factor of \(\Omega\), so \(\Omega=FF'\) — the tool that manufactures orthogonal shocks, and the source of the ordering dependence.
\(\text{IS}_j\)Venue \(j\)'s Hasbrouck Information Share under one specific Cholesky ordering — a number in \([0,1]\), summing to 1 across both venues for a given ordering.

Step 1 — the same VECM, unchanged. Nothing about the starting point differs from the VECM lesson at all:

\[ \Delta Y_t = \alpha_Y Z_{t-1} + (\text{short-run lags}) + \epsilon_{Y,t}, \qquad \Delta X_t = \alpha_X Z_{t-1} + (\text{short-run lags}) + \epsilon_{X,t} \]

Fitting this system (by OLS, exactly as the VECM lesson describes) hands you three things this lesson actually uses: the adjustment coefficients \(\alpha_Y, \alpha_X\), and the residual covariance matrix \(\Omega\) — how large each equation's own leftover shock is, and how correlated the two equations' shocks are with each other. That correlation, \(\sigma_{YX}\), turns out to be the single most important number in this entire lesson.

Step 2 — the Wold/vector-moving-average representation. Any stationary system (which \(\Delta Y_t, \Delta X_t\) are, being the first differences of a cointegrated pair) admits an equivalent representation purely in terms of current and past shocks — no lagged levels, no error-correction term, just an infinite weighted sum of \(\epsilon\)'s:

\[ \Delta P_t = \Psi(L)\,\epsilon_t = \sum_{i=0}^{\infty} \Psi_i\, \epsilon_{t-i}, \qquad \Psi_0 = I \]
Ψ_ia 2×2 matrix — how a shock from \(i\) periods ago is still moving today's \(\Delta Y_t, \Delta X_t\)
Ψ(1)=ΣΨ_ithe cumulative, long-run effect of a single shock, once every lag's contribution is added up

Every VECM has a Wold representation of this form — it's a standard, purely mechanical inversion of the same fitted system, not a new modeling assumption. This lesson doesn't re-derive that inversion in full; the object that actually matters for everything below is just \(\Psi(1)\), the long-run impact matrix.

Step 3 — the common-factor loading vector \(\psi\). Because \(Y_t\) and \(X_t\) are cointegrated on a price-parity relationship — they're alternate observations of essentially one asset's price, not merely two correlated series — any permanent shock has to move both venues' prices by the same amount in the long run, or the cointegrating relationship between them would itself break down. That economic fact forces \(\Psi(1)\) into a very specific, low-rank shape: its two rows come out identical, both equal to one shared row vector \(\psi' = (\psi_Y, \psi_X)\):

\[ \Psi(1) = \iota\,\psi', \qquad \iota = \begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix} \]

This is exactly the permanent-transitory decomposition Gonzalo & Granger (1995) formalize (building on the common-trends idea in Stock & Watson, 1988): every cointegrated system decomposes into one permanent common factor plus purely transitory deviations from it, and \(\psi\) is precisely the vector of weights saying how much of each venue's own shock feeds into that one permanent factor. Its innovation each period is the scalar

\[ \Delta f_t = \psi'\epsilon_t = \psi_Y \epsilon_{Y,t} + \psi_X \epsilon_{X,t} \]

and this lesson derives \(\psi\) directly from the VECM's own \(\alpha\) vector, using the standard result that the long-run impact matrix is built from \(\alpha\)'s orthogonal complement. For the two-venue, price-parity case specifically, that reduces to a clean closed form:

\[ \psi_Y = \frac{\alpha_X}{\alpha_X-\alpha_Y}, \qquad \psi_X = \frac{-\alpha_Y}{\alpha_X-\alpha_Y}, \qquad \psi_Y+\psi_X=1 \]
A sanity check worth internalizing before moving on. Plug in the VECM lesson's own "weak exogeneity" case, \(\alpha_X=0\): \(\psi_Y = 0/(0-\alpha_Y) = 0\) and \(\psi_X = -\alpha_Y/(0-\alpha_Y)=1\). The venue that never responds to disequilibrium ends up carrying all of the common-factor weight. That's exactly the right answer economically — a venue that ignores the spread entirely is behaving like the anchor, the reference price the other venue is chasing, and the math correctly assigns it the entire permanent component. This lesson derives and verifies this two-venue formula explicitly; Gonzalo & Granger (1995) is the source for the fully general, many-venue version of the same construction and should be consulted directly for that algebra.
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine the "true price" is a weighted average of what venue Y says and what venue X says. If venue X never budges no matter how out-of-line it gets (α_X = 0), the only sensible weighted average that behaves like the true price is venue X itself — so X gets 100% of the weight (ψ_X = 1) and Y gets none. If both venues respond somewhat, the weight splits between them according to how little each one reacts to disequilibrium — the less a venue reacts, the more it looks like the fixed reference everyone else is adjusting toward, and the more weight ψ gives it.

Step 4 — Hasbrouck's trick: Cholesky-orthogonalize, then attribute variance. The total variance of the common factor's own innovation is a well-defined, ordering-free quantity:

\[ \sigma_w^2 = \text{Var}(\Delta f_t) = \psi'\Omega\psi = \psi_Y^2\sigma_Y^2 + 2\psi_Y\psi_X\sigma_{YX} + \psi_X^2\sigma_X^2 \]

The problem is splitting \(\sigma_w^2\) into "venue \(Y\)'s share" and "venue \(X\)'s share." If \(\sigma_{YX}=0\) — the two venues' shocks are genuinely uncorrelated — the split is unambiguous: \(\psi_Y^2\sigma_Y^2\) and \(\psi_X^2\sigma_X^2\) already add up to \(\sigma_w^2\) with nothing left over. But whenever the venues share any contemporaneous information — which is nearly always true at anything but the coarsest sampling frequency — \(\sigma_{YX}\neq 0\), and the cross term \(2\psi_Y\psi_X\sigma_{YX}\) is jointly owned by both venues at once, with no natural rule for splitting it.

Hasbrouck's fix: Cholesky-decompose the residual covariance matrix, \(\Omega = FF'\) with \(F\) lower-triangular, to build new shocks that are mutually orthogonal by construction — no cross term left at all. But a Cholesky factorization requires choosing which variable goes "first": the first-ordered venue's new orthogonal shock is just its own raw shock, unchanged; the second-ordered venue's new shock is its own raw shock with whatever's linearly explained by the first venue's shock netted out — its orthogonalized residual variance. Once the shocks are orthogonal, each venue's Information Share is simply its squared common-factor loading, projected through the ordering-specific Cholesky factor, times its own orthogonalized innovation variance, divided by the total variance \(\sigma_w^2\). Worked out explicitly for two venues, with venue "1" ordered first and venue "2" ordered second:

\[ \text{IS}_1 = \frac{(\psi_1\sigma_1^2+\psi_2\sigma_{12})^2}{\sigma_1^2\,\sigma_w^2}, \qquad \text{IS}_2 = \frac{\psi_2^2\,(\sigma_1^2\sigma_2^2-\sigma_{12}^2)}{\sigma_1^2\,\sigma_w^2} \]
IS_1the share claimed by whichever venue is ordered first — it gets the entire shared/correlated portion of the variance
IS_2the share left for the venue ordered second — only its variance net of anything correlated with venue 1
σ₁²σ₂² − σ₁₂²the determinant of Ω restricted to these two venues — venue 2's leftover, orthogonalized variance once venue 1's shock is netted out

This closed form is derived directly from the Cholesky factorization above (and is verified, by direct matrix computation, in this lesson's own worked example below) — swap the labels 1 and 2 throughout to get the formulas for the opposite ordering. \(\text{IS}_1+\text{IS}_2=1\) holds for either ordering by construction, since both formulas are just two different ways of exactly re-partitioning the same \(\sigma_w^2\).

The ordering-ambiguity problem — the standard critique of Hasbrouck IS. Compare the two formulas above: swapping which venue is ordered first swaps which one gets to claim the shared, correlated portion of the variance for itself. Whenever \(\sigma_{YX}\neq 0\) — which is the normal case, not an edge case — \(\text{IS}_Y\) and \(\text{IS}_X\) computed under the two orderings will differ, sometimes dramatically, even though nothing about the underlying economics changed at all. There is no principled, data-driven rule that says which venue "should" go first in the Cholesky decomposition — it is a purely algebraic bookkeeping choice, and the worked example below shows just how large the resulting swing can be with entirely ordinary-looking numbers. This single issue is the most-cited limitation of the entire Hasbrouck IS framework, and it's the reason the two literature responses below exist at all.
↔️

Response 1 — Hasbrouck's own fix: report both, or the midpoint

Hasbrouck's own 1995 paper suggests the direct, practical fix: compute \(\text{IS}_Y\) and \(\text{IS}_X\) under both orderings and report the full range each venue's share can take, or their midpoint as a single summary number. It doesn't remove the ambiguity, but it makes it visible and bounded rather than hiding behind one arbitrarily chosen ordering — the standard practical convention this lesson's own calculator implements below.

🧭

Response 2 — alternative measures without the same ordering problem

Gonzalo & Granger's own (1995) Component Share measure works from the same permanent-transitory decomposition and common-factor loadings \(\psi\) used above, but attributes variance using each venue's raw, non-orthogonalized shock variance rather than a Cholesky-ordered split — sidestepping the arbitrary ordering step entirely, at the cost of not fully accounting for the shared, correlated variance the way Hasbrouck's decomposition explicitly tries to. Putniņš (2013), "What do price discovery metrics really measure?", studies exactly where Hasbrouck IS and Component Share disagree and proposes an Information Leadership Share that combines the two into a single measure designed to behave sensibly in both directions. This lesson describes both at the conceptual level intentionally — consult Gonzalo & Granger (1995) and Putniņš (2013) directly for their precise algebraic definitions rather than treating any specific formula reconstructed secondhand as authoritative.

Worked example

The same fitted system, two orderings, two very different answers

A fitted VECM (illustrative numbers, not a real historical estimate) gives \(\alpha_Y=-0.40\), \(\alpha_X=0.10\) — Y does more of the error-correcting, similar in spirit to the VECM lesson's own AUD/USD example — and a residual covariance matrix \(\Omega=\begin{pmatrix}4 & 1\\ 1 & 1\end{pmatrix}\) (so \(\sigma_Y^2=4\), \(\sigma_X^2=1\), \(\sigma_{YX}=1\), a correlation of \(1/\sqrt{4\cdot1}=0.5\) between the two equations' shocks).

1

Common-factor loadings from α

\(\psi_Y = \dfrac{0.10}{0.10-(-0.40)} = \dfrac{0.10}{0.50}=0.20\), \(\psi_X = \dfrac{0.40}{0.50}=0.80\) (sum to 1, as required). X — the smaller-\(|\alpha|\), closer-to-anchor venue — carries the larger share of the common factor, exactly as the sanity check above predicts.

2

Total common-factor variance

\(\sigma_w^2=\psi_Y^2\sigma_Y^2+2\psi_Y\psi_X\sigma_{YX}+\psi_X^2\sigma_X^2 = (0.2)^2(4)+2(0.2)(0.8)(1)+(0.8)^2(1) = 0.16+0.32+0.64=1.12\).

3

Ordering 1 — Y first

\(\text{IS}_Y=\dfrac{(0.2\cdot4+0.8\cdot1)^2}{4\cdot1.12}=\dfrac{(1.6)^2}{4.48}=\dfrac{2.56}{4.48}=\dfrac{4}{7}\approx0.5714\). \(\text{IS}_X=\dfrac{(0.8)^2(4\cdot1-1^2)}{4\cdot1.12}=\dfrac{0.64\cdot3}{4.48}=\dfrac{1.92}{4.48}=\dfrac{3}{7}\approx0.4286\). Y: 57.1%, X: 42.9%.

4

Ordering 2 — X first

\(\text{IS}_X=\dfrac{(0.8\cdot1+0.2\cdot1)^2}{1\cdot1.12}=\dfrac{(1.0)^2}{1.12}=\dfrac{1}{1.12}=\dfrac{25}{28}\approx0.8929\). \(\text{IS}_Y=\dfrac{(0.2)^2(1\cdot4-1^2)}{1\cdot1.12}=\dfrac{0.04\cdot3}{1.12}=\dfrac{0.12}{1.12}=\dfrac{3}{28}\approx0.1071\). Y: 10.7%, X: 89.3%.

The exact same fitted VECM — same \(\alpha\)'s, same \(\Omega\) — hands venue Y anywhere from 10.7% to 57.1% of the information share, purely as a function of which venue is listed first in the Cholesky decomposition. Hasbrouck's own suggested fix, the midpoint of the two orderings, gives \(\text{IS}_Y\approx(0.5714+0.1071)/2=0.3393\) (33.9%) and \(\text{IS}_X\approx(0.4286+0.8929)/2=0.6607\) (66.1%) — a single number, but one that visibly papers over a genuinely wide range rather than resolving it. Both closed-form results above were verified against a direct numerical Cholesky decomposition of \(\Omega\) as part of building this lesson; the arithmetic is exact, the inputs are illustrative.

Real-world trading scenario

Spot FX vs. the exchange-traded futures contract on the same pair

Suppose a desk trades both spot EUR/USD and a cointegrated, exchange-listed EUR/USD futures contract, and wants to know which venue's price to treat as the reference when the two briefly disagree. A VECM fit on the pair might already show something like the sanity check above: futures with a smaller-magnitude \(\alpha\) (closer to weakly exogenous), spot doing more of the visible correcting — an informal signal that futures leads. Hasbrouck's Information Share makes that claim precise and quantitative: computing IS under both Cholesky orderings and reporting the midpoint gives a genuine number — "futures carries roughly two-thirds of the price-discovery variance share" — rather than a directional impression from comparing two adjustment coefficients. That number is directly actionable: if futures genuinely leads price discovery, a desk pricing off a lagging spot feed during fast-moving news is effectively trading on stale information relative to what futures has already absorbed.

Nothing above should be read as an actual historical finding for any specific FX pair or contract — it's illustrative of how a desk would use the number, not a claim this repo has run this analysis on real data. What genuinely matters here is that the VECM lesson's own informal \(\alpha_Y\)-vs-\(\alpha_X\) comparison and this lesson's Information Share can point in compatible directions (as in this example) but are answering different questions, and a desk relying on only the informal comparison is skipping a real, well-established step.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

In practice, Hasbrouck IS is used almost exclusively as a relative, comparative tool — "which of these two (or more) venues carries more of the price-discovery variance" — rather than as a precise, stand-alone percentage to be trusted to the decimal point, precisely because of the ordering sensitivity covered above. The standard workflow is: confirm genuine cointegration first (the same Engle-Granger or Johansen test the VECM lesson assumes as a precondition), fit the VECM, compute IS under both orderings, and report either the full range or the midpoint as the headline number — treating a wide range between the two orderings itself as a meaningful diagnostic (a narrow range means the conclusion is robust; a wide range, as in this lesson's own worked example, means the ordering choice is doing more work than the data is).

🔀

Venue selection for execution

A venue with a consistently high information share is where genuinely new information shows up first — a natural candidate for the "reference" feed a signal or hedge should react off of first.

🧭

Regime and market-structure monitoring

Re-estimating IS periodically and watching which venue's share is rising or falling over time can flag a genuine structural shift in where price discovery is happening — e.g., a lit venue losing share to a newer, faster one.

📐

A rigorous upgrade path from the VECM's own α comparison

Where the VECM lesson's informal α_Y-vs-α_X comparison is a cheap first pass, IS is the version an academic paper or a serious internal research note would actually cite — same underlying VECM, formalized variance attribution.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where Hasbrouck IS quietly goes wrong

The ordering-ambiguity problem, restated as a checklist item. Never report a single Cholesky-ordered IS split without either computing both orderings or explicitly justifying why one venue's ordering is more defensible than the other (which is rare — usually neither is). A wide gap between the two orderings is itself informative and should be reported, not hidden behind a single midpoint number.

Running this on series that were never actually shown to be cointegrated. Exactly as the VECM lesson warns about its own \(\alpha\) coefficients, the entire common-factor framework here — the claim that \(\Psi(1)\) has identical, rank-deficient rows — is only valid for genuinely cointegrated, price-parity-linked series. Two merely-correlated but economically unrelated assets do not have a shared common efficient price to attribute variance to at all; computing an "Information Share" between them is not meaningful, regardless of how clean the arithmetic looks.

Sensitivity to sampling frequency. Whether a given venue "leads" price discovery can flip depending on whether the VECM is fit on tick-level data, one-minute bars, or daily closes. At very high frequency, bid-ask bounce and other microstructure noise inflate each venue's own apparent shock variance and distort the residual correlation the whole decomposition depends on; at low frequency, genuine intraday leadership by one venue can average out or even reverse in the aggregated data. A price-discovery conclusion reached at one sampling frequency is not automatically valid at another, and this lesson does not derive the correct frequency to use for any specific question — that choice has to match what the underlying research question actually needs.

Information Share answers a variance question, not a liquidity question. A high IS says a venue's price moves first when new information arrives — it does not say that venue is more liquid, has tighter spreads, or is cheaper to trade in. Those are related but genuinely distinct questions, addressed by this repo's own existing microstructure lessons — Glosten-Milgrom, PIN, and Roll's Spread Estimator — rather than by anything in this lesson. A venue can lead price discovery and simultaneously be the less liquid, more expensive venue to actually execute in; nothing here should be read as a liquidity or execution-cost recommendation.

✓Hasbrouck IS decomposes variance, not just adjustment speed. It builds on the same two-equation VECM as the sibling lesson but asks a sharper question: of the variance in the shock moving the common efficient price, how much is attributable to each venue?

Section 05

Self-test

Q1. How does the question Hasbrouck's Information Share answers differ from the question the VECM lesson's α_Y-vs-α_X comparison answers?
Comparing \(|\alpha_Y|\) and \(|\alpha_X|\) is about adjustment speed — which venue does more of the correcting once a spread has already opened up. Information Share is about variance attribution at the moment new information arrives — of the variance in the shock moving the shared common efficient price, how much is attributable to each venue. A venue can adjust slowly (small \(|\alpha|\)) and still be the one where information shows up first; the two questions are related but not the same, and IS is the rigorous answer to the second one.
Q2. Why does the long-run impact matrix Ψ(1) have identical rows in this specific setup, and what economic fact forces that?
Because \(Y_t\) and \(X_t\) are cointegrated on a price-parity relationship — they're alternate observed prices for essentially one asset — any genuinely permanent shock has to move both venues' prices by the same amount in the long run, or the cointegrating relationship linking them would itself be violated. That forces \(\Psi(1)\) to be rank-1 and expressible as \(\iota\psi'\) with \(\iota=(1,1)'\), which makes both of its rows equal to the one shared vector \(\psi'\).
Q3. Mechanically, what causes the Cholesky-ordering sensitivity of Hasbrouck IS?
The two venues' VECM residuals are correlated (\(\sigma_{YX}\neq0\)) whenever they share contemporaneous information, which is the normal case. Splitting the common factor's total variance additively into "venue Y's part" and "venue X's part" leaves a jointly-owned cross term with no natural rule for dividing it. Cholesky-decomposing \(\Omega\) resolves this by manufacturing orthogonal shocks — but doing so requires arbitrarily choosing which venue is listed first, and whichever venue goes first claims the entire shared, correlated variance for itself while the second-ordered venue only gets what's left over.
Q4. Given ψ_Y=0.5, ψ_X=0.5, σ_Y²=2, σ_X²=2, σ_YX=0, compute σ_w² and explain why the ordering doesn't matter here.
\(\sigma_w^2=\psi_Y^2\sigma_Y^2+2\psi_Y\psi_X\sigma_{YX}+\psi_X^2\sigma_X^2 = 0.25(2)+2(0.25)(0)+0.25(2)=0.5+0+0.5=1.0\). Because \(\sigma_{YX}=0\), there is no shared, correlated variance to divide between orderings in the first place — plugging \(\sigma_{YX}=0\) into either ordering's formula collapses both to the same unambiguous split, \(\text{IS}_Y=\psi_Y^2\sigma_Y^2/\sigma_w^2=0.5\) and \(\text{IS}_X=0.5\), regardless of which venue is listed first.
Q5. Name Hasbrouck's own suggested fix for the ordering problem, and name one alternative measure the literature offers instead.
Hasbrouck's own fix: compute the Information Share under both Cholesky orderings and report the midpoint (or the full range) rather than a single ordering's number. Alternative measures that avoid the same ordering dependence: Gonzalo & Granger's (1995) Component Share (built from the same \(\psi\) loadings but using raw, non-orthogonalized variances) and Putniņš' (2013) Information Leadership Share, which combines Hasbrouck IS and Component Share into one measure designed to reconcile cases where they disagree.
Q6. A venue has a consistently high Hasbrouck Information Share relative to a second venue for the same asset. Does this, by itself, mean that venue is more liquid or cheaper to trade in?
No. A high Information Share means new information tends to show up in that venue's price first — a statement about variance attribution in price discovery. It says nothing directly about bid-ask spreads, depth, or execution cost, which are the domain of this repo's own Glosten-Milgrom, PIN, and Roll's Spread Estimator lessons. A venue can lead price discovery and still be the more expensive, less liquid venue to actually trade in — the two questions are related in practice but are not the same claim, and conflating them is a common pitfall.

Further reading
  • Joel Hasbrouck, "One Security, Many Markets: Determining the Contributions to Price Discovery," Journal of Finance, 1995 — the seminal paper defining Information Share, the Wold/VECM-based common-factor construction, and the Cholesky-orthogonalization approach this lesson builds on directly.
  • Jesús Gonzalo and Clive W.J. Granger, "Estimation of Common Long-Memory Components in Cointegrated Systems," Journal of Business & Economic Statistics, 1995 — the permanent-transitory decomposition and Component Share measure referenced conceptually above; consult it directly for the precise general algebra rather than this lesson's two-venue special case.
  • Tālis J. Putniņš, "What Do Price Discovery Metrics Really Measure?," Journal of Empirical Finance, 2013 — studies where Hasbrouck IS and Component Share disagree and proposes the Information Leadership Share as a reconciling measure; described here only conceptually.
  • Robert F. Engle and Clive W.J. Granger, "Co-integration and Error Correction: Representation, Estimation, and Testing," Econometrica, 1987 — the Granger Representation Theorem underlying the VECM/Wold-representation link this lesson relies on, already cited in full in the sibling VECM lesson.
