Judge the Decision, Not the Result

A good trade can lose and a reckless one can win. If you grade yourself on results, luck writes your rulebook. This lesson is about the habits that stop it: separating decision from outcome, catching hindsight in the act, and writing your reasoning down before the market answers.

CONCEPT — explained here; desk verdicts quoted where tested ~20 min read Prereqs: Drawdowns and Risk of Ruin (lesson 5); Bayesian Inference (helpful)

TL;DR
  • Outcome bias ("resulting"): judging a decision by how it turned out. Baron and Hershey (1988) showed people rate the same decision as better when its outcome is good; Annie Duke's Thinking in Bets named the habit "resulting".
  • Hindsight bias: once you know the outcome, it feels as though you always knew. Fischhoff (1975) showed that knowing an outcome raises how predictable people think it was — and they don't notice the shift.
  • One result says almost nothing. A 55% edge loses 45% of the time; a single win moves a fair 50/50 prior only to 55%. It takes on the order of 150 trades to be 95% sure which kind of trader you are.
  • The cure is to write first. A decision journal records what you expect, why, how confident, the base rate, and what would change your mind — before the outcome. A pre-mortem (Gary Klein) asks "it failed — why?" before you commit.
  • The desk's verdict: its own pre-registered prior lost — it expected a drawn funding backstop to warn of wider markets, and found the opposite — and a 54.1% "hit rate" turned out to be exactly the 54.1% base rate. Both are nulls, reported as measured. Whether journals make traders better is untested here.
Section 01

Why this matters

The previous lesson, Drawdowns and Risk of Ruin, showed that a real edge still produces long losing streaks. This one asks the next question: if results are that noisy, how do you learn anything from them? Every trader runs an informal feedback loop — what worked, I do more of; what failed, I stop. When outcomes are mostly luck, that loop learns the luck. Over a career it can talk you out of good habits and into bad ones.

This is the last lesson in the series, and it ties the others together. Loss aversion (lesson 1), the disposition effect (lesson 2), overconfidence (lesson 3), herding (lesson 4) and the drawdown panic (lesson 5) are all easier to see on paper, after the fact, if you wrote down what you were thinking before it happened. Without that record, hindsight edits your memory and the lesson you take is the one luck handed you.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Getting home safely doesn't make the drive a good idea

Someone drives home after too many drinks and arrives without incident. Was it a good decision? Obviously not — the outcome was fine, the decision was bad. Someone else drives home sober, carefully, and is hit by a driver who ran a red light. Bad outcome, good decision. Nobody struggles with these examples, because the luck is obvious.

Markets are the same, except the luck is invisible. A trade made on a whim that wins looks exactly like a trade made on a tested plan that wins. Your P&L cannot tell them apart. Only a record of why you did it — written before you knew the result — can.

Section 02

Four ideas, and a little arithmetic

1. Outcome bias: "resulting"

In a 1988 study, Jonathan Baron and John Hershey gave people descriptions of decisions under uncertainty — for example a doctor's decision to perform an operation — with exactly the same information available at the time, and varied only how things turned out. People rated the decision, and the competence of the person who made it, higher when the outcome was good. Former professional poker player Annie Duke, in Thinking in Bets (2018), calls this habit resulting: treating the quality of the result as if it were the quality of the decision.

Separating the two gives a 2×2 grid. Two of the cells are comfortable — good decisions that worked, bad ones that didn't. The other two are where learning goes wrong: a good decision that lost tempts you to abandon a sound process, and a bad decision that won teaches you to repeat it. That last cell, the lucky mistake, is the most dangerous one in trading, because it pays you to do the wrong thing.

2. Hindsight bias: "I knew it all along"

In 1975 Baruch Fischhoff gave people an account of a little-known historical conflict — the British–Gurkha war of 1814 — and asked how likely each possible outcome had been. Some were told which outcome actually happened. Those people rated that outcome as more likely, and judged that they would have seen it coming, than people who were not told. They did not realise their judgment had been changed. In a companion study with Ruth Beyth, people who had estimated the chances of events around President Nixon's 1972 trips to Beijing and Moscow later remembered having given higher probabilities to the things that then happened.

For a trader this is corrosive in two directions. After a loss, the warning signs look obvious, so you blame yourself for missing what was not knowable. After a win, the logic looks inevitable, so you credit yourself for a call that was partly luck. In both cases the memory of what you actually thought is gone — replaced by a version that fits the ending.

3. How much does one result tell you?

Suppose a good process wins 55% of the time and a poor one 45%, and before any trades you think each is equally likely to describe you. By Bayes' rule, each result multiplies the odds by a likelihood ratio:

\[ \frac{P(\text{good}\mid \text{data})}{P(\text{poor}\mid \text{data})} = \frac{P(\text{good})}{P(\text{poor})} \times \left(\frac{p_g}{p_b}\right)^{w} \left(\frac{1-p_g}{1-p_b}\right)^{n-w} \]

One win takes you from 50% to 55% sure you are the good trader. Seven wins in ten: 69%. Sixty in a hundred: 98%. On average it takes about 150 trades for a genuinely 55% process to reach 95% certainty from a 50/50 start. A single trade — the thing outcome bias grades — barely moves the needle. (The same logic, with full priors, is in Bayesian Inference.)

4. Base rates: the number your hit rate must beat

"This setup is right 54% of the time" sounds like an edge. Whether it is depends entirely on how often the market does that thing anyway. If the Nasdaq rises in 54% of hours regardless, a signal that is right 54% of the time knows nothing. Writing the base rate down next to every claimed hit rate is the simplest guard against fooling yourself — and, as Section 05 shows, it is the exact trap the desk measured in one of its own tests.

5. The decision journal and the pre-mortem

The fix for both biases is the same: capture your reasoning while it still belongs to the past-you who did not know the outcome. A decision journal entry, written before the trade resolves, records:

1

What I expect

The specific outcome, the horizon, and the size of move — concrete enough that it can be checked.

2

Why

The reasons, the evidence for them, and the base rate: how often does this happen anyway?

3

How confident

A number, not a mood. 60% means you expect to be wrong on four in ten of these.

4

What would change my mind

The observation that would make you exit or reverse — decided now, so it cannot be renegotiated later.

5

Pre-mortem

"It is a month from now and this went badly. What is the most likely reason?" Gary Klein's technique: imagining the failure has already happened makes people better at finding the reasons it might.

Klein's pre-mortem draws on research by Deborah Mitchell, J. Edward Russo and Nancy Pennington (1989) on "prospective hindsight": imagining that an event has already occurred helps people generate more reasons for it than asking whether it might. The pre-mortem borrows hindsight's vividness without its distortion, because you use it before the result exists.

Over many entries, a journal also lets you score yourself. The standard score for probability forecasts is the Brier score (Brier, 1950), the average squared gap between the confidence you stated and what happened:

\[ \text{BS} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} (f_i - o_i)^2 \]
fᵢthe confidence you wrote down for entry i, as a probability
oᵢ1 if the expected outcome happened, 0 if not
BS0 is perfect; always saying 50% scores 0.25; lower is better

Philip Tetlock's long-running studies of forecasters (Expert Political Judgment, 2005; Superforecasting, with Dan Gardner, 2015) found that the people who forecast best tended to be the ones who kept score, stated probabilities precisely, and updated in small steps — exactly what a journal forces you to do.

Section 04

It actually happened

It actually happened

February 2015: "the worst call in Super Bowl history"

Super Bowl XLIX, 1 February 2015. The Seattle Seahawks trail the New England Patriots 28–24 with under half a minute left, second down, the ball on New England's one-yard line. Seattle has a powerful running back and most observers expect a run. Instead coach Pete Carroll calls a pass. Russell Wilson's throw is intercepted at the goal line by Malcolm Butler, and New England wins. The next day the call was widely described as one of the worst in Super Bowl history.

Annie Duke opens Thinking in Bets with this play. Her argument is not that the call was certainly right, but that the verdict was pure resulting: interceptions from that position are rare, an incomplete pass stops the clock and leaves time for more plays, and a reasonable case exists that passing on that down gave Seattle more total chances to score. Had the pass been caught, the same call would have been remembered as clever. The decision did not change between those two worlds — only the outcome did.

It actually happened — on this desk

October 2026: a prior written down, and beaten

The desk that runs these lessons tests market folklore with a strict habit: before any data is touched, it writes the claim, the exact definitions, and its own expectation — with the date — and commits that document so it cannot be edited after the result. In October 2026 it tested a popular "plumbing" warning: that when the Federal Reserve's Standing Repo Facility is actually drawn on, cash is scarce and markets will get wider and weaker.

The written expectation was clear: this would be "Real, and the strongest cell here", with a wider S&P 500 range expected the following week. The result came back the other way. Over 21 episodes, the S&P 500's next-five-session range was narrower than matched controls, and the facility turned out to be drawn on nearly a third of all sessions — routine, not a stress signal.

Without the written prior, it would have been easy to say afterwards that the facility being used "obviously" just meant the backstop was working, and that this was expected all along. With it, the record is plain: the expectation lost, and the null was reported as measured. That is hindsight bias defeated by a dated piece of paper.

Worked example

Ten journal entries, scored

1

The record

You wrote 70% confidence on all ten entries. Six came true, four did not.

2

Calibration

Stated 70%, realised 60%. On ten entries that gap is well within noise — but it is the direction overconfidence (lesson 3) predicts. Keep scoring.

3

Brier score

Six hits: \((0.7-1)^2 = 0.09\) each. Four misses: \((0.7-0)^2 = 0.49\) each. \(\text{BS} = (6 \times 0.09 + 4 \times 0.49)/10 = 2.50/10 = 0.25\) — no better than always saying 50%.

4

Bayes on the P&L

If those were ten trades and you won six, how sure should you be you are a 55% trader rather than a 45% one? Odds \(= 1.22^{6-4} = 1.49\), so 60%. Ten trades are nowhere near enough to grade yourself on results — which is why the journal grades the decisions.

5

The grid

Of the four losers, your own after-the-fact grading says three followed the plan (bad luck) and one broke it (just deserts). Of the six winners, one broke the plan (dumb luck). Two plan-breaks out of ten is the number to work on — not the four losses.

Section 05

What this desk found when it tested the claims

Desk test: nothing found A pre-registered prior that lost: "a drawn backstop warns of a wider tape". Expected in writing, before the run, to be the strongest result. Over 21 episodes the S&P 500's next-5-session range was −0.561 ATR [−0.984, −0.181] versus control — calmer, not wider — and negative in both halves. The facility is drawn on 667 of 2,123 sessions (31%): routine plumbing, not stress. The other setup left just 15 episodes, below a floor of 20 set in advance, and was declared untestable rather than quoted. Reported against the desk's own expectation.
Desk test: nothing found The base-rate trap: "a confirmed turn in short-term rates leads the Nasdaq". 2,010 de-clustered events: signed Nasdaq return at one hour −0.005% versus control [−0.021, +0.012], null at 1h, 2h and 4h, both directions, both halves — and well powered. The telling number: a confirmed rates low is followed by an up-move 54.1% of the time, and the base rate is 54.1%. A hit rate quoted without its base rate looked like an edge and was exactly nothing.
Desk data: base rate Hindsight in machines: "a backtest that looks too good has a bug". A backtest is a decision journal written by a program — and it can cheat the same way a memory does, by seeing the outcome. On this desk: one system's whole edge came from stops that were not live for the first hour; a Fibonacci-level system showed an 85.7% backtest win rate against 39.6% live; a volatility tool scored 95% of its "wins" before entry. Audit what the engine could see at decision time.
Not tested here "Keeping a decision journal makes you a better trader." Not tested here, and the outside evidence is mostly indirect: forecasting research shows that scoring and precise probabilities help forecasters, but there is little rigorous evidence on journals and trading P&L specifically. The case for a journal in this lesson rests on what it prevents — hindsight and resulting — not on a measured return.
The habit that works: write the expectation, the confidence and the base rate down before the answer exists, and publish the answer whichever way it goes. Every verdict in this series — including the ones that embarrassed the desk's own priors — exists because of that habit.
Section 06

How practitioners actually use this

📝

Pre-registration

Research teams write the hypothesis, the test and the pass bar before running it — the institutional version of a journal (see From Hypothesis to Backtest and Multiple Testing).

🔍

Process reviews

Post-trade reviews ask "did we follow the plan, and was the plan right given what we knew?" before they ask "did we make money?" Winners get reviewed too — that is where the lucky mistakes hide.

⚰️

Pre-mortems

Before a large position or a new strategy goes live, the team imagines it has failed and lists why. The reasons become stop rules and monitoring, not post-hoc excuses.

🎯

Scoring forecasts

Stated probabilities, Brier scores and calibration charts turn "I'm usually right" into a number that can be checked — and tracked over time.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where process-over-outcome goes wrong

Writing the entry afterwards. A journal filled in after the result is a hindsight generator. If it isn't written before, it doesn't count.

Vague expectations. "EUR should do well" cannot be scored. Name the level, the horizon and the probability.

Using "process" to excuse everything. Process over outcome is not "results never matter". Over many trades, results are exactly how you test whether the process is good — the point is not to judge it on a handful.

Skipping the base rate. A 54% hit rate means nothing until you know the market does the same thing 54% of the time anyway.

Only reviewing losers. The bad-decision-good-outcome cell never gets reviewed unless you look at winners with the same care.

Key Takeaways

✓Decision quality and outcome are different axes. Grade them separately; the lucky mistake is the dangerous cell.
✓Hindsight edits memory. Fischhoff (1975): knowing the outcome makes it seem predictable, without people noticing. Only a record written before beats it.
✓One result is weak evidence. At 55% vs 45%, one win moves 50% to 55%; ~150 trades on average to reach 95%.
✓Journal before, pre-mortem before, score after. Expectation, reasons, base rate, confidence, what would change your mind; then a Brier score.

Section 08

Self-test

Q1. A trader doubles their usual size on a hunch to win back losses, and makes 4R. Where does this go on the grid, and why does it matter?
Bad decision, good outcome — dumb luck. It matters because the reward teaches the trader to repeat a rule-break, and the cost of that lesson arrives later, often larger. It is the cell a results-only review never catches.
Q2. What did Fischhoff's 1975 experiment show, and what does it imply for post-trade reviews?
People told the outcome of a historical event rated that outcome as more likely, and believed they would have predicted it, compared with people not told — and they did not realise their judgment had shifted. A review done from memory after the result will overstate how knowable the result was; only reasoning recorded beforehand gives a fair comparison.
Q3. You start 50/50 between being a 55% trader and a 45% trader. You win 12 of 20. How sure should you be you're the 55% trader?
Odds \(= (0.55/0.45)^{12} \times (0.45/0.55)^{8} = 1.222^{4} \approx 2.23\), so about 69%. Twenty trades have shifted you, but nowhere near certainty.
Q4. A signal "is right 54% of the time". What single extra number do you need before calling it an edge?
The base rate — how often the outcome happens without the signal. In the desk's rates-pivot test the hit rate was 54.1% and the base rate was 54.1%: zero edge.
Q5. You state 80% confidence on five entries; three come true. What is your Brier score?
Hits: \((0.8-1)^2 = 0.04\) × 3 = 0.12. Misses: \((0.8-0)^2 = 0.64\) × 2 = 1.28. \(\text{BS} = 1.40/5 = 0.28\) — worse than always saying 50% (0.25), a sign of overconfidence, though five entries is a tiny sample.
Q6. Why does pre-registering a test — writing the expectation down and committing it before the data is run — protect against hindsight bias?
Because the expectation is fixed, dated and uneditable before the result exists. When the result arrives, there is no room to remember having expected it. The desk's funding-stress test is the example: its written prior said "the strongest cell", the result went the other way, and the record shows that plainly instead of a revised story.
