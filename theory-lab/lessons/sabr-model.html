The SABR Model

Four parameters and one closed-form approximation for the implied-volatility smile — the reason SABR, not the arguably richer Heston model, is what an FX or rates options desk actually recalibrates every time the market moves.

CONCEPT — the FX-options-market-standard smile model ~16 min read Prereqs: The Heston Model
TL;DR
  • SABR has four parameters — \(\alpha\) (current vol level), \(\beta\) (backbone exponent, usually fixed by convention), \(\rho\) (correlation → skew), \(\nu\) (vol-of-vol → curvature) — each controlling a geometrically distinct feature of the smile.
  • Its whole practical advantage over Heston is the Hagan et al. (2002) closed-form algebraic approximation for implied vol: no characteristic function, no Fourier inversion, just exponents/logs/a square root, so a desk can refit it against live ATM/risk-reversal/butterfly quotes in real time.
  • With \(\beta\) fixed by convention, calibration reduces to three unknowns (\(\alpha,\rho,\nu\)) against three standard FX quotes — a small, fast, well-identified fit rather than a slow global optimization.
  • The formula is an asymptotic approximation, not exact — it can misbehave (even imply arbitrage) at extreme strikes/maturities or high \(\nu\), which is what "Arbitrage-Free SABR" (2014) patches numerically.
  • This is the last lesson in the Advanced Stochastic Calculus & Derivatives tier: Itô → Girsanov → Heston (dynamics done right) → SABR (the fast, practitioner-friendly cousin of Heston, not a "better" model, just one built for speed of fit).
Section 01

Why this matters

The Heston lesson built a genuine stochastic-volatility model: volatility itself is a random process, correlated with the underlying's returns through \(\rho\), and that correlation is what produces the skewed, non-flat implied-volatility smile real options markets actually show (something plain Black-Scholes, with its single constant \(\sigma\), structurally cannot produce). Heston is correct in spirit and is a real, self-consistent model of how volatility evolves. But it has a practical problem this lesson exists to solve: pricing a single option under Heston requires evaluating its characteristic function and numerically inverting a Fourier integral, and calibrating Heston's five parameters to match today's observed market smile means doing that numerical inversion over and over inside an optimizer's search loop. That is workable overnight or for a research backtest. It is not what happens at a live FX options desk that needs to re-quote a USD/JPY smile every time spot ticks, dozens or hundreds of times a session.

SABR (Hagan, Kumar, Lesniewski & Woodward, "Managing Smile Risk," 2002) solves exactly that problem, and solves it well enough that it became the de facto industry-standard model for quoting and interpolating implied volatility smiles in both FX options and interest-rate options (swaptions, caps/floors) markets. It is a different kind of stochastic-volatility model from Heston — simpler in its assumed dynamics, not more "sophisticated" — but its authors derived a very good closed-form algebraic approximation for the implied volatility smile directly in terms of its four parameters and the strike and forward. That single fact — no numerical integration, no Fourier inversion, just algebra — is the entire reason SABR won the practitioner adoption race. This lesson covers the model's specification, what each of its four parameters actually controls, the closed-form approximation itself, and the daily desk workflow (fit to a handful of liquid quotes, read off the rest of the smile) that this closed form makes possible.

💡
Plain-English intuition

A volatility model built to be fit fast, not to be exhaustively "correct"

Every stochastic-volatility model faces the same two jobs: describe how volatility might evolve (that's the modeling job), and let a trader match the model's parameters to whatever the market is quoting right now (that's the calibration job). Heston takes the modeling job very seriously — mean-reverting variance, its own volatility, correlation with the spot — and pays for that seriousness with a calibration job that's numerically heavy. SABR asks a different question: what is the simplest stochastic-volatility model whose implied-vol smile can be written down almost in closed form? The SABR dynamics themselves are, if anything, a step down in modeling ambition from Heston — no mean reversion in the volatility process at all, just a volatility level that random-walks (in log terms) forever. What makes SABR valuable isn't a claim that this simpler process is a more faithful description of "true" volatility dynamics. It's that Hagan and his co-authors found an accurate algebraic shortcut from "here are the four parameters" straight to "here is the implied volatility at any strike" — skipping the option-pricing step (no PDE to solve, no Fourier transform to invert) entirely. A trader can type four numbers into a formula and read off an entire smile in the time it takes to hit enter. That speed, not superior realism, is SABR's whole reason for existing in daily practice.

The key distinction: SABR is not "better" than Heston — it answers a different question. Heston gets the risk-neutral dynamics right at the cost of a slow, numerical calibration; SABR trades away some of that dynamical rigor for a closed-form formula fast enough to refit continuously against a live, moving market. Neither wins in the abstract.
Section 02

The math, step by step

Three things to cover in order: (1) the SABR stochastic differential equations — what "Stochastic Alpha, Beta, Rho" actually names, (2) what each of the four parameters does to the shape of the smile, and (3) the closed-form Hagan approximation that turns those four numbers into a full implied-volatility curve.

1. The model specification — two coupled SDEs

SABR models a forward price or forward rate \(F_t\) — a forward FX rate, a forward swap rate, whichever underlying the option is actually struck against — together with its own instantaneous volatility level \(\alpha_t\), which is itself a random process rather than a fixed constant:

\[ dF_t = \alpha_t F_t^{\beta}\,dW_t^{1} \] \[ d\alpha_t = \nu\,\alpha_t\,dW_t^{2} \] \[ \mathbb{E}\big[dW_t^{1}\,dW_t^{2}\big] = \rho\,dt \]

\(F_t\)The forward price or forward rate at time \(t\) — e.g. the 3-month forward EUR/USD rate, or a forward swap rate for a swaption. SABR is specified for the forward, not the spot, and deliberately gives it zero drift (there is no \(\mu\,dt\) or \(r\,dt\) term on \(F_t\) at all) — under the appropriate forward or swap measure a forward price is, by no-arbitrage construction, already a martingale, so this is the natural measure to model it in directly rather than a simplification.
\(\alpha_t\)(alpha) The instantaneous volatility level of the forward at time \(t\). \(\alpha_0\) (today's value, usually just written \(\alpha\)) is one of the four calibrated parameters and is, roughly, "today's at-the-money volatility level" — though not exactly equal to it once the correction terms below are included.
\(\beta\)(beta) The backbone exponent — a fixed constant (not itself random) controlling how strongly volatility scales with the level of the forward itself. Explained in full below.
\(\nu\)(nu) The volatility of volatility ("vol-of-vol") — how much \(\alpha_t\) itself fluctuates. Note \(\alpha_t\) follows plain driftless geometric Brownian motion in \(\nu\) — the same lognormal SDE form as the underlying in the Black-Scholes lesson, just applied to the volatility parameter instead of to price. There is no mean-reversion term here at all, unlike Heston's variance process — a deliberate simplification.
\(\rho\)(rho) The correlation between the two driving Brownian motions \(dW_t^1\) (shocks to the forward) and \(dW_t^2\) (shocks to volatility) — the same skew-generating role \(\rho\) plays in Heston.
\(dW_t^1, dW_t^2\)Two correlated standard Brownian motions (Wiener processes) — the two independent sources of randomness in the model, one driving the forward, one driving its volatility, linked by \(\rho\).

"SABR" is simply an acronym for the four objects that fully specify this system: Stochastic Alpha (the random volatility level \(\alpha_t\)), Beta (the backbone exponent), Rho (the correlation). "Vol-of-vol" \(\nu\) is the fourth parameter, present in the dynamics but not in the acronym's letters. Compare this directly to Heston: Heston models the variance \(v_t\) as a mean-reverting (Cox-Ingersoll-Ross) process with its own long-run level, mean-reversion speed, and vol-of-vol, plus a correlation \(\rho\) with the spot. SABR instead lets the underlying's own exponent \(\beta\) do work that Heston does with its CIR specification, drops mean-reversion in volatility entirely, and in exchange gets an implied-vol formula you can write on a whiteboard.

2. What each parameter controls — backbone, skew, curvature

The four parameters are not four interchangeable dials on the same knob — each one governs a geometrically distinct feature of the resulting implied-volatility smile (implied vol plotted against strike, at a fixed expiry). Understanding what's genuinely separable between them is most of what's needed to use the model sensibly.

\(\alpha\) — the current volatility level. This sets the overall height of the smile: roughly, the at-the-money implied volatility level, before the (typically small) correction terms in the closed-form formula below are applied. Raise \(\alpha\) and the entire smile shifts up roughly in parallel; it does the same job as \(\sigma\) in Black-Scholes or \(v_0\) in Heston — "how volatile is this market, right now."

\(\beta\) — the backbone exponent. This controls how volatility itself scales with the level of the forward — not the shape of today's smile directly, but the relationship between today's ATM vol and what ATM vol would look like if the forward itself were sitting at a different level. \(\beta = 1\) makes the model "lognormal-like": \(F_t^{\beta} = F_t\), so the diffusion term \(\alpha_t F_t\,dW_t^1\) makes percentage moves the relevant quantity, exactly like geometric Brownian motion in Black-Scholes — volatility scales roughly proportionally with the forward's level. \(\beta = 0\) makes the model "normal-like": \(F_t^{\beta} = 1\), so \(dF_t = \alpha_t\,dW_t^1\) is arithmetic Brownian motion — volatility is an absolute (not percentage) quantity, unaffected by the forward's own level, which is exactly the "normal model" convention rates desks lean on when yields are near zero or negative (a fractional power of a negative forward rate, \(F_t^{\beta}\) for non-integer \(\beta\), is not even well-defined — a real, recurring problem for lognormal-style, \(\beta\)-near-1 rate models once government bond yields went negative after 2008 and again in 2015, and part of why the "normal" \(\beta=0\) convention became standard in parts of the rates market). Values of \(\beta\) between 0 and 1 interpolate between these two extremes (e.g. \(\beta = 0.5\) resembles a CIR/square-root-style scaling). Market convention very often simply fixes \(\beta\) by asset-class convention (FX desks commonly use \(\beta\) at or near 1; parts of the rates market use \(\beta = 0\) or a shifted variant) rather than treating it as a free parameter to fit — the practical reason why is explained after the closed-form formula below.

\(\rho\) — correlation, the smile's skew. Exactly the same conceptual role \(\rho\) plays in Heston: it sets the sign and strength of the asymmetry between how the forward moves and how its volatility moves. A negative \(\rho\) (forward falls, volatility tends to rise) tilts the smile so that low strikes (puts, downside protection) carry higher implied vol than high strikes (calls) — a downward-sloping skew, the shape seen in most equity index markets and in many FX pairs during risk-off conditions. \(\rho\) is what makes the smile asymmetric rather than a symmetric "smile" shape.

\(\nu\) — vol-of-vol, the smile's curvature. This is the parameter that specifically controls how convex — how pronouncedly "U-shaped" or "smile-shaped" — the implied-vol curve is around its at-the-money point, largely independent of its skew. A low \(\nu\) means volatility itself barely fluctuates, so the smile stays nearly flat across strikes (close to the flat-\(\sigma\) Black-Scholes world). A high \(\nu\) means volatility itself is highly uncertain, which fattens the tails of the distribution of \(F_T\) symmetrically in both directions (deep out-of-the-money puts and calls both become more richly priced relative to at-the-money), producing a pronounced U-shape. The chart below isolates exactly this effect: three smile curves sharing the same at-the-money level and the same \(\rho\) (so no skew tilt), differing only in \(\nu\).

3. The closed-form implied-volatility approximation (Hagan et al., 2002)

This is SABR's central practical result. Hagan, Kumar, Lesniewski and Woodward used a singular-perturbation / asymptotic-expansion technique (matched asymptotics on the SABR SDEs' transition density — a genuinely technical derivation, outside this lesson's scope) to derive a very accurate closed-form algebraic approximation for the Black-76 implied volatility \(\sigma_B\) (the implied vol you'd plug into the standard forward-price option formula to reproduce a SABR-consistent option price) directly in terms of the strike \(K\), the forward \(F\), the time to expiry \(T\), and the four SABR parameters:

\[ \sigma_B(K,F) \approx \frac{\alpha}{(FK)^{\frac{1-\beta}{2}}\Big[1+\frac{(1-\beta)^2}{24}\ln^2\frac{F}{K}+\frac{(1-\beta)^4}{1920}\ln^4\frac{F}{K}\Big]}\cdot\left(\frac{z}{x(z)}\right)\cdot\left[1+\left(\frac{(1-\beta)^2}{24}\frac{\alpha^2}{(FK)^{1-\beta}}+\frac{1}{4}\frac{\rho\beta\nu\alpha}{(FK)^{\frac{1-\beta}{2}}}+\frac{2-3\rho^2}{24}\nu^2\right)T\right] \]
(FK)^((1−β)/2)the moneyness-adjusted scaling factor
z/x(z)the term carrying the smile's characteristic shape
[1+(...)T]the T-scaled correction bracket — flatter for short-dated options
\[ z=\frac{\nu}{\alpha}(FK)^{\frac{1-\beta}{2}}\ln\frac{F}{K}, \qquad x(z)=\ln\left(\frac{\sqrt{1-2\rho z+z^2}+z-\rho}{1-\rho}\right) \]
zcarries ν's effect, vanishing exactly at K=F
x(z)a further transform of z, built from ρ
z/x(z)the ratio responsible for the smile's shape away from ATM
\(\sigma_B(K,F)\)The (Black-76) implied volatility this approximation predicts for an option struck at \(K\), forward at \(F\) — this is the number that finally goes on the desk's quote screen, the entire output the formula exists to produce.
\((FK)^{\frac{1-\beta}{2}}\)A "moneyness-adjusted" scaling factor that reduces to 1 exactly when \(\beta=1\) — one reason \(\beta=1\) is a convenient, simplifying convention (several terms in the formula collapse).
\(\ln(F/K)\)Log-moneyness — how far the strike sits from the forward, in log terms; zero exactly at-the-money. The same quantity that drove \(d_1,d_2\) in Black-Scholes.
\(z\)An intermediate quantity that is (to leading order) proportional to \(\nu/\alpha\) times log-moneyness — it is the variable that carries \(\nu\)'s effect into the formula, vanishing exactly at \(K=F\) (where \(\ln(F/K)=0\)).
\(x(z)\)A further transform of \(z\) built from \(\rho\); the ratio \(z/x(z)\) is the term primarily responsible for the smile's characteristic shape away from at-the-money, and is defined so that \(z/x(z)\to 1\) as \(z\to 0\) (i.e. it reduces to 1 exactly at the money, leaving only the prefactor and the \(T\)-correction bracket).
\(T\)Time to expiry — the correction bracket's contribution (all three \(\rho\)/\(\nu\)-dependent terms) scales with \(T\), so the smile the formula produces is flatter for very short-dated options and more pronounced for longer-dated ones, all else equal.

The formula looks dense, but the practical point is simple: it is pure algebra — exponents, logarithms, a square root, one natural log. Evaluating it at any \(K\) costs essentially nothing computationally. Contrast this with Heston, where getting a single implied vol at a single strike requires evaluating a complex-valued characteristic function and numerically integrating it (or running an FFT across a strike grid, à la Carr-Madan) to get an option price, and then numerically inverting the Black-Scholes formula to back out the implied vol that price corresponds to — a chain of numerical procedures, each with its own tolerance and failure modes, that must be repeated for every candidate parameter set an optimizer proposes during calibration. SABR's formula collapses that entire chain into one direct evaluation. Setting \(K=F\) (at the money) simplifies the general formula considerably (since \(\ln(F/K)=0\) and \(z/x(z)\to1\)) to:

\[ \sigma_{ATM} \approx \frac{\alpha}{F^{1-\beta}}\left[1+\left(\frac{(1-\beta)^2}{24}\frac{\alpha^2}{F^{2-2\beta}}+\frac{1}{4}\frac{\rho\beta\nu\alpha}{F^{1-\beta}}+\frac{2-3\rho^2}{24}\nu^2\right)T\right] \]
α/F^(1−β)the level, simplified at K=F
[1+(...)T]the same T-scaled correction, evaluated at the money

This is why calibration is so fast in practice. With \(\beta\) fixed by convention, only three unknowns remain — \(\alpha\), \(\rho\), \(\nu\) — and the standard FX quoting convention already provides almost exactly three independent market observations to fit them to: the at-the-money volatility, the 25-delta risk reversal (a skew measure — the vol difference between a 25-delta call and a 25-delta put), and the 25-delta butterfly (a curvature measure — how much richer the 25-delta wings are than a pure interpolation between ATM and skew alone would suggest). Three unknowns, three market-quoted constraints, and a closed-form formula to evaluate at each trial point — this is a small, fast, well behaved root-finding or least-squares problem, not a slow global optimization over an expensive pricing function. This is also the practical reason \(\beta\) is usually not included as a fourth free parameter: \(\beta\) and \(\rho\) both influence the smile's skew to some degree (there is a well-documented near-redundancy between them — similar-looking skews can be produced by different \((\beta,\rho)\) combinations), so leaving both free makes the fit poorly identified (multiple, materially different parameter sets fit the same handful of observed quotes about equally well). Fixing \(\beta\) by convention removes that ambiguity and lets \(\rho\) alone carry the skew.

🧮
Worked example

Solving for \(\alpha\) and reading off two off-market-strike vols

Suppose a desk has already fixed \(\beta = 1\) by FX convention, and has previously calibrated \(\rho = -0.25\) and \(\nu = 0.40\) (a modest put skew, moderate vol-of-vol) to the broader shape of the recent EUR/USD smile. Today's 3-month forward is \(F = 1.2000\), time to expiry \(T = 0.25\) years, and the live 3-month ATM implied vol quote is \(10.00\%\). The desk needs \(\alpha\) such that the ATM formula reproduces that quote, then wants the implied vol at two strikes 200 pips either side of the forward.

With \(\beta=1\), the ATM formula's \((1-\beta)^2\) term vanishes and \(F^{1-\beta}=1\), collapsing it to \(\sigma_{ATM} \approx \alpha\big[1+\big(\tfrac14\rho\nu\alpha + \tfrac{2-3\rho^2}{24}\nu^2\big)T\big]\). Solving this (numerically — the correction term is small, so it converges in a couple of iterations from a starting guess of \(\alpha \approx 0.10\)) for \(\sigma_{ATM}=0.10\) gives:

\[ \alpha \approx 0.0998 \;(9.98\%) \]

Note \(\alpha\) came out only trivially below the 10.00% ATM quote — for a 3-month option the \(T\)-scaled correction terms are small, so \(\alpha\) is close to, but not identically equal to, the ATM vol itself; the gap would widen for a longer-dated option or a larger \(\nu\). Plugging this \(\alpha\) (with the same \(\beta,\rho,\nu,F,T\)) into the full closed-form formula at \(K=1.2200\) (a 200-pip out-of-the-money call) and \(K=1.1800\) (a 200-pip out-of-the-money put) gives:

\[ \sigma_B(K{=}1.22,F) \approx 9.92\%, \qquad \sigma_B(K{=}1.18,F) \approx 10.09\% \]

The put strike prices richer than the call strike — exactly the downward-sloping skew \(\rho=-0.25\) was chosen to produce — and both numbers came from one direct algebraic evaluation each, no repricing loop, no numerical inversion. That is the entire calibrate-then-read-off workflow described in the trading scenario below, done by hand on two strikes; a real desk would do this across a full strike ladder, instantly, every time the market moves.

How ν (vol-of-vol) controls smile curvature, holding ATM fixed
Low ν = 0.10 (nearly flat) Medium ν = 0.40 (mild smile) High ν = 0.80 (pronounced smile)
All three curves use the same \(\beta=1\), \(\rho=0\) (skew deliberately switched off so curvature is isolated), \(F=1.20\), \(T=0.25\), with \(\alpha\) re-solved for each \(\nu\) so every curve shares the identical 10% at-the-money level. Only \(\nu\) differs between the three curves, and only the wings move — this is exactly the sense in which \(\nu\) controls the smile's curvature while leaving its symmetric shape and its ATM level untouched. Compare to \(\rho\), which (holding \(\nu\) fixed) would tilt these same curves left or right rather than pulling their wings up or down symmetrically.
Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

SABR's daily job on an options desk is narrow and specific: given a handful of liquid, actually-tradeable market quotes for a given expiry, produce a full, arbitrage-consistent-looking implied-volatility curve across every strike a client might ask about — including strikes nobody is actively quoting a two-way market in right now. FX and rates option markets don't trade a continuous strike ladder; they trade (and quote tightly) a small standard set of points — the at-the-money straddle, the 25-delta risk reversal, the 25-delta butterfly, sometimes a 10-delta version of each. Everything in between and beyond has to be interpolated or extrapolated, and it has to be done in a way that doesn't introduce arbitrage (e.g. a butterfly spread with a negative price, or an implied density that goes negative somewhere). SABR, fit to those liquid points, is the industry's default tool for producing that full curve, precisely because refitting it is cheap enough to do continuously as the market moves rather than once a day.

🎯
Real-world trading scenario

Quoting a full USD/JPY smile from three numbers, live

An FX options desk needs to be able to quote a two-way price on a USD/JPY option at essentially any strike a client asks for, expiring next week — not just the two or three strikes with an active interdealer market. What the desk actually observes live, streaming from the interdealer brokers, is a small handful of standard points for that expiry: the at-the-money implied vol, the 25-delta risk reversal (the call-vol-minus-put-vol skew measure), and the 25-delta butterfly (the curvature measure). That's it — three numbers, refreshed continuously.

The desk fixes \(\beta\) by convention (FX desks commonly run \(\beta\) at or near 1), then solves the small, well-posed three-equation system above — \(\alpha\), \(\rho\), \(\nu\) fit to ATM/risk-reversal/butterfly — using the closed-form Hagan formula as the pricing function inside a fast local root-finder. Because each trial evaluation is pure algebra rather than a Fourier inversion, this fit runs in effectively real time. Once fit, the desk reads the implied vol for any strike — including one nobody is currently making a market in — directly off the closed-form curve, and that same curve is what feeds the pricing engine when a client asks for an odd-strike or odd-tenor quote. As spot moves and the ATM/RR/BF quotes update through the session, the desk re-solves the same three equations again — and again, and again. This exact workflow — fit SABR to a few liquid quotes, read off (and continuously refresh) the rest of the smile — is standard daily practice on FX and interest-rate options desks, not a research exercise; it is the practical machinery underneath every FX options quote a desk publishes at strikes away from the standard delta points.

That distinction matters under this repo's own honesty discipline (CLAUDE.md's "built ≠ works ≠ has edge") — nothing in this lesson has been calibrated to real market data or validated out-of-sample here; it's knowledge to have on hand, not a claim about anything running in production.

Where it's genuinely relevant: education/open-interest-course-notes.md teaches that dealers who are short gamma must mechanically buy as price rises and sell as it falls, and that this hedging flow is what makes high-open-interest strikes act like magnets and walls — a fact this Theory Lab's Black-Scholes lesson derives formally (gamma as \(\partial^2 C/\partial S^2\)) using a single flat \(\sigma\). Real dealer books are not priced or hedged off a single flat \(\sigma\); every strike in a real book carries its own implied vol, read off a smile exactly like the one this lesson builds, and a dealer's true aggregate gamma exposure across strikes depends on the shape of that smile (via \(\rho\) and \(\nu\)), not just an ATM level. SABR is, in that sense, the practical, calibration-friendly cousin of the Heston lesson this Theory Lab already covers — where Heston teaches the genuine risk-neutral dynamics that produce a smile, SABR is what a real desk actually turns those market-observed smiles into, day to day, and is the missing layer between "dealers have OI-weighted gamma exposure at a strike" (the course notes' claim) and "here is exactly how a real book prices and risk-manages every strike in between" (what an actual dealer's systems do, using something in the SABR family or a close relative of it).

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where the model is misused or over-trusted

Treating the Hagan formula as exact. It is a singular-perturbation asymptotic approximation, not an exact solution — excellent for typical, liquid at-the-money-ish strike ranges and moderate maturities, but known to degrade, and in some parameter regimes to produce outright arbitrage (a non-monotonic, even locally negative, implied probability density), for very low strikes, very long maturities, or unusually high \(\nu\). This is a well-documented, actively studied weakness — Hagan and co-authors themselves published a follow-up, "Arbitrage-Free SABR" (2014), using a PDE/finite-difference approach specifically to patch this failure mode for the strike/maturity combinations where the original closed form breaks down.

Free-fitting \(\beta\) alongside \(\rho\). As noted above, \(\beta\) and \(\rho\) both influence skew and are close to redundant with each other when both are left free — a fit with both floating can land on very different-looking parameter sets that reproduce today's smile about equally well, which is a real identification problem, not a numerical inconvenience to code around. Fixing \(\beta\) by market convention (and letting \(\rho\) alone carry the skew) is the standard, deliberate fix, not a shortcut being taken for lack of computing power.

Confusing a good same-day fit with a good model of smile dynamics over time. SABR, fit fresh each time to today's ATM/RR/BF, can reproduce today's smile shape very well — that's a cross-sectional (single-snapshot) fit. It is a separate, harder question whether the SABR dynamics correctly predict how the whole smile should move as the forward itself moves over the following days (the "backbone" prediction) — a documented critique in the literature is that SABR's backbone prediction (governed by \(\beta\)) does not always match how real market smiles are observed to shift as the underlying moves. Refitting SABR from scratch every day papers over this by construction, but that is refitting, not evidence the dynamical assumption was right.

Forgetting \(F\) is a forward, and forward measures are instrument-specific. SABR is specified under the forward or swap measure appropriate to the specific instrument being priced — an FX forward measure for an FX option, a swap measure for a swaption. It is not a single universal model applied identically everywhere; the "right" \(F\), and the discounting/measure conventions layered on top to get from \(\sigma_B\) to an actual price, differ by asset class and require care that is genuinely outside the scope of what this lesson's implied-vol formula covers on its own.

Section 04

Closing this tier: from Itô's Lemma to the market's smile

This is the last lesson in the Advanced Stochastic Calculus & Derivatives tier, and the four lessons in it form one continuous argument rather than four unrelated topics. Itô's Lemma is the calculus rule that makes it possible to say anything precise at all about a function of a randomly moving quantity — without it, "how does an option's value change as the stock moves" has no rigorous answer. Girsanov's theorem is what licenses the specific mathematical move at the heart of every model in this tier: that you're allowed to change the probability measure you're computing under (from real-world to risk-neutral, or to a forward/swap measure) and have prices still come out consistent — it's the theorem that makes "risk-neutral pricing" more than a convenient fiction. Heston is the first serious attempt to take those two tools and build a model where volatility itself is honestly treated as random, rather than assumed away as a constant — and it succeeds at that, at the cost of needing numerical Fourier methods to actually price anything. SABR, this lesson, is the market's practical answer to the same problem Heston solves in principle: a simpler stochastic-volatility specification, paired with a closed-form approximation good enough that a desk can refit it continuously against live quotes. Together, the four lessons are the rigorous version of a single sentence: Black-Scholes assumes volatility is a known constant; real markets disagree, quoting a whole smile instead of one number; Itô and Girsanov are the machinery that lets you build a model where volatility is genuinely random and still price consistently; and Heston and SABR are the two standard ways that machinery gets used in practice — one for getting the dynamics right, the other for getting the fit fast. Neither is "better" in the abstract; they answer different questions, and knowing which one you're reaching for, and why, is the actual takeaway of this tier.

Key Takeaways

✓SABR's whole advantage is speed, not superior realism. The Hagan et al. closed-form approximation replaces Heston's Fourier inversion with pure algebra — exponents, logs, a square root.
✓Four parameters, four distinct jobs. α sets the level, β the backbone (vol-vs-forward scaling), ρ the skew, ν the curvature — not four interchangeable dials.
✓Fixing β by convention makes calibration well-identified. β and ρ are close to redundant on skew; fixing β leaves a small, fast, three-unknown fit against three standard FX quotes.
✓The worked example: α≈9.98% reproduces a 10% ATM quote, then reads off 9.92%/10.09% at 200-pip strikes either side — one algebraic evaluation each, no repricing loop.
⚠The formula is an approximation, not exact. It can misbehave — even imply arbitrage — at extreme strikes/maturities or high ν, which "Arbitrage-Free SABR" (2014) patches numerically.
⚠A good same-day fit isn't the same as a correct smile-dynamics model. SABR's backbone prediction for how the whole smile should move doesn't always match how real markets shift as the underlying moves.
⚠This repo has no options-pricing or smile-calibration module today. This lesson and the Heston lesson are pure theory background, not a description of anything built or validated here.
Section 06

Self-test

Q1. Why do practitioners often prefer SABR to Heston for day-to-day smile quoting, even though Heston is arguably the more complete stochastic-volatility model?
The deciding factor is calibration speed, not modeling realism. Heston requires evaluating a characteristic function and numerically inverting a Fourier integral to get a single option price, then inverting Black-Scholes to recover the implied vol that price corresponds to — a chain of numerical procedures that has to be repeated for every trial parameter set inside a calibration optimizer. SABR's authors derived a closed-form algebraic approximation for implied volatility directly in terms of the four SABR parameters, the strike, and the forward — no numerical integration or inversion at all. That makes SABR calibration fast enough to run continuously against a live, moving market, which is what an FX or rates options desk actually needs; Heston's added realism (genuine mean-reverting variance dynamics) doesn't outweigh that practical cost for routine smile-quoting, even though it may matter more for other uses (e.g. pricing certain exotics where the dynamics, not just the snapshot smile, matter).
Q2. What does the beta backbone parameter actually control, and how is that distinct from what rho and nu each control?
\(\beta\) controls how volatility scales with the level of the forward itself — \(\beta=1\) makes the model behave like geometric Brownian motion (volatility roughly proportional to the forward's level, "lognormal-like"), \(\beta=0\) makes it behave like arithmetic Brownian motion (volatility an absolute, level-independent quantity, "normal-like"). This is a statement about the relationship between the forward's level and its volatility over time (the "backbone" traced out by ATM vol as the forward moves) — a different question from either \(\rho\) (which sets the skew — the asymmetry between low and high strikes in today's snapshot smile) or \(\nu\) (which sets the curvature — how convex/U-shaped today's snapshot smile is). All three can influence the shape of a fitted smile to some degree, but only \(\beta\) is really about how the volatility level itself is tied to the forward's level, which is why it's normally fixed by convention rather than fit alongside the other two.
Q3. In the chart above, all three curves share the same at-the-money level and the same rho. Why does only nu move the wings, and why don't the curves tilt left or right?
The chart deliberately sets \(\rho=0\) for all three curves, removing the term that would otherwise tilt the smile asymmetrically toward one side. With skew switched off, changing \(\nu\) only changes how much the volatility parameter itself fluctuates — a higher \(\nu\) fattens the distribution of possible volatility outcomes symmetrically, which symmetrically fattens the tails of the implied forward-price distribution in both directions, raising implied vol for both deep out-of-the-money puts and deep out-of-the-money calls roughly equally. That produces a symmetric, more pronounced U-shape as \(\nu\) rises, with the at-the-money point untouched (since the wings move but the curves are pinned to meet at \(K=F\)) — exactly the curvature effect the chart isolates. A nonzero \(\rho\) would instead pull one wing up and the other down, tilting the whole curve rather than symmetrically lifting both wings.
Q4. Why is beta typically fixed by market convention instead of calibrated freely alongside rho?
Because \(\beta\) and \(\rho\) both influence the smile's skew to a significant degree, and their effects are close to redundant with each other — different \((\beta,\rho)\) pairs can produce very similar fitted smiles for the handful of market quotes actually available. If both are left free, the calibration is poorly identified: multiple, materially different parameter combinations fit today's observed quotes about equally well, which makes the fitted \(\rho\) (and any risk sensitivity computed from it) unstable and hard to trust day to day. Fixing \(\beta\) by asset-class convention (near 1 for FX, commonly 0 or a shifted variant for rates in low/negative-rate environments) removes that ambiguity and lets \(\rho\) alone, unambiguously, carry the skew, with \(\nu\) alone carrying curvature — a well-identified three-parameter fit against three market quotes.
Q5. Walk through the practical calibration workflow a desk uses to go from a handful of quoted points to a full smile.
The desk observes a small set of liquid, standard market quotes for a given expiry — typically the at-the-money implied vol, the 25-delta risk reversal (a skew measure), and the 25-delta butterfly (a curvature measure). With \(\beta\) already fixed by convention, this leaves three unknowns (\(\alpha,\rho,\nu\)) and three market-quoted constraints — a small, well-posed root-finding or least-squares problem. The desk solves it by repeatedly evaluating the closed-form Hagan formula (fast, pure algebra) at trial parameter values until the model-implied ATM vol, risk reversal and butterfly match the market-quoted ones. Once fit, the same closed-form formula is evaluated at any strike the desk needs a quote for — including strikes with no active two-way market — to read off the rest of the smile directly. As the underlying market quotes update through the session, the desk re-solves the same three-equation system again, continuously.
Q6. What are the known limitations of the Hagan closed-form approximation, and how does "Arbitrage-Free SABR" relate to them?
The Hagan formula is an asymptotic (singular-perturbation) expansion, not an exact solution to the SABR SDEs' pricing problem — it is very accurate for typical, liquid strike ranges and moderate maturities, but can degrade, and in some parameter regimes produce outright arbitrage (e.g. a non-monotonic or even locally negative implied risk-neutral density), for very low strikes, very long-dated options, or unusually high vol-of-vol. Hagan and co-authors published a follow-up paper, "Arbitrage-Free SABR" (2014), specifically to address this: rather than relying on the closed-form asymptotic expansion in the problematic regimes, it solves the underlying forward Kolmogorov (Fokker-Planck) PDE numerically (finite differences) to get an arbitrage-consistent density and price directly. That is slower than the original closed form but removes the arbitrage failure mode — a real, actively used patch for exactly the strike/maturity combinations where the fast closed form is known not to be reliable.
