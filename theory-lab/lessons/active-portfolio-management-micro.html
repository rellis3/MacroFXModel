Portfolio Construction 4 (final) · Advanced Portfolio Construction

Active Management, Scored Honestly

Alpha vs. Beta Separation showed how to split a return stream into a market bet and a residual. Factor-Based Portfolio Construction then showed how to build that residual out of deliberate, engineered tilts instead of accidental exposures. This closing lesson in Advanced Portfolio Construction asks the question sitting underneath both: how good is that residual, really — and is there even a way for the "average active dollar" to win at all? Four ideas do the work: the Information Ratio (a Sharpe ratio for active bets), the Fundamental Law connecting raw forecasting skill to breadth, Active Share as a second axis alongside tracking error, and the zero-sum accounting identity that bounds the entire active-management industry.

Section 1

The Information Ratio — a Sharpe ratio for active bets

Sharpe asks "did I beat cash." The Information Ratio asks a narrower question: "did I beat this specific benchmark."

Sharpe ratio

S = (r_p − r_f) / σ_p — benchmark-agnostic. Numerator is return over cash. Denominator is the portfolio's total volatility. Only asks whether the ride beat sitting in cash.

Information Ratio

IR = α / ω — benchmark-relative. Numerator is return over a chosen benchmark. Denominator is tracking error, the volatility of just that gap. Asks whether a specific yardstick was beaten.

α is annualized excess return over the benchmark — not over cash. ω (omega) is tracking error, the annualized standard deviation of that benchmark-relative excess return. A manager can have a mediocre Sharpe ratio (a bumpy total ride) while still posting an excellent Information Ratio (that bumpiness consistently beat the benchmark) — the two ratios genuinely answer different questions.

A manager who never mentions a benchmark is quietly answering the Sharpe question. The moment a fee is charged for "beating the index," IR — not Sharpe — is the number that actually judges the job being paid for.
Section 2

The Information Coefficient — raw skill per forecast

IC = corr(forecast, realized return)
+1

Perfect skill

Forecasts ranked every realized outcome exactly right, across the whole universe of calls.

0

Zero skill

Forecasts carried no information at all about what actually happened next.

.05–.10

Genuinely good, in practice

IC is a correlation over an entire forecast universe, not a per-trade win rate — a real, professional-grade edge looks this small, not 0.5.

This concrete number matters for what comes next: it's the input the Fundamental Law multiplies by √BR. A manager never needs anywhere close to IC = 0.5 to build a strong track record — provided breadth is large enough, and genuinely independent.

Checked against this repo's actual code

The full lesson states flatly that "no manager, forecast series or portfolio in this repo has an IR, IC, breadth or Active Share computed for it." That's now stale on the IC piece specifically: rankIC() in js/statsCore.js computes exactly this — a Spearman correlation between a forecast score and its realized forward outcome — and it's real, wired-in code, not a stray utility. js/rankICEngine.js runs it against several trading-signal scores (a day-type composite, momentum slope, a regime call) with a true in-sample/out-of-sample split and a significance test (|t|≥2), and rankICLiveEngine.js reuses the same brick live. What's still true: those are internal signal scores, not portfolio-manager return forecasts in the active-management sense this lesson means — and nothing in the repo computes an Information Ratio, an aggregate breadth statistic, or an Active Share number. Three of the four pieces of this lesson's machinery remain unbuilt; the fourth, IC, is real and tested, just for a different kind of forecast than the one this lesson is about.

Section 3

The Fundamental Law of Active Management

Grinold & Kahn's famous connection: skill per bet, compounded across independent bets.

IR ≈ IC × √BR
IC

Skill per forecast

How good each individual call is — the correlation from the previous slide.

BR

Breadth

The number of statistically independent bets made per year — not the number of trade tickets.

√BR

Diversification arithmetic

The same square-root law behind why a portfolio of uncorrelated positions beats any single one on Sharpe: noise cancels, a repeated edge doesn't.

The breadth trap

BR counts independent bets, not trade tickets. Five G10 currency positions that all move together in a dollar-strength regime are much closer to one independent bet than five — treating correlated positions as independent inflates BR, and makes a target IR look far more achievable than the true, correlation-adjusted opportunity set supports. The next slide makes this concrete.

Section 5

What breadth buys you, for a fixed skill level

Three managers, three fixed skill levels, the same √BR law. Note how each curve keeps rising but flattens — the first 20 independent bets matter far more than the next 200.

At BR = 250 independent bets a year: IC = 0.02 → IR ≈ 0.32. IC = 0.05 → IR ≈ 0.79. IC = 0.10 → IR ≈ 1.58. The gap between the curves is set entirely by skill; the flattening within each curve is set entirely by the square root — doubling breadth never doubles IR.

Section 8

Active Share — measuring the holdings, not the returns

Everything so far is built from returns. Active Share (Cremers & Petajisto, 2009) is built from positions instead.

Active Share = ½ Σ |w_p,i − w_b,i|

A 25% Active Share is, on its own, fairly modest — real over/underweights in four names, but two of six positions (GBP and the untouched "rest of G10") sit exactly on the benchmark. Whether 25% counts as "active enough" depends entirely on what tracking error this same portfolio produced — a question the holdings alone can't answer.

Section 9

Two axes that don't have to agree

Active Share (horizontal) is computed from positions. Tracking error (vertical) is computed from returns. A fund can score high on one and low on the other.

Bottom-left and top-left are both dangerous for an allocator paying an active fee, for opposite reasons: bottom-left does almost nothing different at all; top-left's apparent "activeness" is really just a factor or beta timing bet on an otherwise benchmark-like book — the pattern Cremers & Petajisto call closet indexing, discoverable only by checking holdings directly.

Section 10

The zero-sum floor everything above sits on

Sharpe's 1991 "Arithmetic of Active Management" — an accounting identity, not a claim about skill.

Before costs

Passive investors, by construction, hold the market portfolio. Every unit of every security must be held by someone — so whatever active investors hold, in aggregate, must also equal the market portfolio. Their average return must equal the market's, dollar for dollar.

After costs

Fees, trading costs, and the bid-ask spread crossed on every rebalance push the average active dollar's return below the average passive dollar's — by exactly the amount of those costs.

This holds regardless of whether active managers, as a group, are skilled, unskilled, or anything in between — it's true by the market-clearing definition of "the market," not by any claim about market efficiency. For every investor who beats the benchmark, someone else must lag it by the same amount, before costs. Everything on the previous nine slides — IR, IC, breadth, Active Share — is machinery for finding which manager sits on the winning side of that split. None of it makes finding one easy, or common.
Section 11

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

What goes in the Information Ratio's denominator?

IR = α/ω. ω (tracking error) is the volatility of the benchmark-relative excess return only — not the portfolio's total volatility, which is Sharpe's denominator instead.
Question 2

Why is an Information Coefficient of 0.05–0.10 called "genuinely good" rather than weak?

IC runs from −1 to +1 and is computed across hundreds of forecasts at once. A correlation that small looks unimpressive by intuition, but the Fundamental Law shows it compounds into a meaningful IR once breadth is large enough — 0.05–0.10 is real, working skill.
Question 3

A manager makes 200 trades a year, but they're all really one "dollar strength" view repeated 200 ways. What is BR closer to?

BR counts statistically independent bets, not trade count. Correlated positions that move together are much closer to one bet than many — inflating BR by counting them as independent overstates the predicted IR.
Question 4

A fund reports a modest, plausible Information Ratio and tracking error. Why check Active Share separately?

Tracking error comes purely from the return series; Active Share comes purely from the holdings. A fund can generate the same tracking error either from genuinely different positions or from a mostly benchmark-like book with a single large factor tilt — only Active Share, computed on the actual position file, tells those apart.
Question 5

Does Sharpe's zero-sum result depend on active managers being unskilled?

It's an accounting identity: passive investors hold the market by construction, so whatever's left over — everyone else, in aggregate — must also equal the market. That's true before any assumption about skill enters the picture at all.
Section 12

What to carry forward

01

IR = α/ω is Sharpe's benchmark-relative cousin — excess return over a chosen benchmark, divided by tracking error, not excess return over cash divided by total volatility.

02

IC = corr(forecast, realized) is raw per-forecast skill — 0.05 to 0.10 is genuinely good, because it's a correlation across an entire forecast universe, not a win rate.

03

The Fundamental Law, IR ≈ IC × √BR, lets modest skill compound into a strong IR — provided breadth means genuinely independent bets, not trade count.

04

Active Share (holdings) and tracking error (returns) are different axes that can diverge — low Active Share plus high tracking error is the closet-indexing signature.

05

Before costs, the average active dollar's return must equal the market's — a pure accounting identity (Sharpe, 1991), not a claim about skill or efficiency. After costs, it must underperform.

06

This repo's own rankIC() genuinely computes an Information Coefficient for internal trading-signal scores — but no Information Ratio, breadth statistic, or Active Share exists anywhere in the codebase yet.

What this now lets you do

Judge a manager's benchmark-relative skill with the right ratio, not Sharpe. Read a headline IR and immediately ask whether it comes from rare high-conviction insight or from many small, genuinely independent bets — and whether "many" is real or a correlated illusion. Cross-check tracking error against Active Share before trusting either alone. And treat the zero-sum floor as the honest starting assumption for any active claim, not an afterthought.
