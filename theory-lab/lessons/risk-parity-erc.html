Risk Parity & Equal Risk Contribution

Markowitz needs a return forecast for every asset, and that forecast is the least reliable number in the whole model. Risk parity's answer is blunt and elegant: stop forecasting returns at all — allocate so every asset contributes the same slice of total risk instead.

CONCEPT — theory only, no multi-instrument allocator built or tested in this repo ~16 min read Prereqs: Markowitz & Black-Litterman
TL;DR
  • Markowitz's mean-variance optimizer is dominated by its expected-return inputs — the least reliable numbers in the model — and small forecast errors can flip "optimal" weights into extreme corner solutions.
  • Risk parity sidesteps the problem by dropping expected returns entirely: allocate so that every asset contributes the same amount to total portfolio risk, not the same amount of capital.
  • Define each asset's risk contribution \(RC_i = w_i (\Sigma w)_i / \sigma_p\) — its weight times its marginal contribution to portfolio variance, normalized so all \(RC_i\) sum to the portfolio's total volatility \(\sigma_p\).
  • The Equal Risk Contribution (ERC) portfolio solves for weights such that \(RC_i = RC_j\) for every pair of assets, subject to the weights summing to 1.
  • Worked case: when every pairwise correlation is the same number, the ERC solution reduces exactly to inverse-volatility weighting, \(w_i \propto 1/\sigma_i\) — derived explicitly below. The general case, with a real, uneven correlation structure, has no such closed form and needs numerical solving.
Section 01

Why this matters

The Markowitz & Black-Litterman lesson built the honest case against naive mean-variance optimization: the optimizer is mathematically correct and, in practice, dangerously unstable, because its output is extraordinarily sensitive to the expected-return vector — exactly the input nobody can estimate reliably. Black-Litterman's fix was to moderate that fragile input with a market-equilibrium prior and confidence-weighted views. Risk parity takes a different, more radical line: instead of moderating the expected-return input, it throws it out.

That's a real trade-off, stated plainly rather than sold. Dropping expected returns means a risk-parity portfolio has no opinion at all about which asset will perform better — it only has an opinion about how much risk each asset brings to the table, which (as the Markowitz lesson showed) is the one piece of the model that can be estimated with reasonable stability. Whether that trade is a good one depends entirely on how much genuine signal was in the expected-return forecasts being discarded — a question this lesson does not resolve, and this repo has not tested.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Equal dollars is not equal risk

Split $300 evenly across a government bond fund, a broad equity index, and a commodity index — $100 each, "balanced" by the only definition most people reach for first. It is nothing of the sort. The commodity sleeve is several times more volatile than the bond sleeve, so on any given day almost all of the portfolio's actual up-and-down movement is coming from that one-third of the capital. The bond sleeve is along for the ride, contributing a rounding error's worth of the portfolio's real risk despite being an equal dollar share. Equal weight is not equal risk — and equal risk, not equal capital, is what risk parity actually targets. To get there, the lower-volatility assets have to be held in larger dollar amounts than the higher-volatility ones, until each one's contribution to the portfolio's total wiggle, not its total dollars, comes out the same.

Section 02

The math, step by step

This builds directly on the same quadratic-form portfolio variance from the Markowitz lesson, \(\text{Var}(R_p) = w^\top \Sigma w\), just asking a different question of it: not "what weights minimize this for a given return," but "what weights split this evenly across assets."

1

Marginal contribution to risk

Start from portfolio variance, \(\sigma_p^2 = w^\top \Sigma w\). Each asset's marginal contribution to that variance — how much the variance changes for a small change in \(w_i\), holding everything else fixed — is the \(i\)-th entry of the vector \(\Sigma w\) (the covariance matrix acting on the weight vector, exactly the same operation the Markowitz lesson reads as "\(\Sigma\) acting on \(w\)"):

\[ \frac{\partial \sigma_p^2}{\partial w_i} = 2(\Sigma w)_i \]
2

Risk contribution

Scale that marginal contribution by the asset's own weight, and normalize by total portfolio volatility \(\sigma_p\) (not variance) so the units come out as "risk," not "risk-squared." This is the risk contribution:

\[ RC_i = \frac{w_i (\Sigma w)_i}{\sigma_p} \]
wᵢasset i's portfolio weight
(Σw)ᵢasset i's marginal contribution to portfolio variance
σ_ptotal portfolio volatility — the normalizer
RC_iasset i's slice of total portfolio risk, in the same units as σ_p

This decomposition has one property worth stating explicitly because it's what makes "risk contribution" a meaningful, additive concept rather than an arbitrary definition: the \(RC_i\) sum exactly to the portfolio's total volatility, \(\sum_i RC_i = \sigma_p\), for any set of weights at all — a consequence of \(w^\top \Sigma w\) being a homogeneous quadratic form (Euler's theorem for homogeneous functions, applied to \(\sigma_p\)). Risk contribution isn't an approximation of "how much risk this asset adds" — it's an exact accounting split of the portfolio's total risk into per-asset pieces that add back up to the whole.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Risk contribution is like splitting a restaurant bill by what each person actually ordered, instead of splitting it evenly per head. And it's not a rough guess — it's an exact accounting split: everyone's share adds up perfectly to the total bill, no matter how unevenly the orders were spread.

3

The Equal Risk Contribution (ERC) condition

Risk parity's defining requirement is exactly what the name says: find the weight vector \(w\) such that every asset's slice of that exact accounting split is the same number, subject to the weights summing to 1 (fully invested, no leverage in the base case):

\[ RC_i = RC_j \quad \forall\, i,j, \qquad \text{s.t. } \sum_i w_i = 1 \]

With \(N\) assets this is \(N-1\) independent equations (each \(RC_i = RC_j\) pair, though only \(N-1\) of them are independent) plus the budget constraint, for \(N\) unknown weights — a determined system, but a nonlinear one, because \((\Sigma w)_i\) and \(\sigma_p\) both depend on every entry of \(w\) simultaneously. There is, in general, no algebraic formula that hands back the answer directly; it has to be solved numerically (an iterative scheme, e.g. gradient-based, converging on the weights that equalize every \(RC_i\)).

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Think of ERC as handing out backpacks so everyone in a group ends up carrying the exact same amount of strain — but how much strain any one person feels depends on what everyone else is carrying too. There's no clean formula for that; you just have to shuffle the load back and forth, a little at a time, until it balances out evenly.

4

The tractable special case — equal pairwise correlation

One case does have a clean closed form, and it's worth deriving in full because it's the intuition the general numerical solution is built on. Suppose every pair of assets shares the same correlation \(\rho\) (an idealization, but a useful one). Then the covariance matrix entries are \(\Sigma_{ii} = \sigma_i^2\) and \(\Sigma_{ij} = \rho\,\sigma_i\sigma_j\) for \(i \neq j\). Try the candidate solution \(w_i = k/\sigma_i\) for some constant \(k\) (inverse-volatility weighting) and check what happens to \(w_i \sigma_i\): it equals \(k\) for every asset — a constant, independent of \(i\). Expanding \((\Sigma w)_i\) under this candidate:

\[ (\Sigma w)_i = \sigma_i^2 w_i(1-\rho) + \rho\,\sigma_i S, \qquad S \equiv \sum_j \sigma_j w_j \]

Substituting \(w_i \sigma_i = k\), the term \(\sigma_i^2 w_i = \sigma_i \cdot (\sigma_i w_i) = \sigma_i k\), so \((\Sigma w)_i = \sigma_i k(1-\rho) + \rho\sigma_i S = \sigma_i\big[k(1-\rho) + \rho S\big]\). Then \(RC_i = w_i(\Sigma w)_i/\sigma_p = (k/\sigma_i)\cdot \sigma_i\big[k(1-\rho)+\rho S\big]/\sigma_p = k\big[k(1-\rho)+\rho S\big]/\sigma_p\) — and this final expression has no \(i\) left in it at all. Every asset's \(RC_i\) is the same number, for any \(N\), any set of individual volatilities, as long as the pairwise correlation is uniform. Inverse-volatility weighting doesn't just approximate ERC in this case — it satisfies the ERC condition exactly.

\[ w_i^{ERC} = \frac{1/\sigma_i}{\sum_j 1/\sigma_j} \qquad \text{(exact, under uniform pairwise correlation)} \]
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Under this special assumption, the recipe for equal risk turns out to be simple: give the calm, steady assets bigger piles of money and the wild, jumpy ones smaller piles, in exact inverse proportion to how jumpy each one is. It's the seesaw idea again — put more weight closer to the middle to keep things balanced.

Worked example

Verifying the reduction with real numbers

Three assets: bonds (\(\sigma_1=5\%\)), equities (\(\sigma_2=15\%\)), commodities (\(\sigma_3=25\%\)), every pair sharing correlation \(\rho=0.30\). Inverse-volatility weights: \(1/0.05=20\), \(1/0.15=6.667\), \(1/0.25=4\), summing to \(30.667\), so \(w = (0.6522,\ 0.2174,\ 0.1304)\).

Building the 3×3 covariance matrix from \(\sigma_1,\sigma_2,\sigma_3,\rho\) and computing \((\Sigma w)_i\) directly for each asset gives \((\Sigma w) = (0.002609,\ 0.007826,\ 0.013043)\). Multiplying each entry by its own weight: \(w_i(\Sigma w)_i = (0.001701,\ 0.001701,\ 0.001701)\) — identical to four decimal places for all three assets, exactly as Step 4's derivation predicts. Summing those three equal terms and taking the square root gives portfolio variance \(0.005104\), so \(\sigma_p = 7.144\%\), and each asset's risk contribution is \(RC_i = 0.001701/0.07144 = 2.381\%\) — one-third of total portfolio risk each, on the nose.

Contrast that with equal-dollar weighting, \(w=(1/3,1/3,1/3)\), on the exact same three assets: portfolio volatility comes out higher, at \(\sigma_p = 11.64\%\), and the risk splits unevenly — bonds contribute only about 7% of total risk, equities about 30%, and commodities alone carries roughly 64% of the portfolio's total risk — despite being an equal third of the capital in both portfolios. Same three assets, same correlation assumption, two very different pictures of where the actual risk sits.

🎯
Real-world trading scenario

An FX book with one low-vol pair and one high-vol pair

Suppose a trader wants exposure to both EUR/CHF (a historically low-volatility, tightly managed pair) and a higher-beta pair like AUD/JPY (routinely several times more volatile) in the same book, with no particular directional view on either — the goal is just balanced exposure to both markets' dynamics. Splitting capital 50/50 between them is the equal-dollar instinct, and it produces a book where nearly all of the day-to-day P&L swings come from the AUD/JPY leg; the EUR/CHF leg is, in risk terms, close to a rounding error, no matter how much capital sits in it.

An equal-risk-contribution sizing instead holds a much larger notional in EUR/CHF than in AUD/JPY — inversely proportional to their relative volatilities, under the equal-correlation simplification — so that a typical day's move in either pair contributes a genuinely comparable amount to the book's total swing. This is a sizing discipline, not a forecast: it says nothing about whether AUD/JPY or EUR/CHF is more likely to be profitable, only that the book shouldn't be a leveraged bet on whichever pair happens to be loudest.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

Risk parity is a real, widely used institutional allocation style — most visibly in "balanced risk" and "all-weather" style multi-asset funds that split risk (not capital) roughly evenly across equities, bonds, and commodities, on the reasoning that no one asset class's economic regime should be allowed to dominate the portfolio's outcome. Because lower-volatility asset classes (government bonds, in particular) end up needing a much larger dollar allocation to match higher-vol assets' risk contribution, real-world risk-parity implementations frequently apply leverage to the low-vol sleeve to reach a target overall return — a design choice, not an accident, and one that concentrates real, well-documented tail risk if the low-vol asset's volatility or correlation regime shifts abruptly (bonds behaving very differently in an inflation shock than in a growth scare is the textbook case).

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Because a calm asset like government bonds barely moves, a risk-parity fund has to put a huge pile of money into it just to match the risk of a much smaller pile in a wilder asset like commodities. To still earn a decent overall return with so much money parked in the calm stuff, funds often borrow extra money to buy even more of it — like putting a bigger engine in the slow, steady car so the whole trip still finishes on time. That's a real bet, though: if the calm car suddenly turns wild, the extra engine (leverage) makes the crash much worse.

The equal-correlation reduction derived above is rarely used as the literal production allocator — real cross-asset correlation matrices are not uniform — but it is exactly the intuition-building special case practitioners reach for first, and inverse-volatility weighting alone (ignoring correlation entirely) is itself a common, cheaper approximation to full ERC when the correlation structure is roughly diffuse. The general, correlation-aware ERC solve is run numerically — an iterative search for the weight vector that equalizes every \(RC_i\) — and is standard tooling in cross-asset risk systems at multi-asset managers.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where risk parity goes wrong in practice

Dropping expected returns is a real cost, not a free lunch. If one asset genuinely has a better risk-adjusted expected return than another, risk parity has no way to express that — it will still size by volatility alone. The appeal ("no fragile forecast needed") is real, but it comes with giving up any legitimate return information you actually had.

The uniform-correlation reduction is an idealization. Real asset correlations are never all equal, and the gap between "inverse-vol weighting" and "true numerically-solved ERC" grows exactly as the correlation structure becomes more uneven — assets that are more correlated with the rest of the basket should, in a true ERC solve, be sized down relative to what pure inverse-vol weighting would give them, because they're contributing risk the basket already has plenty of.

Volatility and correlation both drift. Risk parity still depends on a covariance matrix estimated from historical data — more stable than expected returns, as the Markowitz lesson establishes, but not fixed. A regime shift that changes an asset's volatility or, especially, its correlation to the rest of the book (bonds and equities decoupling from their usual negative correlation is the most cited real-world case) can leave a risk-parity book carrying materially different risk than intended until the estimate catches up.

Leverage on the low-vol sleeve is a real tail-risk source. Because low-volatility assets need outsized notional to match high-volatility assets' risk contribution, hitting a competitive overall return often requires leveraging that low-vol sleeve — a design choice that magnifies losses if that sleeve's own risk character changes (its volatility spiking, or its correlation to the rest of the book flipping sign) at the same time market stress hits everything else.

Section 04

Key Takeaways

✓Risk contribution is an exact accounting split. \(RC_i = w_i(\Sigma w)_i/\sigma_p\) sums exactly to \(\sigma_p\) across all assets — not an approximation, a homogeneous-quadratic-form identity.
✓ERC solves for \(RC_i = RC_j\) for every pair. Equal dollars is not equal risk — the whole point is sizing by risk contribution instead of capital.
✓Under uniform pairwise correlation, ERC reduces exactly to inverse-volatility weighting, \(w_i \propto 1/\sigma_i\) — derived, not asserted, above.
⚠The general case needs numerical solving. Real correlation matrices are never uniform, so production ERC allocators solve an iterative, nonlinear system — there is no closed-form shortcut once correlations differ pair to pair.
⚠Dropping expected returns is a real cost. Risk parity has no mechanism for expressing a genuine view that one asset's expected return is better than another's — it sizes by risk alone, full stop.
✗Not built or validated in this repo. Every strategy here is single-instrument; this lesson is the theory behind a multi-instrument allocator this repo has not built, tested, or run out-of-sample.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. Why is "equal dollar weighting" different from "equal risk contribution," and why does the difference matter?
Equal dollar weighting allocates the same capital to each asset regardless of how volatile that asset is; equal risk contribution allocates so each asset's slice of total portfolio risk is the same, which requires holding more capital in low-volatility assets and less in high-volatility ones. The difference matters because a portfolio's realized swings are driven by risk, not capital — an equal-dollar portfolio can be, in risk terms, almost entirely a bet on its single most volatile holding, which defeats the diversification the equal split was meant to provide.
Q2. Why do the individual risk contributions \(RC_i\) sum exactly to the portfolio's total volatility \(\sigma_p\), rather than just approximately?
Because portfolio variance \(w^\top \Sigma w\) is a homogeneous quadratic function of the weights, and by Euler's theorem for homogeneous functions, a function's value can always be written as the sum of each variable times its own partial derivative, scaled appropriately. Applying that identity to \(\sigma_p\) rather than \(\sigma_p^2\) gives \(\sum_i w_i (\Sigma w)_i / \sigma_p = \sigma_p\) exactly, for any weight vector — this is a mathematical identity, not an empirical regularity, so it holds whether or not the portfolio is at the ERC solution.
Q3. In the equal-pairwise-correlation derivation, why does substituting \(w_i = k/\sigma_i\) make \(RC_i\) come out identical across every asset?
Because \(w_i \sigma_i\) equals the constant \(k\) for every asset under that candidate, both of the two terms that make up \((\Sigma w)_i\) — one proportional to \(\sigma_i w_i\), the other proportional to \(\sigma_i^2 w_i = \sigma_i(\sigma_i w_i) = \sigma_i k\) — end up depending only on \(\sigma_i\) through an overall multiplicative factor. When \(RC_i = w_i(\Sigma w)_i/\sigma_p\) is expanded fully with \(w_i = k/\sigma_i\), that single remaining \(\sigma_i\) factor cancels against the \(1/\sigma_i\) in \(w_i\), leaving an expression built entirely out of the shared constants \(k\), \(\rho\), \(S\), and \(\sigma_p\) — none of which vary by asset. That's why the reduction is exact, not approximate, whenever pairwise correlation is genuinely uniform.
Q4. Why does a real-world risk-parity fund often apply leverage to its bond (low-volatility) sleeve?
Because matching a low-volatility asset's risk contribution to a high-volatility asset's requires holding a much larger dollar notional in the low-vol asset. On an unlevered basis, a bond-heavy risk-parity portfolio built this way tends to have low overall expected return (most of the capital sits in the lowest-returning, lowest-vol sleeve); leveraging that sleeve is a deliberate choice to bring the whole portfolio's expected return back up to a competitive level, at the cost of amplifying losses if the low-vol asset's risk character changes unexpectedly.
Q5. What real information does risk parity give up, compared to a mean-variance optimizer with accurate expected-return inputs?
It gives up any ability to size a position based on that asset having a genuinely better (or worse) expected risk-adjusted return than another. If two assets have identical volatility and correlation profiles but very different true expected returns, risk parity treats them identically, while a mean-variance optimizer with accurate inputs would correctly overweight the higher-expected-return one. Risk parity's appeal is precisely that real expected-return forecasts are usually not accurate enough to trust — but if a genuinely reliable return edge exists, risk parity has no mechanism to exploit it.
Q6. Why can't the uniform-correlation closed-form solution simply be used as the production ERC allocator for a real multi-asset basket?
Because real correlation matrices are never uniform across every pair — some assets are much more correlated with the rest of the basket than others, and a true ERC solve needs to size those more-correlated assets down relative to what inverse-volatility weighting alone would give them, since they're contributing risk the basket already has a lot of from elsewhere. Once correlations differ pair to pair, the neat cancellation that made the closed-form derivation work no longer applies, and the equalizing weights have to be found by numerical iteration instead.
