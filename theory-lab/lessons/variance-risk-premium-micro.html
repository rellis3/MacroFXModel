Lesson 5 · Options in Practice

The Variance Risk Premium, or Why Selling Options Pays Until It Doesn't

Options have, on average, been priced for more movement than then arrived. That gap pays the people who sell insurance, and it is the reason selling options looks so good right up to the month it does not. Educational only, not advice: options can lose the whole premium, and a short option can lose many times it.

The whole idea
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

I keep hearing that selling options is free money, because implied volatility is almost always higher than what the market then does. Is that true?

Half true, which is the dangerous kind. Start with the true half. Implied volatility is an option's price expressed as a speed: how much movement the market is charging for. Realised volatility is how much it then moved. Over thirty-five years of one-month windows on the S&P 500, the market charged for about nineteen, and the index then moved at about fifteen.

So it charged for nineteen and delivered fifteen.

On average, yes. Call it four points of volatility. And in about eighty-four months out of every hundred, the seller of that insurance came out ahead.

Eighty-four percent. That does sound like free money.

Look at the other sixteen. They are not the mirror image of the good months. In the window that began in March twenty-twenty, the market charged for about fifty-four and then moved at about eighty-nine.

So the average hides a tail.

A short option can only ever earn the premium, but it can pay out many times that. In variance terms, the five worst windows out of four hundred and thirty-one made up more than half of all the losses.

Why would the market overcharge at all? Surely somebody arbitrages it away.

It is not a mispricing you can lock in. It is insurance pricing. Investors buy puts to protect portfolios. The dealers who sell them cannot hedge perfectly, because prices jump, so they charge for that. And volatility spikes exactly when stocks fall, so the seller loses when everyone else is already hurting. Being paid for that is like being paid to sell hurricane cover.

Does the research back it up?

Yes. Carr and Wu, in two thousand and nine, measured it using variance swaps. Coval and Shumway, in two thousand and one, found that buyers of at-the-money straddles lost about three percent a week on average. Bondarenko found that S&P five hundred puts looked too expensive to fit standard models.

Give me one number I can hold on to.

Sell a thirty-day straddle priced at twenty percent volatility, and say the market then moves at fifteen. You collect about four point six percent of the price. On average you pay back about three point four. You keep about one point one percent.

One percent, with the risk of losing fifteen in a bad month.

Exactly. In a simulation with a small chance of a crash each month, the average month is positive, and even so the typical worst drawdown over ten years is about seventeen percent. Triple the size, and more than four paths in ten fall by half at some point.

And the one-line version?

The premium is real on average, and it is the price of a tail you will eventually meet. Size for the worst month, not the average one.

Section 1

An option seller is an insurer

Implied volatility is the speed of movement an option is priced for. Realised volatility is the speed the market then moved. The average gap between them is the premium.

🏠

The insurer's business

A home insurer charges more than it expects to pay out, on average. That is not a trick: buyers pay for protection and the insurer is paid for standing ready.

📈

The option version

Index options have, on average, cost more than the movement that then arrived. The VIX averaged 19.3 in our count, and the next 21 days' realised volatility 15.4.

🌀

The hurricane year

An insurer with thirty good years looks wonderful until a storm takes a decade of profit in a week. The average was real and it was the price of the storm.

✅

What the seller earns

Gains are capped at the premium. Losses are not. That asymmetry is what the rest of the lesson is about.

"It makes money in 85% of months" and "it makes money on average" are both true, and neither says what it does to your account in the other 15%.
Section 2

Two volatilities and the gap between them

One is a price. One is what happened. The difference is what a variance seller is paid.

Follow it through
σimp: the volatility that reproduces the option's price in Black-Scholes
It is a price, quoted as a speed. A variance swap's strike, Kvar, is the same thing as a variance, and the VIX is the 30-day version for S&P 500 options.
σreal: the annualised standard deviation of the daily returns that actually happened
It is an outcome, known only afterwards. Variance is its square, so big moves count quadratically.
VRP ≈ σ²imp − σ²real
Implied minus realised. 20 against 15 is 5 volatility points but 20² − 15² = 175 variance points. Positive means the seller was paid. (Carr and Wu quote it the other way round.)

The seller's average profit on a one-month straddle is about 0.8 × √T × (σimp − σreal) of spot. The calculator on the next slide computes it exactly.

Section 5

Why the premium exists

It is insurance pricing, not a loophole. Sellers are paid for carrying something unpleasant.

🛡

Demand for protection

Funds buy index puts to cap downside and are price-insensitive. One-sided demand lifts prices (Garleanu, Pedersen and Poteshman, 2009).

🏦

Dealer risk

Dealers hedge by trading the underlying. The hedge slips when prices jump, and hedging ties up capital. They charge for both.

📉

It pays in bad times

Volatility tends to rise when stocks fall, so the seller loses when wealth is already low. That is what insurance is.

⚡

Jumps

No hedge works against a gap. The premium includes a price for that risk.

Where this needs qualifying

The premium is not a law of nature. Carr and Wu report much smaller premia for individual stocks than for indices, and FX variance premia are generally smaller and less consistent than the equity-index one.

Section 7 · It actually happened

February to March 2020: 15% priced, over 80% delivered

It actually happened

On 19 February 2020 the S&P 500 closed at a record 3,386.15 and the VIX at 14.38. By 23 March the index closed at 2,237.40, 33.9% lower. The VIX closed at 82.69 on 16 March, the highest close in Cboe's daily history (from 1990), the day the index fell 12.0%. Realised volatility over the 23 trading days from 20 February was about 84% a year. A seller of a 30-day straddle at the VIX of 15.04 on 10 February collected about 3.4% of the index level, and the index fell 18.2% by 11 March: a loss of roughly 15% (treating the VIX as an at-the-money volatility, which it is not exactly).

Section 8

What this does not say

🚫

Not free money

The average pays for a tail. After the bid-ask spread, commissions and the cost of margin, a 1%-a-month edge is thin.

📊

Not a timing signal

The gap is bigger after spikes, and so is the risk: a window that began at a VIX of 53.9 on 11 March 2020 realised 89%.

🌍

Not the same everywhere

An equity-index premium measured over 35 years says little about an FX, rates or single-stock premium. Measure it for what you trade.

🧱

One history

One market, 35 years, a few crises. The average depends on whether disasters of that size are in the sample.

Where the textbook version breaks

The premium assumes you are still there to collect it. Margin requirements rise when volatility rises, so the call for more money comes at the worst time. The average accrues only to a seller with enough capital and discipline to be there for all of it.

Section 9

Quick check

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Implied 20, realised 15, 30 days, zero rates. About what does a straddle seller make on average, as a share of spot?

Premium 4.57% minus average payout 3.43% is 1.14%. The quick formula 0.8 × 0.287 × 5 points gives the same.
Question 2

A short straddle wins in 85% of months. Why can it still ruin an account?

The hit rate says how often you win, not how much you lose when you do not. Large positions can also be closed by margin calls before the average arrives.
Question 3

Why does the premium exist?

It is compensation for bearing crash risk, not a loophole. Realised volatility is not always lower: in 67 of 431 windows it was higher.
Question 4

The VIX is at 54, far above its average. Is that a safe moment to sell options?

High implied volatility has gone both ways: the 2008 spike was followed by the largest positive gap in our sample, and March 2020 by one of the worst losses.
Question 5

In the simulation, the size is raised from 1× to 3× with the same edge. What happens to the drawdowns?

The premium scales with size, and so does the loss in a crash month. The same edge at 3× turns a tolerable ride into one that regularly halves the account.
Your call
Scenario drill · 3 November 2017

The quietest market on record, and a perfect year

You have sold a one-month at-the-money S&P 500 straddle every month for two years, sized to your comfort, and every month has made money. Today the VIX closes at 9.14, the lowest close in Cboe's daily history, and the index has realised only about 5% volatility over the last month. A 30-day straddle at 9.14% volatility pays about 2.1% of the index level. What do you do with your size?

  1. Add size. The gap is still positive and the strategy has not lost in two years.
    The premium is thin: about 2.1% of the index for a month, against a tail that is as large as ever. Two perfect years are a short sample for a strategy whose losses come from rare events. Adding size here buys the least pay per unit of tail risk you will ever be offered.
  2. Go the other way. Volatility cannot go lower, so buy straddles instead.
    Low can stay low, and the same research says buyers lose on average, month after month, to the premium they pay. "Cheap" is not the same as "about to rise". You would be paying to be early.
  3. Keep size set by the worst month you could survive, not by the calm of the last two years, and consider capping the tail with bought options.
    The premium on offer is at its lowest, so the case for taking more risk is at its weakest. Sizing from the worst case and paying for protection costs some of the thin premium and keeps you in the game for the month that matters.
  4. Carry on unchanged. The long-run average says the premium is positive.
    Defensible, and it is what a rule-based seller would do. But it ignores that sizes chosen in a calm market tend to be too large for a volatile one, and that a thin premium is thin pay for the same tail.

What happened. On 2 February 2018 the VIX closed at 17.31. On Monday 5 February it closed at 37.32, up 20.01 points or 115.6% in a day, and the S&P 500 fell 4.1%. Realised volatility over the 21 trading days from 5 February was about 25%, against about 7% across 2017. A single episode cannot prove a rule; it shows the shape. Calm months paid small amounts, then a few days returned many months of them. Later in this series we look at how short-volatility products and books failed that week.

Section 10

What to carry forward

01

The variance risk premium is implied variance minus the variance that then arrives, on average: positive for S&P 500 options. VIX averaged 19.3 and realised 15.4 in our count.

02

The seller's average profit on a one-month straddle is about 0.8 × √T × (σimp − σreal) of spot: 1.14% at 20 against 15 over 30 days, before costs.

03

Research from Carr and Wu, Coval and Shumway, Bondarenko, and Bakshi and Kapadia finds sellers paid on average in index options.

04

It is insurance pricing: protection demand, dealer hedging risk, and variance that rises when stocks fall.

05

The average hides a left tail. Gains are capped at the premium; the five worst windows made up 57% of all losses.

06

It is not free money and not a timing signal. 19 February to 23 March 2020: 15% priced, more than 80% delivered.

What this now lets you do

Read a claim like "implied is usually above realised, so sell it" for what it actually says: a documented average, with a price attached. Ask how big the left tail is, what the position does to your account in the worst month, and whether the gap survives costs in the market you trade.
