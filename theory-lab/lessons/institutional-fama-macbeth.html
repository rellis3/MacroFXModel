Fama-MacBeth Two-Stage Regression

Fama-French gives you a factor loading — how exposed an asset is to a risk factor. It never answers a genuinely separate question: what does the market actually pay, in return per unit of exposure, for bearing that risk — and how do you get an honest standard error when every asset's return in a given month is buffeted by the same shared shock? Fama-MacBeth (1973) answers both, one period at a time.

CONCEPT — a two-stage panel risk-premium estimator; a repo-wide search confirms no Fama-MacBeth (or equivalent per-period cross-sectional regression) implementation exists anywhere in this codebase ~18 min read Prereqs: Fama-French & Carhart, Correlation & Linear Regression
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • A factor-loading regression (Fama-French, Carhart) tells you how exposed an asset is to a factor (\(\beta_i\)); it says nothing about what the market pays per unit of that exposure. Fama-MacBeth (1973) estimates that price — the factor's risk premium \(\lambda\) — with a standard error that is actually trustworthy.
  • The procedure runs OLS twice, on two different axes: Stage 1 is one time-series regression per asset (across periods) to get each asset's fixed \(\hat\beta_i\); Stage 2 is one cross-sectional regression per period (across assets) using those fixed betas as the regressor, producing one \(\hat\lambda_t\) per period.
  • The risk premium estimate is the plain average of the \(T\) period-by-period \(\hat\lambda_t\)'s; its standard error comes from the time-series standard deviation of the \(\hat\lambda_t\) series itself, divided by \(\sqrt{T}\) — never from a single pooled regression's residuals.
  • This sidesteps the single biggest trap in panel asset pricing: pooling every asset-period observation into one giant OLS regression understates standard errors badly, because returns across different assets in the same period are correlated — they share common-period shocks. Fama-MacBeth's per-period estimates reduce each period to one number, so that correlation stops mattering.
  • Stage-1 betas are themselves noisy estimates, not the true population values — using an estimated regressor in Stage 2 biases \(\hat\lambda\) toward zero (classical errors-in-variables attenuation). Shanken (1992) derived the standard correction, which inflates the reported standard errors to account for that extra layer of uncertainty.
  • How it reached trading: Fama & MacBeth's 1973 month-by-month regressions became the standard way to test whether a factor or signal is priced, and quant equity desks use the same routine to vet new signals.
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

I've got betas for every currency portfolio from the factor regression. Isn't that enough?

A beta tells you how exposed a portfolio is. It doesn't tell you whether the market pays you for that exposure. That's a separate question, and Fama and MacBeth answered it in 1973.

So how do you measure what the market pays?

In two stages. First you measure each portfolio's exposure once, using its whole history. Then you freeze those numbers.

And the second stage?

Take one month. Across all the portfolios, did more exposure line up with a better return that month? The slope of that one regression is the month's price of risk. Then you repeat it for every month.

So I end up with one price per month.

Exactly. The premium is just their average. And the honest error bar comes from how much those monthly prices bounce around.

Why not throw every portfolio and every month into one big regression? More data points.

That's the trap. In a bad month nearly everything falls together, because one shared shock hits every asset at once. A hundred portfolios in one month are not a hundred independent facts. They're closer to one story, told a hundred times.

And the big regression counts it a hundred times.

Right, so its error bar comes out far too small. You think you've found a premium when you've found noise. Fama-MacBeth squeezes each month down to one number first, so the echo can't fool you.

Give me numbers.

Three carry portfolios with betas of point seven, one, and one point three. Four months. The monthly prices come out at about four point two, minus two and a half, a half, and three point eight. The average is one and a half percent per unit of exposure.

That sounds like a decent premium.

It looks it. But those four numbers swing wildly, so the standard error is about one point six. One and a half divided by that gives a t-statistic just under one. You can't tell it from zero.

Anything else that flatters the result?

Yes. The first-stage betas are estimates, not facts. Using noisy betas drags the slope toward zero and leaves the plain error bars too tight. Jay Shanken's 1992 correction widens them. And if the monthly prices drift in runs, you widen them again.

So if it survives all that, I've got an edge?

Not yet. You've got an honest estimate on one stretch of history. Whether the factor was mined from that same data, and whether it survives costs and new data, are separate questions.

Give me the one line.

Measure exposure once, price it every month, and judge the average by how much the monthly prices disagree, never by one pooled regression.

Section 01

Why this matters

Fama-French & Carhart hands you a genuinely useful number for every asset: its factor betas — how much a stock, or an FX carry basket, moves per unit move in the market factor, the size factor, value, momentum. That regression answers "how exposed is this asset to this risk." It does not, and structurally cannot, answer a second and completely separate question that any investor actually holding the factor exposure needs answered: is the market compensating anyone for bearing that risk, and how much? A factor with a large, positive, statistically reliable average payoff per unit of exposure is one worth being exposed to; a factor whose exposure earns nothing on average, or earns a payoff indistinguishable from zero once measured honestly, is not — no matter how cleanly its betas were estimated in the first stage.

That "measured honestly" clause is where this lesson actually lives. Getting a point estimate of a risk premium is not hard — regress returns on betas and read off the slope. Getting a standard error you can trust for that estimate is the hard part, because asset returns within the same month, week, or day are not independent draws: a bad month for equities is a bad month for nearly every stock at once; a dollar-strength shock moves nearly every G10 cross the same direction on the same day. Every asset-period observation quietly shares information with every other asset-period observation from the same period, and any inference procedure that doesn't account for that will report standard errors far smaller than the truth. Fama and MacBeth's 1973 paper — "Risk, Return, and Equilibrium: Empirical Tests" — is built around a specific, elegant answer to exactly this problem, and it remains the standard textbook procedure for estimating a factor's price of risk fifty years later.

💡
Plain-English intuition

An assembly line: measure exposure once, then price it fresh every single period

Picture a research desk holding a basket of currency portfolios, each with a known, already-measured sensitivity to a carry factor — some portfolios are heavily exposed, some barely at all, established once from years of history. Now for a single specific month, take that month's actual returns across every portfolio in the basket and ask one question: this month, did more carry exposure actually correspond to a better or worse return, and by how much per unit of exposure? That "how much per unit of exposure, this month" is a single cross-sectional regression — one number, \(\hat\lambda_t\), telling you the price the market charged (or paid) for carry risk during that specific month.

Now repeat that same one-month exercise separately for every month in the sample — the exposures never change (they were measured once, up front, from the full history), but the price of bearing them bounces around from month to month, because different months bring different shocks. Some months carry pays off handsomely; others it gets punished. The final risk-premium estimate is simply the average of all those monthly prices, and — this is the entire trick — the standard error comes from how much those monthly prices themselves bounce around, not from pretending every asset in every month was an independent coin flip. If the monthly prices bounce around a lot, the average is uncertain and the standard error says so honestly. If a single giant regression pooled every asset and every month together instead, it would have no idea the ups and downs it's staring at are really just a much smaller number of monthly stories repeated across many highly correlated assets — and it would report a standard error far too small to be believed.

How it reached trading

Does the market actually pay for this signal?

Cross-sectional regressions · Fama & MacBeth, 1973 → standard in quant equity research

Knowing a stock's beta to a factor says how exposed it is, not whether that exposure is rewarded. Pooling every stock-month into one regression to find out gives standard errors that are far too small, because stocks in the same month share the same shock, so researchers kept finding "significant" premia that were not there.

Fama & MacBeth's 1973 test of CAPM ran one cross-sectional regression per month and judged the premium by how much the monthly estimates varied over time. It became the default method in empirical asset pricing, and Petersen (2009) later surveyed how often finance papers' standard errors ignored this kind of correlation.

On quant equity desks the same monthly cross-sectional regression is how a candidate signal is tested: regress next month's returns on today's characteristic, average the slope, and use its time-series t-statistic. Commercial risk models estimate their factor returns the same way.

Section 02

The two-stage procedure, precisely

Watch the formula build
From fixed betas to the t-statistic on the premium, one step at a time
\[ \color{#fbbf24}{\hat\beta_i = \frac{\mathrm{Cov}_t(R_i, F)}{\mathrm{Var}_t(F)}} \]

Stage 1. For one asset, run over time: how much does it move per unit move in the factor? Do it once per asset, then freeze the answer.

\[ \hat\lambda_t = \frac{\mathrm{Cov}_i(\color{#fbbf24}{\hat\beta}, R_t)}{\mathrm{Var}_i(\color{#fbbf24}{\hat\beta})} \]

Stage 2. The frozen betas become the regressor. Within one period, across assets: how much extra return did each unit of exposure earn? One \(\hat\lambda_t\) per period.

\[ \bar\lambda = \color{#fbbf24}{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}} \hat\lambda_t \]

Average the period prices. In the worked example, four months give \(\bar\lambda = 1.50\).

\[ SE(\bar\lambda) = \frac{\color{#fbbf24}{s_\lambda}}{\color{#fbbf24}{\sqrt{T}}} \]

The error bar comes from how much the period prices disagree with each other over time, not from a pooled regression. Here \(3.1388/\sqrt{4} \approx 1.5694\).

\[ \color{#10b981}{t = \frac{\bar\lambda}{s_\lambda/\sqrt{T}}} = \frac{1.50}{1.5694} \approx 0.96 \]

The test statistic. Below about 2, the average premium cannot be told apart from zero, however large it looks.

Both stages are ordinary least squares — the exact machinery derived in Correlation & Linear Regression, \(\hat\beta = \mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)/\mathrm{Var}(X)\). What changes between the two stages is not the formula — it's which axis of the data plays the role of "the sample." Stage 1 treats time as the sample, holding the asset fixed. Stage 2 treats assets as the sample, holding the period fixed. Keeping that one distinction straight is the whole procedure.

Stage 1 — time-series regression, once per asset, using the full sample. For each asset \(i\) separately, regress that one asset's returns on the factor across every period \(t=1,\dots,T\) in the sample:

\[ R_{i,t} = a_i + \beta_i F_t + \varepsilon_{i,t}, \qquad t = 1,\dots,T \]
R_{i,t}asset i's realized return in period t
F_tthe factor's realized return in period t (e.g. a carry-factor return)
β_iasset i's exposure to the factor — estimated once, fixed forever after
ε_{i,t}whatever this period's return isn't explained by the factor

This is precisely the single-factor version of the regression the Fama-French & Carhart lesson runs (there with several factors at once; here, one, to keep the arithmetic below trackable by hand — nothing about the logic changes with more factors). Using exactly the OLS slope formula from the regression primer, but now with time \(t\) running over the sample for one fixed asset \(i\):

\[ \hat\beta_i = \frac{\mathrm{Cov}_t(R_i, F)}{\mathrm{Var}_t(F)} \]

Do this once, separately, for every asset in the universe — \(N\) completely independent time-series regressions, each run over the same \(T\) periods. The output is a fixed number per asset, \(\hat\beta_1, \hat\beta_2, \dots, \hat\beta_N\), that never changes again for the rest of the procedure. Stage 1 is finished the moment these are estimated; Correlation & Linear Regression and the Fama-French & Carhart lesson already cover every detail of how a regression like this is actually fit, so it is not re-derived here — this lesson takes stage-1 betas as a given input and starts precisely where those lessons leave off.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Stage 1 is like measuring how sensitive each kid's mood is to rainy days, using a whole year of data per kid. You do this once per kid, using everything you know about them, and then you write that sensitivity number down and stop touching it — it's now a fixed fact about that kid for the rest of the exercise.

Stage 2 — cross-sectional regression, repeated separately for every single period. Now flip axes entirely. Pick one period \(t\), hold it fixed, and look across all \(N\) assets at once. Regress that period's realized returns on the (already-known, already-fixed) Stage-1 betas:

\[ R_{i,t} = \lambda_{0,t} + \lambda_t\,\hat\beta_i + \eta_{i,t}, \qquad i = 1,\dots,N \]
R_{i,t}this period's actual return, across every asset — the dependent variable this time
β̂_ithe fixed Stage-1 exposures — now playing the role of the regressor, X
λ_tthis period's estimated price of factor risk — the slope this regression fits
λ_{0,t}this period's zero-beta return — what a hypothetical asset with no factor exposure earned

Notice exactly what swapped: in Stage 1, time indexed the sample and the asset was fixed; here, assets index the sample (\(i=1,\dots,N\)) and the period \(t\) is fixed. It is the identical OLS slope formula, only now the covariance and variance are computed across assets, within one period, rather than across periods for one asset:

\[ \hat\lambda_t = \frac{\mathrm{Cov}_i(\hat\beta, R_t)}{\mathrm{Var}_i(\hat\beta)} \]
Cov_i(β̂, R_t)
Cross-sectional co-movement
Across assets, this period only: did higher exposure line up with a higher return?
Var_i(β̂)
Spread of exposures
How much the betas themselves vary across the asset universe — fixed, doesn't change period to period
λ̂_t
This period's price of risk
Return earned per unit of factor exposure, in this one period alone

Run this cross-sectional regression separately, once per period — \(T\) completely independent regressions, one for every date in the sample, each one producing its own \(\hat\lambda_t\). Unlike Stage 1 (where the betas are fixed inputs, estimated once and reused), Stage 2 genuinely reruns from scratch every single period, because the actual realized returns \(R_{i,t}\) — the thing being explained — are different every period even though the regressor \(\hat\beta_i\) never changes.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Stage 2 asks the same question fresh every single day: today, across all the kids, did the ones with a higher measured rain-sensitivity actually end up moodier than the ones with lower sensitivity — and by how much per point of sensitivity? You get one answer for today, a different answer for tomorrow, a different one the day after, because the weather and the mood are different every day even though each kid's own sensitivity number never moves.

Final step — average across time, and get the standard error from the time series of estimates itself. The Stage-2 procedure hands back \(T\) separate estimates, \(\hat\lambda_1, \hat\lambda_2,\dots,\hat\lambda_T\) — one price of risk per period. The Fama-MacBeth risk-premium estimate is nothing more exotic than their plain average:

\[ \bar\lambda = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \hat\lambda_t \]

The standard error is where the entire procedure earns its keep. It is computed from how much the \(\hat\lambda_t\)'s themselves vary across time — exactly the ordinary standard-error-of-a-sample-mean formula, treating the \(T\) period-level estimates as \(T\) observations of a single random variable:

\[ s_\lambda = \sqrt{\frac{1}{T-1}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\big(\hat\lambda_t-\bar\lambda\big)^2} \qquad SE(\bar\lambda) = \frac{s_\lambda}{\sqrt{T}} \]
s_λthe sample standard deviation of the T period-level λ̂_t estimates
SE(λ̄)standard error of the average — shrinks as more periods are added, exactly like any sample mean

A t-statistic for testing whether the average risk premium is distinguishable from zero follows immediately, \(t = \bar\lambda / SE(\bar\lambda)\). Nothing here touches the within-period cross-sectional correlation between assets at all — and that omission is deliberate, and is the entire point of the next section.

\(R_{i,t}\)Asset \(i\)'s realized return in period \(t\).
\(F_t\)The factor's realized return in period \(t\) (a single factor here; the same logic extends to a vector of factors, as in Fama-French/Carhart).
\(\hat\beta_i\)Asset \(i\)'s Stage-1 factor exposure — estimated once from the full time series, then held fixed for every Stage-2 regression.
\(\hat\lambda_t\)The Stage-2 estimated price of factor risk for period \(t\) alone — one per period.
\(\hat\lambda_{0,t}\)Period \(t\)'s estimated zero-beta return (Stage-2 intercept).
\(N\)Number of assets in the cross-section (the sample size for every Stage-2 regression).
\(T\)Number of time periods (the sample size for every Stage-1 regression, and for the final time-series average).
\(\bar\lambda\)The Fama-MacBeth risk-premium estimate — the plain average of the T period-level \(\hat\lambda_t\)'s.
\(s_\lambda\)Sample standard deviation of the \(\hat\lambda_t\) time series.

Section 03

Why pooling is wrong, and this is right

The single most important idea in this lesson is not the two-stage mechanics above — it's why those mechanics were designed this way instead of the far more obvious alternative: stack every asset's return in every period into one giant panel of \(N\times T\) observations and run one pooled OLS regression of \(R_{i,t}\) on \(\hat\beta_i\) directly. That regression is trivial to run, and it produces a point estimate of the risk premium that is not even unreasonable. Its standard error, however, is badly, systematically wrong — and it is wrong in a predictable direction: too small, making a risk premium look far more statistically reliable than it actually is.

The reason is cross-sectional correlation. Ordinary least squares' standard-error formula assumes every observation's error term is independent of every other observation's error term. That assumption is reasonable across time for many financial series (today's surprise doesn't mechanically determine tomorrow's), but it is badly false within a single period, across assets: a common macro shock — a surprise rate decision, a risk-off day, a dollar-funding squeeze — hits nearly every asset in the cross-section at once, in the same direction. A pooled regression with \(N\times T\) rows is not actually looking at \(N\times T\) independent pieces of information; it is looking at something closer to \(T\) independent monthly "stories," each one repeated \(N\) times with only asset-specific noise added on top. Treating it as \(N\times T\) independent draws overstates the genuine sample size enormously, and understates the standard error to match.

Pooled panel OLS — wrong

Stack all \(N\times T\) asset-period observations and run one OLS regression of \(R_{i,t}\) on \(\hat\beta_i\). The standard-error formula assumes every one of the \(N\times T\) error terms is independent. It has no mechanism to notice that, within any single period, the \(N\) error terms all lean the same direction because they share that period's common shock — the formula is simply blind to it, and reports a standard error far smaller than the truth.

Fama-MacBeth — right

Run \(T\) separate cross-sectional regressions, one per period, each collapsing that period's entire cross-section of information into a single number, \(\hat\lambda_t\). Whatever cross-sectional correlation existed among the \(N\) assets in period \(t\) is now irrelevant, because period \(t\) contributes exactly one data point — \(\hat\lambda_t\) — to the final average. Inference is then run honestly over \(T\) genuinely separate time periods, not over a fictitious \(N\times T\) independent sample.

The mechanism in one sentence: Fama-MacBeth doesn't eliminate cross-sectional correlation between assets — it makes it irrelevant, by reducing every period's entire cross-section down to a single summary number before that number ever reaches the final averaging step. A pooled regression never performs that reduction, so the correlation it never accounted for is still sitting inside its reported standard error, quietly making it too small.
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine asking 30 kids in one class and 30 kids in another class whether they liked a rainy field trip, and treating that as 60 independent opinions. But if all 30 kids in each class were standing together in the exact same rain at the exact same time, their answers aren't really 30 independent opinions — they're one shared experience, echoed 30 times. If you had asked 100 different classes on 100 different days instead, and only kept each class's overall verdict, you'd have 100 genuinely separate opinions to average — noisier day to day, but honestly counted. That's the whole difference: don't pretend correlated echoes are independent votes.

Section 04

The errors-in-variables problem, and the Shanken correction

There is a second, separate wrinkle worth being honest about, even though this lesson doesn't derive its fix in full. Stage 2 uses \(\hat\beta_i\) — the Stage-1 estimate of asset \(i\)'s exposure — as if it were the asset's true, exactly-known population beta. It isn't. \(\hat\beta_i\) was itself fit from a finite time series and carries its own sampling error. Using a noisy, estimated regressor in place of the true unobserved one is a textbook errors-in-variables problem, and it has a well-known consequence: it biases the Stage-2 slope \(\hat\lambda_t\) toward zero — attenuation bias — because some of the genuine relationship between true exposure and return gets absorbed into what looks like extra noise, simply because the regressor used isn't quite the right number.

Jay Shanken's 1992 paper, "On the Estimation of Beta-Pricing Models" (Review of Financial Studies), derived the standard correction for exactly this. Without reproducing the derivation here, its role in the procedure is simple to state honestly: the Shanken correction inflates the Fama-MacBeth standard errors upward, to account for the extra layer of estimation uncertainty coming from the fact that Stage 1's betas are themselves estimates rather than known constants. Plain Fama-MacBeth standard errors, uncorrected, are typically too optimistic for exactly this reason — a second, independent source of "the reported significance is better than the truth," distinct from (and on top of) the pooling mistake in Section 03.

Worked example

Three currency portfolios, four months, by hand

Three FX carry-sorted portfolios — A, B, C — with carry-factor betas already estimated in Stage 1, from the full available history (given here as inputs; how they'd be estimated is exactly the Stage-1 time-series regression covered above and derived in full in Correlation & Linear Regression):

\[ \hat\beta_A = 0.7 \qquad \hat\beta_B = 1.0 \qquad \hat\beta_C = 1.3 \]

Averaging the four monthly prices of risk.

\[ \bar\lambda = \frac{4.1667 - 2.5000 + 0.5000 + 3.8333}{4} = \frac{6.0000}{4} = 1.5000 \]

Deviations from \(\bar\lambda=1.5\): \([2.6667,\ -4.0000,\ -1.0000,\ 2.3333]\). Squared: \([7.1111,\ 16.0000,\ 1.0000,\ 5.4444]\), summing to \(29.5556\). Dividing by \(T-1=3\) gives \(9.8519\), and:

\[ s_\lambda = \sqrt{9.8519} \approx 3.1388 \qquad SE(\bar\lambda) = \frac{3.1388}{\sqrt{4}} \approx 1.5694 \]

\(t = \bar\lambda / SE(\bar\lambda) = 1.5/1.5694 \approx 0.96\) — nowhere near the conventional \(|t|>2\) threshold. With only four months of data the estimate of \(\bar\lambda\) is honestly, correctly reported as statistically indistinguishable from zero, even though the point estimate itself (1.50% per unit of carry-factor exposure) looks economically sizable. This is not a flaw in the arithmetic — it's exactly what an honest standard error is supposed to do with a tiny sample: refuse to oversell a noisy average as a confident discovery.

The Fama-MacBeth assembly line: measure exposure once, price it fresh every period, then average the prices
Stage 1 runs once, producing fixed exposures. Stage 2 reruns every period, producing one price of risk per period. The final average and its standard error come entirely from that short time series of period-level prices — matching the hand-worked arithmetic above exactly.

Real-world trading scenario

Testing whether a carry factor actually earns a premium across a universe of FX crosses

Suppose a research desk has already computed, for every currency pair in a G10-plus-liquid-EM universe, a Stage-1 carry-factor beta from several years of history — exactly the kind of exposure the Fama-French & Carhart lesson's machinery produces. The desk now wants to know something that beta alone cannot answer: has carry exposure actually been compensated, on average, with a standard error that survives honest scrutiny? Running Fama-MacBeth means computing one cross-sectional regression per month across every pair in the universe, collecting the resulting monthly \(\hat\lambda_t\) series, and only then asking whether its time-series average is reliably different from zero. A large point estimate with a small \(N\) (few pairs each month) or a short \(T\) (few years of history) can easily produce the kind of result seen in the worked example above — an economically large-looking average premium that is, honestly reported, statistically indistinguishable from noise. That gap between "looks big" and "is reliable" is precisely what this procedure exists to expose, not paper over.

A correctly-computed standard error is not the same claim as "this factor has real, tradable edge." Fama-MacBeth is honest machinery for estimating a risk premium and its uncertainty given a fixed set of assets, a fixed factor, and a fixed historical sample — that is a statement about the estimator. It says nothing about whether the factor definition itself was chosen after looking at the same data (a serious multiple-testing risk covered in Multiple Testing), or about whether a premium that held historically will persist out of sample. Nothing here should be read as evidence that any specific FX factor has, or will have, real edge — see The Research Pipeline for the discipline that separate question actually requires.
Section 05

How practitioners actually use this

📐

The standard textbook procedure for pricing a factor

Fama-MacBeth remains the default way academic and institutional research estimates a factor's risk premium and tests whether it is statistically distinguishable from zero — it appears, in some form, in nearly every asset-pricing paper that estimates a \(\lambda\) rather than just a \(\beta\).

🧭

Shanken-corrected standard errors as the reported default

Because plain Fama-MacBeth standard errors ignore Stage-1 estimation error, applied work commonly reports the Shanken-corrected version alongside (or instead of) the raw one, precisely to avoid overstating significance from a source this lesson names explicitly in Section 04.

🔁

A second layer of correction for autocorrelated λ̂_t's

The plain \(s_\lambda/\sqrt{T}\) formula itself still assumes the period-level \(\hat\lambda_t\) estimates are serially uncorrelated. When a factor's price of risk trends across adjacent periods (its own kind of autocorrelation, exactly the failure mode the regression primer's pitfalls section warns about), a Newey-West-style heteroskedasticity-and-autocorrelation-robust standard error is applied to the \(\hat\lambda_t\) series itself, on top of the Shanken correction.

📊

An implicit factor "report card"

A factor with a Fama-MacBeth \(\bar\lambda\) that survives both corrections and remains significant is treated as evidence the factor is priced; one that doesn't is evidence the corresponding beta, however cleanly estimated, isn't being compensated — a completely different and much harder bar to clear than merely having a well-estimated exposure.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where Fama-MacBeth quietly goes wrong

Reporting plain, uncorrected standard errors. The \(s_\lambda/\sqrt{T}\) formula in Section 02 is the honest fix for cross-sectional correlation — it is not, by itself, a fix for the errors-in-variables problem in Section 04. A result that looks significant using plain Fama-MacBeth standard errors can lose that significance entirely once Shanken's correction is applied; quoting the uncorrected number without saying so overstates confidence in exactly the direction this whole procedure was built to avoid.

Too few periods to trust the standard error at all. The worked example above used \(T=4\) purely so the arithmetic could be shown by hand — in practice, a standard deviation computed from only a handful of \(\hat\lambda_t\)'s is itself extremely noisy, and \(SE(\bar\lambda)=s_\lambda/\sqrt{T}\) inherits that noise. A "reliable" Fama-MacBeth estimate generally needs decades of monthly data (or an equivalently large number of independent periods), not a handful of quarters.

Ignoring autocorrelation in the λ̂_t series. The plain standard-error formula assumes the period-level estimates are themselves independent draws over time. If a factor's price of risk trends in stretches rather than bouncing around independently, that autocorrelation understates \(s_\lambda\) the same way autocorrelated residuals understate a plain OLS standard error, covered in Correlation & Linear Regression's own pitfalls section — the fix there is the same in spirit: a robust standard error, applied here to the \(\hat\lambda_t\) series rather than to a single regression's residuals.

Treating Stage-1 betas as forever-fixed truths. This lesson holds \(\hat\beta_i\) fixed across all \(T\) Stage-2 regressions to keep the procedure and the arithmetic tractable, matching Fama and MacBeth's original design — but real betas can drift over long samples. Applied work sometimes re-estimates betas on a rolling window rather than once over the full sample, which changes the bias-variance trade-off of the whole procedure without changing anything about how Stage 2 or the final averaging step works.

Scenario drill · an illustrative research meeting

A carry signal with a t-statistic of 4

A colleague tests a carry signal on 25 currency pairs over 120 months. She stacks all 3,000 pair-months into one regression of next month's return on the signal and reports a t-statistic of 4.0. You rerun it Fama-MacBeth style: one cross-sectional regression per month, then the average slope and its time-series standard error. That gives t = 1.4. The team wants to know whether to put capital behind the signal. What do you recommend?

  1. Go with the pooled result. Three thousand rows is a big sample, and t = 4 is far past 2.
    Those rows are not independent. In any month a dollar shock moves most of the 25 pairs together, so the pooled regression counts each month's story about 25 times and its standard error comes out far too small. The 120 monthly slopes are the honest sample.
  2. Report t = 1.4 as the headline, add the Shanken and autocorrelation corrections (which can only widen it), and treat the signal as unproven until a longer or fresh sample says otherwise.
    This is the procedure doing its job. The monthly slopes disagree too much for their average to be told apart from zero, and both corrections push the same way. Saying "not shown yet" costs nothing; sizing up a noise premium costs money.
  3. Add 25 more pairs to the universe and rerun, to get the t-statistic up.
    More pairs make each month's slope a little less noisy, so it can help at the margin. But the shared monthly shock does not average away across pairs that all react to it. The error bar is still driven by how many separate months you have, and widening the search after seeing the result edges toward data mining.
  4. Drop the few months where carry crashed, since they look like outliers, and rerun.
    Those months are exactly the risk the premium is meant to pay for. Deleting them shrinks the spread of the monthly slopes and inflates t by construction. That is choosing the answer, not measuring it.

What the method says. This meeting is illustrative, with made-up numbers, but the gap it shows is the one the lesson is about: a pooled regression treats correlated pair-months as independent votes and overstates significance, while Fama-MacBeth counts each month once. A t of 1.4 on 120 monthly slopes is not evidence of a priced signal, and the Shanken and serial-correlation corrections only make the case weaker.

Section 06

Key Takeaways

✓Stage 1 measures exposure once; Stage 2 prices it fresh every period. One time-series regression per asset gives fixed \(\hat\beta_i\)'s; one cross-sectional regression per period, using those fixed betas, gives one \(\hat\lambda_t\) per period.
✓The risk-premium estimate is the plain average of the \(\hat\lambda_t\)'s; the standard error comes from their time-series spread, \(s_\lambda/\sqrt{T}\) — never from a single pooled regression's residuals.
✗Pooling every asset-period observation into one OLS regression understates the standard error badly, because assets in the same period share common shocks — a false assumption of independence that Fama-MacBeth's per-period reduction sidesteps entirely.
⚠Stage-1 betas are noisy estimates, not known constants — using them as a Stage-2 regressor biases \(\hat\lambda_t\) toward zero (attenuation); Shanken (1992) inflates the standard errors to correct for it.
✓Both stages are the identical OLS slope formula, \(\hat\beta=\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)/\mathrm{Var}(X)\) — only the axis playing the role of "the sample" (time in Stage 1, assets in Stage 2) changes.
✗An honestly-computed, correctly-significant \(\bar\lambda\) still isn't proof of real trading edge. It's a statement about the estimator on a fixed historical sample — factor mining, out-of-sample survival, and costs are separate, unanswered questions.
ℹOn a desk it is the signal test. Regress next period's returns on today's characteristic, period by period, and judge the average slope by its time-series t-statistic: the routine Fama & MacBeth set in 1973 and the way commercial risk models estimate factor returns.
Section 07

Self-test

Q1. A Fama-French regression already gives every asset a factor beta. What genuinely new question does Fama-MacBeth answer that the beta alone does not?
A beta only measures exposure — how much an asset moves per unit move in the factor. It says nothing about whether the market actually compensates anyone for bearing that exposure, or how much. Fama-MacBeth estimates that price — the factor's risk premium \(\lambda\), in return earned per unit of exposure — together with a standard error that is actually trustworthy, which a single factor-loading regression was never designed to provide.
Q2. Describe precisely what happens in Stage 1 versus Stage 2 — which regression runs how many times, and along which axis of the data each time.
Stage 1 runs once per asset: for a fixed asset \(i\), regress its returns on the factor across every period \(t=1,\dots,T\) in the sample, producing one fixed \(\hat\beta_i\) per asset (\(N\) regressions total, one per asset, each over time). Stage 2 runs once per period: for a fixed period \(t\), regress that period's realized returns across every asset \(i=1,\dots,N\) on the (already-fixed) Stage-1 betas, producing one \(\hat\lambda_t\) per period (\(T\) regressions total, one per period, each across assets). The axis that plays "the sample" swaps completely between the two stages.
Q3. Why is pooling every asset-period observation into one giant panel regression the wrong way to estimate the risk premium's standard error?
Because OLS's standard-error formula assumes every observation's error is independent of every other observation's error, and that assumption is badly false across assets within the same period — returns on different assets in the same month share common-period shocks (a broad risk-off day, a rate surprise) and move together. A pooled regression with \(N\times T\) rows is not really looking at \(N\times T\) independent pieces of information; it's closer to \(T\) independent "stories" each echoed \(N\) times. Treating it as fully independent overstates the effective sample size and understates the standard error to match — usually badly.
Q4. How does Fama-MacBeth's period-by-period design make the cross-sectional correlation in Q3 irrelevant, without needing to model or estimate that correlation directly?
Because Stage 2 collapses each period's entire cross-section of correlated asset returns into a single number, \(\hat\lambda_t\), before that period ever contributes to the final average. Whatever correlation existed among the \(N\) assets within period \(t\) is baked into that one \(\hat\lambda_t\) but doesn't need to be modeled separately, because the final inference step only ever sees \(T\) period-level numbers — one contribution per period — never the raw \(N\times T\) correlated observations directly.
Q5. What is the errors-in-variables problem in Fama-MacBeth, and what does the Shanken (1992) correction actually do about it?
Stage 2 uses \(\hat\beta_i\) — a Stage-1 estimate, not the true, exactly-known population beta — as its regressor. A regressor measured with error, used in place of the true unobserved one, is a classical errors-in-variables setup, and it biases the Stage-2 slope toward zero (attenuation bias), because part of the genuine relationship gets absorbed as apparent noise. Shanken (1992) derived a standard correction for this that inflates the reported Fama-MacBeth standard errors, to account for the extra estimation uncertainty introduced by using noisy Stage-1 betas rather than true ones — it does not change the point estimate \(\bar\lambda\) itself, only how confidently it can be reported.
Q6. In the worked example, four monthly λ̂_t estimates average to 1.50 with a standard error of about 1.57, giving t ≈ 0.96. A colleague argues the 1.50% average is clearly a real, tradable carry premium since it's a big number. What's the correct response, and does a large, statistically significant λ̄ from a longer, more careful Fama-MacBeth run settle the question either?
The correct response to the colleague: an average that is not statistically distinguishable from zero — here \(t\approx0.96\), far below the conventional threshold of about 2 — cannot be treated as evidence of a reliable premium no matter how large it looks in percentage terms; with only \(T=4\) periods the standard error is itself far too noisy to draw a confident conclusion either way. And even a longer, more careful run that clears statistical significance after both the Shanken and autocorrelation corrections still only answers "was this premium reliably nonzero in this historical sample, for this specific factor definition" — it says nothing about whether the factor was chosen after looking at the same data (a multiple-testing risk), or whether the premium survives out-of-sample and after costs. A correctly-computed standard error is a statement about the estimator, not a certificate of tradable edge.
