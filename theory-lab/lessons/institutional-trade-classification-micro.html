Trade Classification, Guessing Who Started It

A raw trade print is a price, a size, and a timestamp — nothing on the tape says whether the buyer or the seller was the aggressor. Every order-flow signal that needs a signed direction, including this curriculum's own VPIN lesson, is quietly resting on an algorithm that infers that sign from price and quote data alone. This deck covers three of them.

Section 1

Why a trade needs a "sign" at all

No trade record says "buyer-initiated." Every method here is an inference from what's actually available.

🏷️

An inference, not a label

A print is price + size + timestamp. Whether it was a buyer lifting an offer or a seller hitting a bid has to be guessed from that alone.

🔗

Already inside this curriculum

The VPIN lesson already uses one of these methods (Bulk Volume Classification) as its buy/sell split — this deck names it explicitly.

➡️

Feeds what's next

PIN and order-flow-imbalance — later lessons in this category — both take signed buy/sell volume as a given input. This is where it actually comes from.

⚠️

Every method here is a guess

Even the academic standard has a published, non-zero error rate — none of this is ground truth, just the best inference available.

Get this inference step wrong, and every metric built on top of it — a toxicity score, an informed-trading probability, an imbalance signal — measures an artifact of the classifier, not the market.
Section 2

The tick rule — price history, nothing else

Compare a trade's price only to the trade immediately before it.

The rule
Uptick → Buy

Price higher than the previous trade.

Downtick → Sell

Price lower than the previous trade.

Zero tick → inherit

Price unchanged — copy the last classified sign.

Needs nothing but a price series — no quote data at all. That's its whole selling point, and exactly why it's the fallback inside Lee-Ready rather than a first choice when better data exists.

On a quiet run of trades at the same unchanged price, every print after the first just repeats whatever sign the trade that set that price level got — the tick rule has no fresh information about any of the repeats individually.

Section 3

The quote rule — compare to the standing midpoint

Above the bid-ask midpoint is a buy. Below it is a sell. Exactly at it, the quote rule has nothing to decide.

Try it
The quote rule needs the prevailing bid and ask, not just the last trade price — it's reading where liquidity actually rested, not just where the last print happened to land.

Section 5

Lee-Ready (1991) — the long-standing academic standard

1

Quote rule first

Compare the trade to the midpoint of the quote in effect a short lag — commonly five seconds, in the original paper — before the print.

2

Not at the midpoint? Done

Above it: buy. Below it: sell. Most trades resolve right here.

3

Exactly at the midpoint? Tick rule breaks the tie

The only job the tick rule does inside Lee-Ready — resolve the one case the quote rule can't.

📉

A documented modern-market weakness

Published research (Chakrabarty, Li, Nguyen & Van Ness) finds its accuracy degrades on electronic/high-frequency markets — odd lots, sub-penny pricing, and quotes updating far faster than the five-second convention assumes.

Not editorializing — a published finding

This degradation is a specific, documented empirical result, not an opinion inserted into this deck. It's stated exactly as honestly in the full lesson: a real, cited weakness of a still-widely-used method, not a reason to discard it.

Section 7

BVC — classify a whole bar, not one trade

Bulk Volume Classification steps back from individual trades entirely.

The idea

Instead of asking "was this specific trade a buy," BVC asks: given how far price moved over this whole bar, what fraction of the bar's total volume was probably buy-initiated? Buy-fraction = Φ(ΔP / σΔP) — the bar's standardized price change, passed through the standard normal CDF.

This is exactly the classification method this curriculum's own VPIN lesson already builds its toxicity measure on top of. Not a coincidence — it's the same formula, same paper (Easley, López de Prado & O'Hara, 2012), named explicitly here instead of left implicit.
📈

Big rise → mostly buy

A large positive standardized price change pushes Φ(·) toward 1 — nearly the whole bar classified buy.

➖

Flat bar → 50/50

Φ(0) = 0.5 exactly, by the symmetry of the normal distribution — "no directional evidence" becomes an even split.

✅

Needs no quote data

Only price and volume — which is exactly why VPIN, built for real-time use where clean quote history isn't cheap, chose BVC over Lee-Ready.

⚠️

A bar-level guess, not a trade-level fact

Many small informed trades and one large trade lifted by a single buyer can produce the identical aggregate move — BVC can't tell them apart.

Section 8

Drag the price change — watch the split move

Fixed volatility σ⊂ΔP⊂ = 0.0004. Drag ΔP and the buy/sell bar redraws live.

Try it
Price change ΔPΔP = 0.0000
z = ΔP/σ = 0.00 → Φ(z) = 0.500 → 50.0% buy / 50.0% sell

Notice the split moves fastest near the center and flattens out toward the edges — that's Φ(·) compressing as it approaches 0 or 1, exactly the same normal-CDF shape behind a z-table, a Black-Scholes N(d), or VPIN's own buy/sell split.

Section 10

Which method actually gets used, and where

Lee-Ready
  • Needs a genuine, correctly time-stamped quote history
  • Default on historical exchange-listed (TAQ-style) equity and futures data
  • Documented accuracy degradation on modern electronic markets
Bulk Volume Classification
  • Needs only a price and volume series — no quote data at all
  • The natural fit for FX/OTC, where a consolidated quote tape doesn't exist
  • Classifies a bar's volume in aggregate, never an individual trade
Verified against this repo's actual code

A repo-wide search for Lee-Ready, tick-rule, or quote-rule implementation code finds zero matches anywhere in this codebase — the only mention is inside this project's own VPIN lesson's prose, as a contrast case for BVC, not as code that exists. That same VPIN lesson confirms, in its own words, that BVC is exactly what its toxicity measure is built on. Consistent with FX/OTC trading typically lacking the consolidated, time-stamped quote history Lee-Ready specifically requires.

Section 11

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Under the tick rule, how is a trade classified if its price exactly matches the previous trade's price?

A "zero tick" has no fresh price movement to react to, so the tick rule simply repeats whatever sign the most recently classified trade received.
Question 2

When does Lee-Ready fall back to the tick rule?

Lee-Ready uses the quote rule as its primary classifier. The tick rule's only job inside the algorithm is breaking the one tie the quote rule can't resolve on its own.
Question 3

A bar has volume 2,000 and Φ(ΔP/σ) = 0.30. What is V_sell under BVC?

V_buy = 2,000 × 0.30 = 600, so V_sell = 2,000 − 600 = 1,400. A buy-fraction below 0.5 means most of the bar's volume gets classified sell.
Question 4

What has published research found about Lee-Ready's accuracy on modern electronic markets?

Work re-examining Lee-Ready on contemporary data (Chakrabarty, Li, Nguyen & Van Ness) documents meaningfully lower accuracy than on the slower markets the algorithm was originally built for — a published finding, not a guess.
Question 5

Why does BVC need no quote data at all, unlike Lee-Ready?

BVC's entire input is a bar's price change and a volatility estimate — that's exactly why VPIN, needing a real-time signal without cheap access to reliable quote history, was built on BVC rather than Lee-Ready.
Section 12

What to carry forward

01

No raw trade record says who initiated it — every classification scheme is an inference from price and quote data that is actually available.

02

The tick rule needs only a price series; the quote rule needs the prevailing bid-ask midpoint and is generally the stronger of the two when reliable quote data exists.

03

Lee-Ready (1991) uses the quote rule as primary and the tick rule only to break exact-midpoint ties — and its accuracy is documented to degrade on modern electronic markets.

04

Bulk Volume Classification splits a whole bar's volume via Φ(ΔP/σΔP) — exactly the method this curriculum's own VPIN lesson already builds on.

05

Neither method is implemented anywhere in this codebase — consistent with FX/OTC trading typically lacking the consolidated quote history Lee-Ready specifically needs.

What this now lets you do

Recognize that "buy volume" and "sell volume" in any order-flow signal are almost always the output of one of these inference methods, not a directly observed fact. Read a VPIN, PIN, or order-flow-imbalance number knowing exactly what classification machinery is sitting underneath it, and what that machinery can and can't actually tell apart.
