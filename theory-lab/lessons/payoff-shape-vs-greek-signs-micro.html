Same Greeks, Different Risk

A short put and a short call are both short gamma, long theta and short vega. One can lose at most the strike. The other can lose without limit. The Greeks are local slopes; the payoff is the whole road — cliff included.

Section 1

Same row of signs…

K = 100, 30 days, σ = 20%, r = 2%. Sell a put for 2.20, sell a call for 2.37.

Short put: Δ +0.477 · Γ −0.0695 · Θ +0.0353/day · ν −0.114
Short call: Δ −0.523 · Γ −0.0695 · Θ +0.0408/day · ν −0.114

Gamma and vega are not just the same sign — they are the same number. Deltas differ by exactly 1. Thetas differ by a tiny financing term.

Short put worst case

−97.80 (price can only fall to zero)

Short call worst case

Unlimited (price has no ceiling)

Section 3

Why: a short call is a short put plus a straight line

−C

−P
−
(S − Ke⁻ʳᵗ)

Put-call parity, negated. The extra piece is a short forward: delta −1, gamma 0, vega 0, almost no theta.

What the Greeks see

Gamma and vega of the forward are zero, so the two shorts show identical curvature and vol exposure.

What the Greeks miss

The forward is a straight line with no floor. It carries the short call's entire unbounded tail.

Section 4

Greeks are a Taylor expansion — local by design

δV ≈ Δ·δS + ½Γ·δS² + Θ·δt + ν·δσ
A ±50 move, Greeks vs reality
Short call, +50: Taylor says −113.0 · true reprice −47.80
Short put, −50: Taylor says −110.7 · true reprice −47.63
The quadratic assumes gamma stays at its ATM value across the whole move. It doesn't: once deep in the money, gamma collapses and delta saturates. Locally the two shorts are mirror images; the Taylor estimate can't tell them apart.
Section 5

Where they part: a 100-point move

Fall to 0

Short put loses 97.80. That's the most it can ever lose.

Rise to 200

Short call loses 97.63 — then 197.63 at 300, 397.63 at 500. No floor.

Sizing rule

Size each short by its stressed loss, not its Greek columns. And for a short call, the stress level has to be chosen — the contract never supplies a worst case.

Deep in the money, a short call behaves like short stock: every point of rally is a point of loss.

Section 7 · It actually happened

October 2008: Volkswagen

On Sunday 26 October 2008 Porsche disclosed control of about 74% of VW's ordinary shares — roughly 43% outright, most of the rest via cash-settled options. Lower Saxony held about 20%. Only a few percent were left to trade, against heavy short interest.

Over two trading days VW rose roughly fivefold, above €1,000 intraday on 28 October — briefly the world's most valuable listed company. The squeeze eased after Porsche said it would release some of its position.

January 2021: the replay

GameStop: retail buying, much of it via short-dated calls, met heavy short interest; the price multiplied many times in weeks. Short-seller Melvin Capital needed about $2.75 billion from Citadel and Point72, and brokers restricted buying on 28 January. Nobody's delta was wrong on a Monday. The payoff had no ceiling.

Section 8

The dealer-level version: what the desk found

A dealer book that has sold options is "short gamma" — this lesson, aggregated. The folk claim: short gamma amplifies moves, long gamma damps them, and so gamma tells you what's next.

Desk test: held up (range only) 793 days of Nasdaq futures, pre-registered. Next-day range vs a Brownian baseline +0.176 wider on short-gamma days (p 0.0008), +0.315 vol-matched; 4/4 specs, 6/6 years. But short-gamma days sit at ≈1.02 — ordinary — while long-gamma days sit at ≈0.85. Long gamma is quiet; short gamma isn't wild.
Desk test: no direction found No direction from gamma — the adjacent wall-magnet test scored 48.8% out-of-sample vs a 49.9% placebo. Not FX — tested there, doesn't carry.
Section 9

Pitfalls

✗

"Same Greeks, same risk"

Identical gamma and vega; worst cases 97.80 vs unlimited.

✗

"Gamma ≈ 0 means safe"

Low gamma means linear, not small. A deep-ITM short call loses one-for-one.

≈

"Bounded means small"

The short put risks 97.80 to earn 2.20 — about 44× the premium.

✗

"Short gamma = breakout"

Tested: short-gamma days are ordinary; gamma carries no direction; nothing on FX.

Section 10

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

At the same strike, short put and short call gamma are…

−C = −P − (S − Ke⁻ʳᵗ). The forward is linear in S, so its gamma is zero and the two shorts' gammas are equal.
Question 2

Where does the short call's unlimited tail come from?

The forward is a straight line with no floor; its gamma and vega are zero, so the Greeks can't register it.
Question 3

The desk's verdict on "dealer short gamma" is…

+0.176 (p 0.0008) wider on short-gamma days vs baseline over 793 Nasdaq days; long-gamma days ≈0.85 are the informative state. Direction falsified (48.8% vs 49.9% placebo); doesn't carry to FX.
Question 4

Why did the Taylor estimate say −113 for a +50 move when the truth was −47.8?

The Greeks are a local expansion. A 50-point move on a 30-day option is not local — the full reprice is the honest number.
Section 11

What to carry forward

01

Short put and short call share gamma, theta and vega signs — gamma and vega are exactly equal.

02

Short call = short put + short forward. The forward is invisible to gamma and vega and carries the whole unlimited tail.

03

Greeks are local slopes; payoffs are global. Size by stressed loss, not Greek columns.

04

Deep ITM, a short call's Greeks go quiet while it loses one-for-one — low gamma means linear, not small.

05

Dealer gamma, tested: a Nasdaq range effect (long gamma quiet). Direction falsified. Not FX.
