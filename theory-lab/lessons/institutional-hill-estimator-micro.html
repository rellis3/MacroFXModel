The Hill Estimator, A Ruler for Tail Heaviness

This curriculum's Extreme Value Theory lesson already builds a fitted Generalized Pareto Distribution — a full portrait of a tail's shape. This deck covers something older and much cruder: sort your data, take the biggest few values, average one log-ratio, and flip it upside down. No fitting, no distributional form — just a number that says how heavy the tail is.

Section 1

A ruler, not a portrait

Two different jobs, using two different amounts of machinery.

The GPD fit (sibling lesson)
  • Fits a full exceedance distribution — shape ξ and scale β
  • Needs a threshold u and a fitting procedure (MLE / PWM)
  • Can be inverted for VaR/CVaR at ANY confidence level
The Hill estimator (this deck)
  • Estimates one exponent, α — no scale, no full distribution
  • Needs only the top k order statistics — sort, sum, divide
  • Cannot be inverted for an arbitrary VaR/CVaR on its own
Read the sibling lesson first if you haven't — this deck assumes its ξ notation and its Balkema-de Haan-Pickands framing throughout, and points back to it by name instead of repeating it.
Section 2

The formula: sort, sum, invert

X₍₁₎ ≤ X₍₂₎ ≤ ... ≤ X₍ₙ₎ — the sample sorted ascending. Pick k.

The Hill estimator
α̂_k
=
[ (1/k) · Σ ln( X₍ₙ₋ᵢ₊₁₎ / X₍ₙ₋ₖ₎ ) ]
⁻¹
Sum the log-ratio of each of the top k values to the (k+1)-th largest value, X₍ₙ₋ₖ₎ — the reference point, playing the same role the sibling lesson's threshold u plays. Average it. Flip it upside down. That's the whole estimator.

The sibling lesson's shape parameter ξ (for its heavy-tailed ξ>0 case) is this same α, inverted: ξ = 1/α, exactly. Not an analogy — the identical fact about the tail, in two conventional units.

Section 3

Why an average log-ratio recovers an exponent

The one-line derivation
If X has an EXACT Pareto tail above some point u — P(X>x | X>u) = (x/u)⁻ᵅ — then Y = ln(X/u) is Exponentially distributed with rate α. The MLE of an Exponential rate is 1 / (sample mean).
Hill's α̂
=
the Exponential-rate MLE, applied to the top k log-ratios

That's why the formula looks like "average, then flip." The estimator is justified more generally, under the weaker regularly-varying-tail assumption, not only for an exact Pareto distribution — but the exact-Pareto case is the cleanest way to see where the arithmetic comes from.

Section 4

Worked example, six numbers, k = 5

Illustrative daily loss magnitudes (bps), largest first — not real market data.

The numbers
187 · 152 · 134 · 121 · 109

ln(187/98)+ln(152/98)+ln(134/98)+ln(121/98)+ln(109/98) = 0.6461+0.4389+0.3129+0.2108+0.1064 = 1.7151. Average over k=5: 0.3430.
α̂ = 1 / 0.3430 ≈ 2.92

Against the sibling lesson's own framing: ξ̂ ≈ 0.34 is a real, moderate heavy tail — well short of the ξ ≥ 0.5 infinite-variance zone that lesson flags.

Section 7

Why bother, when a GPD fit does more?

🧩

Weaker assumption

Only "regularly varying tail" — never commits to the exceedances actually being a Generalized Pareto Distribution.

⚡

Trivial to compute

A sort, k logarithms, a sum, a divide. No optimizer, no PWM algebra, nothing to check for convergence.

📐

Known asymptotics

Consistent and asymptotically normal as k,n→∞ with k/n→0 — variance shrinks roughly like α²/k (Hill, 1975).

🚫

The trade-off

Only ever a shape exponent — no scale, no closed-form VaR/CVaR at an arbitrary confidence level the way a fitted (ξ,β) GPD provides.

Hill's number tells you "this tail is scarier than that one," almost for free. It doesn't tell you the exact loss at the one-in-a-thousand day — that still needs the sibling lesson's fuller machinery.

Ranking tail heaviness across FX pairs

A cheap screening step, before deciding where a full GPD fit is worth the effort. Illustrative numbers only.

➖

A typical G10 cross

α̂ ≈ 3.0 — moderately heavy, a realistic middle of the road for daily FX.

🔥

A crisis-prone EM/commodity currency

A ranking like this is cheap enough to run on an entire watchlist as a first pass, flagging fat-tailed names for a proper GPD fit rather than fitting every instrument with equal effort.

Section 10

Where this quietly goes wrong

📈

The horror plot is normal

As slide 6 shows on real simulated data, α̂ rarely settles into a clean plateau. Reporting one k without showing the range is the single most common misuse.

🚫

Wrong for non-regularly-varying tails

Exponential (ξ=0) or bounded (ξ<0) tails give the formula a number anyway — but there's no true polynomial exponent for it to be estimating.

🌊

Still assumes weak dependence

Volatility clustering can let one turbulent episode dominate the top-k order statistics and skew α̂ — the same caveat the sibling lesson raises for GPD fitting.

🔬

Unvalidated ≠ trustworthy

One α̂ at one k on one sample is a number, not evidence — check stability across k and, ideally, against a GPD fit on the same data.

Section 11

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

What must the data satisfy for the Hill estimator to target a well-defined quantity?

Hill's assumption is strictly weaker than "this is a GPD" — every GPD with ξ>0 is regularly varying, but not every regularly varying tail is a GPD.
Question 2

Order statistics 240, 210, 175, 150 (largest first), k=3. What's α̂?

Reference = 150. Sum of ln-ratios ≈ 0.9606; average over k=3 ≈ 0.3202; α̂ = 1/0.3202 ≈ 3.12. (0.32 is ξ̂ = 1/α̂, not α̂ itself.)
Question 3

How does this lesson's α relate to the sibling lesson's ξ, for a heavy tail?

For the regularly-varying, ξ>0 regime, ξ and α are defined so that ξ=1/α exactly — not an approximation.
Question 4

Too large a k causes what kind of problem, and what's the sibling lesson's analogous choice?

Too small k = high variance (too few points). Too large k = bias (pulling in the ordinary bulk of the distribution) — exactly the sibling lesson's "threshold u too low" failure mode, restated in ranks.
Question 5

What can a fitted GPD do that a plain Hill estimate cannot?

Both can indicate tail heaviness. Only the fitted (ξ,β) GPD pair, combined with the threshold, plugs into a closed-form quantile/CVaR formula at an arbitrary confidence level — the Hill estimator alone gives only the exponent.
Question 6

Does this repo run a live Hill estimator in production?

hillEstimator is defined and exported but never imported elsewhere. fitGPD/potFit from the same file ARE wired into js/analyticsDesk.js, called live from server.js.
Section 12

What to carry forward

01

The Hill estimator (Hill, 1975) sorts the data, takes the top k order statistics, averages a log-ratio to the k-th largest, and inverts — no fitted distribution, no numerical optimizer.

02

Its output α̂ is the sibling lesson's ξ, inverted: ξ = 1/α exactly, for the heavy-tailed case — one fact, two units.

03

It needs a weaker assumption and near-zero computation, but only ever produces a shape exponent — not a full distribution to invert for an arbitrary VaR/CVaR.

04

Choosing k is choosing the sibling lesson's threshold u, restated in ranks: too small is noisy, too large is biased — the "Hill horror plot" is the normal case, not an edge case.

05

A correct, ready-made hillEstimator already sits in this repo's own extremesCore.js — unused, next to the GPD/POT code that genuinely is live.

What this now lets you do

Get a quick, defensible read on how heavy a tail is — and tell it apart from a full GPD fit's fuller, but heavier, machinery — without confusing "estimated a shape exponent" for "fitted a distribution I can invert for any quantile."
