PIN, Turning Adverse Selection Into a Single Number

Glosten-Milgrom explains, one trade at a time, why an informed order moves the quote. PIN takes that same informed-vs-noise structure and fits it — by maximum likelihood, to nothing more than a stock's daily buy and sell counts — into one estimated probability: how much of this stock's trading, this period, is expected to come from someone who knew something the market didn't yet. It's also the slow, rigorous model VPIN was built to approximate in real time.

The whole idea
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

Glosten-Milgrom says informed traders widen the spread. But I can't see who's informed. Can anyone actually measure it?

That's what PIN does, the probability of informed trading. Easley, Kiefer, O'Hara and Paperman built it in 1996 from nothing more than how many buys and sells a stock gets each day.

Just daily counts?

Just counts. The model tells a story about each day. Uninformed traders turn up every day on both sides, say a hundred buys and a hundred sells. On some days private news arrives, say one day in four. On those days informed traders join in, a hundred and fifty of them, and they all trade the same way.

So a bad-news day looks like a hundred buys and two hundred and fifty sells.

Exactly, and a good-news day is the mirror image. Most days look balanced. A few look sharply lopsided. That fingerprint is what the fit uses.

How does it get the numbers out?

Maximum likelihood over many days of counts. How often days are lopsided tells you how often news arrives. How lopsided tells you how many informed traders come. The balanced days give the background rate.

And then PIN is?

The informed share of expected trading. News one day in four, times a hundred and fifty informed trades, is thirty-seven and a half a day on average. Uninformed trading is two hundred a day. Thirty-seven and a half out of two hundred and thirty-seven and a half is about nought point one six.

Does it matter whether the news tends to be good or bad?

Not for PIN. The direction drops out completely. PIN asks how much of the flow is informed, not which way it pushes.

Can I run it live, intraday?

No. It needs a long history of days and a slow numerical fit. That's exactly why the same group built VPIN in 2012, a rougher version that updates as volume trades.

Is PIN settled science?

It's widely cited and contested. Duarte and Young argued in 2009 that part of what it picks up is ordinary imbalance from illiquidity, not private information. And it can only be as good as the buy and sell classification feeding it.

One line?

Informed traders show up as lopsided days, and PIN is their share of all the trading, whichever way the news points.

Section 1 · How it reached trading

Putting a number on how informed the flow is

PIN · Easley, Kiefer, O'Hara & Paperman, 1996 → information-risk measure, then VPIN

How it reached trading

Easley, Kiefer, O'Hara & Paperman (1996) estimated the informed share of flow from daily buy/sell counts, and found it higher in thinly traded stocks.

Easley, Hvidkjaer & O'Hara (2002) priced it as information risk; VPIN (2012) made a real-time version for toxicity monitoring. Duarte & Young (2009) dispute what it measures.

Section 2

Two populations of traders, one stock

Everything PIN estimates comes down to who shows up to trade, and when.

🔁

Uninformed traders — every day

Buyers arrive at Poisson rate ε, sellers at Poisson rate ε, on every single trading day, event or not. This is the ordinary background churn of trading unrelated to private information.

🎯

Informed traders — event days only

Arrive at Poisson rate μ, but only on days when a private information event has occurred — and they all trade the same direction the news points.

🎲

Whether an event happens is random

Probability α on any given day, independent across days. Most days, for most stocks, no event occurs at all.

↕

Which direction, if one does

Conditional on an event, bad news with probability δ, good news with probability 1−δ. This decides which side the informed crowd trades on.

A researcher never sees "an event happened" directly — only the day's total buy and sell counts. PIN is the technique for reading that fingerprint back out of the counts alone.
Section 3

The day-type tree

Every trading day is one draw down this tree. Three possible outcomes, three different expected arrival rates.

The whole model, drawn
The three leaves are the three possible day types feeding a mixture-of-Poissons likelihood; PIN itself, once fit, only needs the numbers from two of the four parameters shown here plus α — δ drops out entirely, which the next few sections explain.
Section 4

Fitting it — a mixture likelihood, maximized

A researcher never sees which branch of the tree fired on a given day — only that day's (B, S) counts.

Σ

Each day is a weighted mixture

A day's likelihood is (1−α)·[both sides at rate ε] + αδ·[sells jump to μ+ε] + α(1−δ)·[buys jump to μ+ε] — every branch of the tree, weighted by how likely it was.

📅

Multiply across many days

Assuming days are independent, the full-sample log-likelihood is just the sum of each day's log-likelihood, over the whole estimation window.

🖥️

Maximize numerically

No closed form exists for α, δ, μ, ε — a numerical optimizer searches for the parameter values that make the observed daily counts most probable.

⚠️

Not a browser-widget exercise

Real PIN estimation needs many days of trade-classified data and a careful optimization — known to have local-optima and boundary-solution issues. This deck starts from already-fitted numbers instead.

Where do the daily (B, S) counts themselves come from? A trade-classification scheme applied to the raw tape — covered in this category's own Trade Classification lesson. PIN's likelihood takes those counts as a given input, not something it derives.
Section 5

Unpacking PIN = αμ / (αμ + 2ε)

↑

αμ — expected informed arrivals

Probability an event occurs (α), times the informed arrival rate conditional on one occurring (μ). This is a rate: expected informed trades per day.

↕

2ε — expected uninformed arrivals

Uninformed buyers at rate ε plus uninformed sellers at rate ε, every day — total uninformed arrivals per day is ε + ε.

%

PIN — the ratio

Expected informed arrivals divided by expected total arrivals — the fraction of a day's order flow expected to come from someone trading on private information.

0–1

Bounded like a probability

PIN near 0 means flow is almost entirely noise; PIN closer to 1 means informed arrivals dominate whenever an event fires, and events are frequent.

Section 6

Why δ is nowhere in that formula

The distinction that matters

δ governs which direction an information event points — bad news versus good news. PIN is built to answer a direction-agnostic question: overall, how much of this stock's trading is information-driven at all, regardless of which way that information points. Two stocks with identical α, μ and ε but completely different δ — one always bad news, one always good — have identical PIN values.

δ shapes what informed trading looks like on an event day — net buying pressure or net selling pressure. It says nothing about how often (α), or how intensely (μ, against baseline ε), information-driven trading occurs — and PIN measures exactly that second question, not the first.

Section 7

Worked example: from fitted numbers to PIN

Already-fitted parameters
α = 0.25
μ = 150/day
ε = 100/day
Expected informed arrivals: αμ = 0.25 × 150 = 37.5/day. Expected uninformed arrivals: 2ε = 2 × 100 = 200/day. PIN = 37.5 / (37.5 + 200) = 37.5 / 237.5 ≈ 0.158.
About 15.8% of this stock's expected daily order flow is estimated to be information-driven. On days that were actually no-event days, buy and sell counts would tend to sit close together near 100 each; on actual event days, one side would spike toward ~250 while the other stays near 100 — exactly the lopsided fingerprint an MLE fit uses to back out α, μ and ε in the first place.

Drag α — watch the informed share move

μ = 150/day and ε = 100/day held fixed. Only the event probability α changes.

Push α toward 1 — events happen almost every day — and the informed bar keeps growing while the uninformed bar never moves, since ε is unrelated to how often events occur. PIN climbs toward 1 right alongside it.

Section 10

PIN vs. VPIN

The same underlying question, two very different tools for answering it.

Compare
Daily buy/sell counts
→
MLE fit (α, δ, μ, ε)
→
PIN
A genuine structural model with an explicit likelihood, fit to many days of history by numerical maximum likelihood. Theoretically rigorous — and slow. Cannot update intraday.
Equal-volume buckets
→
Bulk Volume Classification
→
VPIN
No likelihood, no MLE. Classifies each volume bucket's buy/sell split from its price move alone, then averages the imbalance over a rolling window. Updates continuously — at the cost of PIN's theoretical grounding.
Easley, López de Prado & O'Hara are explicit about the lineage: VPIN exists because PIN's own MLE approach is too slow and data-hungry to compute in anything close to real time. It's PIN's fast, approximate cousin — not a newer replacement for it.
Section 11

Not a settled measure — a real academic dispute

The Duarte & Young (2009) critique

A meaningful share of what standard PIN estimates capture, they argue, is ordinary illiquidity-driven order-flow imbalance — buy and sell orders simply arriving unevenly for reasons having nothing to do with private information — not genuine adverse selection. Their proposed decomposition splits PIN into a pure adverse-selection piece and a symmetric-imbalance piece, and finds much of what PIN "prices" empirically loads onto the latter.

Treat "this stock's PIN is high" the way this curriculum treats any contested measure: a real, well-defined number under one specific interpretation, alongside a serious published argument that part of what it captures is a different phenomenon entirely. Widely cited is not the same claim as universally settled.

Also worth knowing

PIN's likelihood is numerically delicate — local optima and boundary solutions are documented issues. And because PIN's only input is classified daily counts, any bias in the underlying trade-classification scheme propagates straight into the final PIN number.

Section 12

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

On a day with no information event, what generates the buy and sell counts?

Informed traders only ever arrive on event days. A no-event day's flow is entirely the ordinary uninformed baseline, buys and sells both drawn from Poisson(ε).
Question 2

α = 0.20, μ = 120, ε = 80. What is PIN?

αμ = 24, 2ε = 160. PIN = 24 / (24+160) = 24/184 ≈ 0.130.
Question 3

Why doesn't δ appear in the PIN formula?

δ governs the expected imbalance/direction on an event day (bad vs. good news). PIN answers a direction-agnostic question about the overall rate of information-driven trading — two stocks with identical α, μ, ε but different δ have identical PIN.
Question 4

Why was VPIN built, given that PIN already existed?

VPIN is explicitly framed by its own authors as a real-time-computable substitute for PIN's informed-trading estimate — same underlying question, deliberately cruder but continuously updating answer.
Question 5

What does the Duarte & Young (2009) critique argue?

They decompose PIN into a pure adverse-selection component and a symmetric order-flow-imbalance component, and find much of what standard PIN "prices" loads onto the latter — a real, unresolved methodological dispute.
Your call
Scenario drill · an illustrative research screen

The stock at the top of the PIN screen

A research screen ranks stocks by PIN fitted on a quarter of daily buy and sell counts. Top of the list is a thinly traded small cap at about 0.31, roughly double a typical name in your universe. Its fitted \(\delta\) is high, so most of its information events look like bad news. A colleague wants to treat it as a stock full of informed sellers and short it. What do you do?

  1. Short it. High PIN and high δ together mean informed money is selling.
    PIN is direction-free and says nothing about which way the next move goes. A fitted δ from one quarter is a weak basis for a trade, and the whole reading may not be information at all.
  2. Treat it as a flag to investigate: check the trade classification behind the counts, refit from several starting points to rule out a local optimum, and test whether ordinary illiquidity-driven imbalance explains the number.
    Each of these is a documented weak point: classification errors flow straight into PIN, the likelihood has local optima and boundary solutions, and Duarte and Young argue part of PIN is symmetric imbalance in illiquid names rather than private information.
  3. Switch to VPIN instead, since it is newer and updates in real time.
    VPIN answers a related question faster, but it is a rougher approximation of the same idea, not a strictly better replacement, and it does not resolve whether the imbalance is information.
  4. Ignore the screen. PIN is contested, so it is useless.
    Contested is not the same as empty. PIN is a well-defined, widely used measure of information risk; the right response to the dispute is to check what drives a given reading, not to throw the measure away.

What the lesson says. This is an illustrative situation, not a historical episode. The lesson's points decide it: PIN measures how much of the flow is informed, never which way; it is only as good as the classification and the numerical fit behind it; and Duarte and Young (2009) argue a real share of it reflects illiquidity-driven imbalance. A high reading in a thin stock is a question to investigate, not a trade.

Section 13

What to carry forward

01

PIN estimates Glosten-Milgrom's informed-vs-noise split by maximum likelihood, fit to daily buy/sell counts — an estimation technique, not a new theory of the spread.

02

Four parameters — α, δ, μ, ε — fit to a mixture-of-Poissons likelihood across three day types: no event, bad-news event, good-news event.

03

PIN = αμ / (αμ + 2ε): expected informed arrival rate over expected total arrival rate.

04

δ never enters the formula — it decides direction, not overall informed-trading rate.

05

VPIN exists because PIN's MLE can't run live — same question, a faster and cruder answer.

06

Duarte & Young (2009): a real, unresolved critique — some of what PIN measures may be ordinary imbalance, not information.

07

How it reached trading: PIN (1996) became a research measure of information risk and the parent of the real-time VPIN toxicity monitor.

What this now lets you do

Read the PIN formula and know exactly what each piece — and each conspicuously absent piece — means. Place PIN correctly relative to Glosten-Milgrom (the theory it estimates) and VPIN (the real-time approximation it inspired). Treat a published PIN number the way any contested empirical measure deserves: taken seriously, not taken as settled.
