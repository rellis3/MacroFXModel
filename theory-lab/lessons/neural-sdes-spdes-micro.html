Neural SDEs, the Frontier This Category Ends On

This is the seventh and last lesson of the Stochastic Differential Equations category — the arc ran from proving a solution even exists, to simulating one numerically, to handling many correlated processes at once, to naming specific short-rate models built on that machinery, to the Fokker-Planck equation and full stochastic PDEs. Every one of those six lessons shared one quiet habit: a human wrote down the exact formula for the drift and the diffusion. This lesson asks the obvious next question — what if that formula were learned instead of written down — and is honest that the answer is current research, not a finished tool.

The whole idea
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

Every model in this sequence has someone deciding the drift and the volatility. Square roots, mean reversion, power laws. Why trust their choice?

You shouldn't trust it blindly. That choice is a real commitment, and it can be wrong in ways you only find out later. A neural SDE asks: what if the data chooses instead?

How does that work?

Keep the exact same skeleton: a drift term plus a noise term. But instead of writing a formula for each, you plug in two small neural networks. Their weights are the knobs.

And you tune the knobs how?

Simulate paths from the current networks. Compare them with real market paths. Nudge the weights so the fakes look more like the real thing. Kidger and co-authors framed this in 2021 as a forger and a detective: one network makes paths, another tries to tell them from real ones.

Sounds expensive to train.

The obvious way is. Ordinary training has to remember every step of every simulated path. Take a curve of fifty points simulated over a thousand steps. That is fifty thousand numbers to store, before you count the networks' own workings. Double the steps and you double the memory.

So what's the trick?

The adjoint method, from Li and colleagues in 2020. Instead of storing the forward path, you solve a second random equation that runs backward in time and hands you the gradients directly. Its memory does not grow with the number of steps.

And the neural partial differential version?

Same idea for a whole field. Think of an implied volatility surface: a number for every strike and every tenor, moving every day. A neural version tries to learn how the whole surface moves at once.

Has it reached a trading desk?

Mostly as research. Deep hedging, from Buehler and colleagues in 2019, trained networks to hedge with costs. From around 2020 groups calibrated neural SDEs to option prices. It is not a desk standard yet.

What's the catch?

Flexibility cuts both ways. A model that can fit anything will fit the noise in your training data too. A close fit in sample proves nothing until it survives data it has never seen.

One line to remember?

Letting data write the drift and volatility removes one assumption and adds a lot of knobs, so judge it on out-of-sample evidence, not on how well it fits.

Section 2 · The arc so far

Six lessons, one quiet assumption

Every named model this category has built shares the same skeleton — only what's plugged into μ and σ changes.

01

Existence & Uniqueness

Proved a solution is even guaranteed to exist, under Lipschitz and linear-growth conditions on μ and σ — conditions every later lesson quietly assumes hold.

02

Numerical Schemes

Almost no SDE worth building has a closed form, so Euler-Maruyama and Milstein simulate one step at a time — the same recursion a neural SDE trains through.

03

Multivariate & Correlated SDEs

Extended the machinery to many correlated processes driven by more than one source of randomness — a curve or a surface, not just one number.

04

Vasicek, CIR & Hull-White

Three named short-rate models, each a specific, hand-picked μ and σ chosen to fix one flaw in the model before it — decades-old, industry-standard.

05–06

Fokker-Planck & Stochastic PDEs

The PDE a process's density obeys, then what happens when the evolving object is an entire curve or field rather than one number.

Notice what never changed across all six: a person always supplied the exact functional form of μ(x,t) and σ(x,t) — a constant, a linear pull toward a mean, a square root enforcing positivity. That choice is a real modelling commitment, made ahead of time, and it can be quietly wrong.

Section 4

What if μ and σ were learned, not written down?

A neural SDE keeps the identical skeleton — it only replaces the hand-picked formulas with two neural networks.

The idea, as a diagram
θ is the combined set of weights inside both networks — the same kind of learnable number a network learns in image recognition, here shaping a drift and a diffusion instead of a decision boundary.

Training means simulating paths from the current μ_θ, σ_θ, comparing them against real observed data, and nudging θ with ordinary gradient descent so the simulated behaviour looks more like the real thing. Nothing about the SDE itself changes — dX_t = μ_θ(X_t,t) dt + σ_θ(X_t,t) dW_t is the exact same equation shape as every model on the previous slide.

Section 5

Why this is genuinely appealing

🔓

No forced fit to a named template

OU, GBM, CIR, and Heston are each a bet that reality looks like one specific, tractable formula. A neural SDE removes that constraint entirely — the network can in principle learn whatever shape the data actually calls for.

📐

One architecture, many regimes

Instead of picking a new named model every time a stylized fact changes, the same drift/diffusion network architecture can in principle absorb mean reversion, fat tails, or regime shifts directly from data.

🌊

Extends to whole fields, not just numbers

The neural SPDE generalization (Section 10) applies this same idea to an entire evolving surface — an implied-volatility surface, for instance — not just a handful of state variables.

The appeal is real and worth taking seriously: markets don't know they're supposed to look like Vasicek. A sufficiently flexible learned function could, in principle, capture dynamics that no small number of named, closed-form-friendly templates fits well.

Section 7

Training isn't free: the memory problem, and its fix

Naive backpropagation through a long simulated path is expensive. The stochastic adjoint method is the standard fix.

Naive backprop through Euler-Maruyama
  • Stores every intermediate simulated state to replay backward
  • Memory cost scales directly with the number of simulated steps
  • A long, fine time grid can require storing an enormous number of states at once
Stochastic adjoint method (Li, Wong, Chen & Duvenaud, 2020)
  • Solves a second, backward-running SDE instead of storing the forward path
  • Memory footprint doesn't grow with the number of forward steps
  • The direct stochastic analogue of the Neural-ODE adjoint (Chen et al., 2018)

Kidger, Foster, Li & Lyons (2021) framed the whole training procedure as a generator/discriminator pair, exactly like an ordinary GAN: the neural SDE generates simulated paths, and a separate discriminator network is trained to tell them apart from real observed data — except the "generated image" here is an entire stochastic path, not a single picture.

A network's close fit inside the training range says nothing about what it does outside it. Unlike CIR's √X_t term, which enforces non-negativity everywhere by construction, a learned μ_θ, σ_θ has no such guarantee once asked about a region of (x, t) it never saw — it can extrapolate smoothly, or it can produce dynamics that are quietly implausible.

Section 10

Neural SPDEs: the same idea, for a whole surface

A neural SDE learns a handful of numbers evolving in time. A neural SPDE learns an entire field evolving in time.

📈

An implied-volatility surface is a field

A value for every combination of strike and tenor, changing day to day — exactly a field indexed by two spatial-like coordinates, evolving stochastically in a third: time.

🧩

Salvi, Lemercier & Gerasimovics (2021)

Built a neural SPDE architecture with a specific, genuine property: resolution invariance — trained at one grid resolution, it can be evaluated at a different one without retraining.

⚠

Resolution-invariant ≠ accurate

That property says nothing about whether the learned dynamics are correct at any given resolution — two separate claims, easy to conflate.

Instead of the standard practitioner approach — modelling a surface's parameters (a local-vol grid, a handful of factor loadings) evolving independently — a neural SPDE would attempt to learn the entire surface's joint stochastic evolution directly, as one continuous object. It's a natural extension of everything this category has built — and also a genuinely advanced, unvalidated-in-this-repo research question, not a description of an existing tool.

Section 11

Say this plainly

Current research — not a production technique

Neural SDEs and neural SPDEs are current, active machine-learning research, published mostly within the last five to seven years, with genuine open questions still being worked out in the literature. They are considerably further from a testable trading signal than anything else in this category, including the decades-old, industry-standard Vasicek, CIR, and Hull-White models three lessons prior. They are much harder to fit reliably than a 2–3 parameter classical model, they can overfit and extrapolate implausibly outside their training range (Section 8), and they sacrifice interpretability almost entirely (Section 6) — there is no learned equivalent of "this parameter is the mean-reversion speed."

Verified against this repo's actual code, 2026-09-04

The full lesson states there is no built, tested, or validated neural-SDE / neural-SPDE implementation anywhere in this repo. A repo-wide search for neural-SDE, neural-SPDE, torchsde, and learned drift/diffusion code — across every model, bot, and analysis directory, not just Theory Lab — found nothing. That claim checks out as still accurate: this remains a knowledge lesson, not a description of a built tool.

Section 12

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

What, precisely, does a neural SDE change compared to OU, GBM, Heston, or CIR?

The skeleton is identical across every model in this category. A neural SDE only replaces the functional forms of μ and σ with networks μ_θ, σ_θ — same equation, learned coefficients.
Question 2

Why does naive backpropagation through a simulated Euler-Maruyama path get memory-expensive?

Reverse-mode backprop walks the forward pass in reverse, so it needs every intermediate state kept in memory — a cost that scales directly with the number of simulated steps.
Question 3

What does "resolution-invariant" mean for a neural SPDE — and what does it NOT mean?

Resolution invariance (Salvi et al., 2021) is a real architectural property — evaluable at a different resolution than it was trained on — but it says nothing about whether the underlying learned dynamics are actually correct.
Question 4

A close in-sample fit from a neural SDE tells you...

Flexibility buys fit; it does not buy validated generalization. A hand-specified model's inflexibility is itself a form of regularization that a neural SDE gives up.
Question 5

Is a neural SDE or neural SPDE built, tested, or validated as a trading tool anywhere in this repo?

A repo-wide search found no neural-SDE, neural-SPDE, or learned drift/diffusion implementation anywhere in this repo. This lesson is explicitly a knowledge lesson, not a claim about a built, validated tool.
Your call
Scenario drill · an illustrative vendor pitch

"Our neural SDE fits the surface far better than Heston"

A vendor shows you a neural SDE calibrated to two years of your market's option prices. Its in-sample fit to the volatility surface is far tighter than your Heston calibration, and it produces beautiful simulated paths. They want you to price and hedge with it from next month. What do you do?

  1. Adopt it. A tighter fit means a better model.
    In-sample fit is what flexibility always buys. A model with many more knobs will fit two years of data more closely whether or not its dynamics generalise. That is a statement about the past sample, not about next month.
  2. Ask for out-of-sample tests: fit on one period, then price and hedge on later data it never saw, including a stressed regime, with realistic costs, compared against Heston.
    This is the same discipline you would apply to a simple moving-average rule, applied more urgently because there are far more parameters to overfit. If it still wins out of sample, the flexibility is earning its keep.
  3. Use it only to generate extra market scenarios for stress tests, not for pricing.
    A reasonable, lower-stakes use, and one of the few active practitioner uses today. It still needs checking that the scenarios are realistic, but a bad scenario costs less than a bad hedge.
  4. Reject it outright, because neural networks cannot be trusted in finance.
    "Neural" carries no evidence either way. Rejecting it on the label skips the test just as surely as adopting it on the label. The question is out-of-sample evidence.

The lesson. This is an illustrative situation, not a dated event. Neural SDEs are active research, with deep hedging (2019) and neural-SDE calibration (from around 2020) the main routes into finance and no desk standard yet. A close fit says nothing until it holds on data the model never saw.

What this now lets you do

Read a "neural SDE" or "neural SPDE" claim in a paper or a vendor pitch honestly — recognise exactly what it replaces (μ and σ, nothing else), what training it actually requires (a memory-aware adjoint method, not just ordinary backprop), and what it costs (data, reliability, interpretability) versus a named classical model. That's the whole seven-lesson SDE category, closed on its own honest frontier rather than a false finish line.
