Triple-Barrier Labelling, the Label That Matches the Trade

The obvious way to label training data — "was the return over the next fixed N bars positive?" — describes a trade nobody with real risk controls actually places. A real position exits at a stop-loss, a take-profit, or a time limit, whichever comes first. This is the final lesson of the category: it closes out the same question the previous lesson asked from a statistical angle — does an apparent edge reflect something real? — by asking it one layer earlier, at the label itself.

Section 2

The rule, drawn

Three barriers around one entry. Walk forward; the label is whichever is touched first.

Visualize it
The path wobbles for six bars, staying clear of both horizontal barriers, then crosses the upper one well before the vertical line is reached — the walk stops there, and the vertical barrier never comes into play.

Whichever of the three is touched first decides everything. Not "closest to" at some fixed point — first, in real time, walking the path exactly as a live position would experience it.

Section 5

Why the barriers scale with volatility, not a fixed distance

📏

A fixed pip width doesn't adapt

A 20-pip stop is comfortably wide in a calm regime and gets clipped by ordinary noise in a violent one — the same distance means something different depending on how much the market is actually moving.

📈

Volatility-scaled barriers stay comparable

Setting U and L as multiples of a volatility estimate — GARCH's conditional variance, or a range-based Garman-Klass/Yang-Zhang estimate — keeps each barrier's real difficulty roughly consistent across regimes.

🕐

Point-in-time, not peeked

The volatility estimate must use only information available at or before entry — the exact same point-in-time discipline this curriculum's leakage lesson builds from scratch, applied here to where the barriers get placed.

Two lessons back, the GARCH conditional-variance framework and a family of range-based (open/high/low/close) volatility estimators were built for their own sake. Here they show up again as a direct, practical input: whatever the barrier width should be today depends on today's volatility estimate, not on a number chosen once and left alone.
Section 6

Meta-labelling: a second, separate idea

Not "how do I label a trade" — "given that a signal already fired, should I take it, and how big?"

Primary model — direction only

Some rule or model decides long, short, or no signal. It can be a simple technical rule, a hand-built heuristic, or even a genuinely weak, noisy model — it does not need to be profitable on its own.

Secondary (meta) model — confidence only

Trained only on instances where the primary signal fired, using the same triple-barrier labels. Its one output: the probability this specific fired instance wins — used to filter or size, never to pick direction.

A mediocre stock-picking friend, right about 52% of the time, isn't worth trusting blindly. But if they're right far more often when they sound confident, you don't need them to become a better picker — you just start listening only when they sound sure. The friend is the primary signal; learning when to trust their confidence is the meta-model.

Section 9

Where this quietly goes wrong

Look-ahead hides in the volatility estimate

The path-walking step is inherently safe — it only ever uses real future prices exactly as a live position would experience them. The risk sits entirely upstream: a barrier width computed from a full-sample or centered volatility estimate leaks knowledge of how volatile the future actually turned out to be into where the barriers get placed today.

Meta-labelling cannot manufacture alpha

If a primary signal's fired instances are, in truth, no better than random, there is no real win/lose structure in them for a meta-model to find. A meta-model can still report an improved in-sample win rate at some threshold — with a small sample, some split that looks like it separates winners almost always exists — but that split fits noise, and it will not survive genuine out-of-sample testing.

Section 10

Verified against this repo's actual code

Not just similar vocabulary — the real thing

pylego/barrier_race.py's _first_touch() is a genuine upper/lower/vertical triple-barrier walker: a running high/low race between a take-profit price and a stop-loss price, falling back to a 'timeout' at max_bars_ahead — reused by AnalogML/ml_walkforward.py, the Volatility Bot's check_barriers(), and the range-line engines. bot/modules/ml_confidence.py is a genuine two-stage architecture: direction is inherited from upstream primary modules, while a separately-trained XGBoost + LightGBM ensemble (trained on closed-trade outcome_hit_tp/outcome_hit_sl columns) predicts a win-probability. One honest difference from the textbook version: this module only ever scales size down below a 0.5 probability — it never blocks a trade outright the way a classic meta-label filter can.

Section 11

Closing this category: one question, two angles

The previous lesson asked whether an apparent backtested edge, once you account for having tried many strategies, actually reflects something real rather than the best-looking result of a big enough search. This lesson asked whether the training label a model learned from ever described a real, tradable payoff to begin with. Both exist because a backtest can look convincing for reasons that have nothing to do with a genuine, persistent relationship — one from testing too many things, the other from measuring the wrong thing.
Section 12

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Why can a fixed-horizon label disagree with what a real position actually earned?

The fixed-horizon rule only checks price at one fixed future point; a real position with risk controls can already be closed, profitably or not, well before that point.
Question 2

What decides the triple-barrier label?

It's a genuine race in real time — the first barrier actually touched wins, not a comparison made at some fixed later point.
Question 3

Why scale the upper/lower barrier widths to a volatility estimate instead of a fixed pip distance?

The same 20-pip stop is trivially touched by noise in a violent regime and unrealistically wide in a calm one — volatility-scaling keeps the barrier's real difficulty roughly consistent.
Question 4

In meta-labelling, what does the secondary (meta) model actually predict?

Direction is already decided by the primary model; the meta-model's only job is a confidence estimate on instances the primary signal already flagged.
Question 5

If a primary signal's fired instances are, in truth, no better than random, can a meta-model still produce a genuinely profitable filtered subset out-of-sample?

An in-sample split that looks like it separates winners can usually be found even in pure noise, but it fits the noise realization, not a real relationship — it won't survive out-of-sample testing.
Section 13

What to carry forward

01

A fixed-horizon label describes a trade nobody with real risk controls actually places — a real position exits at whichever of a stop-loss, take-profit, or time limit comes first.

02

Triple-barrier labelling walks the real forward path and labels by whichever of an upper, lower, or vertical barrier is touched first.

03

Barrier widths scale with a point-in-time volatility estimate, so the same barrier logic adapts across calm and violent regimes.

04

Meta-labelling separates direction (a primary model, which can be weak) from confidence (a secondary model, trained on the same triple-barrier labels restricted to fired instances).

05

Filtering by meta-probability raises precision on trades taken, at the cost of taking fewer of them — and it can never manufacture alpha from a primary signal with zero real information content.

What this now lets you do

Recognize when a label built on a fixed lookahead window is quietly misdescribing the payoff a real strategy would actually realize. Read a two-stage primary/secondary signal architecture for what it actually is — a direction call and a separately-trained confidence filter — rather than one model doing both jobs at once. Treat a meta-model's apparent improvement with the same out-of-sample scrutiny as any other backtested claim.
