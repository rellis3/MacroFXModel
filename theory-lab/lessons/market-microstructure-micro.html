Market Microstructure 1 · Market Microstructure

The Bid-Ask Spread, Underneath the Quote

A market maker facing an anonymous order never knows whether it comes from someone who knows something they don't, or someone who simply needs to trade — and that single piece of uncertainty is the formal origin of both the bid-ask spread and the fact that trading a bigger size moves the price against you. This deck walks through two results from 1985 — Glosten-Milgrom and Kyle's Lambda — that turn "spreads exist" and "slippage gets worse with size" from folklore into two clean, derivable formulas.

The whole idea
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

Why is there a spread at all? Is it just the market maker's fee?

Not mostly. It is self-defence. The market maker can't see who is sending each order. Most are noise traders, who trade for reasons that have nothing to do with value. Some are informed, and they only trade when the price is wrong.

So against the informed ones, the market maker always loses.

Exactly. So they quote two prices, a bid below their best guess and an ask above it. What they earn from noise traders crossing that gap pays for what they lose to the informed ones.

How wide does the gap need to be?

Glosten and Milgrom worked it out in 1985. Say the true value is either a hundred and one or ninety-nine. If thirty percent of orders are informed, a buy order is evidence that the value is high. The break-even quotes come out at ninety-nine seventy and a hundred thirty. A spread of sixty cents.

And if more of the flow is informed?

At sixty percent informed, the spread doubles to a dollar twenty, and the midpoint stays at a hundred. The spread is the informed share times the uncertainty about value. Nothing more mysterious than that.

That's one order. What about size?

That's Kyle, the same year. The price moves in proportion to the size of the order. How steeply depends on how much ordinary two-way trading there is to hide your order in. In the lesson's example, a five-hundred unit order moves the price five dollars in a deep session. In a thin session with a fifth of the noise volume, the same order costs twenty-five.

So thin markets punish size.

And sometimes the depth vanishes completely. On the sixth of May, 2010, a fund started selling seventy-five thousand stock index futures through an algorithm keyed to volume, not price. Market makers couldn't tell why prices were falling, so they widened or left. Buy-side depth in the futures fell below one percent of the morning's level.

What did that look like in stocks?

In some books the only bids left were placeholder quotes at a penny. Market sell orders hit them. Accenture traded at one cent, and more than twenty thousand trades were cancelled afterwards. The Dow fell nearly a thousand points and recovered most of it within about twenty minutes.

So what do I actually do differently?

Treat the spread and impact as real costs that change with the hour. Slice big orders. Size down in thin windows. And never send a market or stop order into a book you can't see.

One line?

The spread is the price of not knowing who you're trading with, and impact is how much of your size the market can hide.

Section 1

The market maker's dilemma

Someone submits an order to buy from you. You have no idea who they are, or why.

Maybe they're a corporate treasury converting export revenue — a reason completely unrelated to whether the "true" value is about to move. Or maybe they know the fair price is higher than where you're quoting it. Quote one "best guess" price and trade with anyone who shows up, and you'll do fine against the first kind — but you'll systematically lose against the second, because they only trade the side that's bad for you.
Noise (liquidity) trader
  • Trades for reasons unrelated to fundamental value
  • Buys or sells with equal probability, whatever the "truth" is
  • A market maker wins as often as they lose against this flow
Informed trader
  • Knows the true value V exactly
  • Always trades the direction that's profitable for them
  • By construction, only shows up on the side that's bad for the market maker

The fix: don't quote a single price. Quote a bid (where you buy) below your best guess, and an ask (where you sell) above it — far enough apart that profits from noise traders passing through the gap cover the losses expected from informed traders. The width of that gap isn't an arbitrary fee. It's calibrated to survive adverse selection.

Section 2

Setting up Glosten-Milgrom

One asset, one true value the market maker doesn't know, one number that drives everything.

V

A true value, unknown to the market maker

V takes one of two values with equal prior probability: high (V_H) or low (V_L), with V_H > V_L. Orders arrive one at a time, each a buy or a sell.

α

The informed-trading share

Any given order is informed with probability α (they know V exactly, and always trade the profitable direction) or noise with probability 1−α (they trade a coin flip regardless of V).

0

Zero expected profit

Free entry from competing market makers drives expected profit on every quote to exactly zero. That condition is what pins down the bid and the ask.

E

"Best guess, given the evidence"

Each quote must equal the market maker's best updated estimate of V, conditional on having just seen a buy (ask) or a sell (bid) — a job for Bayes' theorem.

Section 3

A trade is itself evidence

Even with zero public news, watching what people do quietly shifts the best guess. See why.

Try it
P(buy | V_H)
=
(1 + α) / 2
A buy happens either because an informed trader showed up (probability α, and they always buy here) or a noise trader happened to buy (probability (1−α)×½). Both paths add up.
P(buy | V_L)
=
(1 − α) / 2
An informed trader would never buy when they know the asset is overvalued — so a buy here can only come from a noise trader who happened to buy.
Prior 50%
→
Posterior (1 + α) / 2
Bayes' theorem converts the asymmetry above into an updated belief. At α = 0 there's no asymmetry at all — a buy is uninformative, and the posterior stays at 50%. The larger α is, the more a single buy shifts the belief toward V_H.
This is the formal version of "prices move on trades even without public news" — the trade itself is the news, filtered through how much of the flow the market maker believes is informed.
Section 4

From posterior to quoted spread

Plug the posterior into the zero-expected-profit condition, and almost everything cancels.

The derivation, boiled down
Ask
=
E[V | buy]
Bid
=
E[V | sell]
Spread = Ask − Bid
=
α · (V_H − V_L)
The spread is exactly the informed-trading share times the total fundamental-value uncertainty. Nothing arbitrary about it — it's the exact gap a competitive market maker needs to break even.
A detail worth sitting with

The midpoint of the bid and ask, (Ask + Bid)/2, always works out to (V_H + V_L)/2 — the unconditional prior mean, completely unaffected by α. The market maker isn't shading their central guess up or down as informed risk rises; they're widening symmetrically around it.

Section 7

Kyle's Lambda: the same logic, for size

Glosten-Milgrom prices one indivisible order. Real order flow has a size — and size should cost more.

Q

Signed order flow

Positive for net buying, negative for net selling, bigger in magnitude for a bigger order or a bigger net imbalance.

Δp

Price impact

How far the price moves as a direct result of that order flow: Δp = λ · Q — Kyle's headline linear equation.

λ

Kyle's Lambda

The price-impact coefficient — price moved per unit of order flow. This is the object the whole model exists to explain.

1/λ

Market depth

Kyle's own term for the inverse of λ — literally how much order flow it takes to move the price by one unit.

Imagine dropping stones into two ponds. A small pond ripples a lot from just one stone — there's not much water to absorb the splash. A big lake barely reacts. Order size is the stone, price impact is the ripple, and λ is how rippled a particular pond gets per stone. λ = σ_v / (2σ_u): it rises with fundamental-value uncertainty (σ_v — more for an insider to know) and falls with noise-trading depth (σ_u — more ordinary volume dilutes the informed share).
Section 8

Price impact vs. order size

Same 500-unit order, two liquidity regimes. Nothing about the order changed — only the depth it landed in.

Both lines pass through the origin — no order, no impact — and both are straight, which is Kyle's linear assumption: impact scales with size, not a step function. The identical 500-unit order costs $5 in the deep market and $25 in the thin one — a five-fold gap purely from a fifth as much noise-trading depth to absorb it into.

Where this needs qualifying

Real markets deviate from perfect linearity at very large sizes — empirical microstructure research finds impact grows somewhat sub-linearly, closer to a square-root law, for the biggest orders. Kyle's straight line is the tractable first-order approximation, not a claim that the line never bends.

Section 10

How this plays out on a desk

Nobody solves a Bayesian update or a fixed-point equilibrium before placing an order — but both models motivate real habits.

📅

Widen quotes around scheduled events

A market maker quoting ahead of a rate decision or jobs report widens mechanically because they know α is about to spike.

✂️

Slice large orders over time

TWAP, VWAP, and implementation-shortfall schedules exist specifically to avoid dumping one large Q at once, since Δp = λQ says a single big clip costs more than the same size spread across many smaller ones.

📐

Estimate λ empirically

Run the regression the formula suggests, Δp_t = λ·Q_t + ε_t, on real trade or bar data, rather than relying on a textbook constant.

A retail trader places a moderately large market order right at the thin low-liquidity transition between the Asia and London sessions. The fill comes back meaningfully worse than the quoted price. That's not a broken platform or bad luck — it's λ = σ_v/(2σ_u) running high because σ_u, the depth of ordinary liquidity trading, has collapsed. The practical takeaway isn't "avoid the market" — it's size down, or avoid market/stop orders, during known thin-liquidity windows.
Factual grounding — checked against this repo's actual code

The full lesson (and this project's own strategy checklist in CLAUDE.md) describes cost modeling as "slippage on stop/breakout entries" specifically — a targeted, entry-type-aware friction that sounds like it's already implementing a Kyle's-Lambda-style, higher-cost-for-riskier-fills idea. Checking the actual backtester, bot/backtest.py, shows that isn't quite what runs today: its apply_costs() function charges the flat --slip-pips value on every replayed trade, not just stop/breakout ones, because "the journal records carry no entry-type field... slippage is charged on ALL trades (stop entries cannot be isolated)" — the code calls this choice conservative, and says so in its own output. So the differentiated-by-entry-type rule is a stated design intent, not yet a distinction the code can draw. More broadly: nowhere in this repo does any cost model scale with order size or with a measured λ — both the spread and the slippage applied in backtest.py are flat, pip-based constants per pair. The theoretical case this lesson makes for a size- and liquidity-scaled cost model is sound; it isn't yet implemented as such.

Section 11

Where these models get misread

Common mistake
  • Treating λ as a fixed constant of an instrument
  • Treating the whole quoted spread as pure adverse-selection premium
  • Assuming Δp = λQ holds exactly at any size
  • Reading a wide spread as proof informed traders are active right now
More accurate view
  • λ depends on time-varying σ_v and σ_u — low at London/NY overlap, high at a thin session transition
  • Real spreads also bake in order-processing cost and inventory-risk compensation (Ho-Stoll-style models, outside this lesson)
  • Empirical impact grows sub-linearly at very large size, closer to a square-root law
  • A wide spread reflects the market maker's belief about α, which can be wrong or stale

One more distinction worth keeping straight: the spread is a roughly fixed, per-trade cost you pay once crossing bid to ask; price impact scales with the size of the order itself. A small retail order mostly pays the spread — a large institutional order can pay far more in impact than in spread, which is exactly why slicing algorithms exist to manage λQ, not the spread.

Section 12 · It actually happened

6 May 2010: the market makers left

It actually happened

At 2:32 pm Eastern, on an already nervous day, a mutual fund complex began selling 75,000 E-mini S&P 500 contracts (about $4.1 billion) through an algorithm keyed to volume, not price. Intermediaries bought, then passed the contracts back and forth ("hot potato"). E-mini buy depth fell to about $58 million, under 1% of the morning's. In stocks, many liquidity providers paused, unsure their data was right. The only orders left were stub quotes: Accenture traded at one cent. The Dow fell nearly 1,000 points, then recovered most of it within about twenty minutes. Over 20,000 trades more than 60% off their 2:40 pm prices were cancelled.

Section 13

Where the textbook breaks, and the desk's verdict

Where the textbook version breaks

Glosten-Milgrom's market maker always quotes; Kyle's σ_u is a fixed amount of noise trading. In May 2010 neither held. Market makers can leave, and the depth that sets λ = σ_v/(2σ_u) disappears exactly when a big one-sided order needs it. With nothing between the last real bid and a one-cent stub, impact is a jump, not the straight line Δp = λQ.

Desk test: held up execution-gate: the spread is a cost, not a rounding error. Above a spread/ATR of 0.15, zero of 30 strategy × timeframe cells were profitable on this desk's data. Cost outruns edge as the timeframe speeds up. This is the ordinary quoted spread on normal days, not size impact or a crash-day withdrawal.
Section 14

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

With V_H = 101, V_L = 99, and α = 0.4, what is the Glosten-Milgrom spread?

Spread = α(V_H − V_L) = 0.4 × (101 − 99) = 0.4 × 2 = 0.80.
Question 2

Why does a single buy order shift the market maker's belief about V toward V_H, even with zero public news?

P(buy | V_H) = (1+α)/2 exceeds P(buy | V_L) = (1−α)/2 whenever α > 0 — the asymmetry is entirely caused by informed traders always trading the profitable direction. Bayes' theorem turns that asymmetric likelihood into a shifted posterior, no public news required.
Question 3

Holding fundamental-value uncertainty σ_v fixed, why is Kyle's Lambda higher in a thin market?

Small σ_u means little ordinary noise-trading volume to dilute or absorb any given order, so the market maker must price a bigger concession per unit of flow. Kyle's own term for 1/λ is market depth — small (shallow) exactly when λ is large.
Question 4

A 500-unit order costs $5 of impact when σ_u = 100 and σ_v = 2. What does it cost when σ_u drops to 20, everything else unchanged?

λ = σ_v/(2σ_u): at σ_u=100, λ=0.01; at σ_u=20 (a fifth as much), λ=0.05 — five times larger. Impact is λ×Q, so the same 500-unit order costs 5× more: $25 instead of $5.
Question 5

According to this repo's actual bot/backtest.py code (not the aspirational house rule), how is the extra --slip-pips slippage currently applied?

apply_costs() in bot/backtest.py charges slip_pips on every trade with a usable pnl_r, explicitly because the journal replay records carry no entry-type field. The differentiated, stop/breakout-only rule described in CLAUDE.md's strategy checklist is a design intent the current backtester doesn't yet implement — and no code in this repo scales cost with order size the way Kyle's Lambda would prescribe.
Your call
Scenario drill · 6 May 2010

The market makers are leaving, and you need to sell

It is a little after 2:40 pm Eastern. US stocks have been falling all afternoon, and a large sell programme in E-mini futures is running. Quotes in the stock you hold are thinning out fast as liquidity providers pause or pull out, and your risk limit says the position has to be cut. How do you sell?

  1. Send one market sell order for the whole position and get out now.
    A market order takes whatever bids are left. With the real buyers gone, the impact line is close to vertical: in some books the only bids left were stub quotes at a penny, and market sell orders hit them.
  2. Place a limit order at a price you can accept, or stand aside until two-way quotes return, and accept some delay.
    You stop demanding immediacy at the exact moment it is most expensive. Glosten-Milgrom and Kyle both say the cost of trading now is set by depth and suspected information, and both just went to extremes.
  3. Break the position into small market orders spread over the next few minutes.
    Slicing is the right habit on a normal day, because each clip lets the book refill. Here each small market order can still fall through an empty book to a stub quote, so it helps far less than usual.
  4. Leave a stop-loss below the market and let it fire if prices keep falling.
    A triggered stop becomes a market order, sent automatically into the thinnest book of the day. It turns a falling price into a fill at whatever is left.

What happened. At 2:45:28 the exchange paused E-mini trading for five seconds; sellers thinned out, buyers came in, and prices started to recover. In individual stocks, market sell orders hit stub quotes: Accenture traded at one cent, and more than 20,000 trades in over 300 securities that executed more than 60% away from their 2:40 pm prices were cancelled. The Dow fell nearly 1,000 points and recovered most of it within about twenty minutes. The SEC then banned stub quotes and introduced single-stock circuit breakers.

Section 15

What to carry forward

01

A spread isn't a fee — it's compensation for adverse selection. Spread = α(V_H − V_L), the exact gap a competitive market maker needs to break even.

02

A trade is itself Bayesian evidence about V — the market maker's belief shifts on order flow alone, with zero public news required.

03

Zero-expected-profit pins both quotes to conditional expectations of V — the ask given a buy, the bid given a sell — and the midpoint stays fixed regardless of α.

04

Kyle's Lambda, Δp = λQ with λ = σ_v/(2σ_u), extends the same logic to order size: bigger orders cost more, and the rate depends on available liquidity.

05

Thin markets mean high λ — the identical order can cost five times more when noise-trading depth collapses to a fifth.

06

Neither model is the whole cost story — inventory-risk spread components and sub-linear real-world impact both sit outside what's derived here.

07

Liquidity isn't a constant: on 6 May 2010 market makers left and stocks traded at stub quotes. On normal days the spread alone already kills short-horizon ideas above spread/ATR 0.15 (execution-gate, Validated: 0 of 30 cells).

What this now lets you do

Reason about why spreads mechanically widen ahead of scheduled news, and why slicing a large order over time reduces its total cost, instead of treating both as vague trading folklore. Recognize a thin-liquidity session transition as a formally high-λ regime, not just "it always seems to fill worse around then." Estimate your own λ from real fill data — Δp_t = λQ_t + ε_t — rather than assuming one flat slippage number applies at every hour of the day.
