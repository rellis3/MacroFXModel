Order Flow Imbalance & the Microprice

Every method earlier in this category reconstructs something about trading from historical data after the fact — who was probably the aggressor, how much of the spread was adverse selection, how much of the day's volume was informed. This lesson closes the category by building the two signals a live electronic desk actually watches update by update, tick by tick, right now.

TL;DR
  • Order Flow Imbalance (OFI), following Cont, Kukanov & Stoikov (2014), is built directly from changes to the best bid and ask price and size between consecutive book updates — not from the sign of executed trades. It captures net buying or selling pressure building up at the top of the book, a genuinely different and often more immediately predictive signal than watching the last few trades' signs.
  • The microprice improves on the naive midpoint \((P_b+P_a)/2\) by weighting each side's price by the opposite side's resting size: \(P_{\text{micro}} = \frac{P_bQ_a + P_aQ_b}{Q_b+Q_a}\). Heavier resting size on one side signals more supply (or demand) waiting to be consumed there, which pulls the fair-value estimate away from that side and toward the lighter, more easily-moved side.
  • Queue imbalance, \(QI = \frac{Q_b-Q_a}{Q_b+Q_a}\), is the simpler quantity underneath the microprice's logic — and algebraically, \(P_{\text{micro}} = M + \tfrac{S}{2}\cdot QI\), the naive midpoint plus half the spread times the queue imbalance. The two concepts aren't just related in spirit; one is a rearrangement of the other.
  • A worked four-snapshot toy example below shows the microprice pulling further and further from the naive midpoint as a book's queue imbalance builds from 0 to 0.5 — and then shows the price actually ticking in the direction that imbalance predicted, exactly as the theory says it should.
  • This closes the category's arc: trade classification signs individual trades from public quotes; Roll and Glosten-Milgrom explain and estimate why a spread exists at all; PIN estimates how much of a day's trading is informed. OFI and the microprice are the real-time, order-book-level signals that translate all of that historical-statistical machinery into something a live desk can watch move, update by update.
Section 01

Why this matters

Limit Order Books & Queue Dynamics already did the structural work this lesson leans on directly: a book is two ladders of price levels, the best bid \(P_b\) and best ask \(P_a\) are the touch prices, and \(Q_b\) and \(Q_a\) are the sizes resting at those exact prices. Nothing here reintroduces that structure — this lesson takes it as given and asks a narrower, more urgent question: given the book's current state and how it just changed, what does that say about where the price is likely to go in the next few ticks?

That question is deliberately different from everything else in this category. Trade classification, Roll's estimator, Glosten-Milgrom, and PIN all work primarily from historical trade prints — a record of what already executed, summarized statistically after the fact. A market maker adjusting quotes twenty times a second cannot wait for statistical inference over a day's worth of trades; they need a number that updates on every single book event and says, right now, which way the pressure is leaning. Order Flow Imbalance and the microprice are exactly that number — built not from trades at all, but from the book's own bid/ask price and size updates, the same raw material the previous lesson used to describe queue position and walking the book.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Watching the two lines at the checkout, not just who already got rung up

Picture two adjacent checkout lines at the deli counter from the earlier lesson — one for buyers, one for sellers, resting at the best price on each side. A trade-classification approach is a security camera reviewing footage of people who already got served, guessing after the fact who seemed eager. OFI is a completely different vantage point: someone standing at the front of the store, watching the two lines themselves grow and shrink, minute to minute. If the buyer line keeps growing while the seller line keeps getting pulled shorter — people leaving before they're even served — that is information about pressure building up, visible immediately, well before any of those waiting people actually transact.

The microprice asks a related but distinct question: given how long each line currently is, right now, which way is the counter more likely to move its price next? If the seller line is short and thinning, there is less resistance on that side — a few more impatient buyers could exhaust it and push the price up before the counter can restock. The naive midpoint between the two lines' posted prices ignores this entirely; the microprice leans the estimate toward whichever side looks easier to blow through.

Section 02

The math, step by step

1. Order Flow Imbalance — pressure from book updates, not trade signs

Cont, Kukanov & Stoikov (2014) define OFI event by event, comparing each new book update (indexed \(n\)) to the one immediately before it (\(n-1\)). Each side contributes a signed quantity capturing whether that update added resting interest, removed it, or improved the price outright:

\[ e^b_n = \begin{cases} Q^b_n & P^b_n > P^b_{n-1}\quad\text{(bid price improved up)} \\[2pt] Q^b_n - Q^b_{n-1} & P^b_n = P^b_{n-1}\quad\text{(bid price unchanged, size moved)} \\[2pt] -Q^b_{n-1} & P^b_n < P^b_{n-1}\quad\text{(bid price dropped)} \end{cases} \qquad e^a_n = \begin{cases} Q^a_n & P^a_n < P^a_{n-1}\quad\text{(ask price improved down)} \\[2pt] Q^a_n - Q^a_{n-1} & P^a_n = P^a_{n-1}\quad\text{(ask price unchanged, size moved)} \\[2pt] -Q^a_{n-1} & P^a_n > P^a_{n-1}\quad\text{(ask price rose)} \end{cases} \]
e^b_nthe bid side's signed contribution at update n — positive when buy-side resting interest grows
e^a_nthe ask side's contribution, defined with mirrored signs so it also reads positive when it implies more buying pressure
P^b_n, Q^b_nbest bid price and size at update n; superscript a is the same for the ask side

A single update's OFI is \(OFI_n = e^b_n - e^a_n\); summing \(OFI_n\) over every update in a short window gives OFI for that window. The subtraction is what makes the ask side's logic mirror the bid side's correctly: an ask size decrease at an unchanged price gives \(e^a_n < 0\), and subtracting a negative number adds to OFI — exactly right, since resting sell interest disappearing (consumed or pulled) is itself a sign of building buy-side pressure, even though no trade against the bid ever happened.

1

Bid price improves up, or its size grows

More resting buy interest, or a willingness to buy at a better price than a moment ago — both read as positive buy-side pressure.

2

Bid size shrinks, or its price drops

Resting buy interest is being pulled or consumed without being replaced — negative pressure.

3

Ask size shrinks, or its price drops

Resting sell interest thinning out or getting more aggressive toward buyers — the mirror case, and it also reads as positive buy-side pressure once the sign flip from \(OFI_n = e^b_n - e^a_n\) is applied.

4

Sum over a short window

Adding \(OFI_n\) across every update in, say, the last second or the last 50 events gives one number: net order-book pressure over that window, positive for net buying pressure, negative for net selling pressure.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine watching a tug-of-war rope, not the two teams' faces. You don't need to know who looks more determined (that's trade classification, guessing from behavior after something happened) — you just watch which way the rope is actually sliding, right now, as people join or let go of each side. OFI is watching the rope slide.

Why this beats "just look at the last few trade signs." A trade only happens when a market order crosses the spread — it is a lagging signal, visible only after an aggressor already acted. OFI is built from every book update, including the far more numerous events where nothing traded at all: a limit order added, cancelled, or improved. Pressure frequently builds up at the top of the book — size quietly accumulating on the bid, size quietly draining from the ask — well before it shows up as a run of same-signed trades. That head start is exactly why OFI is a genuinely different, often more immediately predictive signal for very short-term price moves.

2. The microprice — weighting price by the opposite side's size

The naive midpoint treats the book as if only the two touch prices mattered: \(M = (P_b+P_a)/2\), with no reference to how much size backs each side. The microprice (Stoikov, 2018, formalizing an idea long used informally on trading desks) fixes this by weighting each side's price by the opposite side's resting size:

\[ P_{\text{micro}} = \frac{P_b\,Q_a + P_a\,Q_b}{Q_b + Q_a} \]
P_b, P_abest bid and ask prices — unchanged from the LOB lesson's notation
Q_b, Q_aresting size at the best bid and best ask
P_b·Q_a, P_a·Q_bcross-weighting — the bid price is weighted by the ask's size, and vice versa

The cross-weighting is the entire idea, and it is worth deriving the intuition rather than just accepting the formula. Suppose the ask side has far more resting size than the bid (\(Q_a \gg Q_b\)) — a thick wall of supply sitting just above the market, and comparatively little demand resting just below it. All else equal, that lopsidedness makes it more likely the price gets pulled or pushed down, toward the bid, over the next few ticks: there simply isn't much resting size that has to be exhausted before an incoming seller reaches a favorable price, while an incoming buyer has to work through a much larger wall on the other side. The microprice formula encodes exactly this: because \(Q_a\) is large, \(P_b\) — the price the market is more likely to move toward — gets the large weight, while \(P_a\) gets weighted by the comparatively small \(Q_b\). The heavier side's price is down-weighted; the lighter, more easily-moved side's price dominates the average. That is why the microprice sits closer to whichever side has less resting size backing it — the side under less resistance to being reached.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

If one team in tug-of-war has ten people holding the rope and the other has two, you don't bet the rope stays exactly in the middle — you bet it drifts toward the two-person side, because there's less resistance there. The microprice is that bet, written as a formula: it leans the "fair value" guess toward whichever side has less weight holding it in place.

3. Queue imbalance, and the algebra connecting it to the microprice

Queue imbalance is the simpler, closely related quantity underneath the microprice's logic — a normalized measure of exactly how lopsided the top of book currently is:

\[ QI = \frac{Q_b - Q_a}{Q_b + Q_a} \]

\(QI\) ranges from \(-1\) (every unit of resting size sits on the ask — maximally seller-heavy) to \(+1\) (every unit sits on the bid — maximally buyer-heavy), with \(QI=0\) meaning a perfectly balanced book. The connection to the microprice isn't just qualitative — it can be derived exactly. Write the bid and ask prices in terms of the midpoint \(M\) and half-spread \(h = (P_a - P_b)/2\), so \(P_b = M - h\) and \(P_a = M + h\), and substitute directly into the microprice formula:

\[ P_{\text{micro}} = \frac{(M-h)Q_a + (M+h)Q_b}{Q_b+Q_a} = \frac{M(Q_a+Q_b) + h(Q_b - Q_a)}{Q_b+Q_a} = M + h\cdot\frac{Q_b-Q_a}{Q_b+Q_a} \]
M(Q_a+Q_b)the midpoint term factors out completely once M is common to both weighted prices
h(Q_b−Q_a)everything else collapses to the half-spread times exactly the numerator of QI
÷(Q_b+Q_a)dividing through leaves QI's own definition sitting inside the result

Which gives the clean identity used throughout the rest of this lesson:

\[ \boxed{P_{\text{micro}} = M + \frac{S}{2}\cdot QI}\qquad\big(S = P_a - P_b,\ \text{the spread}\big) \]

The microprice is exactly the naive midpoint, nudged by half the spread in the direction the queue imbalance points. A perfectly balanced book (\(QI=0\)) makes the microprice collapse to the plain midpoint — no adjustment, because there's no lopsidedness to correct for. A bid-heavy book (\(QI>0\)) pulls the microprice up, toward the ask — consistent with the intuition above: heavy resting size on the bid signals demand that could push price up toward the thinner ask side. The worked example below confirms this identity numerically, to the sixth decimal place, on an actual toy order book.

\(P_b, P_a\)Best bid / best ask price — same notation as the Limit Order Books lesson.
\(Q_b, Q_a\)Resting size at the best bid / best ask.
\(M\)Naive midpoint, \((P_b+P_a)/2\).
\(S, h\)Spread \(P_a-P_b\), and half-spread \(h=S/2\).
\(QI\)Queue imbalance, \((Q_b-Q_a)/(Q_b+Q_a)\) — ranges from \(-1\) to \(+1\).
\(P_{\text{micro}}\)The microprice — the size-weighted fair-value estimate, \(= M + h\cdot QI\).
\(e^b_n, e^a_n\)Each side's signed contribution to OFI at book update \(n\).
\(OFI_n\)Net order-book pressure at update \(n\), \(=e^b_n-e^a_n\); summed over a window for that window's OFI.

1

n = 1 → n = 2: the bid quietly builds size

Neither price moves. The bid's size grows from 400 to 600 (\(e^b_2 = 600-400=200\)); the ask's size is unchanged (\(e^a_2 = 400-400=0\)). \(OFI_2 = 200 - 0 = 200\): mild buy-side pressure, invisible to a naive midpoint (which hasn't moved at all) but already visible in \(QI\) rising to 0.2 and the microprice edging up to 1.100460.

2

n = 2 → n = 3: the ask thins out too

Bid size keeps growing, 600 → 900 (\(e^b_3 = 300\)). Now the ask side loses size, 400 → 300 (\(e^a_3 = 300-400=-100\)). \(OFI_3 = 300 - (-100) = 400\): the ask thinning out adds to buy-side pressure exactly as the mirrored-sign construction says it should. \(QI\) reaches 0.5 — the bid now holds three units of resting size for every one on the ask — and the microprice has pulled to 1.100475, a full quarter-pip above the still-unmoved 1.100450 midpoint.

3

n = 3 → n = 4: the price actually moves — up, as predicted

Both touch prices tick up by one pip: the old bid (1.10040) is gone, replaced by a new best bid at 1.10050 with 500 resting; the old ask (1.10050) is gone too, replaced by a new best ask at 1.10060 with 350 resting. Both price moves are price improvements from the perspective of the side that just moved, so \(e^b_4 = Q^b_4 = 500\) and, mirrored on the ask side, \(e^a_4 = -Q^a_3 = -300\). \(OFI_4 = 500-(-300) = 800\) — the largest single-update reading yet, confirming the move was consistent with, not contrary to, the pressure that had been building. The book was buyer-heavy for two straight updates, and the price it then moved to was up — exactly the direction the growing \(QI\) and the diverging microprice had been leaning toward.

Summed across the whole window, \(OFI_2+OFI_3+OFI_4 = 200+400+800=1{,}400\) — one number, built entirely from book updates with no trade prints involved, that was strongly positive throughout the entire buildup. This is a small, hand-built illustration of the mechanism, not a live market run — but every number in it is the actual output of the formulas above, checked against each other to six decimal places.

Real-world trading scenario

Skewing quotes before a single trade has told anyone anything

A market-making desk quotes both sides of a currency pair continuously, updating its own bid and ask every time the book it watches updates. If it only re-marks its quotes off the naive midpoint, it is implicitly assuming the book is always balanced — an assumption that is false almost constantly. Watch OFI turn strongly positive and \(QI\) climb toward 0.4–0.5, exactly as in the worked example above, and a desk running this logic will lean its own quotes up before the price actually moves: quoting a slightly higher bid and ask than the stale midpoint would suggest, both to avoid being picked off by an informed buyer about to lift the thin ask, and to capture some of the move the imbalance is telegraphing. This is the single most common practical use of both signals together — OFI as the early-warning pressure gauge, the microprice as the actual reference price the desk re-centers its own quoting around, tick by tick, well before a naive midpoint would ever budge.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

📊

Short-horizon price-move features

OFI and \(QI\), computed over rolling short windows, are common inputs to short-horizon (sub-second to a few-seconds) prediction models — genuinely different in character from the daily or intraday-bar features used elsewhere in a research pipeline, because they exist only at book-update resolution.

🧭

A truer reference price than the midpoint

Desks that mark P&L, decide whether a resting order is "at a good price," or benchmark execution quality often use the microprice instead of the naive midpoint specifically because it already accounts for the book's current lopsidedness, not just its two touch prices.

⏱️

Timing aggressive orders

An execution algorithm deciding whether to rest passively or cross the spread right now can use OFI as a real-time signal for whether the book currently favors its side — crossing when the imbalance already favors the intended direction is cheaper than crossing into resistance.

🧩

A live complement to this category's historical tools

PIN and Glosten-Milgrom estimate, from history, how much of a market's trading is information-driven and why a spread compensates for it. OFI and the microprice don't replace that — they are the tick-by-tick instrument a desk actually watches while those historical estimates sit in the background informing how aggressively to trade at all.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where OFI and the microprice quietly mislead

Treating displayed size as committed size. Just like the resting size discussed in the LOB lesson, the \(Q_b\) and \(Q_a\) feeding both formulas here can be cancelled an instant after being observed — "spoofing" (posting size with no intention of letting it trade) exploits exactly this, manufacturing a false \(QI\) reading to bait a reaction before pulling the order. A signal built entirely from displayed size inherits this vulnerability by construction.

Confusing OFI's sign with "who is right." A strongly positive OFI says buy-side pressure is building at the top of the book right now — it says nothing about whether that pressure reflects genuine new information (the PIN framing) or a large but uninformed order working through the book. Both this lesson's signal and PIN's estimate answer genuinely different questions and shouldn't be substituted for one another.

Extrapolating past the horizon the signal was built for. Both OFI and the microprice are constructed from, and are most informative over, very short horizons — the next few updates or ticks. Treating a snapshot of either as a multi-minute or multi-hour forecast is a category error; the underlying queue can refill, cancel, or reverse entirely within the same second the reading was taken.

Only ever looking at top-of-book. Both formulas here use only the single best level on each side. A book can be thin and lopsided at the touch while holding substantial resting size one or two levels back — the signals in this lesson say nothing about that deeper structure on their own, and desks that need it extend both ideas to multiple levels rather than treating level-1 as the whole story.

Closing the arc — five lessons, one desk's actual workflow

This is the last lesson in Market Microstructure Extensions, and it is worth naming plainly how the five lessons in this category actually fit together, because none of them was ever meant to stand alone:

1

Trade Classification

Signs individual historical trades as buyer- or seller-initiated when true intent was never recorded — the raw ingredient every later lesson in this category, and market microstructure research generally, is built on top of.

2–3

Roll & Glosten-Milgrom

Explain and estimate why a bid-ask spread exists at all, and how much of it compensates a market maker for the risk of trading against someone who knows more — from limited, historical price or trade data.

4

PIN

Takes the informed-vs-noise framing further and estimates a single number — the probability of informed trading — for how much of an instrument's overall trading, over some historical window, is information-driven.

5

OFI & the microprice (this lesson)

Takes everything the first four lessons say about historical trading behavior and asks the live-desk version of the same question: what does the book's own tick-by-tick updates say about pressure and fair value, right now, this instant.

The gap this closes is real: lessons 1 through 4 are the statistically rigorous, after-the-fact machinery a researcher uses to understand a market's microstructure from historical data. OFI and the microprice are the parts of that same intellectual tradition that actually get computed, live, on an electronic trading desk, updated on every single book event. Neither replaces the other — a desk that only watches OFI without ever having studied why spreads and informed trading exist in the first place is operating on intuition it can't explain; a researcher who only studies PIN and Glosten-Milgrom without knowing what a modern desk actually watches in real time is missing where all that theory is actually put to work.

Section 05

Key Takeaways

✓OFI is built from book updates, not trade prints. Each side contributes a signed value based on whether its price improved, held with a size change, or worsened — summed with mirrored signs so both sides consistently read as buy-side or sell-side pressure.
✓The microprice weights each side's price by the opposite side's size, \(P_{\text{micro}}=(P_bQ_a+P_aQ_b)/(Q_b+Q_a)\) — pulling the fair-value estimate toward whichever side has less resting size standing in its way.
✓Queue imbalance and the microprice are algebraically the same idea: \(P_{\text{micro}} = M + \tfrac{S}{2}\cdot QI\) — the naive midpoint, nudged by half the spread in the direction the book is lopsided.
⚠Both signals live and die on displayed size that can vanish an instant later. Spoofed or fleeting resting orders can manufacture a misleading reading in either formula.
⚠Both signals are short-horizon by construction. Treating a single reading as a forecast beyond the next few ticks or updates misuses what they were built to measure.
✓This closes the category's arc: lessons 1–4 explain and estimate spreads and informed trading from historical data; OFI and the microprice are the real-time, order-book-level signals a live desk actually watches, tick by tick.
Section 06

Self-test

Q1. OFI and trade-classification methods (like the tick rule or Lee-Ready, covered earlier in this category) both try to say something about buying versus selling pressure. What is the fundamental difference in what data each one uses?
Trade classification works from executed trade prints — it looks at trades that already happened and infers, after the fact, which side was the aggressor. OFI is built entirely from changes to the best bid/ask price and size between consecutive book updates — including the far more numerous updates where nothing traded at all (an order added, cancelled, or improved). OFI can therefore reflect pressure building up before any trade occurs; trade classification is inherently a lagging signal that only exists once a trade has already crossed the spread.
Q2. Why does a decrease in resting ask size (with the ask price unchanged) count as a positive contribution to OFI, even though nothing traded against the bid?
Resting sell interest disappearing — whether consumed by a trade or simply cancelled — means less supply is available at that price, which is itself a sign of effective buy-side pressure: it becomes easier for the price to move up because there is less resistance in the way. Formally, \(e^a_n = Q^a_n-Q^a_{n-1} < 0\) when ask size shrinks, and since \(OFI_n = e^b_n - e^a_n\), subtracting that negative number adds a positive amount to OFI — exactly capturing that a thinning ask is bullish pressure, not neutral.
Q3. Why does the microprice weight each side's price by the OPPOSITE side's resting size, rather than its own side's size?
Heavier resting size on one side represents more supply (if it's the ask) or demand (if it's the bid) sitting there waiting to be worked through — and all else equal, that makes it less likely the price moves toward that heavy side, since more size has to be consumed first. Weighting a side's price by the opposite side's size means a heavy bid pulls weight onto the ask price (making the average lean up, toward the ask), and a heavy ask pulls weight onto the bid price (making the average lean down, toward the bid) — in both cases, the estimate is pulled toward the lighter, more easily-moved side, which is exactly the intended behavior.
Q4. Using \(P_{\text{micro}} = M + \tfrac{S}{2}\cdot QI\): a book has a midpoint of 1.2050, a spread of 0.0004, and \(QI = -0.4\). What is the microprice, and which side is it leaning toward?
Half-spread \(h = 0.0004/2 = 0.0002\). \(P_{\text{micro}} = 1.2050 + 0.0002\times(-0.4) = 1.2050 - 0.00008 = 1.20492\). A negative \(QI\) means the ask side holds more resting size than the bid, so the microprice is pulled down, below the midpoint, toward the bid — the lighter side, exactly as the formula's derivation predicts.
Q5. In this lesson's worked example, the naive midpoint never moves between snapshots n=1 and n=3, while OFI stays strongly positive and QI climbs from 0 to 0.5. What actually happens at snapshot n=4, and why is it consistent with the earlier readings rather than contradicting them?
Both the best bid and best ask tick up by one pip (1.1004/1.1005 to 1.1005/1.1006), and OFI at that update is +800 — the largest single reading in the sequence, since both the bid's price improvement and the ask's price deterioration count as strongly positive contributions. This is consistent with, not contrary to, the earlier readings: OFI and QI had been signaling building buy-side pressure for two full updates before the price actually moved, and when it did move, it moved up — the exact direction that pressure had been pointing toward.
Q6. How does this lesson's content relate to PIN (lesson 4 of this category), rather than replacing it?
PIN estimates, from historical trade data over some window, roughly how much of an instrument's overall trading is driven by informed traders — a statistical, after-the-fact characterization of a market. OFI and the microprice answer a different, live question: given the book's current state and its most recent updates, which way is pressure leaning and where does fair value actually sit, right now. A high PIN reading is context that might make a desk trade more cautiously in general; OFI and the microprice are what that same desk actually watches update by update while doing so. Neither one substitutes for the other — they operate at different timescales answering different questions.

  • Álvaro Cartea, Sebastian Jaimungal & José Penalva, Algorithmic and High-Frequency Trading, Cambridge University Press, 2015 — the same book-structure reference cited in the Limit Order Books lesson, with further material on order-flow-based signals built directly on top of it.
