Bates Model: Stochastic Volatility + Jumps

Heston's stochastic variance can shape a smile reasonably well — until you get to short-dated options, where real markets show a skew far steeper than smooth diffusion alone can build in time. Bates (1996) staples a Merton-style jump term straight onto Heston's own price process. No new machinery gets derived here: two models this Theory Lab already covers in full, combined into one.

TL;DR
  • Bates (1996) is literally the union of two lessons already in this Theory Lab: Heston's stochastic-variance SDE for \(v_t\), plus Merton's compound-Poisson jump term \(dJ_t\), stapled onto the same price process. Nothing about either piece is re-derived here.
  • Why you need both. Pure Heston's stochastic vol diffuses too slowly to build the steep short-dated smile/skew real markets show; pure Merton jumps produce that steep short-dated tail behavior but have no mean-reverting machinery to explain why elevated volatility persists after a shock. Bates lets jumps handle short-dated tails and stochastic vol handle longer-dated shape and vol clustering.
  • Because the jump process is independent of the correlated Brownian pair driving price and variance, the two pieces' log-characteristic functions simply add: \(\varphi_{\text{Bates}}(u) = \varphi_{\text{Heston}}(u)\times\varphi_{\text{jump}}(u)\) — this is why Bates stays tractable enough for the exact same Carr-Madan/FFT machinery Heston alone uses.
  • The cost is parameters. Bates has 8 free parameters — Heston's \((\kappa,\theta,\xi,\rho,v_0)\) plus the jump trio \((\lambda,\mu_J,\sigma_J)\) — versus 5 or 3 alone. More flexibility to fit an observed surface, but a genuinely harder, less identified, more overfitting-prone calibration.
  • A live calculator below applies the standard compound-Poisson moment formulas and shows, numerically, why fixed jump parameters matter far more at short maturities: the jump share of total variance stays constant across horizons, but the jump contribution to skewness decays like \(1/\sqrt{T}\) and to excess kurtosis like \(1/T\) — the actual mechanism behind "jumps dominate the short end, stochastic vol dominates the long end."
  • Jump risk is not perfectly hedgeable by continuous delta-hedging the way pure diffusion risk is under Black-Scholes-style replication — an incomplete-markets subtlety flagged honestly here, without inventing a specific risk-premium formula this lesson isn't certain of.
Section 01

Why this matters

The Heston Model lesson ends by naming its own limitation honestly: no single stochastic-volatility model perfectly reproduces every observed smile at every expiry simultaneously, and it points forward to exactly the model this lesson covers. Here's the specific gap. Fit Heston's five parameters \((\kappa,\theta,\xi,\rho,v_0)\) to a long-dated slice of an implied-vol surface — six months, a year out — and the correlated stochastic-variance mechanism that lesson derives can produce a genuinely convincing smile and skew. Fit the same five parameters to a one-week slice of the same surface, and it typically can't get there: real short-dated FX and equity smiles are often dramatically steeper than anything Heston's diffusion, however correlated, can generate in that little time. \(v_t\) simply hasn't had enough time to wander far from \(v_0\), and \(\rho\)'s leverage effect needs the underlying's diffusion to actually accumulate a meaningful correlated co-movement — both mechanisms are continuous and gradual by construction, and a few trading days is not much runway for a continuous process to build a steep skew.

The Merton Jump-Diffusion lesson has the opposite problem from the opposite direction. A discontinuous jump can inject a large move in an instant, independent of how much calendar time has passed — which is exactly what a steep short-dated skew needs. But Merton's jump term has no memory: each jump is drawn independently, with no mechanism at all describing why a day's volatility should stay elevated after a jump lands rather than immediately reverting to the same flat \(\sigma\) as before. Real markets don't behave that way — the GARCH and Heston lessons both build entire arguments around exactly the opposite empirical fact, that volatility clusters and mean-reverts over calendar time following a shock. Pure Merton jump-diffusion has no machinery whatsoever for that persistence.

David Bates' 1996 paper is the direct response to both gaps at once, and it is not a new derivation — it is an explicit combination. Take Heston's two correlated SDEs exactly as that lesson leaves them. Take Merton's compound-Poisson jump term exactly as that lesson leaves it. Add the jump term onto Heston's price equation. That's the whole model specification. The genuinely new content in this lesson is not "how does stochastic volatility work" or "how does a jump term work" — both are settled, cross-linked, and not repeated here — it's how the two pieces divide the labor, why that division is the actual point of combining them, and what combining them costs in calibration difficulty.

💡
Plain-English intuition

A river with its own weather, plus an upstream dam

The Heston lesson's own analogy: volatility is "weather that has its own weather" — a slow-moving climate system of calm spells and turbulent spells, drifting and mean-reverting over time, tending to get stormier exactly when the river (price) is falling. The Merton lesson's own analogy: a calm river with an upstream dam that, every so often, releases a sudden burst of water with no warning — a genuine discontinuity, not a fast wiggle.

Bates is both analogies describing the same river at once. Most of the time, the river's level drifts with its own slow-moving weather system exactly as the Heston lesson describes — calm spells, turbulent spells, each one tending to spill more when the water's already dropping. And, independently of that weather, the dam upstream can still release without warning, on top of whatever the weather's doing that day. A dam release close to the current moment (a short-dated option) can move the level far more than the slow-moving weather system could have produced by itself in so little time; a dam release six months from now, averaged in with six months of ordinary weather variation, barely changes the overall picture. That's the entire complementarity this lesson exists to explain, told as one river instead of two separate ones.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Heston's model says volatility has its own slow weather. Merton's model says price can suddenly jump, like a surprise fire alarm. Bates just says: why not both? Most days it's the slow weather doing its thing, and every once in a while, completely separately, the fire alarm goes off too. A fire alarm going off THIS AFTERNOON changes your whole day a lot; a fire alarm that might go off sometime in the next six months barely changes what you'd plan for six months from now, because six months of ordinary weather swamps it. That's why the alarm (jumps) matters most for "what happens really soon" and the weather (stochastic vol) matters most for "what happens over the long run."

Section 02

The math: two lessons' equations, stapled together

Recall Heston's two correlated SDEs exactly as that lesson derives them — reproduced here without re-derivation, purely as the starting point:

\[ dS_t = \mu S_t\,dt + \sqrt{v_t}\,S_t\,dW_t^{1}, \qquad dv_t = \kappa(\theta - v_t)\,dt + \xi\sqrt{v_t}\,dW_t^{2}, \qquad \mathrm{Corr}(dW_t^{1}, dW_t^{2}) = \rho \]

And recall Merton's jump term exactly as that lesson derives it — a compound Poisson process built from a Poisson arrival clock \(N_t\) with intensity \(\lambda\), and independently-drawn jump sizes \(Y_i\) with \(\ln(1+Y_i)\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_J,\sigma_J^2)\):

\[ dJ_t = \left(\prod_{i=N_{t^-}+1}^{N_t}(1+Y_i)\right) - 1 \]

Bates' entire model specification is: take Heston's price SDE, and add Merton's jump term to its right-hand side, unchanged:

\[ \frac{dS_t}{S_{t^-}} = \mu\,dt + \sqrt{v_t}\,dW_t^{1} + dJ_t \]
√v_t dW_t¹Heston's diffusion term, unchanged — variance is still its own stochastic process
dJ_tMerton's jump term, unchanged — a separate, independent compound-Poisson process

Heston's variance SDE itself is left completely untouched — \(v_t\) still mean-reverts toward \(\theta\) at speed \(\kappa\) with its own CIR-type noise \(\xi\sqrt{v_t}\,dW_t^2\), and \(\rho\) still links that noise to the price Brownian motion \(dW_t^1\) exactly as before. Bates adds a third, independent source of randomness — the jump process \(J_t\) — on top of Heston's already-correlated pair, rather than modifying either of Heston's two SDEs in any way:

\[ dv_t = \kappa(\theta - v_t)\,dt + \xi\sqrt{v_t}\,dW_t^{2}, \qquad \mathrm{Corr}(dW_t^{1}, dW_t^{2}) = \rho \]
κ, θ, ξ, ρ, v₀
Heston's five, unchanged
Mean-reverting stochastic variance and its correlation with price — reused verbatim from that lesson
λ, μ_J, σ_J
Merton's three, unchanged
Jump intensity, and the mean/spread of the lognormal jump size — reused verbatim from that lesson
J_t independent of (W¹, W²)
The one new modeling assumption
The jump process is assumed independent of the correlated Brownian pair — see Section 03 for why this is the assumption that keeps the model tractable

Every symbol here already has a full entry in one of the two sibling lessons' symbol tables — nothing below is a new definition, only a pointer to where each one was actually derived:

\(S_t, \mu\)Price and drift — same role as in the Heston and Merton lessons alike.
\(v_t,\ \kappa,\ \theta,\ \xi,\ \rho,\ dW_t^{1},\ dW_t^{2}\)Instantaneous variance and its five parameters, plus the two correlated Brownian motions — all defined in full in the Heston Model lesson's own symbol table. Not redefined here.
\(N_t,\ \lambda,\ Y_i,\ \mu_J,\ \sigma_J,\ dJ_t\)The Poisson arrival process, its intensity, the lognormal jump size and its two parameters, and the compound-Poisson jump term itself — all defined in full in the Merton Jump-Diffusion lesson's own symbol table. Not redefined here.
\(J_t \perp (W_t^{1}, W_t^{2})\)The jump process is assumed independent of both Brownian motions — a jump's timing and size have nothing to do with the current level of \(v_t\) or with which way the diffusion happens to be moving that instant. This is the one genuinely new assumption Bates adds, and Section 03 explains why it's the specific assumption that keeps the model's characteristic function tractable.
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

If you already understand the Heston lesson's two equations and the Merton lesson's jump term separately, you already understand Bates — there is no third new equation to learn. The only genuinely new sentence is: "the fire alarm (jumps) is assumed to have nothing to do with today's weather (v_t) or which way the weather's currently blowing (ρ)." That's it.

Section 03

Why independence buys tractability: the characteristic function just multiplies

The FFT Option Pricing (Carr-Madan) lesson explains, and doesn't re-derive, why Heston's tractability rests entirely on having a known closed-form characteristic function \(\varphi_T(u)\) — the Fourier transform of the log-price distribution — which Carr-Madan's method then turns into an entire strip of option prices with a single FFT call. Bates needs the identical property to be pricing-tractable at all, and it's worth being explicit about exactly why adding a jump term doesn't break it.

A standard fact about independent random variables: the characteristic function of a sum of two independent random variables is the product of their individual characteristic functions — equivalently, their log-characteristic functions simply add. Bates' log-price at time \(T\) is, informally, "the Heston part" (drift plus correlated diffusion) plus "the jump part" (the accumulated compound Poisson jumps), and because Section 02 assumed the jump process is independent of the Heston diffusion, that sum's characteristic function factors cleanly:

\[ \varphi_{\text{Bates}}(u) = \varphi_{\text{Heston}}(u)\times\varphi_{\text{jump}}(u) \]
φ_Heston(u)Heston's own known closed-form characteristic function, unchanged — same object the Heston/FFT lessons already use
φ_jump(u)the compound-Poisson jump process's own characteristic function — a standard, simple closed form for a Poisson-lognormal jump mixture

Neither factor is derived here — \(\varphi_{\text{Heston}}\)'s derivation is the substantial applied mathematics the Heston lesson explicitly declines to reproduce, and \(\varphi_{\text{jump}}\)'s is a standard, considerably shorter result for a Poisson-lognormal compound process. The point worth sitting with is structural, not computational: combining two tractable models by adding an independent term keeps the combination tractable, because independence is exactly the condition under which characteristic functions multiply. Had Bates instead let jump timing or size depend on \(v_t\) or on \(\rho\)'s correlated diffusion — a plausible-sounding refinement, and one some later papers explore — this clean factorization would no longer hold, and the model would lose the tractability that makes it usable in practice at all.

This is exactly what makes Bates FFT-pricing-compatible. Once \(\varphi_{\text{Bates}}(u)\) is known in closed form — which it is, precisely because of the multiplication above — Carr & Madan's method applies to it exactly the way it applies to Heston alone: the FFT Option Pricing (Carr-Madan) lesson's damping-and-FFT machinery doesn't care which tractable characteristic function it's fed, only that one exists. This lesson doesn't re-derive that machinery; it only establishes that Bates supplies it with a valid input.
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

If two things happening are totally unrelated to each other, the "recipe" for their combined randomness is just multiplying the two separate recipes together — no new math needed. Bates gets to do exactly that because it assumes the jump alarm has nothing to do with the slow weather. That's why the same fast pricing trick (Carr-Madan/FFT) built for the weather-only model still works once the alarm gets added — the combined recipe is still simple enough for the trick to use.

Section 04

The actual mechanism: variance share is flat, but skew and kurtosis decay with horizon

Section 01 asserted, in words, that jumps matter more at short maturities and stochastic vol matters more at long ones. This section makes that precise using the same standard compound-Poisson moment formulas the Merton lesson's fat-tails discussion rests on, now applied specifically to how they scale with the horizon \(T\).

Over a horizon \(T\), a compound Poisson process's cumulants (raw building blocks of variance, skewness, and kurtosis) each scale as \(\lambda T\) times a raw moment of the jump-size distribution \(Y\). For \(Y\) lognormal in the Merton/Bates sense (\(\ln(1+Y)\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_J,\sigma_J^2)\), approximated here — as the Merton lesson does — by treating \(Y\) itself as approximately \(\mathcal{N}(\mu_J,\sigma_J^2)\) for clean, standard closed-form moments):

\[ \text{Var}_{\text{jump}} = \lambda T(\mu_J^2+\sigma_J^2), \qquad \kappa_3 = \lambda T(\mu_J^3+3\mu_J\sigma_J^2), \qquad \kappa_4 = \lambda T(\mu_J^4+6\mu_J^2\sigma_J^2+3\sigma_J^4) \]

Add Heston's own diffusive variance contribution, \(v_0 T\) (approximately, over a short horizon where \(v_t\) hasn't moved far from \(v_0\)), and total variance is:

\[ \text{Var}_{\text{total}} = v_0 T + \lambda T(\mu_J^2+\sigma_J^2) \]

Every term on both sides of that equation is proportional to \(T\) — the jump share of total variance is therefore the same fraction at every horizon, short or long, for fixed parameters. That's a genuinely non-obvious fact worth sitting with: adding jumps doesn't disproportionately inflate short-dated variance relative to long-dated variance at all.

Skewness and excess kurtosis behave completely differently, because they divide a cumulant by a power of total variance:

\[ \text{Skew} = \frac{\kappa_3}{\text{Var}_{\text{total}}^{3/2}}, \qquad \text{Excess Kurtosis} = \frac{\kappa_4}{\text{Var}_{\text{total}}^{2}} \]

The numerator of skewness scales as \(T\) (one power of \(\lambda T\)); the denominator scales as \(T^{3/2}\) (total variance to the 3/2 power). Skewness therefore scales as \(T/T^{3/2}=T^{-1/2}\) — it decays like \(1/\sqrt{T}\). Excess kurtosis's numerator also scales as \(T\), but its denominator scales as \(T^2\), so excess kurtosis scales as \(T/T^2=T^{-1}\) — it decays like \(1/T\), faster than skewness. This is exactly the same mathematics behind the Central Limit Theorem: aggregate enough independent shocks together and a distribution's higher moments wash out toward normality, with skewness fading faster than the raw spread and kurtosis fading fastest of all.

This is the precise mechanism behind "jumps dominate the short end, stochastic vol dominates the long end." Fixed jump parameters contribute a constant share of variance at every maturity, but a rapidly vanishing share of skew and (especially) kurtosis as maturity lengthens. Implied-vol skew and smile curvature are driven by exactly those higher moments, not by raw variance — which is why a jump term can dramatically steepen a one-week smile while barely nudging a one-year smile, using the very same \((\lambda,\mu_J,\sigma_J)\) the whole way through. Heston's stochastic vol, by contrast, needs calendar time to accumulate its correlated co-movement — it has the opposite problem, contributing comparatively little smile-shaping power at very short horizons and considerably more at longer ones. The two mechanisms hand off almost exactly where the other one runs out of room.
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine one firework going off during a one-minute video versus during a whole two-hour movie. The firework adds roughly the same SHARE of total noise-and-light either way, minute for minute — but it makes the one-minute video look dramatically, memorably lopsided and spiky, while in a two-hour movie it's one moment among thousands and barely changes the overall feel. Jumps are the firework: same fair share of the total "action" no matter the length, but a much bigger effect on how lopsided and spiky a SHORT clip looks than a long one.

Section 05

The calibration trade-off, stated honestly

Everything above is the case for Bates. The case against it is just as real and needs equal weight: Bates has eight free parameters where Heston alone has five and Merton alone has three (plus the shared diffusive \(\sigma\)) — \((\kappa,\theta,\xi,\rho,v_0,\lambda,\mu_J,\sigma_J)\). More parameters mechanically buy more flexibility to fit whatever shape an observed vol surface happens to have on a given day. That flexibility is not free.

What more parameters buys you

A genuinely richer set of achievable surface shapes: Heston's correlated diffusion for the smooth, longer-dated smile and clustering behavior, plus jump parameters with three separate degrees of freedom (how often, how big on average, how variable) to independently target the short-dated wings without having to distort the longer end to compensate.

What it costs

A harder, less stable, more overfitting-prone optimization problem. With eight parameters and a finite, noisy set of observed option prices to fit them to, multiple, meaningfully different parameter combinations can produce statistically similar-looking fits — a classic identification problem, not a numerical bug. A slightly lower \(v_0\) and slightly higher \(\lambda\) can trade off against each other and land on nearly the same fitted surface, without the calibration having any way to tell you which combination is the economically "right" one.

This is not a hypothetical concern specific to Bates — the Merton lesson's own pitfalls section flags an almost identical problem for Merton alone: "separating a lot of small jumps from high diffusion volatility from a few large jumps ... is a notoriously ill-conditioned estimation problem." Bates inherits that exact tension between \(\sigma\)-like and jump-like parameters, and layers a second one on top of it: distinguishing "the smile is steep because \(\rho\) is strongly negative and \(\xi\) is large" from "the smile is steep because \(\lambda\) is elevated and jumps are large" is its own, largely separate identification problem, and a calibration that fits the surface well today gives no guarantee it has resolved either ambiguity correctly.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine trying to guess exactly how much of a cake's height comes from three different layers by only looking at the cake from outside, with no way to slice it open. If the cake looks a certain height, lots of different combinations of layer-thicknesses could produce that same outside height. Adding a jump layer on top of Heston's two-piece cake gives you more knobs to match any outside shape you see — but it also gives you more ways to get the INSIDE wrong while still nailing the outside. That's the calibration trade-off in one sentence.

Section 06

How practitioners actually use this

📐

Calibrate jointly across the whole maturity ladder, not slice by slice

Because jump and stochastic-vol effects separate cleanly by maturity (Section 04), a common practical discipline is calibrating all 8 parameters simultaneously to the entire observed surface at once, rather than fitting short and long maturities separately and hoping the pieces agree — a joint fit lets the short end constrain the jump parameters and the long end constrain Heston's own five, reducing (not eliminating) the identification problem above.

⚡

FFT/Carr-Madan pricing, exactly as for Heston alone

Once \(\varphi_{\text{Bates}}(u)\) is available (Section 03), the FFT Option Pricing (Carr-Madan) lesson's machinery prices an entire strike strip per candidate parameter set in one FFT call — the same computational path Heston alone uses, unmodified, because Bates' characteristic function slots into exactly the same interface.

🔧

Regularize or constrain, don't just optimize freely

Given the identification issues above, practitioners commonly add parameter bounds, penalty terms toward "reasonable" prior values, or stability-across-time regularization rather than trusting an unconstrained least-squares fit to land on an economically sensible parameter set on its own.

🧭

A practical middle ground between Heston alone and full local-vol

Bates sits between Heston (parsimonious, may underfit short maturities) and the local-volatility family the Heston lesson names but doesn't derive (exactly fits an observed surface at a point in time, at the cost of a less clean forward-looking, parametric story) — a genuinely useful middle option when both smoothness and a decent short-dated fit matter.

🎯
Real-world trading scenario

A desk's Heston calibration nails 3-month vol but misprices weekly options around a data release

Suppose an FX desk calibrates Heston to its 3-month and 1-year implied vols and gets a tight fit — the smoothly correlated diffusion story works well at that range, exactly as Section 01 describes. The same calibrated parameters, used to price a one-week option straddling a known scheduled release, systematically underprice the wings relative to where the market actually trades them — the desk's model says the smile should be far flatter than the quotes it's seeing. Re-fitting Heston's five parameters only to the one-week slice might patch that specific maturity, but at the cost of parameters that no longer make sense for the 3-month slice the desk already had right — exactly the "slice by slice" trap the practitioner note above warns against.

Adding Bates' jump term gives the desk a mechanism that targets the short-dated wings specifically (Section 04's kurtosis math) without requiring \(\kappa,\theta,\xi,\rho\) to be distorted away from the values that already fit the longer end well. The honest caveat, not to be skipped: getting a better joint fit is not the same claim as having correctly identified why the short end is steep — per Section 05, a similarly good fit could in principle come from a different \((\lambda,\mu_J,\sigma_J)\) combination entirely, and the desk's hedging decisions (how much of that short-dated risk is really "vega" versus really "jump risk") depend on getting that distinction right, not just on the fitted price matching.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where Bates-style thinking is misused or overextended

Treating a good joint fit as proof the parameter split is correct. Section 05's identification problem is the headline pitfall of this entire lesson: a calibration that reproduces the observed surface well says the combined model fits, not that the specific split between "how much is stochastic vol" and "how much is jumps" is the economically real one. Two different calibrations that both fit today's surface equally well can disagree meaningfully about how much of tomorrow's risk is vega-hedgeable versus jump risk — and that disagreement matters for hedging, not just for reporting a fit quality metric.

Jump risk is not perfectly hedgeable the way pure diffusion risk is. Black-Scholes-style continuous delta-hedging relies on the underlying's path being continuous — trade the delta continuously and a small, continuous price move is (in the idealized theory) perfectly offset. A genuine discontinuous jump defeats that logic: there is no way to continuously rebalance across an instantaneous discrete move, so jump risk generally cannot be perfectly replicated by dynamic trading in the underlying alone. This is a real market-incompleteness subtlety in the option-pricing literature — it typically means jump risk carries its own risk premium, priced into the market rather than eliminated by hedging, the same diversifiability caveat the Merton lesson's own pitfalls section raises about its original derivation. This lesson states that honestly at the conceptual level and does not attempt to write down a specific jump-risk-premium formula it isn't fully confident of — that would be presenting an unfamiliar result with false precision.

Calibration stability across regimes is not guaranteed. Just as the Heston lesson warns that \((\kappa,\theta,\xi,\rho)\) fitted today is a snapshot, not a physical law, Bates' eight parameters can shift meaningfully from one calibration date to the next — and, given Section 05's identification issue, some of that apparent instability may reflect the optimizer landing in a different, similarly-good local fit rather than a genuine change in market dynamics. Don't treat one day's calibrated \((\lambda,\mu_J,\sigma_J)\) as a stable structural description of "how jumpy this underlying really is."

A richer model is not automatically a better trading tool. Exactly the same caution the Heston lesson closes on applies here, doubled: fitting Bates to today's option prices is curve-fitting by construction with even more degrees of freedom than Heston alone to do the fitting with. A tighter in-sample fit says nothing, by itself, about whether a strategy built on top of that calibration would show real out-of-sample edge — that would need to be tested, per this repo's validation discipline, not assumed from the model having more knobs to turn.

Section 07

Key Takeaways

✓Bates = Heston's two SDEs + Merton's jump term, stapled onto the price process. Neither piece is re-derived here — both sibling lessons already cover the full mechanics, reused verbatim.
✓The complementarity is the whole point. Pure Heston diffuses too slowly to build a steep short-dated smile; pure Merton jumps have no mean-reverting machinery to explain persistent elevated vol. Bates lets jumps own the short end and stochastic vol own the long end.
✓Independence makes the characteristic function multiply — φ_Bates = φ_Heston × φ_jump — which is exactly why Bates stays tractable for the same Carr-Madan/FFT pricing path Heston alone uses.
✓Jump share of variance is flat across horizons; skew decays as 1/√T and excess kurtosis as 1/T. This is the precise, arithmetic reason fixed jump parameters matter far more at short maturities than long ones.
⚠Eight parameters buy flexibility at the cost of identification. Multiple, meaningfully different (κ,θ,ξ,ρ,v₀,λ,μ_J,σ_J) combinations can produce statistically similar fits — a real, honest calibration-stability concern, not a throwaway caveat.
✗Jump risk is not perfectly hedgeable by continuous delta-hedging. A genuine discontinuity defeats the continuous-rebalancing logic behind Black-Scholes-style replication — an incomplete-markets subtlety, stated honestly without a specific unverified risk-premium formula.
✗This repo has no Bates, Heston, or Merton implementation anywhere. This lesson is pure theory background — the repo's own "jump_diffusion"-named modules do model-free realized-variance jump detection, not parametric jump-diffusion or stochastic-vol fitting.
Section 08

Self-test

Q1. In one sentence, what is Bates' model specification, and which parts of it does this lesson re-derive?
Bates takes Heston's price SDE and adds Merton's compound-Poisson jump term \(dJ_t\) to its right-hand side, leaving Heston's variance SDE untouched. This lesson re-derives neither Heston's two SDEs nor Merton's jump mechanics — both are taken as given from their own sibling lessons; the only genuinely new content is how the two combine, why that combination is useful, and what it costs to calibrate.
Q2. Why can pure Heston struggle to fit a steep short-dated (e.g. one-week) implied-vol skew, even with its own best-fit parameters?
Heston's skew-generating mechanism is continuous and gradual: variance \(v_t\) needs time to wander meaningfully away from \(v_0\), and the leverage correlation \(\rho\) needs the diffusion to actually accumulate a correlated co-movement with price over calendar time. A few trading days is not enough runway for either mechanism to build a large effect, so pure Heston tends to produce a much flatter short-dated smile than real markets often show.
Q3. Why does the log-characteristic function of Bates equal the SUM of Heston's and the jump component's log-characteristic functions, and why does that matter practically?
Because the jump process is assumed independent of the correlated Brownian pair driving price and variance, and the characteristic function of a sum of independent random variables is the product of their individual characteristic functions — equivalently, log-characteristic functions add. Practically, this means Bates' characteristic function is available in closed form the moment Heston's and the jump component's are, which is exactly what makes Bates compatible with the same Carr-Madan/FFT pricing machinery used for Heston alone, with no new pricing infrastructure required.
Q4. In the worked example, why does excess kurtosis fall by a factor of 52 from 1 week to 1 year, while the jump share of total variance stays exactly the same (38.6%) at both horizons?
Every cumulant of a compound Poisson process (variance, the 3rd cumulant behind skewness, the 4th behind kurtosis) scales linearly with \(\lambda T\). Variance's numerator and total variance itself both scale as \(T\), so their ratio (the jump variance share) is horizon-invariant. Excess kurtosis, though, divides a \(T\)-scaling numerator by total variance SQUARED (\(T^2\)), so it scales as \(1/T\) overall — and 1 year is 52 weeks, so kurtosis at 1 week is almost exactly 52 times its 1-year value, matching the arithmetic exactly.
Q5. A colleague says "Bates fits the observed vol surface better than Heston alone, so its calibrated λ, μ_J, and σ_J must be the true jump parameters driving this market." What's the precise problem with that claim?
A better joint fit shows the combined 8-parameter model can reproduce the observed surface — it does not show that the specific split between "how much is stochastic vol" and "how much is jumps" is uniquely correct. With 8 parameters fit to a finite, noisy set of option prices, multiple meaningfully different parameter combinations can produce statistically similar-looking fits — a genuine identification problem, not a numerical error. The fit quality alone cannot distinguish between competing parameter combinations that all fit about equally well.
Q6. Why is jump risk described as not "perfectly hedgeable" the way pure diffusion risk is under Black-Scholes-style replication?
Continuous delta-hedging relies on the underlying's price path being continuous, so a small price move can (in the idealized theory) be offset by continuously rebalancing a hedge position. A genuine discontinuous jump breaks that logic outright — there is no way to continuously rebalance across an instantaneous discrete move — so jump risk generally cannot be perfectly replicated by dynamic trading in the underlying alone. This is a real market-incompleteness issue in the option-pricing literature, typically meaning jump risk carries its own priced risk premium rather than being hedged away entirely; this lesson states that conceptually without asserting a specific unverified risk-premium formula.
