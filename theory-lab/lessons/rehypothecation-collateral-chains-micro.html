Rehypothecation, or Who Actually Has Your Collateral

The previous lesson in this category covered how collateral gets posted, haircut, and lent out through the repo market. This closing lesson asks the question that comes right after: once a broker is actually holding a client's collateral, what is it legally allowed to do with it? The answer is "re-use it," in many jurisdictions and within limits — and that single permission is what turns one posted asset into a chain of stacked claims, and turned Lehman Brothers' 2008 collapse into a client-asset-protection failure that took years to unwind.

Section 1

Holding it vs. using it — a small legal distinction with large consequences

Rehypothecation isn't a synonym for "custody." It's a specific, named permission a broker either has or doesn't.

Segregated custody

The broker holds a client's collateral solely for that client's benefit and never touches it for its own funding, lending, or trading. If the broker fails, the client's assets are, in principle, straightforwardly identifiable and returnable — they were never anyone else's.

Rehypothecation

The broker is permitted, under the client agreement and the rules of the governing jurisdiction, to re-pledge that same collateral onward to one of its own counterparties or lenders — or to lend it out directly. The collateral stops being merely held and starts being used.

💡

Why a broker wants to

Re-pledging client collateral is a real, ordinary source of funding — raising cash or borrowing securities elsewhere using assets already on hand, typically cheaper than sourcing that funding independently.

📄

It's usually contractual, not hidden

Standard prime brokerage agreements grant this right explicitly. A client's account statement can still show the position exactly the same either way — segregation vs. rehypothecation is a matter of legal rights, not of what's visible day to day.

One book, lent out three times: a straightforward custody arrangement means a friend just holds your book — it's still yours. Rehypothecation means the friend is permitted to lend it onward to someone else, who — if their own arrangement allows it — can lend the same book onward again. One physical book, now backing three separate promises. If the last link in that chain can't produce it, the disruption doesn't stay contained to one relationship — it ripples backward through every link, because every promise depended on the book actually still being there.

One asset, re-posted down a chain

Try it
One claim so far: A has a claim on B for the return of its $10M collateral. Nothing has re-used it yet.
Two stacked claims now: A on B, and B on C — same $10M securities, referenced twice. B raised its own funding using an asset it doesn't ultimately own.
Three stacked claims, one asset: if C — sitting in the middle — can't return what it owes, the disruption hits both directions at once: B (owed by C) and D (relying on C's pledge), not just one bilateral relationship.

Each additional link doesn't create a second physical security — it creates an additional claim on the same one. This is what the literature calls collateral velocity: roughly, total collateral-secured exposure created across a chain, divided by the size of the pool that seeded it. A four-party chain like this one has a velocity of about 3× — three re-uses of one original asset.

The number of re-pledge links is always one fewer than the number of parties — and every party downstream of the original owner is now exposed to a failure anywhere upstream of them in the chain, not just to the counterparty they personally dealt with. Length itself is the risk multiplier: nothing about any single link looks unusual, but the chain as a whole is only as strong as its weakest one.

Section 4

Lehman Brothers, 2008 — the case that made this concrete

What actually happened
Lehman Brothers Holdings files Chapter 11 (Sept 2008, US)
→
LBIE (its London broker-dealer) separately enters UK administration
→
Rehypothecated client assets get tied up in that process
LBIE operated under UK law, whose rehypothecation rules were, at the time, historically more permissive than the comparable US framework — standard prime brokerage agreements let LBIE rehypothecate a broad scope of client collateral, with clients contractually consenting as part of doing business with the firm.

Clients who believed they were dealing with a broker simply holding their collateral found themselves, instead, as general creditors in a lengthy administration — waiting years to learn what they would actually get back, because their claim on their own posted collateral was suddenly just one unsecured claim in a queue, not a guaranteed return of a specific asset. This is a real, widely documented episode, not a hypothetical one, and it prompted lasting regulatory scrutiny of rehypothecation and prime-brokerage client-asset protection across multiple jurisdictions.

Stated carefully

This deck deliberately doesn't reconstruct precise dollar figures or the exact legal outcome for any specific LBIE client — those details were resolved over years of litigation and administration, and are complex and disputed in places. What's genuinely and widely documented is the structural fact: LBIE's rehypothecation exposure under the then-prevailing UK regime is the case that crystallized this risk for regulators and market participants.

Section 5

The jurisdictional gap that made it worse

A global prime brokerage relationship could be subject to very different rehypothecation limits, depending purely on which legal entity and jurisdiction it was booked through.

SEC Rule 15c3-3 (US) — the Customer Protection Rule

Caps a US broker-dealer's rehypothecation of customer margin securities at roughly 140% of the customer's aggregate debit balance — what the customer actually owes the broker. Excess client securities beyond that must sit in a segregated customer-reserve account, untouched by the broker.

UK-law practice at the time (as applied to LBIE)

Historically permitted broader rehypothecation under standard prime brokerage agreements, with clients able to contractually consent to broader use of posted collateral — without an equivalent statutory percentage cap tied to a customer's debit balance the way Rule 15c3-3 imposes.

Two neighborhoods, two different rules about how many times a landlord can re-rent a spare room you're already renting. One caps it, tied to what you actually owe. The other, at the time, had no such cap written into the rulebook at all. Same underlying activity — very different amount of protection, depending purely on which neighborhood's rules applied to your specific contract.
Where this needs qualifying

A US-domiciled fund's assets, held through a UK-regulated broker-dealer entity, were subject to UK rules — not Rule 15c3-3 — regardless of where the fund itself was based. A home-jurisdiction cap doesn't automatically follow a client's assets to a foreign affiliate; it's the booking entity's jurisdiction that governs.

Section 7

A collateral chain is a real exposure network

This is the direct link back to the earlier lesson on systemic risk and contagion — not an analogy stretched to fit.

↝

Each link is a directed, capital-relevant exposure

A's claim on B, B's claim on C, C's claim on D — exactly the kind of directed edge that lesson's DebtRank framework is built to trace distress through.

⇆

A default in the middle hits both directions

If C defaults, it's simultaneously unable to return what it owes upward to B and unable to honor the pledge it made downward to D — a shock propagating both ways along one chain, at once.

📈

Longer chains amplify, they don't just redistribute

The same amplification the four-bank DebtRank example showed — total settled distress exceeding the initiating shock — applies here: more links means more parties whose own weakening feeds forward again.

Section 8

How practitioners actually respond to this risk

📝

Negotiating rehypothecation limits

Sophisticated clients, especially post-2008, routinely negotiate explicit caps on how much of their collateral can be rehypothecated, rather than accepting a broker's standard, maximally permissive terms by default.

🔀

Diversifying prime brokers

A documented post-LBIE shift: many hedge funds now spread prime brokerage relationships across multiple firms rather than concentrating collateral and financing with a single broker.

🔒

Tri-party and third-party custody

Collateral can instead be held with an independent custodian under a tri-party arrangement, structurally limiting the broker's ability to re-use it at all.

🏛

Regulators tracking collateral velocity

Post-crisis, bodies including the Financial Stability Board studied rehypothecation explicitly as part of the "shadow banking" reform agenda — treating chain interconnectedness as a systemic-risk concern in its own right.

None of this treats rehypothecation itself as illegitimate — it's a widespread, legal, commercially ordinary practice within whatever limits apply. The risk sophisticated clients and regulators actually manage is in the scale, the jurisdictional gaps, and the hidden interconnectedness a long chain can create — not in the existence of the practice.
Section 9

What people get wrong about this

Common misreading
  • "My statement still shows the position, so it can't have been rehypothecated"
  • "My home country's cap protects assets held anywhere, including a foreign affiliate"
  • "Each link in the chain must reference a distinct copy of the asset"
  • "A default in the middle of a chain only hurts the one relationship it happened in"
What's actually true
  • Segregation vs. rehypothecation is a legal right, not something a statement discloses either way
  • The booking entity's jurisdiction governs — not the client's home jurisdiction
  • It's the same underlying security, referenced by multiple stacked claims — not multiple assets
  • A mid-chain default disrupts both directions at once: whoever it owed, and whoever was relying on its pledge
Said plainly

This is institutional market-structure and regulatory knowledge — useful for understanding counterparty risk, custody risk, and systemic interconnectedness. It is not a trading signal, and it does not describe anything built, tested, or traded in this repo.

Section 10

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

What precisely distinguishes rehypothecation from segregated custody?

Segregated custody means the broker holds collateral solely for the client's benefit. Rehypothecation means it's permitted to re-pledge or lend that same collateral onward for its own funding or lending activity.
Question 2

In a four-party collateral chain (A→B→C→D), how many separate claims reference the one underlying asset?

Each re-pledge adds a claim, not a new physical asset. Four parties in a chain means three re-pledge links, and three stacked claims on the same collateral.
Question 3

What specific regulatory gap did the Lehman Brothers International (Europe) case expose?

LBIE operated under UK law, historically more permissive on rehypothecation than the US's SEC Rule 15c3-3, which caps rehypothecation at roughly 140% of a customer's debit balance. The booking entity's jurisdiction — not the client's home jurisdiction — governed which rules applied.
Question 4

$10,000,000 posted collateral, an 80% maximum-rehypothecation-of-collateral cap — how much can the broker legally re-use?

$10,000,000 × 80% = $8,000,000. (SEC Rule 15c3-3's own 140% figure applies to a customer's debit balance, not the posted collateral value, which is why it can legitimately exceed 100% — a different base than the straight collateral-percentage cap modeled here.)
Question 5

Why is a collateral re-use chain a literal instance of the exposure network from the systemic-risk lesson, not just an analogy?

A's claim on B, B's on C, C's on D are directed, capital-relevant exposures — the same structure DebtRank formalizes. If C defaults mid-chain, the shock hits both B (owed by C) and D (relying on C's pledge) simultaneously, exactly the both-directions propagation that lesson's worked example quantifies.
