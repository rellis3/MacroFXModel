Base Rates First

Before you ask what is special about this setup, ask how often this kind of thing happens at all. Then move only as far as the evidence earns.

The whole idea
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

There's a reversal pattern on the chart this morning. It shows up before most big turns. I'd call it sixty percent.

Before you look at the chart, answer a boring question. Out of all days, how many are turns?

Not many. Say five in a hundred.

That is your base rate. It is where you start, before any of today's details. Now, how often does the pattern show up on ordinary days that are not turns?

I've never counted that. Maybe one day in five?

Then picture a thousand days. Fifty are turns, and the pattern shows on thirty of them. Nine hundred and fifty are ordinary, and the pattern shows on one hundred and ninety of those.

So two hundred and twenty days show the pattern.

And only thirty of them are turns. That is about fourteen percent, not sixty. The pattern helped. It nearly tripled the odds. But a turn is still about six times less likely than an ordinary day.

So where did my sixty come from?

From the story. It is the hit rate: how often you see the pattern before a turn. On its own, the hit rate leaves out every false alarm. You need both numbers. Divide the hit rate by the false-alarm rate and you get how strongly the evidence should move you.

And if the two are the same?

Then the evidence tells you nothing. A signal followed by a rally fifty-four percent of the time is worthless if rallies follow fifty-four percent of all days anyway. Always ask for the second number.

Is this just a trading problem?

No. Daniel Kahneman tells a story about his own team in the nineteen seventies, writing a school textbook. They each guessed about two years to finish. Then he asked their curriculum expert how long similar teams had taken.

And?

About four in ten never finished. None of the rest finished in less than about seven years. The team carried on anyway. The book took about eight years, and by then it was never used. The base rate was asked for, given, and ignored.

So how do I stop doing that?

Make it mechanical. Name the group of past cases most like today, write its rate down first, and only then let today's evidence move you, by an amount you can measure.

The one-line version?

Write the base rate down before you read the chart, and never trust a hit rate without its false alarms.

Section 1 · How it reached trading

Line up the events and count

How it reached trading

In 1969 Fama, Fisher, Jensen and Roll lined up many stock-split announcements and measured what usually happened around them, compared with what happens anyway. That was the first event study, and it is the base-rate method at its core.

Kahneman and Tversky showed in the 1970s that people skip this step. Kahneman and Lovallo (1993) named the fix the "outside view".

Section 2

Outside view first, inside view second

Inside view

The chart, the story, the reasons it should work. Feels like analysis. Usually where people start.

Outside view

How often have situations like this worked out? Feels boring. Should come first, written down.

A base rate is a frequency in a reference class: past situations similar enough to today that their outcomes tell you something. For a stop-and-target trade the simplest one is S/(S+T), the hit rate a random market gives you.

Section 3

Bayes in one multiplication

posterior odds = prior odds × LR
LR = hit rate ÷ false-alarm rate = P(E|H) / P(E|not H)

Cab problem: 15% of cabs are Blue. A witness who is right 80% of the time says "Blue". Prior odds 15:85, LR 4 → 60:85 → 41%. Most people say 80%.

LR = 1 doesn't move you at all, however good the hit rate sounds.

Section 5

Rare stays rare

5% of days are turns. A reversal pattern shows up before 60% of turns, and on 20% of ordinary days.

Of 1,000 days: 50 turns → 30 show the pattern
950 ordinary days → 190 show it too
Pattern days: 30 + 190 = 220, of which 30 are turns
P(turn | pattern) = 30/220 = 13.6% · LR = 3

The pattern nearly tripled the odds, and the day is still about six times more likely not to be a turn.

Section 6

Choosing the class

1

Too broad

"All days": stable, but maybe not like today. Use it as the outer anchor.

2

Too narrow

Seven cases is mostly noise. Shrink: p̂ = (k + m·p₀)/(n + m).

3

Use a ladder

Two or three nested classes. If they agree, you're on firm ground.

4

Check the halves

A rate that differs between halves of the sample describes a regime, not a constant.

Section 8 · It actually happened

The textbook that took eight years

Kahneman's own story (Thinking, Fast and Slow). His curriculum team estimated about two years to finish. Asked how similar teams had done, their curriculum expert said roughly 40% never finished, and none that did took less than about seven years.

The team carried on regardless. The book took about eight years and was never used.

The point

The base rate was asked for and given, and nobody used it. The specifics of their own case felt more real.

Section 11 · Your template

The base-rate card

1 · The question, exactly: outcome, horizon, threshold
2 · Broad class: n and rate → p₀
3 · Narrow class: k of n → shrunk p̂; check both halves
4 · Each extra fact as hit ÷ false-alarm = LR; no data → LR 1
5 · Odds × LRs → final p; hand to the EV card, log it for scoring

Write it before you look at the chart.

Section 12

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

10% of days are trend days. A filter flags 90% of them and 10% of other days. Flagged day: P(trend day)?

Odds 1:9 × LR 9 = 1:1. Of 1,000 days: 90 true flags, 90 false flags.
Question 2

"After this signal the index rallied 54% of the time." What do you ask first?

Without the base rate there's no lift. The desk's rates-pivot test: 54.1% against 54.1%, exactly nothing.
Question 3

Prior 20%, evidence with LR = 3. Posterior?

Odds 1:4 × 3 = 3:4 → 3/7 ≈ 42.9%. Still more likely to fail than not.
Question 4

Narrow class 7 of 8, broad rate 45%, m = 10. Carry forward?

(7 + 4.5)/(8 + 10) = 11.5/18 ≈ 63.9%. Eight cases can't support near-certainty.

Scenario drill · an illustrative morning

The reversal pattern fires, and a colleague says "this is the turn"

The reversal pattern from this lesson has just printed on your chart. Your history says 5% of days are turns, the pattern shows up before 60% of turns, and it also shows up on 20% of ordinary days. A colleague says it "looks exactly like" the last big turn and wants to bet heavily on it. The trade risks 1 to make 3. What do you do?

  1. Treat it as a 60% call. The pattern appears before 60% of turns.
    That is the hit rate, not the probability. It leaves out the 190 false alarms in every 1,000 days. Sizing a 13.6% chance as if it were 60% is a large over-bet.
  2. Start from 5%, multiply the odds by an LR of 3 to get about 13.6%, and check the payoff against that.
    Posterior odds are 3:19. At 3 to 1 the expected value is 0.136 × 3 − 0.864 × 1, about −0.45 per unit risked. The trade is a pass at this payoff. It would need to pay more than about 6.3 to 1 to break even.
  3. Ignore the pattern and use the 5% base rate.
    Safer than 60%, and here it gives the same answer: pass. But the pattern is real evidence with an LR of 3. Throwing it away would cost you on a trade with a bigger payoff.
  4. Wait for two more momentum indicators to agree, then multiply all three LRs.
    Indicators built from the same price move are one piece of evidence, not three. Multiplying their LRs double-counts it and pushes the probability up for no real reason.

What the numbers say. This is an illustrative morning built on the lesson's reversal-pattern example, not a real date. Of 1,000 days, 220 show the pattern and only 30 of those are turns. At a 3-to-1 payoff the trade loses money on average, however much the chart resembles a famous turn. Writing the 5% down first is what stops the resemblance from setting the size.

Section 13

What to carry forward

01

Write the base rate down first, from a named reference class.

02

Posterior odds = prior odds × LR. LR = hit rate ÷ false-alarm rate. LR = 1 → don't move.

03

Rare stays rare: LR 3 on 5% is 13.6%. Cab problem: 41%, not 80%.

04

Shrink narrow classes toward broad ones; check both halves.

05

Never quote a hit rate without its base rate. In the desk's rates-pivot test, 54% was exactly the base rate.
