Base Rates First

Before you ask what is special about this setup, ask how often this kind of thing happens at all. Then move only as far as the evidence earns.

Section 1 · How it reached trading

Line up the events and count

How it reached trading

In 1969 Fama, Fisher, Jensen and Roll lined up many stock-split announcements and measured what usually happened around them, compared with what happens anyway. That was the first event study, and it is the base-rate method at its core.

Kahneman and Tversky showed in the 1970s that people skip this step. Kahneman and Lovallo (1993) named the fix the "outside view".

Its job: give the probability before the specifics, and the yardstick any hit rate must beat.

Section 2

Outside view first, inside view second

Inside view

The chart, the story, the reasons it should work. Feels like analysis. Usually where people start.

Outside view

How often have situations like this worked out? Feels boring. Should come first, written down.

A base rate is a frequency in a reference class: past situations similar enough to today that their outcomes tell you something. For a stop-and-target trade the simplest one is S/(S+T), the hit rate a random market gives you.

Section 3

Bayes in one multiplication

posterior odds = prior odds × LR
LR = hit rate ÷ false-alarm rate = P(E|H) / P(E|not H)

Cab problem: 15% of cabs are Blue. A witness who is right 80% of the time says "Blue". Prior odds 15:85, LR 4 → 60:85 → 41%. Most people say 80%.

LR = 1 doesn't move you at all, however good the hit rate sounds.

Section 5

Rare stays rare

5% of days are turns. A reversal pattern shows up before 60% of turns, and on 20% of ordinary days.

Of 1,000 days: 50 turns → 30 show the pattern
950 ordinary days → 190 show it too
Pattern days: 30 + 190 = 220, of which 30 are turns
P(turn | pattern) = 30/220 = 13.6% · LR = 3

The pattern nearly tripled the odds, and the day is still about six times more likely not to be a turn.

Section 6

Choosing the class

1

Too broad

"All days": stable, but maybe not like today. Use it as the outer anchor.

2

Too narrow

Seven cases is mostly noise. Shrink: p̂ = (k + m·p₀)/(n + m).

3

Use a ladder

Two or three nested classes. If they agree, you're on firm ground.

4

Check the halves

A rate that differs between halves of the sample describes a regime, not a constant.

"Hawkish surprise. Dollar higher in 5 sessions?"

The story says about 80%. The desk's measured frequencies say:

Section 8 · It actually happened

The textbook that took eight years

Kahneman's own story (Thinking, Fast and Slow). His curriculum team estimated about two years to finish. Asked how similar teams had done, their curriculum expert said roughly 40% never finished, and none that did took less than about seven years.

The team carried on regardless. The book took about eight years and was never used.

The point

The base rate was asked for and given, and nobody used it. The specifics of their own case felt more real.

Section 11 · Your template

The base-rate card

1 · The question, exactly: outcome, horizon, threshold
2 · Broad class: n and rate → p₀
3 · Narrow class: k of n → shrunk p̂; check both halves
4 · Each extra fact as hit ÷ false-alarm = LR; no data → LR 1
5 · Odds × LRs → final p; hand to the EV card, log it for scoring

Write it before you look at the chart.

Section 12

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

10% of days are trend days. A filter flags 90% of them and 10% of other days. Flagged day: P(trend day)?

Odds 1:9 × LR 9 = 1:1. Of 1,000 days: 90 true flags, 90 false flags.
Question 2

"After this signal the index rallied 54% of the time." What do you ask first?

Without the base rate there's no lift. The desk's rates-pivot test: 54.1% against 54.1%, exactly nothing.
Question 3

Prior 20%, evidence with LR = 3. Posterior?

Odds 1:4 × 3 = 3:4 → 3/7 ≈ 42.9%. Still more likely to fail than not.
Question 4

Narrow class 7 of 8, broad rate 45%, m = 10. Carry forward?

(7 + 4.5)/(8 + 10) = 11.5/18 ≈ 63.9%. Eight cases can't support near-certainty.
Section 13

What to carry forward

01

Write the base rate down first, from a named reference class.

02

Posterior odds = prior odds × LR. LR = hit rate ÷ false-alarm rate. LR = 1 → don't move.

03

Rare stays rare: LR 3 on 5% is 13.6%. Cab problem: 41%, not 80%.

04

Shrink narrow classes toward broad ones; check both halves.

05

Never quote a hit rate without its base rate. In the desk's rates-pivot test, 54% was exactly the base rate.
