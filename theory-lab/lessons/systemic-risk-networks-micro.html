Systemic Risk & Contagion, One Exposure at a Time

The first lesson in this category asked which node in a network is structurally most important. The second turned a correlation matrix into a graph and pruned it to its backbone. This closing lesson asks a different kind of question — one about dynamics, not structure: if one institution takes a large loss or fails outright, how far does that damage actually travel through the web of who-owes-whom before it stops? DebtRank is the algorithm built to answer that, and it's the reason "too interconnected to fail" became as important to regulators as "too big to fail."

Section 1

A network again — but the edges mean something new

Same idea as the last two lessons — nodes and edges — but this time the edges are contracts, not statistics.

1

Lesson 1 — centrality

Nodes and generic edges. The question was purely structural: which node sits in the most important position, by degree, betweenness, or eigenvector centrality?

2

Lesson 2 — correlation networks

Edges built from a statistical relationship — how closely two return series move together, turned into a distance and pruned to a minimum spanning tree.

3

This lesson — exposure networks

Edges built from a contractual, economic relationship: bank A lent to, or holds a claim on, bank B. If B can't pay, A specifically loses money — not a statistical tendency, an actual liability.

↝

Directed and weighted, not symmetric

A's exposure to B is generally not the same size as B's exposure to A. The edge A→B carries its own weight: the fraction of A's own capital that would be wiped out if B defaulted completely.

This directed, weighted graph of counterparty exposures is the whole system: every institution is a node, every "who owes whom, how much, relative to their own capital" relationship is a directed edge. Nothing here is new machinery — it's the same graph vocabulary from lessons 1 and 2, pointed at a new kind of data.

Section 2

Why the structure matters: contagion, not containment

The chain reaction
Bank fails
→
Direct counterparties absorb losses
→
Their counterparties absorb second-round losses
Each arrow is a real capital loss, sized by the exposure weight on that specific edge — not the whole failed bank's balance sheet, but a real fraction of it, proportional to how exposed each neighbor actually was.
A financial network isn't a single row of dominoes — it's a tangle, where each institution is propped up partly by its own capital and partly by the expectation that its counterparties will make good on what they owe it. Knock one over and every institution exposed to it absorbs a real fraction of that loss. Those institutions are now somewhat weaker, which means their counterparties absorb a smaller, second-round hit — and so on, rippling outward until capital cushions finally absorb it, or don't.

This is exactly the question regulators started asking hard after 2008: not "which institution's own failure hurts itself the most," but "which institution's failure does the most damage to everyone else, once every round of knock-on weakening has played out?" That's a question about propagation through a network, and it needs an algorithm built specifically to trace it.

Section 3

DebtRank, step by step

Four banks, one default. Same worked example as the full lesson — step through how the shock actually spreads.

Try it
Bank C fails outright — h_C set to 1.00. A, B and D start perfectly healthy at h=0.00. Nothing has propagated yet.
Every bank directly exposed to C absorbs W×1.00 of C's distress. B (W_BC=0.4) took the biggest direct hit; A (W_AC=0.1) took the smallest.
The cross-exposures (W_AB=0.3, W_BA=0.2, W_DA=0.1) keep feeding further, shrinking increments back and forth until the system settles, ~7-8 rounds later. Total induced distress: 0.24+0.48+0.32 = 1.04 — slightly more than the 1.00 that started it. That amplification is exactly what DebtRank is built to reveal.
Impact factor W_ij = the fraction of i's own capital that would be wiped out if j defaulted completely. Read W_BC=0.4 as "if C fully defaulted, B would lose 40% of its own capital on that exposure alone."
Section 4

The rule underneath the picture

h_i(t+1) = min(1, h_i(t) + Σⱼ W_ij · [h_j(t) − h_j(t−1)])

h

Distress level, h_i ∈ [0,1]

0 is fully healthy, 1 is fully defaulted. Everything between represents partial distress — a large mark-to-market loss, a downgrade, a funding squeeze.

W

Impact factor, W_ij

The fraction of node i's own capital exposed to j. Directed and capital-weighted — the same $10m exposure means something different to a small bank than a giant one.

Δ

Only the newest change propagates

h_j(t) − h_j(t−1), not the raw level, is what gets passed on — so the same distress doesn't get counted twice as it echoes back and forth between two connected banks.

min

Capped at 1

Fully defaulted can't get "more" defaulted — the min(1, …) is the floor-and-ceiling of the whole simulation.

This is worth pausing on, because the shape of the rule is a close cousin of something from Lesson 1: eigenvector centrality is also computed by repeatedly combining a node's neighbors' values, round after round, the way power iteration converges to a leading eigenvector — the same recursive machinery PageRank uses to propagate importance across links. DebtRank borrows that exact recursive shape, but points it at something moving forward through time — a real shock spreading — instead of a fixed, timeless importance score.
A simplified version, stated plainly

The published DebtRank algorithm tracks explicit node states (undistressed / transmitting / already-processed) so distress is never re-sent around a cycle more than once. The change-based rule above is a linearized teaching illustration of that same core mechanic — not a literal, publication-accurate implementation.

Section 5

Turn up the shock, watch it spread

A different, six-bank network. Bank A is the one that gets shocked this time — drag the slider to change how big.

Try it
Bank A's initial shocks = 0.50
3 of 5 downstream institutions significantly affected (settled distress ≥ 10%)

Because only one node is shocked here, each downstream bank's settled distress is just the shock size times the product of the impact factors on the path back to A — a small shock can die out completely before it ever reaches E or F, while a large one (drag the slider to the far right) leaves every institution in the chain measurably weaker. Reach isn't just about how big the shock is — it's about how big the shock is relative to how thickly connected the path is.

Section 6

Try your own one-step cascade

A shocked bank's initial loss, and its exposure weight to three neighbors — how much distress does each neighbor absorb in round one?

Try it — change any input, live
Neighbor X absorbs: W_X × h₀ = 0.40
Neighbor Y absorbs: W_Y × h₀ = 0.20
Neighbor Z absorbs: W_Z × h₀ = 0.10
Blank, negative, or out-of-range inputs (h₀ or a weight outside 0–1) show "—" instead of a number — invalid inputs don't get a fabricated answer.

This is exactly round one of the propagation rule from Section 4, isolated as its own calculator: each neighbor's newly-absorbed distress is simply its impact factor times the shock it's exposed to. Later rounds repeat this same multiplication using each bank's newest change, not its raw level — which is the entire mechanical content of DebtRank, run enough times to settle.

Section 7

DebtRank vs. eigenvector centrality

Cousins in mechanism, strangers in meaning.

Eigenvector centrality — Lesson 1

A static, timeless property of the network's structure: which nodes are connected to other important nodes, right now, with no shock involved. Computed by repeatedly combining neighbors' scores until the ratios stop changing — power iteration toward a leading eigenvector.

DebtRank — this lesson

An actual event simulated forward through time: one real shock at one node, propagating in rounds until it settles. Same recursive shape — combine neighbors' values, round after round — but the output is a score about a hypothetical cascade, not a permanent structural property.

Put plainly: eigenvector centrality answers "who is well-connected?" DebtRank answers "if this node had been the one to fail, how much total damage would the system absorb once every knock-on round played out?" Both questions live on the same graph — only one of them requires a shock to ask.

Section 8

From an academic paper to genuine regulatory practice

⚠

2008's clearest lesson

Some institutions that looked unremarkable by balance-sheet size turned out to be critically important once their web of interconnections was traced through — "too interconnected to fail," not just "too big to fail."

📄

Battiston et al., 2012

The original DebtRank paper applied the method to Federal Reserve emergency-lending data from the crisis and found several institutions far more systemically central than their size alone suggested.

🏛

Now a standard stress-test tool

Variants of DebtRank feed into stress tests, systemic-risk surcharge calculations for globally important banks, and interbank network monitoring at central banks and regulators worldwide.

📊

Connectivity can matter as much as size

A moderately-sized but densely-connected institution can produce a higher DebtRank than a much larger, more isolated one — exactly the distinction "too interconnected to fail" is meant to capture.

Said plainly

DebtRank and the network-contagion framing here are a financial-stability and market-structure research tool — the kind of analysis central banks and regulators run on interbank exposure data to stress-test the system. It is not, and is not presented here as, a component of any trading strategy in this repo. A repo-wide check for DebtRank, exposure-network, or contagion-modeling code found none — confirming this is still accurately described as theory, not built infrastructure.

Section 9

Where this breaks down in practice

⊘

Correlation networks ≠ exposure networks

Lesson 2's network said "these two tend to move together" (statistical). This lesson's says "if one fails, the other specifically loses money" (contractual). Running a DebtRank cascade over a correlation-derived network, as if correlation implied real capital-loss transmission, is a category error.

🔒

Real exposure data is the hard part

Detailed bilateral counterparty exposure data is generally confidential, held only by regulators through mandatory reporting. A private researcher or desk essentially cannot replicate a regulator's DebtRank exercise without it.

≈

Results only as good as the impact factors

The whole cascade depends on the W_ij matrix — loss-given-default assumptions, which exposures even get counted, whether fire-sale price effects are modeled. Different reasonable assumptions can produce materially different rankings.

Where this needs qualifying

Everything on this page uses a simplified, linearized propagation rule for teaching purposes — the published DebtRank algorithm's explicit node-state bookkeeping (undistressed / transmitting / already-processed) exists specifically to guarantee no distress is double-counted around a cycle, and isn't reproduced here in full.

Section 10

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

What makes an exposure network different from the correlation network in Lesson 2?

Correlation says "these tend to move together." An exposure edge says "if j defaults, i specifically loses money" — a real, directed, capital-weighted liability, not a statistical tendency.
Question 2

What does the impact factor W_ij represent?

It's capital-weighted, not raw-dollar-weighted — a $10m exposure means something very different to a bank with $50m of capital than to one with $5bn.
Question 3

In the four-bank worked example, why does the total settled distress (≈1.04) exceed the size of the initiating shock (1.00)?

The shock doesn't just transfer once and stop — each newly-distressed bank's own worsening then hits its own counterparties in later rounds, adding on top rather than merely redistributing the original 1.00. That amplification is exactly what DebtRank is built to reveal.
Question 4

How is DebtRank different from the eigenvector centrality from Lesson 1, despite the similar recursive math?

Both are iterative, recursive computations over a network's structure — but eigenvector centrality answers "who is well-connected, right now, no shock involved," while DebtRank answers "how much damage would this specific node's failure cause, once a cascade played out."
Question 5

Why does "too interconnected to fail" matter separately from "too big to fail"?

2008 showed some institutions were critically systemic because of who they were connected to, not how large they were on their own — exactly what DebtRank was built to quantify and rank.
Section 11

What to carry forward

01

Exposure networks are contractual, not statistical — a directed, capital-weighted edge means "if j defaults, i specifically loses money," a different relationship than Lesson 2's correlation edges.

02

Systemic risk is a propagation question, not a structure question — the first two lessons asked "which node matters" and "what's the backbone"; this one asks "how far does one failure actually spread."

03

DebtRank propagates distress iteratively, using only each neighbor's newest change, capped at 1 — until the cascade settles into a single comparable score per institution.

04

Cascades can amplify, not just redistribute — the worked example's total settled distress (≈1.04) exceeded the size of the initiating shock (1.00).

05

DebtRank is eigenvector centrality's dynamic cousin — same recursive shape, but simulating a real shock forward through time instead of describing a fixed structural score.

06

Real bilateral exposure data is confidential and regulator-held — this machinery is genuinely used at central banks, but only because they have reporting data nobody else can access.

What this now lets you do

Read a systemic-risk headline about "interconnectedness" or a stress test correctly — as a claim about network propagation, not just about an institution's size. Tell an exposure network apart from a correlation network on sight, and know why conflating them is a category error. Recognize DebtRank's family resemblance to eigenvector centrality, and its genuine difference: a simulated shock in time, not a static score.
