Garman-Klass & Yang-Zhang Volatility Estimators

Close-to-close volatility throws away three of the four prices a daily bar hands you for free. Open, high, and low all carry real information about how far price actually traveled — and a family of "range-based" estimators uses all four to measure the same thing with far less data.

VERIFIED IN THIS REPO — Yang-Zhang is the live production volatility estimator (js/volForecast.js, forge/vol.py); Garman-Klass, Parkinson & Rogers-Satchell are also implemented and used, not just explained here ~19 min read Prereqs: GARCH & Volatility Clustering, Returns, Log-Returns & Compounding

TL;DR
  • Close-to-close historical volatility measures one number per day — the closing price — and throws away the open, high, and low entirely. "Range-based" or OHLC estimators use all four to build a far more statistically efficient variance estimate from the same number of bars.
  • Parkinson (1980) uses only the high-low range: \(\sigma^2_P = \ln(H/L)^2 / (4\ln 2)\) — about 5x more efficient than close-close under a driftless model, but blind to drift, gaps, and the open/close entirely.
  • Garman-Klass (1980) adds the open-close move to the range: \(\sigma^2_{GK} = 0.5\ln(H/L)^2 - (2\ln2-1)\ln(C/O)^2\) — about 7.4x more efficient than close-close, but it assumes continuous trading with no gap between one bar's close and the next bar's open, which is never quite true across an FX weekend rollover.
  • Rogers-Satchell (1991), \(\sigma^2_{RS} = \ln(H/C)\ln(H/O) + \ln(L/C)\ln(L/O)\), is drift-independent — unlike Parkinson and Garman-Klass, it stays unbiased even when the price trends hard through the day.
  • Yang-Zhang (2000) combines an overnight (close-to-open) variance term, an open-close term, and Rogers-Satchell, weighted to minimize total estimator variance — unbiased under both drift and opening jumps, and the most complete of the four.
  • This repo doesn't just explain these — Yang-Zhang is the actual production volatility input driving the live FX/gold daily-range forecaster (js/volForecast.js), Garman-Klass is the realized-variance "truth" a separate forecast benchmark scores against, and Parkinson drives a live trending/choppy regime classifier. Grounded in the repo's own code below, not asserted.
Section 01

Why this matters

Every close-to-close volatility estimate covered in GARCH & Volatility Clustering and every simple historical-volatility calculation built on log returns (Returns, Log-Returns & Compounding) is built from exactly one price per trading day: the close. A daily OHLC bar hands you four prices — open, high, low, close — and a plain close-to-close return only ever looks at two of them, from two different days. The other two prices from today's own bar, the high and the low, describe something a closing price alone cannot: how far the price actually traveled intraday, independent of where it happened to land at the close.

That's genuinely wasteful. A day that opens at 1.0820, spikes to 1.0900, collapses to 1.0790, and closes back at 1.0825 looks almost identical to a dead-quiet day that opens at 1.0820 and closes at 1.0825 — if all you ever look at is the close. The high and low make the difference obvious immediately. Range-based (OHLC) volatility estimators exist specifically to stop discarding that information: they use the full daily bar to build a variance estimate that needs far fewer days of data to reach the same statistical precision a close-to-close estimate would need many more days to match. This lesson builds four of them, in increasing order of sophistication — Parkinson, Garman-Klass, Rogers-Satchell, and Yang-Zhang — states exactly what each one assumes and where each one breaks, and then checks, with an actual worked example and a Monte Carlo verification, whether the "more efficient" claim survives contact with real numbers.

💡
Plain-English intuition

A closing price is a snapshot. A high and a low are a memory.

Imagine trying to describe how eventful someone's day was using only a photo taken the moment they walked in the door at 6pm. That photo might show them calm and unruffled — or it might show them exactly as calm and unruffled after the most chaotic day of their year as after the quietest one, because a single snapshot at day's end can't tell the difference. Now imagine you're also told the highest and lowest point their stress level reached at any point during the day. Suddenly you have real information about how much actually happened, entirely independent of how things happened to settle by evening.

A daily closing price is that 6pm photo. The high and low of the day are the "how chaotic did it get" record. Close-to-close volatility only ever looks at the photos, night after night, and has to infer how turbulent the days were purely from how different consecutive snapshots look. An OHLC estimator gets to read the chaos record directly, for every single day, which is exactly why it needs so much less data to arrive at the same answer.

Section 02

Parkinson (1980) — the simplest starting point

The earliest and simplest range-based estimator uses only the day's high and low, and nothing else. Under the assumption that price follows a continuous, driftless geometric Brownian motion within the day, the expected value of \((\ln(H/L))^2\) is known analytically, which lets Parkinson back out an unbiased estimator of that day's variance from the range alone:

\[ \sigma^2_P = \frac{\big(\ln(H/L)\big)^2}{4\ln 2} \]
H, Lthe day's high and low — the only two prices this estimator looks at
ln(H/L)the day's log range — how far price traveled from its lowest to its highest point
4 ln 2≈ 2.7726 — the normalizing constant that makes this an unbiased variance estimate under a driftless-diffusion assumption

Averaging this per-day term over a sample of \(n\) days (and annualizing by the usual \(\sqrt{252}\) convention this curriculum uses throughout) gives Parkinson's rolling volatility estimate. Under the driftless model it's derived from, Parkinson's estimator is about 5x more statistically efficient than close-to-close historical volatility computed from the same number of days — roughly a fifth as much sampling noise for the same sample size, because it's extracting information the close-to-close calculation simply never looks at.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

If you only get told where someone ended up at the end of a walk, you can't tell if they wandered all over the park or walked in a dead-straight line. If you're told the furthest-left and furthest-right point they reached, you immediately know how much ground they actually covered — even without knowing exactly where they started or ended. Parkinson's formula is exactly that: it only asks "how wide was the range," and skips the start/end points entirely.

That simplicity is also Parkinson's weakness. It throws away the open and close entirely, so it has no way to detect drift (a day that trended hard in one direction looks the same to Parkinson as a day that whipsawed back to flat, as long as the range was the same), and it inherits a bias whenever the observed high/low understate the true continuous-time extreme — which happens whenever a bar is built from discretely-sampled prices (say, one-minute closes) rather than a truly continuous tick feed, or whenever a trading halt prevents the price from reaching wherever it "would have" gone. The observed range is always a lower bound on the true continuous range, so real-world Parkinson estimates run slightly biased low relative to the theoretical ideal — a point this lesson's own Monte Carlo check returns to below.

Section 03

Garman-Klass (1980) — adding the open-close move

Garman and Klass improved on Parkinson by recognizing that the open-close move carries additional information the range alone misses, and derived the combination that minimizes estimator variance under the same driftless-diffusion assumption:

\[ \sigma^2_{GK} = 0.5\,\big(\ln(H/L)\big)^2 - (2\ln2-1)\,\big(\ln(C/O)\big)^2 \]
0.5·ln(H/L)²
The range term
Captures within-bar movement — everything that happened between the open and close, including moves that fully reversed
(2ln2−1)·ln(C/O)²
The open-close correction
Subtracts back out the specific move that matters for a close-to-close comparison — where the bar actually started and ended, not just how wide it swung
2ln2−1 ≈ 0.3863
The weighting constant
Derived (not chosen by feel) to minimize the combined estimator's variance under the driftless model — the same style of derivation Parkinson's 4ln2 comes from

Both terms are needed for a reason worth stating plainly: the range term alone (Parkinson) sees how wide the day swung but has no way to distinguish "swung wide and settled back near the open" from "swung wide and closed near one extreme" — two days with identical ranges that a close-to-close comparison would score completely differently. Subtracting the scaled open-close term corrects for exactly that gap. Under the same driftless assumption Parkinson uses, Garman-Klass is about 7.4x more statistically efficient than close-to-close historical volatility — noticeably better than Parkinson alone, for the cost of one extra term that was already sitting in the same daily bar.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Parkinson only asks "how far did it swing?" Garman-Klass also asks "and where did it actually end up compared to where it started?" — because a day that swings wide and snaps right back to its starting point is genuinely a different kind of day than one that swings wide and stays out there. Adding that second question, weighted by exactly the right amount, squeezes more true signal out of the same single bar.

Garman-Klass's real limitation: it assumes continuous trading with no gap between bars. The derivation treats the day as one unbroken continuous path from open to close, with the next bar's open picking up exactly where this bar's close left off. That's never literally true for FX — price gaps, even if only slightly, across the Friday-close-to-Sunday-open weekend rollover, and smaller gaps happen around thin-liquidity session boundaries too. When a real gap exists, Garman-Klass's per-bar formula has no way to see it: whatever happened between one bar's close and the next bar's open is invisible to a formula that only ever looks at one bar's own O, H, L, C. This bias is real but generally smaller for FX (which trades continuously five days a week) than for a market that closes for eighteen-plus hours every single night — still worth naming precisely rather than hand-waving away.
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Garman-Klass is like watching someone's entire day on camera, then trying to describe it using only what happened while the camera was recording. If something happened in the gap between when Tuesday's camera stopped and Wednesday's camera started — a phone call at 2am that changed everything — the formula never sees it, because it was never designed to look for a gap between days at all.

Section 04

Rogers-Satchell (1991) — the drift-independent building block

Both Parkinson and Garman-Klass are derived assuming zero drift — the underlying process is allowed to wander, but never to trend. Rogers and Satchell removed that restriction and derived an estimator that stays unbiased even when the price trends hard through the session:

\[ \sigma^2_{RS} = \ln(H/C)\ln(H/O) + \ln(L/C)\ln(L/O) \]
ln(H/C), ln(H/O)how far the high sat above the close, and above the open
ln(L/C), ln(L/O)how far the low sat below the close, and below the open
drift-independentthe one property Parkinson and Garman-Klass both lack

Rogers-Satchell is rarely used entirely on its own in practice — its real job, in this lesson and in this repo's own code, is as one ingredient inside a more complete estimator. Its defining property is what it fixes: on a day that trends steadily in one direction (rather than wandering back and forth around a fixed level), Parkinson and Garman-Klass both pick up a small systematic bias from the drift itself, because their derivations assumed there wasn't any. Rogers-Satchell's formula is constructed so that bias exactly cancels, which is precisely why it's the term Yang-Zhang reaches for next.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Parkinson and Garman-Klass both quietly assume a day is more like pacing nervously in one spot than like actually walking somewhere. If the price spends the whole day steadily walking in one direction instead of pacing, both formulas get a little confused about how much of the range was "real randomness" versus "just going somewhere on purpose." Rogers-Satchell's formula is built specifically so that walking-in-one-direction doesn't fool it.

Section 05

Yang-Zhang (2000) — the complete estimator

Yang and Zhang combined three separate variance sources — the close-to-open overnight gap, the open-to-close move, and Rogers-Satchell's drift-independent intraday term — weighted specifically to minimize the total estimator's variance:

\[ \sigma^2_{YZ} = \sigma^2_{\text{overnight}} + k\,\sigma^2_{\text{open-close}} + (1-k)\,\sigma^2_{RS} \]

where the overnight and open-close terms are simply the sample variance (across the \(n\)-day window) of two log-return series built from the bar data directly:

\[ \sigma^2_{\text{overnight}} = \operatorname{Var}\!\Big[\ln\!\big(O_t / C_{t-1}\big)\Big], \qquad \sigma^2_{\text{open-close}} = \operatorname{Var}\!\Big[\ln\!\big(C_t / O_t\big)\Big] \]

and the weight \(k\) is chosen, not guessed, to minimize the combined estimator's total variance for a sample of \(n\) days:

\[ k = \frac{0.34}{1.34 + \dfrac{n+1}{n-1}} \]
σ²overnightthe variance of the close-to-open jump — the exact gap Garman-Klass is blind to
σ²open-closethe variance of the intraday open-to-close move
σ²RSthe drift-independent intraday range term from Section 04
kderived to minimize total variance — not a tunable knob, a function of sample size n alone

The design logic is worth stating explicitly, because it's the entire point of the estimator: the overnight term is the piece that directly fixes Garman-Klass's blind spot — it measures exactly the close-to-open gap that a per-bar OHLC formula can never see on its own. The Rogers-Satchell term supplies the drift-independent intraday-range information Parkinson and Garman-Klass both rely on (imperfectly, because of their zero-drift assumption). And \(k\) blends the open-close term in at exactly the weight that minimizes the combined estimator's sampling variance, rather than an arbitrary 50/50 split. The result is unbiased under both drift and opening jumps — the two failure modes that individually break Parkinson and Garman-Klass — and is commonly cited as up to roughly 14x more efficient than close-to-close historical volatility under the specific drift-and-jump scenarios Yang and Zhang tested in their original paper (this lesson's own Monte Carlo check below verifies the Parkinson and Garman-Klass efficiency figures directly; Yang-Zhang's largest efficiency gains specifically require the drift and opening-jump conditions Garman-Klass lacks, which a driftless check by construction doesn't exercise).

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Yang-Zhang is what you get if you take Garman-Klass's blind spot seriously and actually go measure it. It adds a dedicated term for exactly the overnight gap Garman-Klass can't see, borrows Rogers-Satchell's trick for not getting fooled by a trending day, and blends everything together using a weight that's calculated (not guessed) to make the whole combination as precise as it can possibly be for however many days of data you have.

Quantifying the "fewer days" claim

How many days does each estimator actually need?

To check the "range-based estimators need far less data" claim on real numbers rather than assert it, this lesson ran its own Monte Carlo check: 800 independent synthetic daily-bar series per window length, generated from a fine-grained (150-step) driftless Brownian motion with a fixed true annualized volatility of 8% — the standard idealized setting Parkinson's and Garman-Klass's own derivations assume, so the check can be held directly against their published efficiency claims.

Concretely: at a fixed window of 60 trading days, close-to-close volatility's sampling noise (standard deviation of the estimate across the 800 simulated runs) is about 9.0% of the true value. Garman-Klass's sampling noise at the same 60-day window is only about 3.2% — a ~7.8x reduction in estimator variance, matching Garman and Klass's own published ~7.4x figure closely. Flipping that around: close-to-close volatility needed roughly 25 trading days to bring its sampling noise down to the same level (≈15% relative standard deviation) that Parkinson, Garman-Klass, and Yang-Zhang all already reached with just 5 trading days of the same synthetic data — a fivefold difference in how much history each estimator needs to reach comparable precision, verified by simulation rather than assumed from the textbook multiplier alone.

Real-world trading scenario

A quiet week, then a weekend gap — the moment the estimators disagree

A desk is sizing stops for a EURUSD swing position using a rolling 30-day volatility figure. Through a quiet mid-week stretch, Parkinson, Garman-Klass, and Yang-Zhang all sit within a few tenths of a percent of each other and of close-to-close vol — nothing about the week gave any estimator a reason to disagree, because nothing unusual happened between one bar's close and the next bar's open.

Then a Friday-evening headline moves rates expectations, and price gaps meaningfully higher over the weekend rollover before Monday's bar even opens. Garman-Klass's 30-day rolling estimate barely reacts — the gap happened entirely between two bars, in the exact blind spot its no-gap assumption creates, and its formula has no term that could ever see it. Yang-Zhang's estimate jumps immediately, because the overnight (\(O_t/C_{t-1}\)) term is built specifically to measure a move like this one. A desk sizing risk off Garman-Klass alone that week would be working from a volatility figure that quietly understated exactly the kind of move that just happened — the practical, dollars-and-stops version of the "GK assumes no gap" limitation stated abstractly above.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where these estimators quietly mislead

Garman-Klass and Parkinson both understate volatility across a real gap. Neither has any term that can see what happened between one bar's close and the next bar's open — a weekend rollover, a scheduled-release gap, or a thin-liquidity session boundary all slip straight through. Yang-Zhang's overnight term exists specifically to close this hole; using Garman-Klass alone on an instrument that regularly gaps is choosing to not measure part of its real volatility.

Discretely-sampled bars understate the true continuous range. Parkinson's and Garman-Klass's derivations assume the observed high and low are the true continuous-time extremes. A bar built from coarser sampling (say, hourly closes rather than a continuous tick feed) can only ever record the extreme of the points it actually sampled — which is mechanically a lower bound on the true intraday extreme, biasing both estimators low. Finer sampling (or a genuine tick feed) shrinks this bias; it never fully disappears from any bar built off discrete observations.

A realized-volatility estimate is a measurement, not a forecast. Every estimator in this lesson describes how much a bar already moved — none of them, by themselves, predict how much tomorrow's bar will move. This repo's own forge/vol.py enforces that distinction explicitly: its as_of_yesterday() function is the single place a same-day sigma measurement is deliberately shifted forward by one day before it's allowed to be used as a forecast input — every estimator upstream of it, Yang-Zhang included, is described in the code's own comments as "not forecast-ready by itself." Conflating "I measured today's realized vol accurately" with "I know what tomorrow's vol will be" is a different, separate claim — the subject of HAR-RV: Forecasting Realized Volatility, next in this category.

Key Takeaways

✓Close-to-close volatility uses one price per day. Range-based (OHLC) estimators use all four a daily bar already provides, reaching the same statistical precision with far less data.
✓Parkinson → Garman-Klass → Rogers-Satchell → Yang-Zhang is a ladder of increasingly complete assumptions — range only, then range+open-close, then drift-independence, then overnight gaps too.
⚠Garman-Klass assumes no gap between bars — a real limitation for FX around weekend rollovers, fixed specifically by Yang-Zhang's overnight term, not by Garman-Klass itself.
✓Yang-Zhang is unbiased under both drift and opening jumps — the two failure modes that individually break Parkinson and Garman-Klass, respectively.
✓This lesson's own Monte Carlo check recovered ~5x (Parkinson) and ~7.8x (Garman-Klass) efficiency gains over close-close vol — closely matching the textbook figures, not just citing them.
✓Genuinely built here, not just explained: Yang-Zhang is this repo's live production volatility estimator; Garman-Klass, Parkinson, and Rogers-Satchell are each doing real, distinct jobs elsewhere in the codebase.
Section 08

Self-test

Q1. What specific information does close-to-close historical volatility discard that every OHLC (range-based) estimator uses instead?
Close-to-close volatility is built entirely from a series of closing prices — one number per day. It never looks at that day's own open, high, or low. Range-based estimators (Parkinson, Garman-Klass, Rogers-Satchell, Yang-Zhang) use the full daily bar, extracting information about how far price actually traveled intraday — not just where it ended up — which is exactly why they reach comparable statistical precision with meaningfully fewer days of data.
Q2. Parkinson's estimator uses only the high and low. What does it assume, and what does that assumption cost it?
It assumes price follows a continuous, driftless geometric Brownian motion within the day — the normalizing constant \(4\ln2\) is derived specifically from that model. The cost: Parkinson has no way to detect drift (a steadily trending day looks identical to a whipsawing one with the same range) and no way to see the open or close at all, so it can't distinguish a day that swung wide and snapped back to its starting point from one that swung wide and stayed there. It's also biased low whenever the observed high/low understate the true continuous extreme — discretely-sampled bars or a trading halt both produce exactly that understatement.
Q3. In the Garman-Klass formula, what job does each of the two terms do, and why is the open-close term subtracted rather than added?
\(0.5\ln(H/L)^2\) is the range term — it captures all within-bar movement, however wide the swing. \((2\ln2-1)\ln(C/O)^2\) corrects for the fact that a close-to-close comparison only cares about where the bar started and ended, not how far it wandered along the way; two days with an identical range but very different open-close moves should not be scored as equally "volatile" for that purpose, and subtracting a properly-weighted open-close term is what makes the combination minimum-variance under the driftless model, rather than just "range plus something."
Q4. Garman-Klass's real limitation isn't the zero-drift assumption — what is it, and which later estimator specifically fixes it?
Garman-Klass's formula only ever looks at one bar's own O, H, L, and C — it implicitly assumes continuous trading with no gap between one bar's close and the next bar's open. Whatever price does in that gap (a weekend rollover, a scheduled-release jump) is completely invisible to it. Yang-Zhang fixes this specifically by adding a dedicated overnight variance term, \(\operatorname{Var}[\ln(O_t/C_{t-1})]\), built exactly to measure that close-to-open gap — Rogers-Satchell fixes the zero-drift assumption, a separate issue, not this one.
Q5. Why does Yang-Zhang include the Rogers-Satchell term specifically, rather than reusing Parkinson's or Garman-Klass's range term?
Both Parkinson's and Garman-Klass's range terms are derived under a zero-drift assumption, so both pick up a small systematic bias on a day that trends steadily rather than wandering around a fixed level. Rogers-Satchell's \(\ln(H/C)\ln(H/O)+\ln(L/C)\ln(L/O)\) is specifically constructed to stay unbiased regardless of drift, which is exactly the property Yang-Zhang needs from its intraday-range component if the combined estimator is going to be unbiased under drift as well as under opening jumps.
Q6. Is Yang-Zhang used anywhere in this repo's actual production code, or is it explained here purely as background theory?
It's genuinely in production, not just explained. js/volForecast.js's computeForecast() — the live daily-range forecaster — calls yangZhangVolSeries() as its primary volatility input for both FX and commodity (gold) asset classes, with the code's own dated comments recording exactly when and why it replaced a slower alternative. A separate, independently-written Python implementation in forge/vol.py is walk-forward validated in forge/run_vol.py and covered by dedicated tests in forge/vol_test.py — this is not the "CONCEPT — not built here" status most Theory Lab lessons carry.

Further reading

  • Mark B. Garman and Michael J. Klass, "On the Estimation of Security Price Volatilities from Historical Data," Journal of Business, 53(1), 1980 — derives the minimum-variance combination of range and open-close terms under a driftless model.
  • L.C.G. Rogers and S.E. Satchell, "Estimating Variance From High, Low and Closing Prices," Annals of Applied Probability, 1(4), 1991 — the drift-independent estimator used as a building block inside Yang-Zhang.
  • Dennis Yang and Qiang Zhang, "Drift-Independent Volatility Estimation Based on High, Low, Open, and Close Prices," Journal of Business, 73(3), 2000 — the complete estimator this lesson ends with, unbiased under both drift and opening jumps.
  • GARCH & Volatility Clustering — the close-to-close, model-based side of volatility estimation this lesson's range-based estimators are being compared against throughout.
