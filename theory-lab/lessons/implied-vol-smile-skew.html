The IV Smile & Skew

Implied volatility is a curve across strikes, not one number. Its height says what a move costs, its tilt says which direction costs more, and a falling market that lifts vol can change how dealers hedge.

CONCEPT — drawn live on the OI Lab; its effect on price is untested here ~8 min read Prereqs: Vega & the Volatility Surface, Second-Order Greeks
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — twelve short slides with a live smile you can bend.
TL;DR
  • Every strike has its own implied volatility. Plot it against strike and you get the smile: lowest near the money, higher in the wings.
  • The skew is its tilt. Equity indices have a higher downside wing, because buyers pay up for crash protection.
  • Two numbers summarise it: the risk reversal \(RR(x)=IV(+x)-IV(-x)\) for the tilt, and the butterfly \(BF(x)=\tfrac12[IV(+x)+IV(-x)]-IV_{ATM}\) for the curvature.
  • If the smile stays fixed against strikes, a fall in spot slides the at-the-money point up the put wing, so at-the-money vol rises as price falls. Skew is what turns a spot move into a vol move.
  • Vanna turns a vol move into hedging flow. Which way that flow pushes price depends on who is short which options, and open interest does not tell you.
  • Evidence: implied vol forecasts range, and the gamma sign relates to next-day range. Whether skew predicts range or direction has not been tested here.
Section 01

Why this matters

The volatility surface lesson introduced implied vol as a two-axis surface, strike by expiry. The second-order Greeks lesson showed that vanna is the link between a vol move and a delta move. This lesson joins them: the skew is the reason a spot move comes with a vol move, so vanna matters in the first place. It is also the picture you meet most often on a desk, and the one most easily over-read.

💡
Plain-English intuition

The price of insurance depends on the claim

Think of each option as an insurance policy on a different outcome. A policy against a small move near today's price is cheap per unit of cover. A policy against a big drop costs more per unit, because people want it most when markets fall, and the people selling it need to be paid for that. The smile is just the price of insurance plotted against how far away the claim is.

Equity indices lean to the downside because investors hold stocks and want protection against a fall far more than they want protection against a rally. So the left side of the curve sits higher. When price actually drops, you are standing closer to that expensive left side, and the market's at-the-money vol is read from there.

Section 02

The maths, step by step

Write the smile around today's spot as a simple curve in \(x\), the percent distance of a strike from spot:

\[ IV(x) \;=\; \sigma_{ATM} + a\,x + b\,x^{2} \]

Here \(a\) is the skew slope in vol points per 1% and \(b\) the curvature. For an equity index \(a\) is negative: higher strikes (positive \(x\)) are cheaper than lower ones.

The two summary numbers fall straight out of it, measured at distance \(x\) on both sides:

\[ RR(x) = IV(+x) - IV(-x) = 2a\,x, \qquad BF(x) = \tfrac12\big[IV(+x)+IV(-x)\big]-\sigma_{ATM} = b\,x^{2} \]

Now let spot move by \(m\) percent while the smile stays fixed against strikes (sticky strike). The new at-the-money strike was at \(x=m\) on the old curve, so

\[ \sigma_{ATM}^{new} = IV(m) = \sigma_{ATM} + a\,m + b\,m^{2} \]

With \(a<0\), a fall (\(m<0\)) raises at-the-money vol by about \(|a|\,|m|\). That is the spot-vol link in one line. The opposite assumption, sticky delta, keeps the smile attached to spot, so the at-the-money vol would not move. Real markets sit between the two, which is why this is a mechanism and not a forecast.

\(x\)Percent distance of a strike from spot (negative = below spot).
\(IV(x)\)Implied volatility at that strike.
\(\sigma_{ATM}\)At-the-money implied vol: the level of the curve at \(x=0\).
\(a\)Skew slope, vol points per 1% of moneyness. Negative on equity indices.
\(b\)Curvature: how much both wings sit above the middle.
\(RR\)Risk reversal: call vol minus put vol at equal distance. Measures the tilt.
\(BF\)Butterfly: average wing vol minus at-the-money vol. Measures the tails.
\(m\)Percent move in spot.
A smile with put skew: IV(x) = 20 − 0.5x + 0.05x²
The left wing sits higher than the right, so the risk reversal is negative. A fall of 3% in spot, with the smile fixed in strikes, lifts the at-the-money vol from 20 to about 21.95.
🧮
Worked example

Skew, butterfly and the spot-vol link with real numbers

Take \(\sigma_{ATM}=20\), \(a=-0.5\), \(b=0.05\), and ask for the numbers at \(x=3\%\).

1
\(IV(-3)=20+1.5+0.45=21.95\) and \(IV(+3)=20-1.5+0.45=18.95\).
2
\(RR(3)=18.95-21.95=-3.00\): the put wing is three vol points richer than the call wing.
3
\(BF(3)=\tfrac12(21.95+18.95)-20=0.45\): the average wing is about half a point above the middle.
4
Spot falls 3% and the smile stays fixed in strikes: \(\sigma_{ATM}^{new}=IV(-3)=21.95\). At-the-money vol rises about 1.95 points.
🎯
Real-world trading scenario

Reading SPX on 20 October 2026

On the lab's terrain card the nearest SPX expiry reached +4 vol points about 1.3% below the pinch (near 7620) and about 3% above it (near 7950). Roughly: the downside wing climbs more than twice as steeply as the upside. That is strong put skew. The put wall (7665.7) and the major wall (7615.7) both sit in the expensive zone, so a move down into them would reprice those options fastest.

What a trader takes from it: the market is paying a premium for downside protection, so collars and put spreads cost more to hold. What the trader does not take from it: a forecast that price will fall. Skew is the price of protection, and the price can be high for a long time.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

Desks do not trade "the smile" as a single object. They trade risk reversals (one wing against the other, to take a view on where the premium sits), collars and put spreads (selling the other wing to cut the cost of protection), and calendars (near expiries bend harder than far ones, so the difference can be traded around an event). In FX the risk reversal and butterfly at the 25-delta strike are the quoted prices themselves; the FX options quoting lesson builds them.

Relative value is the other everyday use: compare today's skew with its own history. A skew far steeper than usual is read as unusual fear, a flatter one as complacency. That is a statement about pricing, and the step from there to a forecast of price is exactly the step that has not been shown to work.

Read the smile as context for how much a move costs.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where the smile gets misread

Skew is a price, not a forecast. A steep put wing says protection is expensive. It does not say the protection will pay.

Sticky strike is an assumption. The vol-rises-as-price-falls result holds only to the extent the smile stays fixed against strikes. In a market that re-centres the smile on spot, at-the-money vol barely moves.

The dealer sign is unobserved. Any story that ends in "so dealers must buy" rests on assuming who is long and short. Open interest does not say.

Thin wings are noisy. Far-out strikes trade rarely and their implied vols jump around. The in-the-money half of each curve mostly echoes the other side. Read the out-of-the-money half and ignore kinks.

A delayed snapshot against a saved one. On the lab the smile is live (15 minutes delayed) while levels and open interest come from the last saved snapshot, so the pinch and the spot line can differ.

Section 04

Key Takeaways

✓The smile is the price of protection by strike. Lowest near the money, higher in the wings, and on equity indices higher on the downside.
✓Two numbers describe it. The risk reversal measures the tilt and the butterfly measures the tails.
✓Skew links price to vol. With the smile fixed in strikes, a drop lifts at-the-money vol by about \(|a|\,|m|\).
⚠Vanna's direction rests on an assumption. Who is short which options is not in the open interest.
⚠Evidence stops at range. Implied vol and the gamma sign relate to range. Skew and direction are untested or failed.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. Why do all lines on the OI Lab fan meet at one point?
The plot shows each strike's implied vol minus the at-the-money vol. At the money that difference is zero by construction, for every expiry, so every line crosses there. It is not a coincidence in the data.
Q2. With \(IV(x)=20-0.5x+0.05x^2\), what is the 3% risk reversal and what does its sign say?
\(RR(3)=IV(3)-IV(-3)=18.95-21.95=-3.00\). Negative: the put wing is three vol points richer than the call wing, the usual equity-index tilt.
Q3. Spot falls 4% with the smile fixed in strikes, using the same curve. Where does at-the-money vol land?
\(IV(-4)=20+2.0+0.8=22.8\). At-the-money vol rises by about 2.8 points. The tilt contributes 2.0 and the curvature 0.8.
Q4. Would at-the-money vol rise under "sticky delta"?
No. Sticky delta keeps the smile attached to spot, so the at-the-money point stays at the bottom of the curve and its vol does not move. Real markets sit between sticky strike and sticky delta.
Q5. A trader says "put skew is steep, so SPX is going down." What is wrong with that?
Skew is the price of protection, set by demand and risk appetite. It can stay steep for months while price rises. Skew and positioning have not been shown to predict direction reliably, and the dealer sign that any hedging story relies on is unobserved.
