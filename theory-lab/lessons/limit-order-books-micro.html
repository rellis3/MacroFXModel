Market Microstructure 2 · Market Microstructure

Inside the Order Book, Level by Level

The Market Microstructure lesson treated the spread and price impact as single numbers — one coefficient, one gap. Neither one is the actual object those numbers get read off of: a real limit order book, which is two ladders of resting orders, each level holding a queue, governed by a strict rule about who gets filled first. This deck opens that object up — the ladder, the queue, what a market order does to it, and why the order sitting first in line gets treated completely differently from the one sitting last.

Section 1

A book is two ladders, not one price

Every quoted price hides a whole ladder of resting orders underneath it.

▼

Bid side

Every resting order to buy, sorted from the best bid (highest price, closest to the market) down to progressively worse, lower prices.

▲

Ask side

Every resting order to sell (also "offer"), sorted from the best ask (lowest price) up to progressively worse, higher prices.

≡

A level

One distinct price on either side. A level's size is the total quantity resting there, summed across every order queued at that exact price.

⋯

A queue, not one order

A level is not a single order — it's a queue of possibly many separate orders from many participants, all willing to trade at that exact price, waiting their turn.

Kyle's Lambda and the Glosten-Milgrom spread from the prior lesson both describe the market as a single number. This is the mechanical object underneath both of them — and once you can see the ladder itself, price impact stops being an abstract coefficient and becomes something you can compute directly, level by level.

A tight spread with deep resting size on both sides is what a liquid market looks like — small orders barely move the price. Depth and spread are two different, complementary numbers measuring the same thing: how expensive it is, right now, to trade a given amount immediately.

Section 3

Price-time priority: the matching rule, in order

When an incoming order needs matching, two rules apply — strictly, in this order.

Rule 1
→
Better price always fills first

An incoming market buy fully consumes the lowest resting ask price before it is ever allowed to touch the next-lowest ask price. No order can "skip" a better-priced resting order to reach a worse one.

Rule 2
→
Same price → earliest arrival wins

Among every order resting at the exact same price, the one that arrived first is filled first — strictly first-in-first-out (FIFO), regardless of size or account.

The consequence that matters in practice

When you joined a price level is as economically important as what price you joined at. An order at the back of a long queue at the best price can wait an entire session without a fill, while a smaller order two ticks worse gets filled first simply because its queue cleared faster. Price alone doesn't predict fill probability — queue position does too, and often dominates.

Section 7

Queue position: waiting for other people's trades

For a resting order, the number that matters most is how much size sits ahead of you at your exact price — call it A.

A = 800 units ahead of you
− 300 matched
A = 500 units ahead of you

A market sell order for 300 units arrives and matches against the best-bid queue. Strict time priority means every one of those 300 units is taken from the front of the queue — the orders that arrived before yours. Your ahead-of-you size drops from 800 to 500. You've moved 300 units closer to a fill, but nothing about your own order changed at all — your fill depends entirely on order flow that is unrelated to, and ahead of, you.

Why this matters for passive strategies

A market-making or resting-limit strategy's realized fill rate can differ substantially between two orders posted at the identical price, purely as a function of when each joined the queue. A backtest that only checks "did the market trade through my price," without tracking queue position, will systematically overestimate how often it would actually have been filled.

Section 8

Same price, same size, very different economics

A real-world scenario: two market-making desks, one price, thirty seconds apart.

Desk A — first in line
  • Posts a resting limit buy the instant the book opens, when the queue at the best bid is empty
  • Ahead-of-you size A = 0
  • 1,500 units of market sell orders arrive over the next few minutes
  • Filled completely — captures the spread it was quoting
Desk B — 30 seconds later
  • Posts an identically-sized limit buy at the exact same price, thirty seconds after Desk A
  • By then, 2,000 units have already queued up ahead — A = 2,000
  • Same 1,500 units of market sell orders arrive and match front-to-back
  • Not one unit filled — still 500 units behind when the flow stops
Both desks quoted the identical price, took the identical directional risk, posted the identical size — one made money, the other is still waiting, purely as a function of thirty seconds of queue position. This is not an edge case; it is why sophisticated market-making operations invest heavily in being first to a new price level, not just in picking a good one.
Section 9

Providing liquidity vs. taking it

Every order on the book is one of exactly two kinds.

Limit order — provides liquidity

Specifies a price and rests until matched, cancelled, or expired. Accepts uncertainty about whether and when it fills, in exchange for a better price than crossing the spread — and no adverse price movement of its own making.

Market order — takes liquidity

Specifies only a size, no price, and demands to trade immediately against whatever is resting. Accepts price uncertainty (how far will it have to walk?) in exchange for certainty of instant execution.

Most professional execution algorithms are, at their core, a policy for deciding this trade-off order by order — resting part of a large parent order as limit orders near the touch, and only crossing the spread with a market order for the urgent remainder.

Section 10

How a desk actually uses this, and where it goes wrong

📊

Reading depth before sizing

Check how much size rests at and near the touch to estimate, in advance, how many levels an order will walk and what VWAP it's likely to pay.

🧭

Choosing limit vs. market, deliberately

A trader who can wait posts a limit order to avoid the spread and the walk; one who needs certainty accepts a market order's worse expected price.

⏱

Racing to be first in queue

Because queue position can matter more than price, market-making operations invest heavily in joining a new price level early and tracking their own live queue position.

🧩

Splitting an order across levels

Rather than one market order that mechanically walks the book, execution logic can rest most of a large order and cross the spread only for the urgent remainder.

Where this needs qualifying

A displayed book is a photograph of one instant — resting orders are cancelled and replaced constantly ("phantom" or "flickering" liquidity), so a snapshot isn't a guarantee of what's still there when an order actually arrives. And price-time priority, while dominant, isn't universal: some futures markets use pro-rata matching, allocating fills proportionally to order size at a level rather than strictly by arrival time — which flips the strategy from "be early" to "be large." Always confirm which rule a venue actually uses.

Factual grounding — checked against this repo's actual code

The full lesson tags this concept "not built or tested in this repo," and that check holds up: nothing in this codebase implements order-book depth, queue-position tracking, or price-time-priority matching. Several files explicitly acknowledge the absence rather than fill it — volatilityExhaustion/tier5_liquidity.py notes "we can't see spread or order-book depth (mid candles only)," and js/macroFxZoneEngine.js lists order-book liquidity as a deferred, untested spec item because this repo only has mid-price OANDA data. The theory here is real and correct; the repo simply doesn't have the data feed a live order book requires.

Section 11

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

What does price-time priority actually guarantee, in order?

Two rules, strictly in order: price priority, then FIFO time priority within an identical price. Size, account, and everything else are irrelevant to the ordering.
Question 2

Using this deck's ask ladder (200 @ 1.1005, 300 @ 1.1006, 500 @ 1.1007), does a 450-unit market buy order ever touch level 3?

200 (all of L1) + 250 (part of L2) = 450 — fully filled without ever reaching L3, which is exactly why it pays a better VWAP than the 600-unit example.
Question 3

Order A joins an empty queue (A=0); Order B joins later behind 2,000 units. What single number best explains why they can have very different fill outcomes?

A depends purely on arrival time relative to other orders at the same price, and on how fast the orders ahead get matched or cancelled — not on anything about the order's own price, size, or quality.
Question 4

Why does a market order's true VWAP always look at least as bad as the best quoted price it started at?

The first units always fill at the best price; anything beyond that can only fill at that price or worse — the volume-weighted average can only be pulled away from the best price, never toward it.
Question 5

A 600-unit market buy hits this deck's ask ladder. What's the resulting VWAP and slippage versus the 1.1005 best ask?

[200(1.1005) + 300(1.1006) + 100(1.1007)] / 600 = 660.35 / 600 ≈ 1.10058 — about 0.83 pips worse than 1.1005.
Section 12

What to carry forward

01

A book is two ladders of price levels, each holding a strict FIFO queue — a level's size is the sum of many separate orders, not one.

02

Price-time priority is the matching rule: better price always fills first; at an identical price, earliest arrival wins, strictly first-in-first-out.

03

A market order walks the book — it consumes each level fully before moving to the next, and its realized VWAP is always at least as bad as the first price it touched.

04

Queue position — the ahead-of-you size A — can dominate a passive order's real fill probability, independent of price.

05

Depth and spread are two different, complementary liquidity measures — a tight spread with thin depth can still be expensive to trade in size.

06

A book snapshot is a photograph, not a guarantee — resting size can vanish before an order actually reaches it.

What this now lets you do

Estimate expected slippage before sending an order, by walking the book level by level yourself instead of assuming the quoted price is what you'll pay. Recognize when a resting order's poor fill rate is a queue-position problem rather than a pricing problem. Reason correctly about why two identically-priced, identically-sized orders can have completely different economics — and why market-making desks obsess over being early to a price, not just picking a good one.
