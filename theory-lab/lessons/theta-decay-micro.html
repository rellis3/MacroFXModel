Theta Decay, the Rent You Pay for Convexity

Theta isn't a separate fact bolted onto an option after the fact — it falls straight out of the same Black-Scholes PDE that already gives delta and gamma. Rearrange that one equation and "options lose value over time" turns into a precise number, and "theta pays for gamma" stops being a slogan and becomes an identity: Θ ≈ −½σ²S²Γ.

Section 3

The key insight — theta pays for gamma

Strip a delta-hedged position down: no net delta to finance, and the rC term is small next to the convexity term for a reasonably-dated option. What's left:

Θ ≈ −½σ²S²Γ

A delta-hedged, long-gamma position bleeds theta at a rate directly proportional to its own gamma — not a coincidence, but the PDE forcing them to move together. That bleed is the fair price of convexity. The mirror-image dealer who sold the option is short gamma and, by the same equation with the opposite sign, long theta: they collect exactly what the long-gamma side pays.

Realized vol > implied vol

Gamma-scalping gains from continuous rebalancing exceed the theta paid. The long-gamma, delta-hedged position profits — it paid rent for convexity worth more than the rent charged.

Realized vol < implied vol

Rebalancing gains fall short of the theta bled away. The position loses money on net, even with a perfectly executed hedge and zero directional error.

Whether the rent was fairly priced comes down to exactly one comparison: implied volatility (baked into theta) versus realized volatility (how much the underlying actually moved). Nothing about direction enters this decision at all.

Cross-checking against the gamma-theta identity

Plug the previous lesson's own Γ_ATM values into Θ ≈ −½σ²S²Γ = −200×Γ (since ½(0.04)(10,000) = 200) and compare to the exact closed form.

DTE 1 — Γ = 0.3811 → −200×Γ = −76.22 vs. full closed-form −77.22 (residual ≈ −1.0)

The delta-hedged approximation reproduces the full closed-form theta almost exactly at every expiry, with a near-constant residual of about −$1.00/year — precisely the small financing term (rC − rSΔ) the key insight set aside for intuition. Not a coincidence baked into round numbers; it's the PDE identity doing exactly what it says.

Why the residual doesn't shrink too

The residual is dominated by rC, the risk-free rate times the option's own value. With r = σ²/2 in these round numbers, N(d₂) stays fixed at 0.5 at every τ, keeping the financing term's shape stable across these particular expiries even as the convexity term changes by more than 5× — a feature of this parameter choice, not a claim the residual is always constant.

Section 8

Weekend theta and calendar spreads

Try it

The closed form treats time as continuous — every calendar day contributes the same fraction of √τ's shrinkage, trading day or not. In practice, some desks charge theta non-uniformly around a weekend, since two calendar days pass with no trading and often lower realized volatility over that stretch.

This is a genuine market-practice detail worth knowing exists — but it is a convention, not a single universal rule. Different desks and platforms handle it differently. Treat it as "be aware this exists and can differ," not a formula to memorize.

A calendar spread sells a near-dated option and buys a longer-dated option at the same strike. Both legs have similar delta and gamma near the money, but the near leg's smaller τ gives it a much larger 1/√τ — so it decays faster in dollar terms than the far leg.

As long as the underlying stays reasonably close to the strike, the position's net theta is positive — collecting the acceleration differential between the two legs, not a directional bet. The main risk: a big enough move away from the strike hurts the near leg's relative gamma disadvantage more than the collected theta compensates for — the same gamma-theta tradeoff, expressed across two expiries instead of one option.

Section 10

How practitioners actually use this

📉

Gamma scalping

A long-gamma, delta-hedged trader rebalances continuously and compares the resulting trading gains to theta paid — the realized-vs-implied comparison, run live rather than as a one-time calculation.

📅

Calendar spreads

Selling the faster-decaying near leg against a longer-dated leg at the same strike harvests the 1/√τ acceleration differential directly — a structural theta trade, not a directional one.

🗓️

Weekend theta conventions

Desks and platforms that charge theta non-uniformly around a weekend adjust the continuous-time model's assumption that every calendar day contributes equally — worth knowing without assuming any one convention is universal.

Common pitfalls

Theta is not linear — don't extrapolate today's rate across the whole month; the daily rate itself roughly doubles, then accelerates further, near expiry. The delta-hedged approximation drops a real term — extremely accurate when the financing term is small, but for a deep in-the-money option or a long time to expiry, that dropped term stops being negligible.

Section 11

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Why is theta not an "extra" assumption layered on top of delta and gamma?

Substituting Θ = ∂V/∂t, Δ = ∂V/∂S, Γ = ∂²V/∂S² into the no-arbitrage PDE and isolating Θ gives Θ = rC − rSΔ − ½σ²S²Γ directly — nothing new needs to be estimated once C, Δ, and Γ are known.
Question 2

What does "theta pays for gamma" mean, precisely?

A long-gamma, delta-hedged position's theta bleed is, to a close approximation, its own gamma scaled by a constant. That rent is offset, on average, by rebalancing gains — if realized volatility matches the implied volatility the rent was priced on.
Question 3

Why does theta accelerate near expiry, rather than decay in a straight line?

Since Θ ≈ −½σ²S²Γ and Γ ∝ 1/√τ, theta is forced to inherit gamma's acceleration — the worked example showed roughly 2.0× then 2.6× stepwise jumps going 30→7→1 DTE.
Question 4

What does a calendar spread (sell near-dated, buy far-dated, same strike) actually collect?

The near leg's smaller τ gives it substantially larger daily theta than the far leg's larger τ. As long as the underlying stays reasonably close to the strike, the spread nets positive theta from that acceleration gap.
Question 5

Why does "dealers are typically short gamma" force "dealers are typically long theta," rather than these being two separate observations?

Substituting the DealerGEX bracket into the dealer's own delta-hedged theta identity gives ThetaExp_k ≈ −50σ²×DealerGEX_k. Since σ² can never be negative, −50σ² is always negative, so the sign of ThetaExp always flips relative to DealerGEX — a structural consequence of one PDE, not two empirical facts.

What to carry forward

01

Theta falls directly out of the Black-Scholes PDE: Θ = rC − rSΔ − ½σ²S²Γ — an algebraic consequence of the same equation that gives delta and gamma, not an independent fact.

02

The gamma-theta identity is the practical core: for a delta-hedged position, Θ ≈ −½σ²S²Γ — theta is the fair rent for convexity, settled by realized vs. implied volatility.

03

Theta accelerates near expiry via the same 1/√τ term as gamma — the worked example showed the daily rate roughly doubling from 30→7 DTE, then accelerating again into the final day.

04

05

06

What this now lets you do

Reproduce an option's theta from its price, delta, and gamma alone, with no separate estimation. Read a long-gamma position's theta bleed as rent for convexity, and judge whether that rent was worth it from realized vs. implied volatility. Recognise that "dealers are short gamma" and "dealers are long theta" are the identical claim, not two independent facts about how options markets behave.
