Glosten-Milgrom: Sequential Bayesian Market Making

A market maker facing one anonymous order at a time never learns who it came from — only whether it was a buy or a sell. That single bit of information is enough, applied through nothing but Bayes' rule, to derive a bid-ask spread from scratch, and to explain why quotes tighten as a session's trades accumulate and widen again right before the next scheduled surprise.

TL;DR
  • Glosten-Milgrom (1985) is a discrete, one-trader-at-a-time model: each arrival is informed (probability \(\mu\)) or noise (probability \(1-\mu\)), and the market maker's whole job is picking a bid and ask via Bayes' rule — a completely different mechanism from Kyle's Lambda's continuous, size-driven linear price impact, already covered in this Theory Lab.
  • A trade is evidence, exactly the way this curriculum's Bayesian Inference lesson defines evidence: \(P(V{=}V_H\mid\text{buy}) = P(\text{buy}\mid V_H)P(V_H)/P(\text{buy})\), and because informed traders correlate their side with the truth while noise traders don't, every single buy or sell moves the market maker's belief \(\delta\).
  • Zero-expected-profit pricing forces Ask \(=E[V\mid\text{buy}]\) above \(E[V]\) and Bid \(=E[V\mid\text{sell}]\) below it — the spread is the market maker's exact insurance premium against adverse selection, and it collapses to zero the moment \(\mu=0\): no informed traders, no adverse selection, no spread.
  • Worked numbers used throughout (\(\delta_0=0.5\), \(\mu=0.3\), \(V_H=105\), \(V_L=95\)): the opening quote is Ask 101.50 / Bid 98.50 (spread 3.00); after a sequence of buy, buy, sell the quote tightens to 102.75/100.00/103.65/101.50 and back to 102.75/100.00 — spreads of 3.00 → 2.75 → 2.15 → 2.75, verified by hand and by the live calculator below.
  • This produces a genuine, testable prediction: quoted spreads should widen ahead of scheduled high-information events (a central bank decision, a jobs report) and narrow afterward as the resulting trades reveal the news — a real, documented empirical pattern, not a theoretical curiosity.
Section 01

Why this matters — and why it isn't Kyle's Lambda again

Market Microstructure: Price Impact & the Bid-Ask Spread, already in this Theory Lab, covers two 1985 results side by side: Glosten-Milgrom's spread and Kyle's Lambda's price impact. That lesson introduces Glosten-Milgrom in its simplest possible form — a single quote, a flat 50/50 prior on \(V\) that never moves, informed share written \(\alpha\). This lesson is that same model taken all the way to what actually makes it interesting: a market maker who requotes after every single trade, starting from whatever they believed a moment ago, not a fixed 50/50 reset each time. Setting the prior to exactly 0.5 in every formula below reproduces that earlier lesson's Spread \(=\alpha(V_H-V_L)\) result exactly — confirming these are the same model, not two different ones — but a market maker who only ever quotes once, from a fixed prior, has thrown away the single most practically useful thing this model predicts: that spreads move, systematically, as trading itself reveals information.

It is worth being precise about how different this model's machinery is from Kyle's, since both live in the same lesson elsewhere in this curriculum and are easy to blur together. Kyle's Lambda is a single informed trader continuously choosing an order size to disguise their information inside random noise-trader volume, and the object it produces is a linear, continuous price-impact function, \(\Delta p=\lambda Q\) — price moves smoothly with the size of net order flow. Glosten-Milgrom has no notion of size at all. Traders arrive one at a time, in discrete sequence; each is either informed or noise, full stop; and the market maker's entire task each time is simply to pick a bid and an ask via Bayesian updating before the next arrival shows up. There is no impact function here — only a bid and an ask, revised, one trade at a time, forever. Kyle explains why a bigger order costs more. Glosten-Milgrom explains something more fundamental: why there is a gap between bid and ask at all, for an order of any size whatsoever — and it does this with no inventory-holding cost and no explicit order-processing fee anywhere in the model. Pure adverse selection is sufficient, by itself, to produce a spread.

Kyle's Lambda (already covered)

One insider chooses order size continuously to hide inside noise volume. Output: a linear price-impact coefficient \(\lambda=\sigma_v/(2\sigma_u)\). Answers: "how much does trading more cost?"

Glosten-Milgrom (this lesson)

Traders arrive one at a time, each simply informed or noise. Output: a discrete bid and ask, re-set by Bayes' rule after every arrival. Answers: "why is there a gap between bid and ask in the first place?"

💡
Plain-English intuition

A market maker who learns something from every single ticket

Picture a market maker who has no continuous price feed, no chart, no news terminal — the only information they get, ever, is a stream of orders arriving one at a time, each one either a buy or a sell. They cannot see who sent it. Some fraction of arrivals, call it \(\mu\), are traders who genuinely know whether the asset is really worth more (\(V_H\)) or less (\(V_L\)) than the current quote — and an informed trader who knows this will only ever buy when it's actually worth more, and only ever sell when it's actually worth less. The rest of the arrivals, \(1-\mu\) of them, are ordinary liquidity traders buying or selling for reasons that have nothing to do with the asset's true value — a treasury desk converting cashflows, a retail order, portfolio rebalancing — and they flip a mental coin, buying or selling with equal probability regardless of the truth.

The market maker's entire problem is this: every order they see is slightly more likely to be a buy if the asset is really worth more, and slightly more likely to be a sell if it's really worth less — simply because the informed traders are in there, always pushing in the "correct" direction. That means every single order is evidence, whether the market maker wants it to be or not. A market maker who ignores this and quotes one flat "best guess" price will get run over by the informed traders every time, because informed traders only ever show up on the side of a trade that's bad for the market maker. The fix, exactly as in the Kyle's-Lambda lesson's own framing of the problem, is to quote two prices — a bid below the market maker's best guess, an ask above it — sized so that what's made from noise traders passing through the gap offsets what's lost to informed traders on the other side. What Glosten-Milgrom adds beyond that single insight is the machinery to say exactly how wide that gap needs to be, and — because the market maker's belief keeps shifting after every trade — exactly how it should change, quote by quote, as the session goes on.

Section 02

The math, step by step

1. Setting up the model, precisely

There is a true, fundamental value of the asset, \(V\), which takes one of two possible values: a high value \(V_H\) or a low value \(V_L\), with \(V_H>V_L\). Unlike the earlier, simplified treatment, the market maker's belief that \(V=V_H\) is a genuine variable that moves over time — call it \(\delta\), starting from some prior \(\delta_0\) before any trades are observed. Traders arrive one at a time, in sequence, and each arrival is one of exactly two types:

Informed traderArrives with probability \(\mu\). Knows \(V\) exactly. Always trades in the profitable direction: buys with certainty if \(V=V_H\), sells with certainty if \(V=V_L\).
Noise (liquidity) traderArrives with probability \(1-\mu\). Trades for reasons unrelated to \(V\): buys or sells with probability \(\tfrac12\) each, regardless of the truth.
\(\delta\)The market maker's current belief that \(V=V_H\). Starts at a prior \(\delta_0\) and is updated by Bayes' rule after every single trade — the variable this lesson adds on top of the single-shot treatment elsewhere in this Theory Lab.
\(\mu\)The (assumed known, or estimated from context) proportion of arriving order flow that is informed. Held fixed across the whole sequence below — only \(\delta\) moves.

The market maker cannot see which type just arrived — only whether the order was a buy or a sell — and must post a bid and an ask before that arrival shows up, using only whatever \(\delta\) currently is. As before, assume the market maker is risk-neutral and competitive: free entry from rival market makers drives expected profit on every quote to exactly zero, which pins the bid and ask down to the market maker's best updated estimate of \(V\) conditional on the trade that's about to happen.

2. The Bayesian update, worked out in full

This is a direct application of the machinery already derived in Bayesian Inference & Updating — the same \(P(H\mid E) = P(E\mid H)P(H)/P(E)\), the same law-of-total- probability denominator, nothing new assumed. Let \(H\) be the hypothesis "\(V=V_H\)" and \(E\) be the evidence "the order just observed was a buy." First, the two likelihoods — how likely a buy is, under each possible true value of \(V\):

\[ P(\text{buy}\mid V_H) = \mu\cdot 1 + (1-\mu)\cdot\tfrac12 \qquad\qquad P(\text{buy}\mid V_L) = \mu\cdot 0 + (1-\mu)\cdot\tfrac12 \]
μ·1an informed trader buys with certainty when V is really high
μ·0an informed trader never buys when they know V is really low
(1−μ)·½ (both)a noise trader buys half the time regardless of the truth

Read this exactly as the asymmetry it is: if \(V\) is truly high, a buy can come from an informed trader (who always buys in this state) or a noise trader who happened to buy; if \(V\) is truly low, a buy can only come from a noise trader — an informed trader would never buy something they know is overvalued. That asymmetry, entirely caused by \(\mu\), is the whole engine of the model: whenever \(\mu>0\), a buy is strictly more likely under \(V_H\) than under \(V_L\), which is exactly what "a trade is evidence" means in Bayesian terms.

Apply the law of total probability (the same expansion \(P(E)=P(E\mid H)P(H)+P(E\mid\lnot H)P(\lnot H)\) from the Bayesian Inference lesson) to get the unconditional probability of a buy at all, using the market maker's current belief \(\delta\) as the prior:

\[ P(\text{buy}) = P(\text{buy}\mid V_H)\,\delta + P(\text{buy}\mid V_L)\,(1-\delta) \]
δthe market maker's belief right now — the prior for this update, whatever trade came before it

Now Bayes' rule itself, exactly the form derived in the Bayesian Inference lesson:

\[ P(V_H \mid \text{buy}) = \frac{P(\text{buy}\mid V_H)\,\delta}{P(\text{buy})} \]
P(V_H | buy)the posterior — updated belief that V is high, after seeing this one buy
P(buy | V_H)·δnumerator: likelihood times prior, exactly as derived in the Bayesian Inference lesson

And the mirror-image update for a sell — same law of total probability, same Bayes' rule, with \(P(\text{sell}\mid V_H)=1-P(\text{buy}\mid V_H)\) and \(P(\text{sell}\mid V_L)=1-P(\text{buy}\mid V_L)\):

\[ P(V_H \mid \text{sell}) = \frac{P(\text{sell}\mid V_H)\,\delta}{P(\text{sell})}, \qquad P(\text{sell}) = P(\text{sell}\mid V_H)\,\delta + P(\text{sell}\mid V_L)\,(1-\delta) \]
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Every trade is a little clue, and Bayes' rule is just the exact arithmetic for how big a clue it is. A buy is a slightly stronger clue for "the asset is really worth more" than for "it's really worth less" — because some of the buyers actually know something, and knowing-buyers only ever buy when it's genuinely worth more. The market maker doesn't need to know which buyer just showed up to still correctly update their guess, using nothing but how much more common buys are in the "really worth more" world than in the "really worth less" world.

3. From the posterior to the quote — and why a spread exists at all

Zero-expected-profit pricing says the ask must equal the market maker's expected value of \(V\) given that a buy is about to arrive; set it any lower and informed buyers eat the market maker alive, any higher and a rival market maker undercuts the quote. Using the posterior derived above:

\[ \text{Ask} = E[V\mid\text{buy}] = P(V_H\mid\text{buy})\,V_H + \big(1-P(V_H\mid\text{buy})\big)\,V_L \]
\[ \text{Bid} = E[V\mid\text{sell}] = P(V_H\mid\text{sell})\,V_H + \big(1-P(V_H\mid\text{sell})\big)\,V_L \]
Askthe market maker's sell price — pinned to the belief conditional on a buy having just arrived
Bidthe market maker's buy price — the mirror image, conditional on a sell

Because \(\mu\ge 0\), \(P(\text{buy}\mid V_H)\ge P(\text{buy}\mid V_L)\) always, with equality only at \(\mu=0\). That single inequality is everything: it guarantees \(P(V_H\mid\text{buy}) \ge \delta\) and \(P(V_H\mid\text{sell}) \le \delta\) — a buy can only ever raise the market maker's belief in \(V_H\), a sell can only ever lower it — which in turn guarantees Ask \(\ge E[V] \ge\) Bid, with the unconditional current expectation \(E[V]=\delta V_H + (1-\delta)V_L\) sitting exactly between them. The spread, Ask minus Bid, is strictly positive whenever \(\mu>0\) — pure adverse selection is sufficient on its own to produce a spread, with no inventory-holding cost and no order-processing fee anywhere in this derivation.

The edge case is the whole proof. Set \(\mu=0\) — no informed traders at all — and \(P(\text{buy}\mid V_H)=P(\text{buy}\mid V_L)=\tfrac12\) exactly: a buy is now completely uninformative, the posterior equals the prior no matter what is observed, and Ask \(=\) Bid \(=E[V]\). Zero informed trading produces exactly zero spread in this model. Every dollar of spread Glosten-Milgrom predicts exists because \(\mu>0\) — it is a direct, calibrated read on how much informed-trading risk the market maker believes they are facing, not an administrative fee bolted on separately.
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

If literally nobody trading against you ever knows more than you do, there's no reason to protect yourself with a gap between your buy and sell price at all — you'd just quote one price and be fine on average. The moment even a few of the people you trade with might genuinely know something you don't, quoting one price becomes a losing bet, and the gap you need to survive grows exactly in proportion to how many of them you think there are.

4. Sequential updating: quotes that move, and a testable prediction

Here is the part the single-shot version of this model, covered elsewhere in this Theory Lab, deliberately leaves out: nothing about Bayes' rule cares whether \(\delta\) came from a textbook prior or from the market maker's own posterior one trade ago. After observing a trade and updating \(\delta\), the market maker simply requotes — posts a brand-new bid and ask for the next arrival, computed from the updated \(\delta\) using the exact same two formulas above. Today's posterior becomes the very next quote's prior, exactly the sequential- updating idea already established in the Bayesian Inference lesson.

Two structural facts fall straight out of the formulas above, without needing any new machinery. First, the spread is widest exactly when \(\delta=\tfrac12\) — maximal uncertainty about \(V\) — and shrinks toward zero as \(\delta\) moves toward either 0 or 1, because a market maker who is already nearly certain what \(V\) is has very little left to be adversely selected on. Second, because each buy pushes \(\delta\) away from \(\tfrac12\) (toward more certainty about \(V_H\)) and each sell pushes it the other way, a run of same-direction trades drives \(\delta\) steadily away from indifference and the quoted spread narrows as the sequence accumulates — right up until a trade in the other direction arrives, which pulls \(\delta\) back toward \(\tfrac12\) and widens the spread again. Narrowing is not automatic trade-by-trade; it is what happens, on average, as evidence keeps accumulating in one direction — and the worked example below shows both effects concretely, including the widening-back-up case.

This yields a genuine, testable, real-world prediction, not just a theoretical curiosity: quoted spreads should be systematically wider heading into scheduled high-information-asymmetry moments — earnings, a jobs report, a central bank policy decision — precisely because more participants are trading on private forecasts, faster models, or simply anticipation of the outcome before it's public, which is exactly what a spike in the market maker's perceived \(\mu\) means in this model. Immediately afterward, the burst of trading that follows the release is the market rapidly working through exactly the sequential update derived above: each print pushes \(\delta\) further from indifference, the residual uncertainty about the "true" post-news value collapses fast, and the spread compresses accordingly. This is a well-documented empirical pattern in real markets — spreads visibly widen pre-announcement and compress post-announcement — and it is exactly what this section's math predicts, not an empirical coincidence layered on afterward.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Right before a big scheduled announcement, more of the people trading might already have a good guess what it'll say — so the market maker widens the gap to protect against them. The instant the announcement drops and people start actually trading on it, each trade is a strong, consistent clue, and the market maker's guess firms up fast — so the gap shrinks again almost as quickly as it widened, trade by trade, exactly like turning a dial rather than flipping a switch.

Worked example

A full sequence, computed by hand: buy, buy, sell

Take \(\delta_0=0.5\) (fully uncertain prior), \(\mu=0.3\) (30% of arrivals are informed), \(V_H=105\), \(V_L=95\). First the two likelihoods, fixed for the whole sequence:

\[ P(\text{buy}\mid V_H)=0.3(1)+0.7(0.5)=0.65 \qquad P(\text{buy}\mid V_L)=0.3(0)+0.7(0.5)=0.35 \]

Opening quote, from \(\delta_0=0.5\):

\[ P(\text{buy}) = 0.65(0.5)+0.35(0.5) = 0.500 \qquad P(V_H\mid\text{buy}) = \frac{0.65(0.5)}{0.500} = 0.6500 \]
\[ \text{Ask}_0 = 0.65(105)+0.35(95) = 101.50 \qquad \text{Bid}_0 = 0.35(105)+0.65(95) = 98.50 \qquad \text{Spread}_0 = 3.00 \]
δ₀ = 0.50maximal uncertainty — the widest this quote will ever be under these parameters
Spread₀ = 3.00matches μ(V_H−V_L)=0.3×10=3.00 exactly, confirming the δ=0.5 special case

Trade 1 arrives: a BUY. The market maker updates using \(\delta_0=0.5\) as the prior — this is exactly the calculation above, so \(\delta_1 = P(V_H\mid\text{buy}) = 0.6500\).

New quote, from \(\delta_1=0.65\):

\[ \text{Ask}_1 = \frac{0.65^2(105) + 0.35^2(95)}{0.65^2+0.35^2}= 102.75 \qquad \text{Bid}_1 = \frac{0.65(0.35)(105)+0.35(0.65)(95)}{0.65(0.35)+0.35(0.65)} = 100.00 \]
\[ \text{Spread}_1 = 2.75 \]

Trade 2 arrives: another BUY, updating from \(\delta_1=0.65\): \(\delta_2 = P(V_H\mid\text{buy}) = \dfrac{0.65(0.65)}{0.65(0.65)+0.35(0.35)} = \dfrac{0.4225}{0.5450} = 0.7752\).

New quote, from \(\delta_2=0.7752\):

\[ \text{Ask}_2 = 103.65 \qquad \text{Bid}_2 = 101.50 \qquad \text{Spread}_2 = 2.15 \]

Trade 3 arrives: a SELL, updating from \(\delta_2=0.7752\): \(\delta_3 = P(V_H\mid\text{sell}) = \dfrac{0.35(0.7752)}{0.35(0.7752)+0.65(0.2248)} = 0.6500\).

\[ \text{Ask}_3 = 102.75 \qquad \text{Bid}_3 = 100.00 \qquad \text{Spread}_3 = 2.75 \]
Stageδ (belief \(V{=}V_H\)) — Ask — Bid — Spread
Opening0.5000 — 101.50 — 98.50 — 3.00
After buy #10.6500 — 102.75 — 100.00 — 2.75
After buy #20.7752 — 103.65 — 101.50 — 2.15
After sell #30.6500 — 102.75 — 100.00 — 2.75

Two consistent buys shrink the spread monotonically, 3.00 → 2.75 → 2.15, exactly as the "narrows toward certainty" claim above predicts. The sell that follows doesn't send \(\delta\) all the way back to the original 0.50 prior — it lands exactly back at 0.6500, precisely the belief (and precisely the quote) that existed right after the very first buy. That is not a coincidence: expressed as odds, \(\delta/(1-\delta)\), each buy multiplies the odds by the same fixed factor \(0.65/0.35\), and each sell multiplies by its exact reciprocal — so two buys and one sell net out to exactly one buy's worth of evidence, landing precisely where one buy alone would.     Section 03

    How practitioners actually use this

    Nobody at a real desk runs this exact Bayesian update by hand, trade by trade — but the intuition it formalizes shows up constantly, under different names:

    🧪

    Order-flow toxicity metrics

    Measures like VPIN (Volume-Synchronized Probability of Informed Trading) are direct practical descendants of exactly this idea: estimate, from observed order-flow imbalance, how "informed" current flow looks, and widen protective quotes when it looks more toxic — the empirical cousin of watching \(\mu\) rise.

    ⚡

    Algorithmic requoting after every print

    Electronic market makers update quotes essentially trade-by-trade, not on a fixed clock — a direct, practical echo of Glosten-Milgrom's discrete "requote after every arrival" structure, as distinct from Kyle's continuous impact curve.

    📅

    Pre/post-event spread scheduling

    Desks mechanically widen spreads ahead of scheduled releases and watch them compress in the minutes after, precisely because the perceived informed share and the market's residual uncertainty both spike beforehand and both resolve fast once the first wave of trades has priced the news in.

    🎯
    Real-world trading scenario

    Watching a G10 pair's spread breathe around a rate decision

    In the hour before a scheduled central bank decision, a market maker quoting a major FX pair widens their spread well beyond its normal level — not because liquidity has physically vanished the way it does in Kyle's thin-session example, but because they believe a materially higher share of the flow crossing their book in this window is trading on a private read of the likely outcome. That is a rise in the market maker's perceived \(\mu\), exactly as this lesson's model defines it, and the wider spread is the exact protective response the zero-expected-profit condition demands. See Central Bank Policy for what actually drives the outcome uncertainty behind this.

    The moment the decision is announced, order flow surges — and, crucially, it is no longer a coin flip: the initial wave of trades is heavily one-sided, reflecting genuine reaction to the news. Each of those early trades is powerful evidence in the Bayesian sense derived above, and \(\delta\) (or its real-world analogue, the market's residual uncertainty about the new fair value) collapses fast over the next several prints. Spreads compress accordingly, often within minutes — the exact sequential-updating mechanism from Section 02, playing out live rather than as a hand-worked example.

    ⚠️
    Common pitfalls

    Where this model is misread or over-applied

    Confusing \(\mu\) and \(\delta\). \(\mu\) is a fixed parameter — the assumed share of informed arrivals — and does not move trade to trade in this lesson's setup. \(\delta\) is the variable that actually updates: the market maker's belief about \(V\). Mixing them up is an easy notational trap, since both are probabilities between 0 and 1 that both matter to the quote.

    Treating Glosten-Milgrom and Kyle's Lambda as competing explanations for the same thing. They aren't. Glosten-Milgrom explains the existence and size of a discrete bid-ask gap from a sequence of unit trades; Kyle explains how the price concession scales with order size in a continuous setting. A real quoted spread and real observed slippage can both be present, driven by both mechanisms simultaneously, without either model needing to be "wrong."

    Assuming the spread narrows monotonically, trade after trade. The worked example above shows this directly: two confirming buys narrow the spread, but the very next contrarian sell widens it back up. Narrowing is a statement about what tends to happen as evidence accumulates in one direction — not a one-way ratchet that can only ever tighten as a session goes on.

    Reading the whole observed spread as pure adverse-selection premium. Exactly the same caveat already raised in the Kyle's Lambda lesson applies here: real quoted spreads also bake in order-processing costs and inventory-risk compensation (Ho-Stoll-style inventory models, not covered in either lesson). Glosten-Milgrom isolates one real, provable component of the spread — it is not a claim that adverse selection is the only thing in it.

    Treating \(\mu\) and \(\delta_0\) as directly observable. Neither is handed to a real market maker on a plate. In practice both are estimated — from historical order-flow patterns, from a scheduled calendar of information events, or from toxicity proxies like VPIN — and every conclusion drawn from this model inherits whatever uncertainty exists in those estimates.

    Section 04

    Key Takeaways

    ✓Glosten-Milgrom and Kyle's Lambda are structurally different models. One is discrete, sequential, single-order, and produces a bid/ask via Bayesian updating; the other is continuous, size-driven, and produces a linear impact coefficient. Both are 1985 results, and neither is a special case of the other.
    ✓A trade is Bayesian evidence, derived exactly as this curriculum's Bayesian Inference lesson defines evidence. \(P(V_H\mid\text{buy})=P(\text{buy}\mid V_H)\delta/P(\text{buy})\), with the asymmetry between \(P(\text{buy}\mid V_H)\) and \(P(\text{buy}\mid V_L)\) generated entirely by \(\mu\).
    ✓Pure adverse selection is sufficient to produce a spread — no inventory or order-processing cost required. At \(\mu=0\) the spread collapses to exactly zero; the spread's entire existence in this model is a function of \(\mu>0\).
    ✓The market maker requotes after every trade, using the previous quote's posterior as the new prior. Worked numbers: \(\delta_0{=}0.5,\mu{=}0.3,V_H{=}105,V_L{=}95\) gives an opening spread of 3.00, narrowing to 2.75 then 2.15 after two confirming buys, and widening back to 2.75 after one contrarian sell — verified independently in Node.js.
    ⚠Narrowing is not a one-way ratchet. Spreads shrink as evidence accumulates consistently in one direction and widen back on new, conflicting evidence — the model predicts genuine oscillation, not monotonic tightening.
    ⚠The testable, real-world prediction: spreads widen ahead of scheduled information events and compress after. Both because \(\mu\) is believed higher pre-event and because the resulting trades sharply resolve \(\delta\) post-event — a genuine, documented empirical pattern, not solely a theoretical artifact.
    Section 05

    Self-test

    Q1. What is the single most fundamental structural difference between Kyle's Lambda and Glosten-Milgrom, as market microstructure models?
    Kyle's Lambda is a continuous model in which one informed trader chooses an order size to disguise their information inside random noise-trader volume, producing a linear price-impact function \(\Delta p=\lambda Q\) that moves smoothly with net order flow. Glosten-Milgrom is a discrete, sequential model with no notion of order size at all: traders arrive one at a time, each is simply informed or noise, and the market maker's job is to post a bid and ask via Bayesian updating before each arrival — with no continuous impact function, only discrete quotes revised trade by trade. They answer different questions: Kyle explains why bigger orders cost more; Glosten-Milgrom explains why a bid-ask gap exists in the first place, for an order of any size.
    Q2. Why does observing a single buy order necessarily raise the market maker's posterior belief that \(V=V_H\), for any \(\mu>0\)?
    Because \(P(\text{buy}\mid V_H)=\mu+\tfrac{1-\mu}{2}\) is strictly greater than \(P(\text{buy}\mid V_L)=\tfrac{1-\mu}{2}\) whenever \(\mu>0\) — an informed trader always buys when \(V=V_H\) and never buys when \(V=V_L\), while noise traders buy with equal probability regardless. That asymmetry means a buy is inherently more consistent with the world in which \(V=V_H\) than with the world in which \(V=V_L\), and Bayes' rule mechanically converts a likelihood asymmetry like that into a posterior shift in the same direction — exactly the same logic already established in the Bayesian Inference lesson.
    Q3. Using \(\delta_0=0.5\), \(\mu=0.3\), \(V_H=105\), \(V_L=95\), walk through computing the opening ask.
    \(P(\text{buy}\mid V_H)=0.3(1)+0.7(0.5)=0.65\) and \(P(\text{buy}\mid V_L)=0.3(0)+0.7(0.5)=0.35\). With \(\delta_0=0.5\): \(P(\text{buy})=0.65(0.5)+0.35(0.5)=0.500\), and \(P(V_H\mid\text{buy})=0.65(0.5)/0.500=0.6500\). The ask is \(E[V\mid\text{buy}]=0.65(105)+0.35(95)=68.25+33.25=\mathbf{101.50}\).
    Q4. Why doesn't Glosten-Milgrom need any inventory-holding cost or order-processing fee to produce a positive spread — and what happens to the spread if \(\mu=0\)?
    The spread here is derived purely from the zero-expected-profit condition applied to a market maker who is being adversely selected: Ask \(=E[V\mid\text{buy}]\ge E[V]\) and Bid \(=E[V\mid\text{sell}]\le E[V]\) follow directly from the fact that a buy is more likely under \(V_H\) than \(V_L\) (and the mirror for sell) — nothing about holding inventory or processing an order enters the derivation at all. If \(\mu=0\), then \(P(\text{buy}\mid V_H)=P(\text{buy}\mid V_L)=\tfrac12\) exactly, a trade carries no information whatsoever, the posterior always equals the prior, and Ask \(=\) Bid \(=E[V]\) — the spread is exactly zero.
    Q5. In the worked sequence (buy, buy, sell), why doesn't the third trade — a sell — send \(\delta\) all the way back to the original 0.50 prior?
    Expressed as odds, \(\delta/(1-\delta)\), each buy multiplies the current odds by the fixed factor \(0.65/0.35\) and each sell multiplies by the exact reciprocal, \(0.35/0.65\) — buy and sell are mirror-image evidence of equal strength in opposite directions. Two buys followed by one sell therefore net out to exactly one buy's worth of evidence (two multiplications by the buy factor, one by its reciprocal, leaves one factor of the buy multiplier remaining), which is why \(\delta\) after buy-buy-sell lands at exactly 0.6500 — precisely the belief (and precisely the quote) that existed right after the very first buy alone, not back at the original 0.50.
    Q6. What genuine, testable real-world prediction does sequential Bayesian updating make about spreads around a scheduled event like a central bank decision?
    Quoted spreads should be systematically wider in the period leading up to a scheduled high-information-asymmetry event, because the market maker's perceived share of informed order flow, \(\mu\), is believed to spike as more participants trade ahead of the outcome. Immediately after the announcement, the resulting burst of one-sided, information- revealing trades drives a fast sequence of Bayesian updates that pulls \(\delta\) rapidly away from indifference, collapsing residual uncertainty about the new fair value — and the spread should compress accordingly, often within minutes. This is a real, well-documented empirical pattern in markets around events such as central bank decisions, not merely a theoretical artifact of the model.
