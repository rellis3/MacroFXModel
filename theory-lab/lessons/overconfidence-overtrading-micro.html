The Human Side · Lesson 3 of 6

Overconfidence, and the Cost of Trading Too Much

Most traders think their next trade is better than average. Tens of thousands of real brokerage accounts show what that belief costs. The bill doesn't come from one big mistake. It comes from turnover, a small fee paid every time you act.

Section 1 · The measurement

Your 90% ranges are probably 60% ranges

Calibration: if you say "90% sure", you should be right about 90% of the time.

Calibrated

Ten 90% ranges → about 9 hits. Misses happen, at the rate you said they would.

Typical

Ten 90% ranges → often only 5 or 6 hits. The guesses aren't silly. The fences are drawn too tight.

US finance chiefs gave 80% ranges for the next year's stock-market return. The actual return landed inside them only 36% of the time (Ben-David, Graham & Harvey, 2013).
Section 2 · Try it

Give six 90% ranges, then score yourself

No searching. Wide is allowed if that's how unsure you honestly are. Your answers stay on this device.

Calibration quiz
Fill in a low and a high, then score.
A calibrated person gets all six about 53% of the time, so one miss proves nothing. Three or more misses happen to a calibrated person under 2% of the time.
Section 3 · The evidence

"Trading Is Hazardous to Your Wealth"

Barber & Odean (2000): 66,465 households at a US discount broker, 1991–96, every trade on record.

Market
17.9%/yr
Average household
16.4% net

11.4% net

The average household turned over about 75% of its portfolio a year. Before costs the active traders' picks were roughly as good as everyone else's. The gap opened after the spread and commission, paid again and again.

Boys Will Be Boys (2001)

Same broker's data, split by gender. Men traded 45% more than women. Trading cut men's net returns by 2.65 points a year, against 1.72 for women. That is what overconfidence predicts, since men tend to be more overconfident about finance.

Section 5 · What feeds it

Two quieter biases

🎟

Illusion of control

Langer (1975): people who picked their own lottery ticket asked for substantially more to give it up than people handed one, though the odds were identical. Clicking, adjusting and watching feel like improving the odds.

↺

Hindsight bias

Fischhoff (1975): once people know how events turned out, they rate that outcome as more predictable than they did beforehand. Every chart looks obvious from the right-hand edge, so the future feels predictable too.

★

Self-attribution

Wins are credited to skill, losses to luck. So a winning run raises confidence, and confidence raises turnover.

Section 6 · It actually happened

The 1990s move online: doing well, then trading more

It actually happened

Barber & Odean (2002) followed investors who switched from phone to online trading in the 1990s. They had been beating the market before the switch. Afterwards they traded more actively and more speculatively, and fell behind the market. The paper's title: Do the Slow Die First?

Day trading is the extreme case. In Taiwan, where every trade by every investor is recorded, Barber, Lee, Liu & Odean (2014) found fewer than 1% of day traders could be shown to profit reliably, year after year, net of fees.

Section 8 · What this desk found

Costs are a wall; confidence is not an edge

The folk claim: "if I'm confident, acting on it more often makes money."

Desk test: held up execution-gate: with spread ÷ ATR above 0.15, zero of 30 strategy × timeframe cells were profitable. Cost outruns edge as the timeframe speeds up. Faster charts mean more trades, each costing more as a share of the move.
Desk test: nothing found conviction-vote: one of the desk's own systems scored its conviction on every entry. The vote was anti-predictive. The system lost −£38k on a 30% win rate, not on a few deep losses.
Not tested here Retail turnover vs returns in FX and index CFDs. The household figures are US equities, 1990s.
Section 9 · Do this now

Five questions before your next trade

Two or more "yes" answers mean the trade is coming from the feeling, not the evidence.

?

?

Section 10

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Turnover is 400% a year and a round trip costs 0.5%. What's the annual drag?

D = T × k = 4 × 0.5% = 2% a year. The extra trading has to add at least that much over holding, just to break even.
Question 2

Why did the most active households lag the market by about 6.5 points a year?

Before costs their returns were roughly comparable with everyone else's. The 11.4% vs 17.9% gap opened up after costs on very high turnover.
Question 3

You get 5 of 6 on a 90%-range quiz. What does that tell you?

With N = 6 and c = 0.9, one miss is ordinary. Calibration is judged over many forecasts. Three or more misses would be a real warning (under 2% for a calibrated person).
Question 4

You tag trades A/B/C by conviction. When is it safe to size up on "A"?

Unscored confidence is a feeling. The desk scored one of its own systems' conviction vote and found it anti-predictive.
Section 11

What to carry forward

01

Overconfidence is measurable: 90% ranges catch the truth far less than 90% of the time. CFOs' 80% ranges caught it 36% of the time.

02

It costs money through turnover: the most active fifth of 66,465 households earned 11.4% net against the market's 17.9%. Men traded 45% more than women and lost more to it.

03

Drag ≈ turnover × round-trip cost, and it compounds: 2.5 points a year gives up about 43% of a 20-year gain at 10%.

04

Illusion of control and hindsight bias feed the loop; self-attribution turns a winning run into more trading.

05

Desk: cost against the move is a hard wall (execution-gate, validated: above 0.15 spread/ATR, 0 of 30 cells profitable). Confidence is not edge (conviction-vote, null: anti-predictive, net loser).

What this now lets you do

Put a number on your own calibration, price your turnover before you trade, and check whether your confidence tags have earned the size you put on them.
