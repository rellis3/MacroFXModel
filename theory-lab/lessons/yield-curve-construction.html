Bootstrapping the Yield Curve

A single bond's yield to maturity is a blended average across every one of its cash flows' maturities — never the "true" rate for any one point in time. Bootstrapping is the sequential algorithm that untangles a handful of bond prices into a genuine per-maturity zero curve, and the forward rates embedded inside it.

CONCEPT — not built or tested in this repo ~17 min read Prereqs: Bond Pricing & Yield to Maturity (Duration & Convexity helpful but not required)
TL;DR
  • Par rates are the YTM of a bond priced at par; spot (zero) rates are the discount rate for a single cash flow at a single maturity; forward rates are the rate implied, today, for borrowing/lending between two future dates. All three are different things, and only spot rates are the "correct" discount rate for a cash flow of a given maturity.
  • A single bond's YTM is a weighted blend of the zero rates across all of its cash flows' maturities — using one bond's YTM to discount every cash flow on a whole portfolio is a real, common error, because two bonds with the same YTM but different coupons imply different zero curves.
  • Bootstrapping solves this by working from the shortest maturity outward: the shortest instrument's zero rate is solved first (trivial, since it has only one cash flow), then each successive zero rate is solved using only the previously-solved, shorter zero rates plus that maturity's own market price.
  • The forward rate between two zero-rate maturities follows directly from a no-arbitrage identity: \((1+z_2)^2 = (1+z_1)(1+f_{1,2})\) — investing for 2 years directly must equal investing for 1 year and then rolling over into a locked-in 1-year rate.
  • The worked example bootstraps a 3-point zero curve from three hypothetical par bonds (1-year at 3.00%, 2-year at 3.50%, 3-year at 4.00%, all annual coupons, face 100) and gets \(z_1=3.00\%\), \(z_2\approx3.51\%\), \(z_3\approx4.03\%\) — then computes the 1-year-forward-in-1-year rate as \(f_{1,2}\approx4.02\%\) and the 1-year-forward-in-2-years rate as \(f_{2,3}\approx5.07\%\).
  • Because the par/zero curve here is upward sloping, every forward rate sits above the corresponding spot rate for that horizon — forwards embed the market's expectation (under a pure-expectations reading, a simplification covered in the pitfalls) that future short rates will be higher than today's.
Section 01

Why this matters

Lessons 1 and 2 both used a single yield \(y\) to discount every one of a bond's cash flows. That's a convenient simplification for pricing one bond in isolation, but it quietly hides a real problem: a 5-year cash flow and a 1-year cash flow shouldn't actually be discounted at the same rate, because the market doesn't charge the same "rent on money" for one year as it does for five. A full, internally consistent term structure — a genuine rate for every maturity — is what every serious fixed-income pricing, hedging, and relative-value operation is actually built on, and bootstrapping is the standard, foundational technique for building one from a handful of observed bond (or swap) prices.

This lesson also directly motivates the next one in this category: bootstrapping produces a zero rate only at the specific maturities where you happen to have an instrument (here, exactly three points). Pricing or hedging a cash flow that lands between those points requires a smooth curve fitted through them — which is exactly the problem the Nelson-Siegel-Svensson lesson takes on next.

💡
Plain-English intuition

You can't use one average rate to price a payment that isn't due for years

Imagine you're renting storage lockers, and the landlord quotes you one "average" monthly rate for a bundle that includes a locker you can use starting today and a much bigger locker you won't get access to for three years. That blended average rate is a real, honest number for the bundle as a whole — but it would be wrong to use it to answer "what does the 3-year locker alone actually cost, per month?" You'd need the landlord to quote each locker separately, at its own rate, to answer that. A bond's yield to maturity is exactly the blended, bundled rate; a zero rate is the rate for one single locker (one single cash flow, at one single maturity) alone.

Bootstrapping is how you back out those individual locker rates when all the landlord ever quotes you is bundle prices. Start with the simplest bundle — one that's really just a single locker (the shortest bond, which has only one cash flow left) — and its rate is immediately obvious. Then take the next bundle up, which mixes that already-known short locker with one new, longer one; since you already know the short locker's true rate, you can subtract its cost out and isolate the new, longer locker's rate on its own. Keep doing this, one bundle at a time, moving from short to long, and you build up the whole set of individual rates — the zero curve — one solved rate at a time.

Section 02

The math, step by step

Three different "rates" that get confused with each other

Par rate

The coupon rate that would make a bond of a given maturity price exactly at face value (\(P=F\)) — equivalently, the YTM of a par bond of that maturity. It's a real, quotable market rate, but it's a blend across every cash flow the bond pays, not a rate for any single date.

Spot (zero) rate

The discount rate for a single cash flow arriving at a single future date — the rate on a hypothetical zero-coupon bond of that exact maturity. This is the "true," per-maturity rate: the correct discount rate for a cash flow due at time \(t\) is always \(z_t\), never a blended YTM from some other bond.

Forward rate

The rate, agreed today, for lending or borrowing between two future dates — e.g. "the 1-year rate starting one year from now." It's derived entirely from today's zero curve via no-arbitrage; it doesn't require waiting until that future date arrives to know what it is.

Why the distinction matters in practice: a coupon-paying bond's YTM mixes together the discount rates for every one of its cash flows — its year-1 coupon, its year-2 coupon, and so on — into one single blended number. Two bonds of the same maturity but different coupons will, in general, have slightly different YTMs even in a market with one single true zero curve, purely because their cash flows are weighted differently across maturities (a higher-coupon bond gets more of its value back earlier, so its YTM leans more toward short rates; recall this is exactly Lesson 2's duration idea — a bond's YTM sensitivity is itself a cash-flow-weighted quantity). Using one bond's YTM as "the" discount rate for a different cash flow at a different maturity is therefore not just imprecise, it's a genuine mismatch between what the number represents and what it's being used for.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

A par rate is like the average price of a whole grocery basket; a spot rate is the price tag on one single item inside it; a forward rate is what today's shelf prices already tell you an item will probably cost you next month, worked out by comparing prices for different-length "subscriptions." Mixing these up — using the basket's average price to figure out what one item alone costs — is exactly the mistake bootstrapping exists to prevent.

The bootstrapping algorithm

Bootstrapping recovers the true per-maturity zero rates from a set of observed bond (or swap) prices, working strictly from the shortest maturity to the longest, using each newly solved zero rate to help solve the next one:

1

Start with the shortest-maturity instrument

A 1-year bond has exactly one cash flow left (its final coupon plus face value). Its price equation has exactly one unknown, \(z_1\), so it can be solved directly — no bootstrapping needed yet, this step is just ordinary YTM solving from Lesson 1 with \(n=1\).

\[ P_1 = \frac{C_1 + F}{(1+z_1)^1} \quad\Rightarrow\quad z_1 = \frac{C_1+F}{P_1} - 1 \]
2

Move to the next-shortest instrument

A 2-year bond has two cash flows: a coupon at year 1, and a coupon-plus-face at year 2. Its year-1 coupon should be discounted at \(z_1\) — already known from step 1 — leaving exactly one new unknown, \(z_2\), in its price equation:

\[ P_2 = \frac{C_2}{(1+z_1)^1} + \frac{C_2+F}{(1+z_2)^2} \]

Rearranged, this isolates \(z_2\) directly, since every other quantity (\(P_2\), \(C_2\), \(F\), and now \(z_1\)) is known:

\[ z_2 = \left(\frac{C_2+F}{P_2 - \frac{C_2}{1+z_1}}\right)^{1/2} - 1 \]
3

Repeat outward, one maturity at a time

A 3-year bond's year-1 and year-2 coupons are discounted using the already-known \(z_1\) and \(z_2\); the only new unknown left is \(z_3\), solved the same way. In general, at each step, every earlier cash flow uses an already-solved shorter zero rate, and exactly one new unknown — that maturity's own zero rate — remains to be isolated. This is what makes the algorithm "sequential": each solve depends on every solve before it, and cannot be done out of order.

Why this always works, structurally: at every step, the number of unknowns in the price equation is exactly one — the current maturity's own zero rate — because every shorter-maturity cash flow's discount rate was already pinned down at an earlier step. One equation, one unknown, at every stage: that's precisely what makes each individual solve as simple as Lesson 1's single-bond YTM problem, even though the curve being built up is genuinely multi-point.
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Bootstrapping works like paying off a group dinner bill one course at a time. First you figure out exactly what the appetizer cost (the shortest bond — easy, only one cash flow). Once you know that, you can subtract it from the total bill and see exactly what the main course must have cost (the next bond). Keep doing that, course by course, and you eventually know the true price of every single item, even though you only ever saw combined totals.

Forward rates — what the zero curve implies about future rates

Once you have zero rates for two different maturities, you can ask: what rate is implied, today, for lending between those two future dates? Consider two ways to turn one dollar into a value two years from now, using only today's zero rates and a locked-in forward agreement:

Route 1 — invest directly for 2 years at the known 2-year zero rate: one dollar grows to \((1+z_2)^2\).

Route 2 — invest for 1 year, then roll over at a forward rate \(f_{1,2}\) agreed today (but only realized starting in year 1): one dollar grows to \((1+z_1)^1\) after year 1, then to \((1+z_1)(1+f_{1,2})\) after year 2.

Both routes start with one dollar today and involve no risk in between — every rate is either already known (\(z_1, z_2\)) or locked in today (\(f_{1,2}\)). By the same no-arbitrage logic used throughout this curriculum (identical to the covered interest rate parity argument elsewhere in this curriculum's macro/FX theory, applied here to time instead of currencies), both riskless routes must pay the same:

\[ (1+z_2)^2 = (1+z_1)(1+f_{1,2}) \]

Rearranged to isolate the forward rate directly:

\[ f_{1,2} = \frac{(1+z_2)^2}{1+z_1} - 1 \]

This generalizes to any two maturities \(t_1 < t_2\): \((1+z_{t_2})^{t_2} = (1+z_{t_1})^{t_1}(1+f_{t_1,t_2})^{t_2-t_1}\), with the same no-arbitrage argument behind it — investing to \(t_2\) directly must match investing to \(t_1\) and then rolling over at the locked-in forward rate for the remaining period. Forward rates are therefore not an independent market observation to be separately calibrated — they are entirely derived from the zero curve you already bootstrapped, via pure algebra.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

A forward rate is the interest rate today's market is quietly implying for a loan that hasn't started yet. It's like a moving company today quoting you a locked-in price for moving your stuff next year — not because they're psychic about next year's gas prices, but because today's one-year and two-year prices, put together, only make sense if that's the rate for the second year.

Worked example

Bootstrapping a 3-point zero curve from three par bonds

Three hypothetical bonds, each priced exactly at par (\(P=100\), face value \(F=100\)), all paying annual coupons: a 1-year bond with a 3.00% coupon, a 2-year bond with a 3.50% coupon, and a 3-year bond with a 4.00% coupon. Because every bond is priced at par, its coupon rate is its par yield by definition — but, per the distinction above, not automatically its zero rate (except, as it happens, for the 1-year bond).

1

Solve \(z_1\) from the 1-year bond

One cash flow: a $3 coupon plus $100 face, both at year 1, totaling $103. Priced at par ($100):

\[ 100 = \frac{103}{1+z_1} \quad\Rightarrow\quad z_1 = \frac{103}{100} - 1 = 3.00\% \]

For a single-cash-flow instrument, the zero rate and the par yield are one and the same — this is the trivial base case every bootstrap starts from.

2

Solve \(z_2\) from the 2-year bond, using \(z_1\)

Cash flows: $3.50 at year 1, $103.50 at year 2. Discount the year-1 coupon at the now-known \(z_1=3.00\%\):

\[ \frac{3.50}{1.03} = 3.398 \]

Subtract that from the $100 price to isolate the year-2 cash flow's present value, then solve for \(z_2\):

\[ 100 - 3.398 = 96.602 = \frac{103.50}{(1+z_2)^2} \quad\Rightarrow\quad (1+z_2)^2 = \frac{103.50}{96.602} = 1.07141 \]
\[ z_2 = \sqrt{1.07141} - 1 \approx 3.51\% \]

Notice \(z_2 \approx 3.51\%\) is slightly above the 2-year bond's own 3.50% par yield/coupon — a direct, numerical illustration of the point above that a bond's blended YTM and its maturity's true zero rate are two different numbers, even though they're close.

3

Solve \(z_3\) from the 3-year bond, using \(z_1\) and \(z_2\)

Cash flows: $4 at year 1, $4 at year 2, $104 at year 3. Discount the first two coupons at \(z_1\) and \(z_2\):

\[ \frac{4}{1.03} + \frac{4}{(1.0351)^2} \approx 3.883 + 3.733 = 7.617 \]

Subtract from the $100 price and solve for \(z_3\):

\[ 100 - 7.617 = 92.383 = \frac{104}{(1+z_3)^3} \quad\Rightarrow\quad (1+z_3)^3 = \frac{104}{92.383} \approx 1.12572 \]
\[ z_3 = 1.12572^{1/3} - 1 \approx 4.03\% \]
4

Compute the 1-year-forward-in-1-year rate, \(f_{1,2}\)

Using the two now-known zero rates and the forward-rate identity:

\[ f_{1,2} = \frac{(1+z_2)^2}{1+z_1} - 1 = \frac{1.07141}{1.03} - 1 \approx 4.02\% \]

The market's zero curve implies a 1-year rate of about 4.02% starting one year from now — noticeably above today's 1-year rate of 3.00%, and even above today's 2-year zero rate of 3.51%. (The same identity applied one maturity further out gives \(f_{2,3} = (1+z_3)^3/(1+z_2)^2 - 1 \approx 5.07\%\), used in the chart below.)

🎯
Real-world trading scenario

Pricing a swap's floating leg off a bootstrapped curve, and reading the forward curve for a rate view

A rates desk needs to value the floating leg of an interest-rate swap — a series of future floating-rate payments whose size isn't known yet, but whose fair expected value, under standard swap pricing convention, is set equal to the forward rate for each future period. Rather than guessing at those future rates, the desk bootstraps a zero curve from a set of liquid, observable instruments (in practice, deposit rates and swap rates rather than the toy par bonds here — the exact instrument set differs, but the sequential solve is the same algorithm), then reads off each period's forward rate directly using this lesson's identity. This is precisely why bootstrapping matters operationally: without a full per-maturity zero curve, there is no principled way to price a multi-period floating cash flow at all.

Separately, a macro trader reads the shape of the resulting forward curve itself as a market-implied view: an upward-sloping zero curve, of the kind bootstrapped above, produces forward rates above today's spot rates at every horizon — a market that is, in a pure-expectations reading, pricing in higher short rates down the road. If the trader's own view is that rates will rise by less than the forward curve implies, a position that receives the forward rate (is "long" the view that realized rates undershoot the forward) is a direct, standard way to express that view — precisely the same "compare your own forecast to what's already priced in" logic used throughout macro trading, just applied here to the rates curve itself rather than to FX or equities.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

Every discounting and swap-pricing system on a rates desk is built on a bootstrapped (or otherwise constructed) zero curve — not on any single bond's YTM. Modern curve construction in practice typically bootstraps from a blend of short-dated deposit/OIS rates, exchange-traded futures, and swap rates rather than a handful of coupon bonds (the specifics of using overnight-indexed-swap discounting are their own, more involved topic, covered in the swap-curve lesson later in this category), but the underlying sequential logic — solve the shortest point first, use it to solve the next, work steadily outward — is unchanged from the toy example built here.

Forward rates are the market's pricing mechanism for anything settling in the future — FX forwards (per this curriculum's interest rate parity lesson), swap floating legs, forward rate agreements, and futures all reduce, in one way or another, to "what rate does the current curve imply between these two future dates," which is exactly this lesson's forward-rate formula, generalized to whichever underlying is being priced.

The shape of the curve is itself a standard piece of macro signal. A steep, upward-sloping curve (short rates well below long rates) is widely read as the market pricing in future rate hikes or a return to more "normal" rate levels; an inverted curve (short rates above long rates) has, going back decades, been one of the most closely watched recession-warning signals in macro research, precisely because it says the market expects short rates to be cut from here. None of that macro reading is possible without first separating par yields into genuine, per-maturity zero rates the way bootstrapping does.

Key-rate (partial) duration, the finer-grained successor to Lesson 2's single-number duration, is defined directly on a bootstrapped curve: the sensitivity of a portfolio's value to a small bump in one specific zero rate on the curve, holding all the others fixed — precisely the tool needed to hedge the non-parallel curve moves Lesson 2 flagged as outside plain duration's reach.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where this goes wrong in practice

Using a single bond's YTM to discount an unrelated cash flow. This is the exact mistake bootstrapping exists to prevent. A 5-year bond's YTM is a blend across its own 1-through-5-year cash flows — using that blended number to discount, say, an unrelated 5-year liability cash flow (rather than the bootstrapped 5-year zero rate) is a subtle but real source of mispricing, larger the more the discounting bond's coupon and cash-flow profile differ from the cash flow actually being valued.

Treating forward rates as a forecast, not an arbitrage-free identity. A forward rate is exactly what today's zero curve implies under no-arbitrage — it is not, on its own, a probability- weighted market forecast of the future spot rate, and it is not guaranteed to be realized. (This is the same distinction this curriculum's interest rate parity lesson draws between covered and uncovered interest rate parity: the forward computed here is the "covered," no-arbitrage-pinned number; whether it equals the market's true rate expectation is a separate, much less certain claim, and historically an unreliable one for currency forwards specifically.)

Compounding-convention mismatches. This lesson uses simple annual compounding throughout for clarity, matching Lessons 1 and 2. Real curve construction has to be scrupulous about which compounding and day-count convention each input instrument actually uses (annual bond-equivalent, semiannual, continuously-compounded, Actual/360 vs. 30/360, etc.) — mixing conventions across instruments silently corrupts every zero rate solved downstream of the mismatch, the same category of "boring parsing" error this project's own backtest-discipline notes warn about for any data pipeline.

Assuming the shortest few instruments are liquid and reliable. Bootstrapping is strictly sequential — an error or a stale/illiquid price in the shortest-maturity instrument propagates into every zero rate solved after it, since each step depends on every prior step's result. A curve built from a thin, poorly-traded short end is unreliable at every longer maturity too, not just at the short end where the bad input actually sits.

Section 04

Key Takeaways

✓Par, spot, and forward rates are three different things. A par rate is a par bond's blended YTM; a spot (zero) rate is the true rate for one single cash flow at one maturity; a forward rate is implied, today, between two future dates.
✓Bootstrapping solves shortest-to-longest, sequentially. Each new zero rate is the only unknown left once every shorter cash flow is discounted at its own already-solved zero rate.
✓Forward rates fall straight out of a no-arbitrage identity. \((1+z_2)^2=(1+z_1)(1+f_{1,2})\) — investing directly for 2 years must match investing 1 year and rolling into a locked-in forward.
✓An upward-sloping spot curve implies forward rates above spot at every horizon. The worked example's 4.02%/5.07% forwards both sit above their corresponding zero rates.
✓A bond's own YTM is not the right discount rate for someone else's cash flow. Even a par bond's zero rate came out slightly different from its own coupon/par yield in the worked example (3.51% zero vs. 3.50% par).
⚠A forward rate is a no-arbitrage identity, not a forecast. It says nothing, on its own, about what the market actually expects to happen to rates — the same covered-vs-uncovered distinction this curriculum's interest rate parity lesson draws for currencies applies here too.
⚠An error at the short end propagates through the whole curve. Bootstrapping's sequential dependency means a bad short-maturity input corrupts every zero rate solved after it, not just the short end.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. Why can't you just use a 5-year bond's YTM as "the" discount rate for a 5-year cash flow from some other source?
Because the 5-year bond's YTM is a single blended rate that reflects the discounting of all of that bond's cash flows — its 1-year, 2-year, 3-year, 4-year, and 5-year coupons, plus the final face value — not a rate specific to a cash flow arriving in exactly year 5. Two bonds with different coupons but the same 5-year maturity will generally have slightly different YTMs even when there's a single true zero curve underlying both, because their cash flows are weighted differently across maturities. The correct discount rate for a cash flow due at year 5 is the bootstrapped 5-year zero rate, \(z_5\), which applies to that maturity alone.
Q2. Why must bootstrapping proceed from the shortest maturity to the longest, and not in some other order?
Each step's price equation has exactly one unknown — that step's own zero rate — only because every shorter-maturity cash flow inside that instrument has already been discounted using a zero rate solved at an earlier step. If you tried to solve a longer maturity first, you'd have multiple unknown zero rates (the shorter ones plus the new one) in a single equation with no way to isolate any of them. Working strictly shortest-to-longest is what keeps every individual step down to one equation, one unknown.
Q3. In the worked example, why is \(z_2\) (≈3.51%) slightly higher than the 2-year bond's own coupon/par yield of 3.50%?
Because the 2-year bond's par yield of 3.50% is a blended rate across its year-1 coupon and its year-2 (coupon+face) cash flow, while \(z_2\) is the rate for the year-2 cash flow alone. Since the previously-solved 1-year zero rate (3.00%) sits below the 2-year par yield, and the year-1 coupon is discounted at that lower 3.00% rate, more of the "discounting work" has to be done by the year-2 cash flow's own rate to make the bond's total price come out to par — which pushes \(z_2\) slightly above the blended 3.50% par yield. This is a direct, numerical illustration of par rate and zero rate being genuinely different quantities, even for the same maturity.
Q4. Derive, in words, why \((1+z_2)^2 = (1+z_1)(1+f_{1,2})\) has to hold, assuming no arbitrage.
Consider two riskless ways to turn one dollar into a value two years from now using only today's known rates: invest directly for two years at the known 2-year zero rate, ending with \((1+z_2)^2\); or invest for one year at the known 1-year zero rate, then roll the proceeds into a forward rate \(f_{1,2}\) locked in today (even though it only takes effect starting in year 1), ending with \((1+z_1)(1+f_{1,2})\). Both routes start with the same one dollar today and involve no risk in between — every rate used is either already known or locked in in advance. If the two riskless payoffs weren't equal, there would be a genuine, riskless arbitrage: borrow via the cheaper route, invest via the more expensive one, and pocket the difference for free. No such free lunch can persist, so the two payoffs must be equal, giving the identity.
Q5. Is a forward rate the same thing as the market's expected future spot rate? Why does this matter?
No — a forward rate, as derived in this lesson, is purely a no-arbitrage identity computed from today's zero curve; it is guaranteed to hold today by construction, regardless of anyone's beliefs about the future. Whether it also equals the market's true expectation of the future spot rate is a separate, much stronger claim — precisely analogous to the covered-vs-uncovered interest rate parity distinction elsewhere in this curriculum, where the covered forward rate is pinned down by arbitrage but the claim that it equals the expected future spot rate (uncovered parity) is a behavioral hypothesis that has failed empirically, on average, in FX markets for decades. Treating a rates forward curve as a reliable rate forecast, rather than as an arbitrage-free pricing input, risks the same kind of overconfidence.
