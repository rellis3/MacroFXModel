Lesson 1 · Options in Practice

Options Contract Mechanics, or What You Actually Trade

Before the view, the Greeks or the strategy, there is a contract: a strike, an expiry, a multiplier, a premium and a way of ending. This guide covers what is printed on it, what it costs to get in and out, and how it settles. Options can lose the whole premium, and short options can lose more. This is education, not advice.

The whole idea
🎧 Listen · the five-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

I looked at a call on a stock trading at one hundred dollars. The screen says one fifteen. So it costs a dollar fifteen?

Almost. That is the price per share. One contract covers a hundred shares, so one contract costs a hundred and fifteen dollars. Buy five and it is five hundred and seventy-five.

And what exactly am I buying for that?

A right, not a position. The right to buy a hundred shares at a fixed price, the strike, say one hundred and five, at any time up to an expiry date thirty days away. The seller took your money, and now has the obligation to deliver if you ask.

The stock is at a hundred. Why pay anything for the right to buy at one-oh-five?

Because it might rise. That is time value, the price of the chance. Exercising today would be pointless, so the call has no intrinsic value, and the whole one fifteen is time value. It melts. On expiry day the call is worth whatever the stock is above one-oh-five, or nothing.

So where do I break even?

At expiry, the strike plus what you paid: one hundred and six fifteen. The stock has to rise more than six percent in thirty days before you have made a cent.

And if I want out before expiry?

You sell it, and that is where the spread bites. The screen shows a bid of one ten and an ask of one fifteen. You buy at the ask and sell at the bid. Five contracts, that is twenty-five dollars gone the moment you trade, plus fees. About thirty-two dollars in all, five and a half percent of what you put in.

That is not terrible.

On a busy contract, no. On a thinly traded one the bid might be ninety cents and the ask a dollar thirty. Same five contracts, and the round trip costs two hundred and seven dollars, nearly a third of your money, before the stock has moved at all.

What about when it ends? Do I get shares?

It depends on the market. A US stock or ETF option is settled in shares: exercise, and a hundred shares arrive and you pay the strike. If it finishes even a cent in the money, it is exercised for you automatically unless you say otherwise. An S and P five hundred index option is settled in cash, no shares. Currency options are private contracts. They expire at ten in the morning New York time, and the currencies are delivered two business days later.

And if I am the seller?

You can be assigned. The clearing house picks by a random process and your broker passes it down. With US stock options that can happen before expiry, for example on a deep in-the-money call just before a dividend. And if the stock closes right at the strike on expiry day, you may not know whether you have been assigned until after the close. That is pin risk.

Where did this standard contract come from?

Chicago, the twenty-sixth of April, nineteen seventy-three. Before that, options were one-off private deals. The Chicago Board Options Exchange fixed the strikes, the expiry dates and the contract size, and put a clearing house in the middle. On the first day, nine hundred and eleven contracts traded, calls only. Standardising the contract is what made it tradable.

So the one-line version?

Know what the contract says, what it costs you to get in and out, and how it ends, before you ask whether your view is any good.

Section 1

An option is a voucher with the terms printed on it

The example used throughout: a made-up stock, XYZ, at 100, and its 30-day call struck at 105.

🎯

Strike and expiry

The fixed price (105) and the last moment the right exists (30 days). After expiry the contract is gone.

📦

Multiplier

One US stock or ETF contract covers 100 shares. SPX pays $100 a point. FX is sized by notional.

🏷

Premium

Quoted per share, paid up front. 1.15 a share is $115 a contract. The seller keeps it whatever happens.

🧾

Style and settlement

American or European. Shares, cash or currency on exercise. These decide what you hold on Monday.

A listed option is a voucher where every term is standardised, so strangers can trade the same piece of paper. An over-the-counter option is written to order for two parties.

Section 5

Listed versus over the counter

Listed (US stock, ETF, SPX)
  • Standard strikes, expiries and size
  • A clearing house between buyer and seller: no credit risk to the other side
  • A visible bid and ask, and open interest
  • You accept standard terms
Over the counter (mainly FX)
  • Any strike, expiry and notional
  • Dealt with a dealer under an ISDA agreement: you carry their credit risk unless collateral is posted
  • No central order book: the spread depends on your dealer and your size
  • Premium in a stated currency, quoted as pips or a percentage
Listed means standardised and cleared. It does not mean liquid: many listed strikes trade rarely.

Pin risk

If the stock closes within pennies of the strike, a seller cannot know whether the option will be exercised, and cannot hedge both outcomes. The simple defence is not to be short a near-the-money contract when trading ends.

Early assignment is also possible on American-style shorts, typically before a dividend on a deep in-the-money call.

Before trading, check the spread as a percentage of the mid, the volume and the open interest. Use limit orders: a market order buys at the ask.

Section 8 · It actually happened

Cboe, April 1973: the day options became a standard product

It actually happened

Before 1973, US options were mostly written over the counter, one negotiated deal at a time, with no easy way to sell before expiry. On 26 April 1973 the Chicago Board Options Exchange opened with fixed strikes, fixed expiry dates, a set contract size and central clearing. According to Cboe, 911 contracts traded on 16 listings on the first day. Only calls traded; puts followed in 1977. The Black-Scholes paper appeared the same year.

Section 9

Where contract mechanics quietly cost money

💯

Reading the quote as the bill

2.40 is $240 a contract. Size is premium × multiplier × contracts. SPX at 5,000 is $500,000 a contract of notional.

🧩

Exercising early

Exercise throws away time value. At 108, the 105 call is worth about 4.89 but exercises for 3.00: $189 a contract given away.

📎

Adjusted contracts

After splits, mergers or special dividends the OCC changes the strike and deliverable. Check what an adjusted series delivers.

🛑

Margin is not risk

A broker's margin is a lender's minimum. A defined-risk call spread has a known worst case; a naked short does not.

None of these is an argument against options. They are an argument for reading the contract before reading the chart.
Section 10

Quick check

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

A US stock option is quoted at 2.40. What does one contract cost before fees?

The quote is per share and one contract covers 100 shares: 2.40 × 100 = $240.
Question 2

The stock is at 100 and you hold a 105 call. What is its intrinsic value?

Intrinsic value of a call is max(S − K, 0) = max(100 − 105, 0) = 0.
Question 3

Which of these settles in cash and has no early assignment?

SPX options are European-style and cash-settled: at expiry you receive (settlement value − strike) × $100, and nothing can be exercised earlier. US stock and ETF options are American-style and physically settled.
Question 4

You buy at the ask of 1.15 and immediately sell at the bid of 1.10. Five contracts, fees ignored. What did that cost?

The spread is 0.05, times 100 shares, times 5 contracts: $25. You pay the spread whatever the stock does.
Question 5

You are short a US stock call that finishes expiry day $0.05 in the money. What is the realistic risk?

The OCC exercises options in the money by $0.01 or more unless the holder says otherwise, and holders can decide after the close. That is pin risk.
Your call
Scenario drill · an illustrative expiry Friday

Your short call is ten cents in the money, with minutes left

This is a hypothetical scenario, not a real episode. It is the third Friday of the month, 3:45 p.m. New York time, 15 minutes before the close. You are short 5 contracts of the XYZ 105 call on a US-listed stock, sold weeks ago for a profit if it expires worthless. XYZ is trading at 105.10. The call is quoted 0.10 bid, 0.15 ask. You intended to let it expire. What do you do?

  1. Do nothing. It is only ten cents in the money, so it will probably expire and you keep the premium.
    An option that is $0.01 or more in the money is exercised automatically by the OCC unless the holder says otherwise, and holders can decide after the close with after-hours prices in front of them. You would not know whether you were assigned until after the market has closed. Being assigned leaves you short 500 shares into the weekend. You also cannot hedge for both outcomes.
  2. Buy the five calls back at the ask, about $75 plus fees, before the close.
    It costs 0.15 × 100 × 5 = $75 and removes the assignment and the weekend gap risk completely. You give up a small amount of the premium you collected, but you end trading with a known position. It is the pin-risk rule of thumb: do not hold a short contract that is near the strike when trading ends.
  3. Buy 500 shares of XYZ now, so that if you are assigned the shares are covered.
    This does protect against assignment, but if the call is not exercised you own 500 shares you did not want, over a weekend, with the same gap risk in the other direction. It swaps one uncertainty for another and costs more in commissions than the buy-back.
  4. Sell more calls at the same strike, because the premium is higher than at the open and you think the stock will fall back.
    That doubles the exposure at the worst time. A contract with minutes left and the stock at the strike has the largest risk of a binary outcome relative to what you collect. You would be adding to a position that cannot be managed once the market closes.

What would happen. This is an illustrative situation, so there is no recorded result. The mechanics are real and documented: the OCC's automatic exercise of options in the money by $0.01 or more, the holder's later deadline (about 5:30 p.m. New York time, often earlier at brokers), and the delivery of shares after assignment. The point of the drill is that on expiry day the decision you can still control is the one before the close.

Section 11

What to carry forward

01

An option is a standardised contract: a right to buy or sell a fixed amount at a fixed strike by a fixed expiry, bought for a premium paid up front.

02

Quotes are per share; trades are per contract. Multiply by 100 for US stock and ETF options, or $100 a point for SPX.

03

Premium is intrinsic value plus time value. Only time value decays, and out-of-the-money options are all time value.

04

US equity options are American and physically settled; SPX options are European and cash-settled; FX options are over the counter, cut at 10:00 New York and delivered two business days later.

05

The round trip costs the full spread plus fees both ways: $32 on a busy contract, $207 on a thin one, for the same five contracts.

06

Short options can be assigned, early and at expiry. Pin risk, dividends and margin rules are properties of the contract.

07

It actually happened, Cboe 1973: standard strikes, expiries and size, plus a clearing house, turned bespoke options into a tradable product.

What this now lets you do

Read any options quote as dollars, say what you will hold on settlement day, price the round trip before you trade, and spot the contract terms that turn a good view into a bad outcome.
