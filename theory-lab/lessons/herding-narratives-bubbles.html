Herding, Narratives and Bubbles

Following the crowd is often perfectly rational for each person. That's exactly why a whole market can end up confidently wrong: everyone copies everyone, and nobody's own information gets counted. Add a good story and some leverage, and you have the recipe for a bubble, and for the misery of being right too early.

CONCEPT — explained here; desk verdicts quoted where tested ~22 min read Prereqs: Probability, Bayesian Inference (helpful, not required)
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • Information cascades (Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer & Welch, 1992): once the first two or three people lean one way, it becomes rational for everyone after them to copy and ignore their own information. The crowd stops learning.
  • The cost is computable. With private signals that are right 70% of the time, a copying crowd settles on the wrong answer about 15% of the time. The same 20 people voting independently would be wrong about 3% of the time.
  • Narratives are how cascades spread (Shiller): stories spread from person to person like a contagion, and in a boom the story ("a new era") replaces the cash flows as the reason to buy.
  • Minsky's stages, popularised by Kindleberger: displacement → boom → euphoria → profit-taking → panic. Leverage turns a story into forced buying on the way up and forced selling on the way down.
  • Being early is the same as being wrong, for a while. The Nasdaq nearly quadrupled after the December 1996 "irrational exuberance" warning, peaked in March 2000, then fell about 78% by October 2002. Some of the best-known sceptics gave up before the top.
  • Desk verdicts: the classic "crowding" warnings are not timing signals. Narrow leadership (breadth-narrowing) is null on direction and range. A crowded market (dispersion-crowded-week) is null for the following week. And a crowd starting to unwind precedes a calmer week, the reverse of "the break is starting" (dispersion-reset).
Section 01

Why this matters

The previous lesson was about one person being too sure of their own information. Herding is the mirror image: people discarding their own information because the crowd seems to know better. Both are expensive, and they combine. A trader can be overconfident about the crowd's story without having checked any of it.

It matters for markets in particular because prices are the crowd's decisions, published live. The efficient-markets argument assumes those prices pool everyone's independent information. A cascade breaks that assumption. Prices can then be very confident and very wrong at the same time. That is also why the forced-selling and domino-chain lessons keep finding the same pattern: the herd builds positions slowly on the way up and unwinds them all at once on the way down.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Two empty restaurants

You arrive in a new town and see two restaurants side by side. Your guidebook mildly prefers B. But A has three tables of diners and B is empty. You reason, quite sensibly, that those diners probably knew something, so you go into A. The next couple arrive. Their guidebook also mildly prefers B, but now A has four tables. They go to A too.

Here is the catch. After you, every new diner's choice tells the street nothing, because everyone would have chosen A whatever their own guidebook said. A busy restaurant can be full of people who each privately thought the other one was better. The first two or three diners decided for everyone, and they may simply have been unlucky. That is an information cascade, and the example is the one Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer and Welch used themselves.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

If the first two kids in the line pick the red slide, everyone else picks red too, because "they must know something". Even kids who heard the blue slide is more fun go red. So the whole line can end up on the worse slide just because of the first two kids.

Section 02

The math, step by step

The cascade model is small enough to solve by hand. It uses one idea from Bayes' rule and one result about random walks.

1. The set-up

The world is in one of two states: the asset is Good or Bad, equally likely to start with. People decide one at a time whether to buy (bet Good) or pass (bet Bad). Each person gets a private signal that points to the true state with probability \(p > \tfrac12\), and to the wrong one with probability \(q = 1-p\). Nobody can see anyone else's signal. Everyone can see what everyone before them did.

2. What one signal is worth

By Bayes' rule, each independent signal multiplies the odds of Good by \(p/q\) if it says Good, or by \(q/p\) if it says Bad. So only the net count matters. If the public can infer \(d\) more Good signals than Bad from earlier actions, and your own signal is \(s = +1\) (Good) or \(-1\) (Bad), then:

\[ \frac{\Pr(\text{Good}\mid \text{info})}{\Pr(\text{Bad}\mid \text{info})} = \left(\frac{p}{q}\right)^{d+s} \]
p/qhow much one signal moves the odds; 0.7/0.3 ≈ 2.33
dnet Good-minus-Bad signals the crowd's earlier actions revealed
syour own private signal, +1 or −1

3. The moment a cascade starts

You buy if the odds are above 1, meaning \(d + s > 0\). (If they are exactly even, assume you follow your own signal.) While \(|d| \le 1\), your own signal decides, so your action reveals it and \(d\) moves by one. But once \(d \ge 2\), even a Bad signal leaves \(d + s \ge 1\). You buy whatever you saw.

|d| ≤ 1
Learning
Everyone acts on their own signal, so every action adds information.
|d| = 2
Cascade
Everyone copies. Actions reveal nothing new, so d stops moving.
for ever
Locked in
Until a public shock, all later private information is thrown away.

The cascade locks itself in. Once it starts, each new action is uninformative, so \(d\) never changes again. Nothing irrational happened. Every single person used Bayes' rule correctly.

4. How often the crowd locks onto the wrong answer

Before a cascade, \(d\) is a random walk: +1 with probability \(p\) (if the truth is Good), −1 with probability \(q\), starting at 0 and stopping at +2 or −2. The standard gambler's-ruin result gives the chance of hitting −2 first, which is the chance the crowd cascades onto the wrong answer:

\[ \Pr(\text{wrong cascade}) = \frac{q^2}{p^2 + q^2}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[\text{people before it starts}] = \frac{2}{p^2+q^2} \]
q²/(p²+q²)the first two revealed signals both wrong, out of the first two that agree
2/(p²+q²)the expected number of people whose choices settle the outcome for everyone

You can see it directly. The first two signals agree with probability \(p^2 + q^2\), and when they do, the cascade starts. When they disagree, \(d\) is back at 0 and the process restarts. So it comes down to which comes first, two right signals in a row or two wrong ones: \(p^2\) against \(q^2\). At \(p = 0.7\) that is \(0.09/0.58 \approx 15.5\%\), and the outcome is usually settled within the first three or four people (\(2/0.58 \approx 3.4\)).

Compare that with 20 people who each act on their own signal and take a majority vote. That group is wrong only about 3.3% of the time at \(p = 0.7\). Copying, which is rational for each person, makes the group about five times more likely to be wrong. The crowd ends up holding the information of about two people instead of twenty.

\(q\)\(1-p\), the chance a private signal is wrong.
\(s\)Your own private signal: +1 for Good, −1 for Bad.
\(d\)Net count of Good-minus-Bad signals revealed by earlier people's actions.
\(\Pr(\cdot\mid\text{info})\)Probability after seeing your signal and everyone's earlier actions.

Person 1

Signal Bad (a 40% chance even though the truth is Good). With \(d = 0\), they follow it and pass. The public now infers \(d = -1\).

2

Person 2

Signal Bad again (another 40%). \(d + s = -2\): pass. Now \(d = -2\). This pair of events has probability \(0.4^2 = 0.16\).

Person 3

Signal Good. Odds \(= 1.5^{-2+1} = 0.67\), below 1, so they pass anyway. Their Good signal is lost. On average, 60% of everyone after them holds a Good signal and passes.

Section 03

From cascades to bubbles: stories, stages and leverage

Narratives: the medium the cascade travels through

The cascade model has people watching actions. Real markets also have stories. Robert Shiller's Irrational Exuberance (first published in March 2000, the month the Nasdaq peaked) and his later Narrative Economics (2019) argue that popular stories spread like epidemics. They pass from person to person, peak, and fade, and they move spending and investing as they go. A good market story is simple, has a hero or a villain, and gives you a reason the old rules don't apply ("the internet changes everything", "the hedge funds are trapped"). A rising price is also the most convincing evidence a story can have, and that creates a feedback loop: price up → story more believable → more buyers → price up.

Minsky's stages

Hyman Minsky's financial-instability hypothesis says that stability itself breeds instability. Calm years make borrowing look safe, and the borrowing then makes the system fragile. Charles Kindleberger's Manias, Panics, and Crashes (1978) turned Minsky's ideas into a sequence that fits a long list of historical manias:

Minsky–Kindleberger stages (an illustrative shape, not data)
Displacement: a real innovation or policy change creates genuine profit opportunities. Boom: credit expands, prices rise, the story spreads. Euphoria: buying because prices are rising; valuation is dismissed. Profit-taking: insiders and early buyers sell; the market stalls. Panic: the same leverage that forced buying now forces selling. The shape is schematic. Real episodes vary a great deal in timing and size.

Why being early is the same as being wrong, for a while

If bubbles are visible, why doesn't smart money short them flat? Shleifer and Vishny's "The Limits of Arbitrage" (1997) gives the answer. Professional money is managed for clients who judge it on recent results. A manager who bets against a mispricing that then gets worse shows losses, and clients pull their money at exactly the moment the bet is most attractive. De Long, Shleifer, Summers and Waldmann (1990) call this noise-trader risk: the crowd can push prices further from value before they come back, and you have to survive that. A short seller also faces margin calls and potentially unlimited losses. The line usually attributed to Keynes, that markets can stay irrational longer than you can stay solvent, is probably not something he actually said. The idea itself is exactly what Shleifer and Vishny formalised.

So rational professionals often ride a bubble instead of fighting it. Brunnermeier and Nagel (2004) found that hedge funds as a group were heavily invested in technology stocks during the late-1990s run-up, and on the whole cut back before the collapse rather than shorting it on the way up.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

A great story makes everyone want the toy, so the price goes up, which makes the story sound even truer. The grown-up who says "that toy isn't worth it" is right in the end. But if everyone keeps buying for another year, that grown-up has to keep paying while they wait, and some of them run out of pocket money first.

Section 04

It actually happened

It actually happened

1996–2002: the dot-com bubble, and the sceptics who were right too early

On 5 December 1996 Federal Reserve Chairman Alan Greenspan asked in a speech how we would know when "irrational exuberance" had unduly escalated asset values. Markets wobbled for a day. The Nasdaq Composite then nearly quadrupled over the next three-plus years. The story was a real displacement (the internet really did change everything) stretched into a valuation rule. Profits were said not to matter yet; "eyeballs" and growth did. Companies with little revenue listed and doubled on their first day.

The sceptics paid for being early. Julian Robertson's Tiger Management, one of the most successful hedge funds of the 1990s, was a value investor that would not buy the technology story. After heavy losses and client withdrawals, Robertson announced at the end of March 2000 that he was closing the funds. That was within weeks of the top. Fighting the bubble had been a bad trade for years. The analysis was right; the timing was what cost him.

The Nasdaq Composite closed at its peak, 5,048.62, on 10 March 2000. By 9 October 2002 it closed at 1,114.11, a fall of about 78%. Many of the story stocks went to zero; Pets.com listed in February 2000 and was liquidating by November. The index didn't close above its 2000 peak again until 2015. The displacement was real, and the internet did go on to change everything. The prices had simply run years ahead of it.

It actually happened

January 2021: GameStop and a narrative with a villain

GameStop was a struggling video-game retailer, and a large share of its stock had been sold short by hedge funds. On the Reddit forum r/wallstreetbets a story spread fast. It had a clear villain (the short sellers), a mechanism anyone could follow (if enough people buy, the shorts are forced to buy back and the price spikes), and a sense of belonging. The stock had ended 2020 under $20 (before its 2022 stock split). In late January 2021 it went nearly vertical, touching an intraday high of about $483 on 28 January.

Melvin Capital, one of the best-known short sellers, took a $2.75 billion injection from Citadel and Point72 on 25 January after heavy losses, and closed in 2022. On 28 January Robinhood and other brokers restricted buying in the stock as their own clearing-collateral demands jumped. Within weeks the price had fallen back most of the way, leaving the late buyers, recruited by the story at the top, with the losses. Both sides learned the lesson of this page. The shorts were early-and-wrong long enough to be forced out, and the herd was right about the squeeze and wrong about the price.

What the desk found

The folk claim this lesson is about: "you can see the herd crowding in, and that tells you a break is coming." The desk can't test whether a bubble exists. That needs a fair value nobody agrees on. But it has tested the three most common crowding warnings as timing signals.

Desk test: nothing found breadth-narrowing: narrow leadership, with a few giants carrying the index, is not a signal to act on. 23 years, 35 de-clustered narrowing events against 267 controls. Direction at 20 days −0.5% [−2.70, +1.57]; range +0.37 [−0.10, +0.85]; no reversion of the spread and no continuation of the rotation. The range near-miss dies on its mirror: extreme broadening raises forward range just as much (+0.28). Narrowing is real and worth showing as a description of concentration. It is not a timing signal.
Desk test: nothing found dispersion-crowded-week: a crowded market is not followed by a wild week. 68 setups 2014–2026 with dispersion high and rising. Next-5-session range against controls: SPX500 +0.07 [−0.06, +0.23], NQ +0.07 [−0.03, +0.17]; the chance of a big day was 33% against 30% for controls. A secondary 20-session window ran wider (+0.23 [+0.04, +0.51], +0.21 [+0.08, +0.38]), but it sits in the late half of the sample and rests on a small count.
Desk test: held up dispersion-reset: when the crowd starts to unwind, the next week is calmer, the reverse of "the break is starting". Pre-registered with the opposite expectation. The 31 crowded-and-falling setups (n = 26 in the tested arm, against a floor of 25) preceded a calmer week: SPX500 −0.122 [−0.190, −0.005], NQ −0.083 [−0.151, −0.008]. Crowded-and-still-rising is the wide tape. Direction was null in both arms. The sample is thin, so this informs an expected range and is not a trigger.
Not tested here Can a bubble's top be timed? Nothing here tests it, and the honest base rate from history is discouraging. Tiger closed within weeks of the 2000 top after years of being right on valuation, and the desk's own crowding measures don't time the turn. What the evidence supports is positioning (know how concentrated your exposure is), not timing.
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

People say "when everyone crowds onto one side of the boat, it's about to tip". The desk checked. Crowding didn't tell you when it would tip. And when people started moving back, the boat usually got steadier, not wobblier.

Section 05

How practitioners actually use this

🧭

Ask what you know that the last buyer didn't

If the honest answer is "nothing, but it keeps going up", you're in a cascade. Your trade adds no information, and you're relying on the people ahead of you having been right.

📏

Size to survive being early

If you fade a crowd, assume the move against you can double before it turns. Size so that you are not forced out. Most famous "right but early" losses were sizing failures. The view was fine.

📖

Separate the displacement from the price

The internet was real; Nasdaq 5,000 in 2000 was not. Write down the story and what it would have to deliver, in earnings or cash flows, to justify today's price. Then judge the two separately.

🧺

Measure concentration, not mood

When an index is carried by a handful of names, "long the index" means long those names. Treat that as a fact about your exposure. The desk's null says it isn't a timing signal.

🎯
Self-audit · the narrative check

Before you join (or fight) a crowded trade

  • The thesis without the price. Can I state why this should go up (or down) in one sentence that doesn't mention it already going up?
  • My own signal. What did I see myself, before I knew what everyone else was doing? Write that down separately from what I've heard.
  • The marginal buyer. Who buys from here, and with what money? If the answer is "people who haven't heard the story yet", the cascade is near its end.
  • The forced seller. Who holds this on leverage or margin, and what price forces them out?
  • Survival. If I'm right but early by six months and the move against me doubles first, am I still in the trade?

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where people get this wrong

  • Calling everything a bubble. Some rapid rises are justified by a real displacement. "It went up a lot" is not evidence of a bubble.
  • Confusing "the herd is wrong" with "the herd is about to be shown wrong". The first can be true for years. That's the whole lesson of Tiger in 2000.
  • Treating contrarianism as a strategy. Doing the opposite of the crowd is just copying in reverse. You still haven't used your own information.
  • Using a crowding gauge as a trigger. A crowding measure says a trade is popular, not when it will unwind. Tested here: narrowing, crowding and unwinding do not time the turn, and unwinding points to calmer, not wilder.
  • Forgetting that cascades are fragile. Because a cascade rests on very little information, a small public piece of news can flip it. That fragility is why bubbles end suddenly, and why you can't tell in advance which piece of news will do it.

Section 07

Self-test

Q1. In the cascade model, why does person 3 ignore a Good signal when the first two people both passed?
The first two actions revealed two Bad signals, so \(d = -2\). Adding person 3's Good signal gives \(d + s = -1\), and the odds \((p/q)^{-1}\) are below 1. Bayes' rule says pass. Their action is now the same whatever they saw, so it reveals nothing, and \(d\) stays at −2 for everyone after them.
Q2. Compute the chance of a wrong cascade at p = 0.8, and compare it with an independent 20-person vote.
\(q^2/(p^2+q^2) = 0.04/(0.64+0.04) \approx 5.9\%\). A 20-person independent majority vote at \(p = 0.8\) is wrong only about 0.2% of the time. Copying throws away almost all of the crowd's information, even when signals are good.
Q3. Why can't professional arbitrageurs simply short a bubble flat?
Limits of arbitrage (Shleifer & Vishny, 1997): they manage other people's money, and clients judge them on recent results. A mispricing that widens shows up as losses, clients withdraw, margin calls arrive, and the arbitrageur may be forced out before prices return to value. Noise-trader risk (De Long et al., 1990) means that widening is a real risk, not a theoretical one. Tiger's closure in March 2000 is the textbook case.
Q4. A colleague says "the index is being carried by five stocks, so a correction is coming". What does the desk's evidence say?
Tested null (breadth-narrowing): across 35 events in 23 years, direction at 20 days was −0.5% with an interval of [−2.70, +1.57], and range was not reliably wider. Extreme broadening raised range just as much. The useful reading is about exposure (long the index means long those five stocks), not timing.
Q5. Why do bubbles tend to end suddenly rather than deflate gently?
Partly fragility: a cascade rests on the information of only the first few people, so a small piece of public news can flip it. Partly leverage: in Minsky's stages, the borrowing that fuelled the boom forces selling once prices stall, and forced sellers don't wait for value. The two together make the down-leg much faster than the up-leg.
Q6. Is "doing the opposite of the crowd" a cure for herding?
No. It still bases your decision on other people's actions rather than your own information, just with the sign flipped. The cure is to write down your own signal first, independently, and size so that being early can't force you out.
