Give the Sampler a Push, Not a Jitter

Plain Metropolis-Hastings explores a posterior by jittering blindly and hoping the jitter lands somewhere plausible — a strategy that gets slower, fast, as the number of parameters grows. Hamiltonian Monte Carlo replaces the blind jitter with physics: treat the negative log-posterior as a landscape, give the current position a random push, and let a simulated trajectory slide across that landscape using the landscape's own slope. NUTS then removes the one dial that made HMC itself hard to drive — how far to let that trajectory run — which is why it quietly became the default sampler inside tools like Stan and PyMC.

Section 1

A blind random walk gets lost in high dimensions

The previous lesson's random-walk Metropolis-Hastings proposal has a structural weakness baked in from the start.

🎲

Zero information used

A random-walk proposal is a Normal jitter centered at the current point. It has no idea which direction the target actually extends in, or which parameters move together.

📉

Step size has to shrink

To keep the acceptance rate from collapsing, every dimension needs a short, cautious step at once — and short steps mean the chain covers ground achingly slowly.

🔗

High autocorrelation

Successive samples barely move from one another, so many more iterations are needed to obtain the equivalent of a handful of genuinely independent draws.

📈

Gets worse as dimensions grow

Ten, fifty, a hundred correlated parameters — exactly what a hierarchical model's group means and hyperparameters create — and a blind walk struggles hardest of all.

In a single dimension with a roughly symmetric target, this blindness barely matters. But real posteriors are rarely one dimension — and once correlation and dimensionality both climb, the walk has nothing to fall back on except taking tinier and tinier steps.
Section 2

Borrow physics: turn the posterior into a landscape

The intuition

Build a landscape upside-down on purpose: wherever the posterior is high-probability, make the landscape's potential energy low — a valley. Wherever the posterior is implausible, make it a hill. Then give the current position a random momentum — a push in a random direction with a random amount of "speed" — and let a simulated ball roll, trading potential energy for kinetic energy and back, exactly the way real physics would. A ball rolling this way naturally sweeps a long way across the landscape in one motion, instead of inching along it one blind hop at a time.

U is potential energy — the negative log-posterior, low where the posterior is high. ½pᵀM⁻¹p is kinetic energy carried by the invented momentum p. H, the Hamiltonian, is their sum — approximately conserved as the ball rolls.
θ

Posterior peaks become valleys

High posterior probability ⇒ large π(θ) ⇒ small U(θ). The most plausible region of parameter space is, by construction, the bottom of the landscape.

p

Momentum is invented, not real

p has no meaning in the original problem — it's a computational device, drawn fresh from N(0,M) at the start of every iteration, then discarded.

M

Mass is a tuning knob

M controls how momentum converts into movement in each direction — often just a diagonal matrix of per-parameter scales, estimated during warm-up.

Section 3

Follow the gradient: the leapfrog integrator

Hamilton's equations can't be solved exactly for a real posterior, so HMC simulates them numerically, one small step of size ε at a time.

1

Half-step momentum kick

p ← p − (ε/2)∇U(θ) — nudge momentum downhill using the gradient at the current position.

2

Full-step position move

θ ← θ + εM⁻¹p — move the position using the half-updated momentum as velocity.

3

Second half-step kick

p ← p − (ε/2)∇U(θ) — nudge momentum again using the gradient at the new position.

Chain L of these steps together and the momentum drawn at the start carries the trajectory across a long, connected path — every single step pulled by the gradient ∇U toward higher posterior density. This is the mechanical source of HMC's efficiency: one proposal is the endpoint of a whole simulated journey, not one small jitter.

Still a Metropolis chain underneath

Leapfrog is only approximately energy-conserving, so the whole trajectory is treated as one proposal and accepted with probability min(1, exp(Hstart − Hend)) — the same accept/reject logic as plain Metropolis-Hastings, applied once per trajectory instead of once per tiny jitter.

Section 5

The picture
The ball can slide up the far side and start falling back — that's a real physical possibility this same picture explains later: a trajectory can double back on itself, which is exactly the geometry NUTS is built to detect.
Section 6

Why this wins: less autocorrelation per unit of compute

Plain Metropolis-Hastings
  • One small, direction-blind jitter per proposal
  • Step size forced down to keep acceptance reasonable in high dimensions
  • Little net distance covered per accepted proposal
  • Successive samples stay highly correlated
Hamiltonian Monte Carlo
  • One whole gradient-informed trajectory per proposal
  • Every leapfrog step is pulled toward higher posterior density
  • Can travel far from the start while staying somewhere plausible
  • More distance per proposal at a comparably high acceptance rate
More distance covered per proposal, at a comparably high acceptance rate, means far less autocorrelation between successive samples for the same computational budget — precisely the failure mode Section 1 named as the motivating problem.
Section 7

The catch: two dials, and getting them wrong is costly

Plain HMC still needs a human to choose the step size ε and the number of leapfrog steps L per trajectory.

⏱

L too short

The trajectory barely gets going before the loop ends — the proposal lands close to the start, the exact small-step behavior HMC was meant to fix. Gradient information available, wasted.

↩

L too long

Like a ball that rolls up one side of the bowl, back down, and up the other — the trajectory loops back over ground already sampled, burning real gradient evaluations for nothing.

🐢

ε too small

Each leapfrog step barely advances the trajectory, so far more steps (and gradient evaluations) are needed to cover the same distance — computation wasted the same way a too-small random-walk step wastes iterations.

💥

ε too large

Discretization error per step grows, energy stops being even approximately conserved, and the Metropolis step starts rejecting almost everything — or worse, flags outright "divergent transitions."

Worse, the right L isn't a fixed number — it depends on the local curvature of the target, which can vary across the parameter space. Hand-tuning a single L that works everywhere is often impossible.

Section 10

NUTS: stop guessing L, detect the U-turn instead

🌳

Grows the trajectory automatically

Instead of a fixed L, NUTS extends the path outward — forward and backward in simulated time — doubling its length repeatedly, building a tree of candidate states.

↩

Detects the U-turn

After each doubling it checks: does the momentum at the trajectory's ends still point away from where it started, or has it started curling back? The moment it curls back, NUTS stops extending.

🎚

Tunes step size too

A dual-averaging adaptation phase adjusts ε automatically before real sampling begins, commonly targeting an acceptance rate around 0.8 — a practitioner rarely sets ε by hand either.

✅

The default for a reason

Gradient-informed trajectories with an automatically chosen length is exactly why NUTS — not fixed-L HMC — is the default sampler in modern probabilistic programming languages, including Stan and PyMC.

No one has to guess the right rolling time in advance. The marble's own path — specifically, the moment its momentum stops pointing away from home — tells NUTS when to stop.

Section 11

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Why does plain Metropolis-Hastings mix slowly in high dimensions?

A random-walk jitter uses no gradient information, so the step size must shrink to keep the acceptance rate from collapsing — and small steps mean successive samples stay highly correlated.
Question 2

What does HMC borrow from physics to propose a move?

U(θ) = −log π(θ) is the potential energy; a random momentum p is drawn fresh each iteration; simulating the resulting Hamiltonian dynamics is what produces a long, gradient-informed trajectory instead of a blind jitter.
Question 3

What does HMC's Metropolis correction actually check?

α = min(1, exp(H_start − H_end)). If leapfrog conserved H exactly this would always be 1; the gap measures the leapfrog integrator's discretization error over the whole trajectory.
Question 4

What practical difficulty does plain (fixed-L) HMC introduce that random-walk Metropolis never had?

Too short an L wastes the gradient advantage; too long loops back over ground already covered; ε too large risks divergences, too small wastes steps — and the right L varies across the parameter space.
Question 5

What specific problem does NUTS solve, and how does it detect when to stop?

NUTS extends the trajectory outward, doubling its length, and stops the instant the path's momentum starts pointing back toward its own starting point — a "U-turn" — rather than requiring a fixed number of steps chosen in advance.
Section 12

What to carry forward

01

Plain Metropolis-Hastings is direction-blind — it forces tiny steps in high dimensions, producing slow, highly-autocorrelated chains.

02

HMC turns −log π(θ) into potential energy, adds a random momentum, and uses the gradient to simulate a physical trajectory (the leapfrog integrator) instead of jittering blindly.

03

One trajectory is one proposal — an accept/reject check on the Hamiltonian, not a plain density ratio, corrects for the leapfrog integrator's small energy error.

04

Gradient-informed trajectories cover far more ground per proposal at a comparably high acceptance rate — dramatically less autocorrelation for the same compute.

05

Plain HMC trades one problem for another: step size ε and trajectory length L now have to be hand-tuned, and getting either wrong wastes compute or degrades mixing.

06

NUTS removes L from that list — it grows the trajectory automatically and stops the moment it detects a U-turn, which is why it's the default sampler in Stan and PyMC.

What this now lets you do

Recognise when a posterior's geometry — correlated, high-dimensional, funnel-shaped — is exactly the case where a blind random walk struggles and gradient-based sampling earns its extra complexity. Read a step-size-versus-trajectory-length tradeoff on sight, and understand precisely what NUTS is automating when it replaces a hand-picked L with a detected U-turn. Diagnose a slow or "divergent" chain as a step-size or curvature problem, not a mysterious failure.
