Lesson 04 · Foundation Mathematics

Multivariable Calculus, and What an Option Greek Actually Is

Delta, Gamma, Theta, Vega — four numbers on a risk screen that look like four unrelated facts about an option. They aren't. They're one function, an option's value, differentiated four different ways with respect to four different inputs, everything else held still each time. This is the lesson where "the derivative of a function of several variables" stops being a vague phrase and starts being something you can compute on your own numbers.

Section 1

A function that depends on more than one thing at once

Every function so far in this curriculum took one input, f(x). Almost nothing in real finance is that simple.

◈

An option's value depends on four things at once

V(S, t, σ, r) — the underlying price, time to expiry, volatility, and the interest rate, all simultaneously. Not four separate models; one function of four inputs.

Σ

A portfolio depends on every position at once

Its value is a function of however many holdings it contains, all moving together, not one position's price at a time.

⟋

A bond depends on yield and maturity together

Its price is a function of two variables at once — asking for "the" sensitivity without saying to which one is already an incomplete question.

▦

The picture changes from a curve to a surface

A one-variable function draws a curve on a flat page. A two-variable function draws a landscape — height is the function's value, and the ground is every (x, y) pair you could feed it.

Picture a room where the temperature isn't the same everywhere — warmer near the radiator, cooler by the window — and it also drifts over the course of the day. Ask "how fast is it changing?" and the honest answer is: it depends which direction you mean. That question, made precise, is the entire subject of this lesson.
Section 2

A partial derivative: differentiate one, freeze the rest

∂f/∂x means exactly this: apply the ordinary derivative rules to x, and treat every other variable as if it were a plain number. Step through the simplest possible case.

Try it
V = 0.6·S₁ + 0.4·S₂
A two-asset portfolio, weighted 60/40. Two variables tangled in one sum — "the derivative of V" doesn't mean anything yet. Derivative with respect to which one?

=
0.6
Freeze S₂ completely — treat it as a fixed number, not a variable. The 0.4·S₂ term doesn't involve S₁ at all, so it contributes exactly zero, leaving just the weight, 0.6.
∂V/∂S₂
=
0.4
Freeze S₁ instead. Symmetric: the 0.6·S₁ term now vanishes, leaving the other weight, 0.4. A $1 move in S₁ alone moves V by exactly $0.60, regardless of what S₂ is doing.
Nothing new about differentiation itself is happening here. The entire new idea is "pick one variable, freeze every other one, then differentiate exactly like the single-variable calculus lesson already taught."

This example is deliberately the easiest possible case — because V is a plain sum, the "other" term simply disappears when you differentiate. The next slide keeps the two variables genuinely tangled together, so freezing one actually changes the arithmetic, not just removes a term.

Section 3

A richer example — a value that actually curves

V(S, t) = S·e^(−δt): the forward value of an asset worth S today, paying a continuous dividend yield δ, t years out. This time S and t interact.

∂S

Differentiate with respect to S, freeze t

∂V/∂S = e^(−δt). Since e^(−δt) doesn't involve S at all, it's just a fixed multiplying constant from S's point of view — this is exactly Delta's structure.

∂t

Differentiate with respect to t, freeze S

∂V/∂t = −δ·S·e^(−δt). Now S is the fixed constant multiplying a function of t, and the ordinary chain rule handles the exponential — negative, because more elapsed time means more foregone dividends eroding the forward value.

At S = $100, δ = 3%, t = 2 years: ∂V/∂S = e^(−0.06) ≈ 0.9418 and ∂V/∂t = −0.03 × 100 × 0.9418 ≈ −2.825. Two completely different numbers, from differentiating the exact same function twice — the entire mechanical content of this lesson, worked with real numbers.

Each partial derivative is nothing more exotic than an ordinary single-variable slope, read off one slice of the full V(S,t) surface — freeze one axis, walk along the other, measure the slope.

Nothing about the "formula" for ∂V/∂S changes as you drag the slider — it's always e^(−δt). What changes is where that formula is being evaluated. A partial derivative is itself a function of the point you freeze the other variable at, which is exactly why Delta quoted today is a different number from Delta quoted a year from now, even on the same option.

Section 6

The gradient — every partial, stacked into one vector

Stop asking about one direction at a time. Ask: out of every direction I could walk, which one makes the function rise fastest?

∂

∂f/∂xᵢ — one partial derivative

Sensitivity to variable i alone, every other variable frozen — one entry.

∇

∇f — the gradient

All the partials stacked into one vector, (∂f/∂x₁, ..., ∂f/∂xₙ). It points in the exact direction the function increases fastest, and its length tells you how fast.

−∇

−∇f — steepest descent

The opposite direction — the exact "downhill" direction that gradient descent, a later lesson's core optimization algorithm, repeatedly steps in to hunt for a minimum.

Back to the room with the radiator: the gradient is a compass that always points in the steepest-uphill direction of the temperature field, wherever you're standing. Walk the opposite way it points, and you're headed downhill as fast as possible instead — no single partial derivative alone can answer that, because each one only reports the rate of change along one axis, not along the diagonal in between.
Section 7

The Hessian — curvature, minus the guesswork

Just as a single-variable function's second derivative measures curvature, a multivariable function has a whole grid of second partial derivatives.

Stack every second partial derivative — one for every pair of variables, including differentiating with respect to the same variable twice — into a matrix, and you get the Hessian, H. Under mild smoothness conditions the two "mixed" partials are always equal (∂²f/∂x∂y = ∂²f/∂y∂x), which is why H comes out symmetric.

All-positive eigenvalues

Every direction curves upward from this point. A genuine minimum.

Mixed-sign eigenvalues

Curves up in some directions, down in others. A saddle point — not a minimum, not a maximum.

A stationary gradient alone proves nothing

∇f = 0 only means the function is momentarily flat in every direction at that point. It's equally consistent with a minimum, a maximum, or a saddle. Stopping an optimizer the moment the gradient goes small, without checking the Hessian's eigenvalues, can silently report a saddle point as if it were a solved minimum — this is precisely what convex-optimization checks in a later lesson formalize.

Section 8

The Greeks were partial derivatives all along

One option value function, V(S, t, σ, r). Every Greek is one partial derivative of it, with respect to one variable, everything else held fixed.

Delta, Δ = ∂V/∂S

Sensitivity to the underlying price alone — time, volatility, and rates frozen. Exactly ∂V/∂S from the forward-value example, just applied to an option instead.

Theta, Θ = ∂V/∂t

Sensitivity to the passage of time alone — spot, volatility, and rates frozen. The same partial-derivative mechanics as ∂V/∂t above.

Vega = ∂V/∂σ

Sensitivity to volatility alone — spot, time, and rates frozen. Not usually written with a Greek letter (vega isn't actually one), but structurally identical: one more partial derivative of the same V.

Gamma, Γ = ∂²V/∂S²

The curvature of V with respect to S — a second partial derivative, one diagonal entry of V's Hessian. Not a first partial like the other three; a step up.

A trader reading "Delta 0.60, Gamma 0.04, Theta −0.03, Vega 0.12" off a screen is reading four entries from one gradient-and-Hessian structure, evaluated at today's spot, time, and volatility — not four independent facts that happen to share a Greek-letter naming convention. Summing each Greek's own contribution for a joint move in spot, time, and volatility at once is exactly what the multivariable chain rule does: add up the effect flowing through every channel, rather than picking just one.

Section 9

How practitioners actually use this

🇬🇷

Risk decomposition via the Greeks

A derivatives book's risk is reported as partial derivatives with respect to each market input, precisely so a desk can see which single input is driving P&L without repricing the whole book.

⛰️

Model fitting via gradient descent

Fitting almost any statistical model reduces to minimizing an error function of many parameters at once — gradient descent repeatedly steps in the direction −∇f until the gradient is near zero.

🔍

Certifying a genuine optimum

Once an optimizer reports a stationary point, checking the Hessian's eigenvalues there is standard practice to confirm it's actually a minimum, not a saddle a gradient-only check would miss.

📊

Scenario and stress-testing grids

"What happens if spot moves 2% AND volatility jumps 5 points" is a multivariable question by construction — answered by summing each input's own partial contribution.

The habit worth building: whenever a model has more than one input, the honest first question is "sensitivity with respect to which variable, holding what else fixed?" A bare word like "sensitivity" is ambiguous the moment more than one variable is in play.

Section 10

Reading one Greek alone vs. reading the whole structure

Same risk screen, two very different habits of reading it.

Delta alone
  • Treats spot as the only thing moving — time, volatility, rates silently frozen
  • A real market move rarely holds volatility and time still while spot moves
  • Says nothing about curvature — Gamma is a separate partial, not covered by Delta
  • Breaks down the moment more than one input actually moves at once
The full gradient + Hessian
  • Every input gets its own partial derivative, others explicitly held fixed each time
  • The multivariable chain rule adds them up for a joint move — spot AND vol AND time
  • Gamma, a Hessian entry, catches the curvature Delta alone misses
  • Works identically whether one input moves or all four do at once
Where this needs qualifying

The entire meaning of a partial derivative depends on exactly which other variables are frozen. ∂V/∂S computed with volatility held constant is a genuinely different number from a "spot sensitivity" computed while letting an implied-volatility surface shift with spot too — a real, common practitioner adjustment. Quoting a partial derivative without saying what's held fixed is a common source of confusion comparing numbers across desks.

Section 11

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

What's the key difference between an ordinary derivative f′(x) and a partial derivative ∂f/∂x?

An ordinary derivative has nothing else to hold fixed, because there's nothing else. A partial derivative applies to a function of several variables, and the one new idea is freezing every variable except the one you're differentiating — the actual differentiation step uses identical rules either way.
Question 2

For V(S,t) = S·e^(−δt), why is ∂V/∂t negative while ∂V/∂S is positive?

∂V/∂S = e^(−δt) is always positive — the forward value rises when spot rises, time held fixed. ∂V/∂t = −δSe^(−δt) carries a minus sign because, holding S fixed, letting t grow shrinks the discount factor e^(−δt), continually eroding the forward value as more dividends are given up.
Question 3

Using the worked example (S=$100, δ=3%, t=2 years), what is ∂V/∂S?

∂V/∂S = e^(−δt) = e^(−0.03×2) = e^(−0.06) ≈ 0.9418 — slightly less than dollar-for-dollar, because two more years of dividends are still being given up.
Question 4

Why doesn't a zero gradient (∇f = 0) alone guarantee a minimum has been found?

∇f = 0 just means the function is flat in every direction at that point — a minimum, a maximum, and a saddle all satisfy that. All-positive Hessian eigenvalues mean a genuine minimum; mixed signs mean a saddle.
Question 5

Which of these is literally the same mathematical object as an option's Gamma?

Gamma measures curvature with respect to the underlying price alone, time/volatility/rates held fixed throughout — one diagonal entry of V's Hessian, a second partial derivative, not a first one.
Section 12

What to carry forward

01

A partial derivative is an ordinary derivative with everything else frozen — ∂f/∂x reuses the exact single-variable rules already built, applied one variable at a time.

02

The gradient ∇f stacks every partial into one vector that points where the function increases fastest — its negative is the direction gradient descent walks.

03

The Hessian stacks every second partial derivative — its eigenvalues, not a zero gradient alone, are what actually distinguish a minimum from a maximum or a saddle.

04

Delta, Theta, and Vega are first partial derivatives of one option-value function; Gamma is a second partial derivative — four names for one differentiation exercise, repeated on different variables.

05

The multivariable chain rule adds up the contribution flowing through every path a change can travel — the structure behind combining several Greeks into one joint-move estimate.

What this now lets you do

Read any multi-input formula and ask "sensitivity with respect to which variable, holding what else fixed?" instead of a vague, unqualified "sensitivity." Recognise that a risk screen's Delta, Gamma, Theta, and Vega are one differentiation exercise, not four unrelated facts. Check a fitted model's stationary point with the Hessian instead of trusting a zero gradient alone.
