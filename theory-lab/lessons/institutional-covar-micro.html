CoVaR, Whose Risk Is It Anyway?

Plain VaR asks how bad institution j's own losses can get, on its own. CoVaR asks a genuinely different question: how bad can j's losses get, GIVEN that institution i is already in distress — a measure of spillover and co-movement, not standalone risk. And despite the near-identical name, this is not the same thing as CVaR from the Coherent Risk Measures lesson — this deck draws that line carefully, on purpose.

The whole idea
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

My risk report gives me a VaR for every position. Isn't that enough?

It answers one question well. How bad can this position get on its own, on roughly the worst day in twenty.

And the question it misses?

How bad it gets when something else is already in trouble. A big bank wobbles, or dollar funding seizes up, and everything else gets worse at the same moment. A number computed on each position alone can't see that.

So that's CoVaR.

Yes. Tobias Adrian and Markus Brunnermeier, first as a New York Fed staff report in 2008, then in the American Economic Review in 2016. CoVaR is the VaR of one thing, call it j, given that another thing, call it i, is sitting at its own VaR.

Distress days are rare. How do you even estimate that?

With a quantile regression. Instead of fitting a line through the middle of the cloud, you fit one along its bottom edge: j's worst day in twenty, given i's return. Then you read that one line at two places.

Give me numbers.

In the lesson's toy example the line is minus zero point four, plus zero point eight times i's return. On a normal day for i, a return of zero, j's bad-day return is minus zero point four percent.

And when i is in distress?

i's own bad-day level is minus three percent. Plug it in: minus zero point four, then minus another two point four. j's bad-day return falls to minus two point eight percent.

So i's trouble costs j two point four extra points.

That gap is the number people rank by. It strips out j's own baseline risk and keeps only the extra that comes with i's distress. And it is just the slope times the distance: zero point eight times minus three.

So if the slope were zero?

Then i could have the worst day in history and j's tail wouldn't move. The slope does all the work.

Isn't this just CVaR with a typo?

That's the classic mix-up. CVaR averages the losses inside one series' own tail. CoVaR conditions one series on a different series' distress. One letter apart, two different questions.

Can I point it at currencies?

The paper was about banks. Using it on dollar funding stress and an emerging-market basket is an application by analogy: useful, but say so. And remember two catches. Tail regressions run on few points, so they're noisy. And a big number shows the two move together in the tail, not that one causes the other.

One line to take away?

Plain VaR asks how bad things get on their own. CoVaR asks how much worse they get when someone else is already in trouble.

How it reached trading

Before 2008 VaR was measured one book or bank at a time, saying nothing about losses when a counterparty or funding market was already in distress. That spillover drove the losses of 2007–08.

Adrian & Brunnermeier circulated CoVaR at the New York Fed in 2008 (AER, 2016); with SRISK it became a standard systemic-risk measure. Desks use the same idea for conditional tail checks.

Section 2

Standalone risk misses the whole point of "systemic"

Every risk number computed on one institution alone quietly assumes its distress doesn't change anyone else's risk.

The gap standalone VaR leaves open
VaR(j) alone
answers
"How bad can j's OWN losses get?"
CoVaR(j | i in distress)
answers
"How bad can j's losses get, GIVEN i is already in trouble?"
One large institution's distress — or a currency's funding markets seizing up — can make everyone else's losses more likely AND more severe at the same moment. Standalone risk numbers, computed on each institution separately, have no way to see that at all.
Section 3

CoVaR: a VaR conditioned on someone else's distress

The definition
CoVaRj|i(q)
=
the q-quantile of Xj, GIVEN Xi = VaRq(Xi)
Same style of question as plain VaR — a worst-case percentile — but computed on a completely different, conditioned-on world: the one where i is already sitting at its own distress level.
📉

i, the conditioning institution

Whatever's already in distress — a bank, a currency, a funding-stress indicator.

🎯

j, the target institution

Whose risk is being measured — CONDITIONAL on i's state, not on its own.

⚠️

Sign convention flag

This lesson works in RETURNS, not losses — "distress" means a large NEGATIVE return, the opposite convention from the loss-based VaR/CVaR lesson.

🔁

Still a full VaR-style quantile

Nothing about the "quantile of j" part changed — only the fact that it's now conditioned on a second variable's state.

Section 4

ΔCoVaR — the number actually used to rank systemic risk

The marginal, spillover-only piece
ΔCoVaRj|i
=
CoVaRj|i in distress − CoVaRj|i at median
CoVaR alone still bundles j's own baseline risk together with the extra risk that comes specifically from i's distress. Subtracting the median-state CoVaR nets j's baseline out entirely — what's left is ONLY the spillover attributable to i.
A large, negative ΔCoVaR means i's distress genuinely drags j's tail risk down further. A ΔCoVaR near zero means i's condition barely moves j's risk at all — no matter how risky either institution looks standing alone.
Section 5

Estimating it: one quantile regression, read off twice

Full derivation of the pinball loss and why it targets a quantile, not a mean, lives in the sibling Quantile Regression lesson — reused here, not re-derived.

One fitted line, two readings
Fit qq(Xj|Xi=x) = αq + βqx
via quantile regression
Plug in x = Median(Xi)
→
CoVaR at baseline
Plug in x = VaRq(Xi)
→
CoVaR at distress
ΔCoVaRj|i = βq × [VaRq(Xi) − Median(Xi)] — the fitted SLOPE times the DISTANCE between i's two states. If βq=0, ΔCoVaR is zero no matter how extreme i's distress level is.

Section 7

CoVaR vs. CVaR — one letter apart, completely different questions

CVaR — averages a tail

E[L | L > VaR(L)] — the average loss WITHIN one variable's own tail. Exactly one random variable, throughout. Covered in the Coherent Risk Measures lesson.

CoVaR — conditions on a different variable

A VaR-style quantile of j, conditioned on a DIFFERENT variable i's distress. Always at least two distinct random variables — a co-movement statistic, not a within-tail average.

The distinction in one sentence

CVaR averages OVER a tail; CoVaR conditions ON a different variable's distress. A number labeled "CVaR" and a number labeled "CoVaR" are not interchangeable, not approximately equal, and not variations of the same question — despite sharing every letter but one.

Section 8

From banks to FX: an application by analogy

🏦

The original setting: banks

Adrian & Brunnermeier's own paper studies banking-sector systemic risk — i and j are financial institutions, and the results are empirically about banks.

💱

A natural FX/macro reframing

i = USD funding stress, j = an EM currency basket. Same fitted line, same math: q(j|i) = −0.4 + 0.8i still gives CoVaR = −0.4% at normal funding conditions, −2.8% when funding stress hits its own distress level.

🔧

Why it transfers

The underlying statistics — a conditional quantile of one return series given another — don't actually require i and j to be banks at all.

⚠️

Say so honestly

This is borrowing a technique built for one domain and pointing it at another — not a claim the original paper studied FX, or that this specific spillover size has been empirically estimated anywhere.

Section 10

How practitioners actually use it

📊

Ranking institutions by systemic contribution

Compute ΔCoVaRsystem|i for many institutions i and rank by size — the paper's own headline use, distinguishing systemic importance from raw size.

🏦

Not the same as "too big to fail"

An institution can be small but highly interconnected (large ΔCoVaR) or huge but contribute little marginal spillover — the two rankings genuinely diverge.

📐

Richer conditioning sets

Add macro state variables (VIX-style vol, credit spreads) into the quantile regression alongside i's own return for a fuller picture.

🌐

A pairwise building block

Usually computed for many (i, j) pairs and read as individual spillover strengths — not, by itself, a finished network model.

Two EM currencies with nearly identical plain VaR can have very different ΔCoVaR against USD funding stress — one whose tail losses co-move strongly with funding conditions, one driven mostly by idiosyncratic factors. Plain VaR alone can't tell them apart; ΔCoVaR can.
Section 11

Where CoVaR gets over-trusted

📉

Short, noisy samples

The toy dataset's own fitted line only lands 1 of 10 points (10%) below it, not exactly 5% — fitting a tail quantile is inherently a small-effective-sample exercise.

🎚️

Arbitrary threshold choice

Nothing pins down whether "distress" should mean i's 5%, 1%, or 10% quantile — a different q can meaningfully change the fitted β and ΔCoVaR.

🕸️

Pairwise, not a full network

ΔCoVaR says something specific about i and j — it doesn't describe multi-institution propagation, feedback, or simultaneous distress across many names at once.

🚫

Not proof of causation

A large ΔCoVaR shows co-movement — a shared third factor could drive both i's distress and j's losses without either causing the other.

Section 12

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

What does CoVaRj|i(q) measure?

CoVaR conditions j's quantile on a DIFFERENT variable's (i's) state — that conditioning on a second variable is the whole point.
Question 2

Why is ΔCoVaR, not CoVaR alone, the number used to rank systemic importance?

ΔCoVaR = CoVaR at distress − CoVaR at median. Subtracting the median-state baseline isolates the marginal, spillover-only piece.
Question 3

In q(j|i) = α + βi, what happens to ΔCoVaR if β = 0?

ΔCoVaR = β × [VaR_q(X_i) − Median(X_i)]. If β=0, that product is zero regardless of how far apart i's two states are.
Question 4

Is CoVaR the same thing as CVaR (Conditional VaR / Expected Shortfall)?

CVaR involves exactly one random variable; CoVaR always involves at least two. Sharing letters is the only thing they share.
Question 5

Did Adrian & Brunnermeier's original paper study CoVaR applied to FX or macro risk factors?

The underlying math doesn't require i and j to be banks, but the original paper's own empirical results are about banks — an FX/macro application borrows the technique rather than replicating the paper's own results.
Question 6

A large, negative ΔCoVaRj|i is found. What can you correctly conclude?

ΔCoVaR is a conditional/co-movement statistic. A shared third factor could drive both i's distress and j's losses without either causing the other — causal language overstates what the number demonstrates.

Scenario drill · an illustrative risk meeting

Two EM currencies, the same VaR, and dollar funding starting to tighten

You hold two emerging-market currencies, X and Y, of equal size. Measured on their own, their 5% VaRs are almost identical. A tail regression on a dollar funding-stress indicator gives X a slope of 0.8, the worked example's line, so its ΔCoVaR against funding stress is −2.4%. Y's slope is close to zero, and so is its ΔCoVaR. Funding conditions are starting to tighten and you have to cut risk in one place. What do you do?

  1. Nothing. The two VaRs haven't moved, so the risk hasn't changed.
    Standalone VaR is computed on each currency alone, so it cannot see the spillover. The risk that grows when funding tightens is exactly the part your VaR report leaves out.
  2. Cut both by the same amount.
    It lowers risk, but it spends half the cut on Y, whose tail barely responds to funding stress. You give up Y's return for little protection.
  3. Cut X first. Its tail is the one that worsens when funding is in distress.
    X's ΔCoVaR says its bad-day return drops about 2.4 extra points when funding stress hits its own VaR. Y's barely moves. Cutting X removes the co-moving exposure that standalone VaR cannot show.
  4. Cut Y. Its losses come from its own country, which feels harder to predict.
    That keeps the position whose tail is tied to the shock you are worried about. Y's idiosyncratic risk is already in its VaR; X's extra spillover is not.

What the numbers say. This is an illustrative case built on the lesson's toy line, not a real episode. If funding stress reaches its own 5% level, X's conditional 5% return falls from −0.4% to −2.8%, while Y's stays near its baseline. Two caveats from the lesson still apply: a tail slope fitted on few points is noisy, and a large ΔCoVaR shows co-movement, not proof that funding stress causes X's losses.

Section 13

What to carry forward

01

CoVaRj|i(q) is a VaR-style quantile of j, conditioned on a different variable i's distress — not j's plain, unconditional VaR.

02

ΔCoVaR = CoVaRj|i in distress − CoVaRj|i at median is the actual systemic-risk-ranking tool — it isolates the marginal spillover, netting out j's own baseline risk.

03

Estimation is one quantile regression of j on i: ΔCoVaRj|i = βq[VaRq(Xi) − Median(Xi)] — the fitted slope times the distance between i's two states.

04

CoVaR is not CVaR: CVaR averages within one variable's own tail; CoVaR conditions on a different variable's distress entirely.

05

Applying CoVaR to FX/macro pairs (e.g. USD funding stress → EM FX) is an honest application by analogy, not something the original banking-sector paper itself studied.

06

Threshold choice, small-sample noise, the pairwise (not network) scope of the measure, and the pull toward causal language are all real pitfalls to name plainly alongside any ΔCoVaR number.

07

On a desk CoVaR is a conditional tail check: what your VaR becomes when another market or counterparty is at its own VaR.
