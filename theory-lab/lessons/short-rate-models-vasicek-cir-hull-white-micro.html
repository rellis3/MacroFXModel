Short Rates, Three Fixes Deep

Vasicek, CIR, and Hull-White are the standard textbook progression of one-factor interest-rate models — and each one is a direct, named application of the SDE machinery this sequence just built. Vasicek is literally ou-mean-reversion.html's Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, renamed and pointed at the short rate. CIR is where that earlier sequence's Lipschitz warning stops being abstract. Hull-White is the practical fix that lets a model actually agree with the yield curve built in bond-pricing-ytm.html and yield-curve-construction.html. Three models, each solving the last one's problem.

Section 1

Vasicek: the OU process gets a job title

Same equation as before — a new variable, and a new name attached to it.

a(b − rₜ)dt
+
σ dWₜ
=
drₜ
This is ou-mean-reversion.html's dXₜ = θ(μ − Xₜ)dt + σdWₜ, term for term: a↔θ, b↔μ, rₜ↔Xₜ. Vasicek (1977) didn't invent new math — it took that exact mean-reverting process and pointed it at the instantaneous interest rate instead of a generic price series.
a

Mean-reversion speed

Identical role to θ in the OU lesson — how hard the rate is pulled back toward its long-run level.

b

Long-run mean level

Identical role to μ — the level rₜ reverts to. Nothing here required a new derivation.

σ

Constant volatility

The size of the random wandering, fixed — unaffected by how high or low the current rate happens to be. This is the piece CIR is about to change.

Because it's the identical SDE, everything the OU lesson already derived carries straight over, unchanged: rₜ is Gaussian at every fixed time, the half-life is ln(2)/a, and the stationary variance is σ²/2a. None of that needs re-deriving here — only the label on the variable changed.

Section 2

The flaw: a Gaussian rate can go negative

Because rₜ is Gaussian at every fixed time, it has positive probability of landing below zero — for any choice of a, b, and σ > 0.

Picking a large b relative to σ makes that probability tiny, but never exactly zero — the Gaussian tail always extends past zero on both sides. For decades this was treated as an obvious modelling defect: a policy rate of, say, −0.5% looked like mathematical nonsense, since nobody imagined being paid to borrow money.

A flaw, until it wasn't

Through the 2010s and into the 2020s, the European Central Bank, the Swiss National Bank, and the Bank of Japan actually ran policy rates below zero for extended periods. Vasicek's "flaw" turned out to already be a feature — it needed no structural change to stay consistent with a negative-rate world, while lognormal- and naive CIR-style models built specifically to rule out negative rates had to be patched or abandoned instead. Which behaviour is "correct" depends entirely on the regime being modelled, not on which one looks more elegant on paper.

This is the exact tension CIR is built to resolve — but resolving it isn't automatically an improvement. It's a trade: CIR buys positivity by giving up the simplicity of a constant, level-independent diffusion.
Section 3

CIR: shrink the noise to zero at the boundary

One change from Vasicek: multiply the diffusion by √rₜ.

a(b − rₜ)dt
+
σ√rₜ dWₜ
=
drₜ
🚗

Brakes that strengthen near the wall

As rₜ → 0⁺, the diffusion term σ√rₜ shrinks toward zero too — there's less and less random shove available exactly where a negative outcome would occur.

↑

The drift is already helping

Whenever rₜ < b, the drift term a(b − rₜ) is positive — a genuine pull back up, working alongside the shrinking noise, not instead of it.

⚖

Not a free guarantee

Both mechanisms together can — under one specific condition — keep rₜ strictly positive for all time. Without that condition, they can still fail.

Cox, Ingersoll & Ross (1985) changed nothing about the drift — the mean-reverting pull toward b at speed a is identical to Vasicek. Only the diffusion coefficient changed, from a flat σ to σ√rₜ, proportional to the square root of the current rate.

Section 4

The Feller condition — and a debt to lesson 1

The one condition under which CIR's mechanism actually delivers on its promise.

2ab
≥
σ²
⇒
rₜ never touches zero
Read it for what it says: the mean-reverting pull toward a positive level b, scaled by twice the speed a, has to dominate the variance σ² of the random shocks. If volatility is large relative to how hard and how fast the process is pulled back, the shrinking-diffusion mechanism can still be overwhelmed and the process can touch zero — typically reflected back up rather than going negative.
Cross-link to SDEs 1 — this is where the warning gets real

σ√rₜ is exactly the square-root diffusion flagged in sde-existence-uniqueness.html as a Lipschitz-condition failure at rₜ = 0 — its local slope, σ/(2√rₜ), diverges as rₜ→0, so no finite Lipschitz constant bounds it near the origin. The standard global-Lipschitz existence theorem from that lesson simply doesn't cover CIR. Existence and pathwise uniqueness instead need the weaker Yamada-Watanabe (1971) conditions, built specifically for this kind of Hölder-continuous-but-not-Lipschitz square-root behaviour. That earlier lesson's caveat wasn't hypothetical — this is the model it was warning about.

Section 8

Three models, one lineage

Each fixes the specific flaw named in the one before it.

Try it
drₜ = a(b − rₜ)dt + σ dWₜ
Distinguishing feature: constant diffusion σ — OU applied directly to the short rate. Closed-form Gaussian bond prices, no re-derivation needed from ou-mean-reversion.html.
Main flaw: rₜ is Gaussian at every t, so it can go negative with positive probability, for any parameter choice.
drₜ = a(b − rₜ)dt + σ√rₜ dWₜ
Distinguishing feature: diffusion scales with √rₜ, shrinking to zero as rₜ→0 — the fix for Vasicek's negativity problem.
Main flaw: stays strictly positive only under the Feller condition 2ab≥σ²; the √rₜ term also fails the Lipschitz condition at rₜ=0, needing Yamada-Watanabe existence instead.
drₜ = [θ(t) − a·rₜ]dt + σ dWₜ
Distinguishing feature: the constant ab is replaced by a time-varying θ(t), calibrated so the model exactly reproduces today's entire observed yield curve.
Main flaw/fix: fixes the calibration problem, not the negativity one — it keeps Vasicek's constant-σ diffusion and its same Gaussian, can-go-negative behaviour.
Section 9

Hull-White: let the target level move over time

One substitution over Vasicek. Everything else — constant a, constant σ, Gaussian dynamics — is unchanged.

[θ(t) − a rₜ]dt
+
σ dWₜ
=
drₜ

Vasicek and CIR, with fixed parameters, each produce one particular theoretical shape for the yield curve implied by their own dynamics — and that shape essentially never matches the actual curve bootstrapped from today's market prices in yield-curve-construction.html. A desk pricing a new instrument off this model needs it to agree with the market on instruments that already trade; that's a hard, non-negotiable constraint, not a nice-to-have. Hull-White (1990) solves it by replacing the constant product a·b with an arbitrary time-dependent θ(t), chosen — in closed form, from the observed forward-rate curve behind bond-pricing-ytm.html — so the model's own bond prices reproduce today's curve exactly, before the randomness takes over from there.

1

One substitution, not a new model

ab → θ(t). Nothing about the diffusion, the Gaussian structure, or a itself changes.

📐

Calibrated once, then let run

θ(t) is solved for once, up front, from today's observed curve — the dynamics evolve forward from there unconstrained.

⚠

Doesn't fix negativity

An exact curve fit is a calibration convenience, not evidence the underlying dynamics are "more correct" — Hull-White can still go negative, same as Vasicek.

Section 10

Where this fits — in theory, and in this repo

📐

Vasicek / CIR — intuition and stress-testing

Rarely used directly for pricing exotic rate derivatives today, but still the standard teaching baseline for choosing between Gaussian and positivity-preserving assumptions.

🎯

Hull-White — actual derivatives pricing

Its exact fit to today's curve is what makes it (and its extensions) genuinely usable — a model that disagrees with the market on plain-vanilla instruments can't be trusted on anything exotic built on top of it.

🧮

Feller as a fitted-parameter check

Whenever CIR-type variance processes (including Heston's) are calibrated to data, checking 2ab≥σ² against the fit is standard due diligence — a mild violation doesn't invalidate the model, it just means zero-touching needs explicit handling.

This lesson leans on three earlier ones directly: ou-mean-reversion.html supplies Vasicek's entire derivation unchanged, sde-existence-uniqueness.html's Lipschitz caveat is exactly what CIR's √rₜ term triggers, and bond-pricing-ytm.html / yield-curve-construction.html supply the observed curve Hull-White's θ(t) is calibrated to match.

Checked against this repo's actual code

None of Vasicek, CIR, or Hull-White exist anywhere in this repo's trading code — verified directly across every .js and .html file. This is an FX/macro trading system, not a rates desk, so that's expected. The one OU-style mean-reversion code that does exist, js/rangeBiasCore.js, is a genuinely different application: ADX- and Hurst-based range-bias scoring for FX entry signals, not a model of an interest rate's own dynamics — it contains no drift/diffusion SDE, no θ or half-life, nothing resembling rₜ. Nothing here is stale or contradicted; this confirms the full lesson's implicit assumption rather than correcting it.

Section 11

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

How does Vasicek relate to the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process from earlier in this curriculum?

drₜ = a(b−rₜ)dt + σdWₜ is dXₜ = θ(μ−Xₜ)dt + σdWₜ, term for term. The closed-form solution, half-life, and stationary variance all carry over unchanged.
Question 2

What is Vasicek's well-known flaw, stated precisely?

A Gaussian variable is symmetric and unbounded below, however small the tail is made by choosing a large b relative to σ — that probability is never exactly zero.
Question 3

State the Feller condition, and what it guarantees when satisfied.

2ab ≥ σ² means the mean-reverting pull toward a positive level, scaled by twice the speed, dominates the shock variance — strong enough that the shrinking-diffusion mechanism keeps rₜ strictly positive for all time.
Question 4

Why does CIR need the Yamada-Watanabe conditions instead of the standard existence theorem from earlier in this sequence?

The ratio σ/√rₜ →∞ as rₜ→0, so no finite Lipschitz constant bounds it near the origin. Yamada-Watanabe (1971) is built specifically for this Hölder-continuous-but-not-Lipschitz behaviour.
Question 5

What single substitution turns Vasicek into Hull-White, and what problem does it solve?

Hull-White keeps constant a and σ and Gaussian dynamics — the only change is ab → θ(t), solved from the observed forward curve so the model reprices today's market exactly before evolving forward.

What this now lets you do

Recognise all three canonical short-rate SDEs on sight, and know exactly which one substitution separates each from the last. Understand why a square-root diffusion needs a different existence argument than a Lipschitz one — and why that isn't a technicality. Know why a rates desk reaches for Hull-White, not Vasicek or CIR, the moment a model has to reprice an actual traded yield curve rather than just look plausible on average.
