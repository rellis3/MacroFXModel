Black-Scholes-Merton Option Pricing

A single replication argument turns "what should this option cost?" into an equation that needs no forecast of where price is going — and the same equation, differentiated a second time, is the reason dealer hedging around big open-interest strikes behaves the way it does.

CONCEPT — foundational reading for the OI/gamma material ~18 min read Prereqs: GARCH & Volatility Clustering

Why this matters

Two things in this repo already talk about options without deriving where their behavior comes from. education/open-interest-course-notes.md teaches, as an empirical/operational fact, that dealers who are short gamma must buy as price rises and sell as it falls, and that this mechanical hedging is what makes high-open-interest strikes act like magnets and walls. book-stress.html asks whether a portfolio's claimed diversification survives a liquidity contraction — a question that, for any book holding options, is inseparable from how those options' hedge ratios change as markets move. Neither page derives why an option's hedge ratio changes with price at all, or what "gamma" formally is beyond a word for "the hedge needs constant adjusting."

Black-Scholes-Merton is where that word comes from. It is a 1973 pricing model built on a genuinely elegant argument: you don't need to know which way a stock is going to price an option on it, because you can build a portfolio of the stock and a bond that behaves exactly like the option, and if two things behave identically they must cost the same or an arbitrageur eats a free lunch. That argument is worth understanding on its own terms, independent of whether the model's assumptions hold perfectly in real FX/equity markets (they don't — the next lesson, Merton's own jump-diffusion extension, exists precisely because they don't). It is also the direct mathematical ancestor of every "gamma wall," "dealer hedging," and "0DTE pinning" concept in the OI course notes: delta and gamma are not empirical folklore, they are literally the first and second partial derivatives of this formula with respect to the underlying's price. Understanding Black-Scholes is what turns "dealers have to rehedge, apparently" into "here is exactly why, and here is the equation that tells you how much."

Plain-English intuition

Cloning the option out of stock and cash

Imagine you've sold someone a call option — you've promised to sell them 100 shares of a stock at $100 any time over the next year, no matter how high the stock goes. That sounds like an open-ended risk: if the stock rockets to $200, you're on the hook to deliver $100 shares for $100 each, a $100-per-share loss. How much should you have charged for that promise?

The Black-Scholes insight is that you don't have to just guess and hope, or forecast where the stock is going. Instead, you can build your own copy of the option's payoff using only the stock itself and a risk-free bond (cash earning interest), and continuously adjust how much stock you hold as the price moves. Hold more stock when the option is deep in-the-money (behaving almost exactly like owning 100 shares outright), hold less — down toward zero — when it's deep out-of-the-money (behaving like it's worthless). If you rebalance this stock/cash mix constantly and correctly, at expiration your homemade portfolio's value matches the option's payoff in every possible future — whether the stock went to $50 or $500. Two things that produce an identical payoff in every scenario must have the same price today, or anyone could buy the cheap one and sell the expensive one for a riskless profit. That "replicating portfolio" is what pins down a single fair price for the option — and building it requires knowing only how volatile the stock is, never which direction you expect it to go. That absence of "which direction" is the single most surprising and most important fact about the model, and it's worth sitting with before moving to the algebra: your personal opinion about whether EUR/USD is going up or down next year has, formally, zero effect on the fair price of an option on it. Only how much it moves around matters.

The math, step by step

The model has three layers: (1) an assumption about how the underlying's price moves, (2) the replication/no-arbitrage argument that turns that assumption into a pricing equation, and (3) the closed-form solution to that equation for a European call or put. Take them in order.

1. Geometric Brownian motion — the assumed model of the underlying

Black-Scholes-Merton assumes the underlying's price \(S_t\) (its value at time \(t\)) follows geometric Brownian motion (GBM), a stochastic differential equation — an equation describing how a random quantity changes over an infinitesimally small instant of time \(dt\):

\[ dS_t = \mu S_t\,dt + \sigma S_t\,dW_t \]
\(S_t\)The underlying's price at time \(t\) — the spot FX rate, the stock price, the index level. This is the quantity the whole model is built around.
\(dS_t\)The infinitesimal change in \(S_t\) over the next tiny instant of time — "how much the price moves in the next tick."
\(\mu\)(mu) The underlying's expected (real-world) rate of return per unit time — e.g. "8% per year." This is exactly the quantity everyone argues about when they forecast a market. Keep an eye on it: it appears here, in the assumed model of the world, but — remarkably — it is about to disappear from the pricing formula entirely.
\(\sigma\)(sigma) The underlying's volatility — the standard deviation of its returns per unit time (annualized, e.g. "20% per year"). This is the same conceptual quantity as the \(\sigma\) used throughout this repo's vol math (volSigmaSeries, volBacktestEngine.js), just expressed in continuous-time SDE units. GBM assumes \(\sigma\) is constant — flat across time and across strike. That is the model's single biggest simplifying assumption, and it is false in every real options market (implied volatility famously varies by strike — the "volatility smile/skew" — and by time). It's flagged here because the next lesson's jump-diffusion extension exists specifically to relax it.
\(dW_t\)The infinitesimal increment of a Wiener process (standard Brownian motion) — pure random-walk noise: mean zero, variance \(dt\), no memory of the past, normally distributed. Same object introduced in the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck lesson.
\(\mu S_t\,dt\)The drift term: the predictable part of the next instant's price change — the expected return rate times the current price times the length of the instant. Proportional to \(S_t\) itself, which is what makes this "geometric" rather than "arithmetic" Brownian motion — a $1 move means something different at $10 than at $1000, exactly like compounding percentage returns.
\(\sigma S_t\,dW_t\)The diffusion term: the random shock, also scaled by the current price, so volatility is expressed as a percentage of price rather than a fixed dollar amount.

Solving this SDE (a standard but non-trivial step using Itô's Lemma, which is outside this lesson's scope) shows that \(\ln S_t\) — the log of the price — is normally distributed. Equivalently, \(S_t\) itself is lognormally distributed: it can never go negative (a crucial, realistic property for a stock or FX price), its percentage returns are normally distributed, and large percentage moves compound multiplicatively rather than adding linearly. This is the concrete content of "GBM": constant percentage volatility, a log-normal price distribution, no jumps, no fat tails beyond what a normal distribution of log-returns produces. Every one of those properties is a simplification worth remembering as you read empirical option-pricing critiques — real FX and equity returns have fatter tails and time-varying volatility (see the GARCH lesson) than constant-\(\sigma\) GBM predicts.

2. The replication argument and the no-arbitrage PDE

Here is the core insight from the intuition box, stated precisely. Suppose you hold a portfolio consisting of a short position in one option (you sold it) plus a carefully chosen number of shares of the underlying, \(\Delta\) shares, financed partly by borrowing or lending at the risk-free rate \(r\). Black, Scholes, and Merton showed that if \(\Delta\) is set equal to the option's own sensitivity to the underlying's price (formally \(\Delta = \partial C/\partial S\), the option's price change per $1 move — you'll meet this again below as "the Greek delta") and continuously rebalanced as \(S\) and \(t\) change, the combined portfolio's value becomes momentarily insensitive to the random term \(dW_t\) — the random shock in the stock cancels against the random shock in the option, because the option's own price change is (to first order) exactly \(\Delta\) times the stock's price change. What's left over is a portfolio whose value changes only predictably, deterministically, instant by instant.

A portfolio with a certain (riskless) return, under the assumption that markets don't allow risk-free arbitrage profit, must earn exactly the risk-free rate \(r\) — otherwise anyone could borrow at \(r\) to buy it (if it earned more) or short it and lend the proceeds (if it earned less), for free money. Setting "this hedged portfolio's return" equal to "the risk-free rate" and working through the calculus produces the Black-Scholes partial differential equation (PDE) — conceptually, an equation that any correctly-priced option's value \(V(S,t)\) must satisfy at every instant:

\[ \frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + rS\frac{\partial V}{\partial S} + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} - rV = 0 \]

You do not need to be able to derive or solve this PDE to use the model — it's included here because it is the precise mathematical statement of "no free lunch given continuous rebalancing," and because its terms are literally named after the Greeks introduced below (\(\partial V/\partial S\) is delta, \(\partial^2 V/\partial S^2\) is gamma, \(\partial V/ \partial t\) is (minus) theta). The single most important fact to take from it: \(\mu\), the underlying's real-world expected return, does not appear anywhere in this equation. It canceled out during the hedging step, because the replicating portfolio's return depends only on how the option's value moves relative to the stock's move (governed by \(\sigma\), through the shape of the payoff), not on which direction the stock is expected to drift. This is the formal version of the intuition-box claim: pricing an option requires knowing volatility, not a forecast. The risk-free rate \(r\) takes over the role \(\mu\) would otherwise have played — this is often described as "pricing under the risk-neutral measure": for the purpose of pricing (never for the purpose of predicting what will actually happen), it is mathematically consistent to price as if every asset drifted at the risk-free rate, because that's the drift rate implied by the no-arbitrage replication argument itself.

3. The closed-form solution — the Black-Scholes formula

Solving that PDE, subject to the boundary condition that at expiration a call is worth \(\max(S_T - K, 0)\) (its payoff — the stock price minus the strike, or zero if that's negative), gives the famous closed-form price for a European call option (one that can only be exercised exactly at expiration, not early):

\[ C = S_0 N(d_1) - Ke^{-rT}N(d_2) \]
\[ d_1 = \frac{\ln(S_0/K) + \left(r + \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}, \qquad d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T} \]
\(C\)The fair price of a European call option today — the number the whole derivation has been aiming at.
\(S_0\)The underlying's price right now (time zero) — e.g. today's spot EUR/USD rate.
\(K\)The strike price — the fixed price at which the option holder can buy the underlying. This is the same "strike" the OI course's CME heatmap plots on its vertical axis.
\(r\)The (continuously-compounded) risk-free interest rate over the option's life — for FX options this is more precisely the domestic/foreign interest-rate differential, since you're pricing the right to exchange one currency for another, each of which earns its own rate; the plain single-\(r\) formula above is the equity/single-currency textbook version.
\(T\)Time to expiration, in years (e.g. 30 days = \(30/365 \approx 0.082\)). Together with \(\sigma\), \(T\) controls how much the underlying could plausibly move before expiry — this is exactly the "DTE" (days to expiration) the OI course flags as intensifying gamma effects as it shrinks.
\(\sigma\)Volatility, same as in the GBM model above — the single most important, and single hardest to know in advance, input to the whole formula. (In practice, the market quotes options in terms of the \(\sigma\) that makes this formula match the traded price — "implied volatility" — rather than the other way around.)
\(\ln(S_0/K)\)The natural log of "how far in or out of the money the option currently is," expressed as a ratio. Positive when \(S_0 > K\) (spot above strike, call already in-the-money), negative when below.
\(N(\cdot)\)The standard normal cumulative distribution function (CDF) — for any input \(x\), \(N(x)\) is the probability that a standard normal random variable (mean 0, standard deviation 1) is less than or equal to \(x\). It always outputs a number between 0 and 1. This is the same bell-curve object from any introductory statistics course; you'd look up \(N(x)\) in a z-table or compute it with a `norm.cdf` function.
\(d_1, d_2\)Intermediate quantities — normalized measures of "moneyness adjusted for time and volatility" — that feed into \(N(\cdot)\). They fall directly out of solving the PDE; there isn't a simpler one-sentence derivation, but their outputs have a clean interpretation, given next.
\(Ke^{-rT}\)The strike price discounted back to today at the risk-free rate — "what \(K\) dollars paid at expiration is worth today." \(e^{-rT}\) is the continuous-compounding discount factor (the inverse of continuous compound growth \(e^{rT}\)).

The formula has a clean economic reading once you know what \(N(d_1)\) and \(N(d_2)\) represent. \(N(d_2)\) is (under the risk-neutral pricing measure introduced above) the probability that the option finishes in-the-money — that \(S_T > K\) at expiration. So the second term, \(Ke^{-rT}N(d_2)\), is "the strike price, discounted to today, weighted by the probability you'll actually have to pay it" — the expected cost of exercising, discounted. \(N(d_1)\) plays a closely related role, weighting the expected value you'd receive conditional on the option finishing in the money (it also happens to equal the option's delta, met again below). The whole formula can be read as: the call's value is what you'd expect to receive if it finishes in the money, minus what you'd expect to pay to exercise it, each correctly probability-weighted and discounted back to today under the risk-neutral measure. Note this is not the real-world probability of finishing in the money — it's the probability implied by pricing everything as if it drifted at the risk-free rate, the same substitution that fell out of the replication argument. The corresponding formula for a European put, derivable either by the same PDE argument or via the shortcut "put-call parity" (a no-arbitrage relationship between a call and put on the same strike/expiry, not derived here), is:

\[ P = Ke^{-rT}N(-d_2) - S_0N(-d_1) \]

The Greeks — the formula's sensitivities

A "Greek" is simply a partial derivative of the option price with respect to one of the formula's inputs — how much the price changes per unit change in that input, everything else held fixed. They matter because a dealer who has sold an option is not exposed to "the option price" in the abstract; they're exposed to how that price will move as the world moves, and the Greeks are the precise, formula-derived answer.

Delta (\(\Delta = \partial C/\partial S\)), equal to \(N(d_1)\) for a call: how much the option's value changes per $1 move in the underlying. A delta of 0.50 means the option behaves, for small moves, like holding 50 shares of stock (per 100-share contract, in equity-option convention). Delta is also, loosely, the option's "moneyness gauge" — near 0 for deep out-of-the-money options, near 1.0 (calls) or −1.0 (puts) for deep in-the-money ones, near 0.5/−0.5 for at-the-money ones — and it is exactly the hedge ratio \(\Delta\) the replication argument constructed above: a dealer who sold a call and wants to be hedged against small price moves buys \(\Delta\) shares of the underlying.

Gamma (\(\Gamma = \partial^2 C/\partial S^2 = \partial\Delta/ \partial S\)): how fast delta itself changes per $1 move in the underlying — the second derivative, or "the curvature" of the option's value with respect to price. This is the Greek the OI course notes lean on most heavily. High gamma means delta swings a lot for a small price move, which means a dealer's hedge ratio is constantly, urgently out of date and must be rebalanced often. Gamma is highest for at-the-money options close to expiration — exactly matching the course notes' observation that gamma effects "intensify" as DTE shrinks and peak in the final 48 hours before expiry. A trader cares about gamma because it measures how much rehedging activity — and therefore how much mechanical, price-insensitive buying or selling pressure — a given option position will generate as the market moves.

Theta (\(\Theta = \partial C/\partial t\)): how much the option's value changes per unit of time passing, all else held fixed — "time decay." An option is a wasting asset: holding everything else constant, an option loses value simply because less time remains for it to move into the money. A trader who is long options bleeds theta every day they're wrong about timing (even if eventually right about direction); a trader who is short options collects it, in exchange for taking on the gamma risk described above — this gamma-versus-theta trade-off is the central tension of options market-making.

Vega (\(\mathcal{V} = \partial C/\partial \sigma\), not actually a Greek letter, but the market's name for it anyway): how much the option's value changes per unit change in implied volatility. Because \(\sigma\) is the one input to the formula nobody directly observes (it has to be inferred or forecast), an option position's vega exposure is a bet on volatility itself rising or falling, independent of which direction the underlying moves — this is the sense in which options let you trade "volatility as an asset," which is exactly the connective tissue back to this repo's GARCH/vol-forecasting material: a good \(\sigma\) forecast is directly a view on vega-exposed positions' expected value.

Rho (\(\rho = \partial C/\partial r\)): how much the option's value changes per unit change in the risk-free rate. Usually the least important Greek for short-dated equity/index options, but far from negligible for FX options specifically, since — as noted above — the relevant "rate" for an FX option is really an interest-rate differential between two currencies, and that differential can move meaningfully and is a first-order input to carry-sensitive FX option pricing.

Worked example

Pricing a one-year at-the-money call by hand

Take round numbers: spot \(S_0 = 100\), strike \(K = 100\) (so the option is exactly at-the-money — struck at today's price), risk-free rate \(r = 0.02\) (2%), volatility \(\sigma = 0.20\) (20% annualized), and time to expiry \(T = 1\) year.

Step 1 — compute \(d_1\):

\[ d_1 = \frac{\ln(100/100) + (0.02 + 0.20^2/2)(1)}{0.20\sqrt{1}} = \frac{0 + (0.02 + 0.02)}{0.20} = \frac{0.04}{0.20} = 0.20 \]

Walking through the arithmetic: \(\ln(100/100) = \ln(1) = 0\), since spot exactly equals strike (that's what "at-the-money" means here). \(\sigma^2/2 = 0.20^2/2 = 0.04/2 = 0.02\). Add the rate: \(r + \sigma^2/2 = 0.02 + 0.02 = 0.04\). Multiply by \(T=1\): still \(0.04\). The denominator \(\sigma\sqrt{T} = 0.20 \times \sqrt{1} = 0.20\). Dividing, \(d_1 = 0.04 / 0.20 = 0.20\).

Step 2 — compute \(d_2\):

\[ d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T} = 0.20 - 0.20 = 0.00 \]

Step 3 — look up \(N(d_1)\) and \(N(d_2)\) (standard normal CDF values, e.g. from a z-table):

\[ N(0.20) \approx 0.5793, \qquad N(0.00) = 0.5000 \]

\(N(0.00) = 0.5\) exactly, because the standard normal distribution is symmetric around zero — half its probability mass lies below the mean. \(N(0.20) \approx 0.5793\) is a standard, table-lookupable value slightly above 0.5, consistent with 0.20 being a small positive number (a bit of extra probability mass between 0 and 0.20 above the mean).

Step 4 — assemble the call price:

\[ C = S_0N(d_1) - Ke^{-rT}N(d_2) = 100(0.5793) - 100\,e^{-0.02}(0.5000) \]

\(e^{-0.02} \approx 0.9802\) (the one-year discount factor at a 2% rate — a dollar received in a year is worth about 98 cents today). So the second term is \(100 \times 0.9802 \times 0.5000 \approx 49.01\), and the first term is \(100 \times 0.5793 = 57.93\). Subtracting:

\[ C \approx 57.93 - 49.01 = 8.92 \]

A one-year, at-the-money call on a $100 underlying with 20% annual volatility and a 2% risk-free rate is worth roughly $8.92 — about 8.9% of spot. Sanity check the direction of that number: it's well above the option's intrinsic value (zero, since \(S_0 = K\) exactly) purely because of time value — over the next year, with 20% annualized volatility, there's substantial chance the stock ends up meaningfully above 100, and the payoff is capped at zero loss on the downside, so the asymmetric payoff has real value even though today's expected outcome (under real-world drift, not even considered here) is unknown. This example deliberately used "textbook-clean" \(N(\cdot)\) values consistent with a standard normal table rather than deriving them from scratch — that derivation (the normal CDF has no closed form and is computed numerically) is outside this lesson's scope.

How practitioners actually use this

Almost nobody at a real options desk uses the raw Black-Scholes formula to decide whether an option is "cheap" or "expensive" in the sense of predicting future stock moves — that's not what it's for, and the section above should make clear why: \(\mu\) isn't in the formula, so there's no view of direction to disagree with. What the formula is used for, constantly, industry-wide: (1) as a quoting convention — market makers quote and traders think in "implied volatility" (the \(\sigma\) that makes the formula match the traded price) rather than in dollar premiums, because implied vol is comparable across strikes, expiries, and underlyings in a way raw prices aren't; (2) as a hedging engine — the Greeks computed from the formula tell a market maker exactly how much underlying to buy or sell to stay delta-neutral, and how fast that hedge will need to change (gamma), which is the entire mechanical basis of dealer-hedging flows; and (3) as a common language and starting point — every more sophisticated model used in practice (local volatility, stochastic volatility like Heston, jump-diffusion like the next lesson) is explicitly built as a correction to Black-Scholes, not a replacement of its logic; understanding where the base model's assumptions break is how those extensions get motivated and explained. The famous "volatility smile" — implied vol varying systematically by strike, something plain Black-Scholes (constant \(\sigma\)) cannot produce — is itself evidence, visible in every live options market, that the model's constant-volatility assumption is wrong; the market prices around that known flaw rather than pretending it isn't there.

Common pitfalls

Where the model is misused or over-trusted

Treating the formula's output as a market forecast. Because \(\mu\) isn't in the formula, a Black-Scholes price says nothing about whether the underlying will go up or down — it says what the option is worth given a volatility assumption, under no-arbitrage logic. Reading a rising call price as "the market thinks the stock will go up" confuses a hedging-consistent valuation with a directional prediction; the correct reading is closer to "the market's current volatility expectation, run through this formula, implies this price."

Forgetting \(\sigma\) is constant in this model but not in reality. The volatility smile/skew observed in every real options market — implied vol differing by strike and by expiry — is direct evidence against the model's core assumption. Practitioners handle this by quoting a different implied \(\sigma\) per strike (effectively patching the model empirically) rather than by taking the formula literally as a description of how volatility behaves. Treating a single flat \(\sigma\) as truth rather than a convenient approximation is the most common beginner mistake.

Ignoring that continuous rebalancing is an idealization. The replication argument requires continuous, frictionless rehedging with no transaction costs and infinitely divisible trade sizes. Real dealers rehedge in discrete steps, pay spread and commission on every rebalance, and face position limits — which is exactly why real dealer hedging flows create visible, laggy price pressure (the "walls" and "squeezes" of the OI course) rather than the perfectly smooth, invisible hedging the theoretical derivation assumes. The gap between the idealized model and discrete real-world rehedging is itself a source of the very market structure the OI course studies.

Confusing risk-neutral probability with real-world probability. \(N(d_2)\) is not "the market's actual probability that the stock ends above the strike." It's the probability under the mathematically convenient risk-neutral measure that falls out of the no-arbitrage replication argument, which prices as if every asset drifted at the risk-free rate. Real-world probabilities (what you'd actually bet on) differ from risk-neutral ones by a risk premium — conflating the two is a classic source of "the option looks mispriced" claims that are really just this confusion.

American vs. European exercise. The closed-form formula above prices a European option — exercisable only at expiry. Most retail-traded equity and index options are American — exercisable any time up to expiry — which can be worth more (an early-exercise premium exists, particularly for puts, or for calls on dividend-paying stocks around ex-dividend dates) and generally has no simple closed-form solution; American options are priced with numerical methods (binomial trees, finite differences) that build on the same replication logic but don't reduce to the tidy \(N(d_1), N(d_2)\) formula.

Self-test

Q1. Why doesn't the underlying's real-world expected return \(\mu\) appear anywhere in the Black-Scholes formula, even though it's part of the assumed GBM model of the underlying?
\(\mu\) cancels out during the replication/hedging derivation. The argument constructs a portfolio (short the option, long \(\Delta\) shares of the underlying) whose random component exactly cancels instant by instant, leaving a riskless portfolio that, by no-arbitrage, must earn exactly the risk-free rate \(r\). That condition pins down the option's price using only how the option's value co-moves with the stock's random shocks (governed by \(\sigma\)) — the stock's own expected drift \(\mu\) never enters the hedging condition, because the hedge is rebalanced continuously and locally, not held as a static directional bet. Practically: pricing an option this way requires an estimate of volatility, never a forecast of direction.
Q2. What does it mean, precisely, that \(N(d_2)\) is "the risk-neutral probability the option finishes in the money" — and why is that different from the real-world probability?
\(N(d_2)\) is the probability, computed under the mathematically convenient risk-neutral measure, that the underlying ends up above the strike at expiration. "Risk-neutral" means the probability is computed as if every asset in the economy drifted at the risk-free rate \(r\) rather than at its true expected return \(\mu\) — a substitution that falls directly out of the no-arbitrage replication argument, not out of any belief about what will actually happen. The real-world probability of finishing in the money depends on the stock's actual expected return \(\mu\) (which is generally higher than \(r\) for a risky asset, since investors demand a risk premium) and is therefore higher than the risk-neutral probability for typical assets. The two coincide only in the special case \(\mu = r\).
Q3. Why is a dealer who is short gamma forced to buy as price rises and sell as price falls — and why is that described as "destabilizing"?
A dealer who sold options is short gamma: as the underlying's price rises, the calls they sold gain delta (move closer to behaving like the stock itself), so to stay hedged the dealer must buy more of the underlying — buying into a rally. As price falls, delta on those calls shrinks (or, for puts sold, delta magnitude grows on the downside), forcing the dealer to sell into the decline. This is "buy high, sell low" by construction, not by choice — it is a mechanical consequence of maintaining a delta-neutral hedge on a short-gamma book. It's destabilizing because it adds directional, momentum-following flow to the market precisely when price is already moving: the hedging itself amplifies the move rather than dampening it, in contrast to a long-gamma dealer, whose hedging (sell into rallies, buy into dips) is stabilizing.
Q4. In the worked example (\(S_0=K=100\), \(r=0.02\), \(\sigma=0.20\), \(T=1\)), why is \(d_2\) exactly zero, and what would happen to \(d_1\) and \(d_2\) if \(\sigma\) were doubled to 0.40 with everything else unchanged?
\(d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T}\), and in the example \(d_1\) happened to equal \(\sigma\sqrt{T} = 0.20\), so \(d_2 = 0.20 - 0.20 = 0\) — a coincidence of the chosen round numbers (specifically that \(\ln(S_0/K) = 0\) and \(r = \sigma^2/2\)), not a general property. If \(\sigma\) doubled to 0.40 with \(S_0, K, r, T\) unchanged: the numerator of \(d_1\) becomes \(0 + (0.02 + 0.40^2/2)(1) = 0.02 + 0.08 = 0.10\), and the denominator becomes \(0.40\sqrt{1} = 0.40\), giving \(d_1 = 0.10 / 0.40 = 0.25\). Then \(d_2 = 0.25 - 0.40 = -0.15\), so \(N(d_2)\) falls from \(0.50\) to roughly \(0.44\) — the risk-neutral probability of finishing in the money actually goes down as volatility rises, because \(d_2\)'s numerator contains \(-\sigma^2/2\), which drags the risk-neutral drift of \(\ln S_T\) lower as \(\sigma\) grows (higher variance pulls the median of a lognormal distribution down even when its mean is held fixed). That looks paradoxical next to "higher volatility makes the call more valuable," but it isn't: the call's value depends on the whole probability-weighted payoff, including the fatter right tail that higher \(\sigma\) creates, not on the bare probability of finishing above the strike. This is exactly why vega (sensitivity to \(\sigma\)) is always positive for a plain call or put even though \(N(d_2)\) alone can move either way.
Q5. What is the practical difference between what delta tells you and what gamma tells you, and why do options market makers care more about gamma near expiration specifically?
Delta tells you the hedge you need right now — how many shares of the underlying offset the option's current price sensitivity. Gamma tells you how fast that hedge requirement itself is changing — how urgently and how often the hedge needs to be revisited as price moves. A position can have a modest delta but a large gamma, meaning the current hedge looks fine but will be stale again after a small price move. Gamma for at-the-money options rises as time to expiration shrinks (mathematically, gamma is inversely related to \(\sigma\sqrt{T}\) in a way that blows up as \(T \to 0\) for near-the-money strikes), so near expiry a market maker's hedge can go stale within minutes rather than days, forcing far more frequent rehedging — exactly the mechanism the OI course notes point to when explaining why gamma-wall and pinning effects intensify in the final 48 hours before expiry.
Q6. The model assumes constant volatility \(\sigma\) and a lognormal price distribution with no jumps. Name one piece of real-market evidence that contradicts each assumption, and why that matters for anyone using the formula in practice.
Against constant volatility: the volatility smile/skew — implied volatility (the \(\sigma\) that makes the formula match observed prices) systematically differs across strikes and expiries for the same underlying on the same day, which plain Black-Scholes (one flat \(\sigma\)) cannot produce; the market is effectively pricing in a richer volatility structure than the model assumes. Against no jumps / lognormal returns: real asset prices exhibit sudden discontinuous moves (earnings surprises, central bank announcements, flash crashes) that produce fatter tails in the realized return distribution than a lognormal model predicts — exactly the gap the next lesson's jump-diffusion model is built to address. Practically, this means Black-Scholes prices (with a single flat \(\sigma\)) are a useful first-order approximation and a common quoting language, but a trader relying on it without adjustment will systematically misprice far-out-of-the-money options and underestimate tail risk.
