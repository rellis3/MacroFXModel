The Efficient Market Hypothesis

Prices already reflect what's knowable — so before you believe you've found an edge, you should first ask why the market left it there.

CONCEPT — not a signal, the baseline every signal must beat ~27 min read Prereqs: none — start here
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • EMH's testable core: prices should follow a martingale — \(E[\epsilon_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}] = 0\) — so return autocorrelation \(\rho_1, \rho_2, \dots\) should be statistically indistinguishable from zero if the weak form holds.
  • Fama's three nested forms each rule out a different strategy: weak (past price/volume history) rules out technical analysis, semi-strong (all public info) rules out fundamental analysis on public data, strong (public + private info) would rule out even insider trading — and is essentially never taken as literally true.
  • The joint-hypothesis problem: you can never test market efficiency in isolation, only "efficiency + the assumed pricing model" together — so a single anomalous backtest can't tell you whether the market is inefficient or your pricing model just missed a risk factor.
  • This lesson's own worked example: with \(n=500\) daily returns, the noise-level standard error of \(\hat\rho_1\) is \(\approx 1/\sqrt{500} \approx 0.045\); \(\hat\rho_1=0.03\) is unremarkable noise, but \(\hat\rho_1=0.15\) clears ~3 standard errors — a real statistical signal, though still only the start of a research question, not proof of a tradeable edge (no OOS, cost, or mechanism check yet).
  • EMH doesn't forbid all profit — only riskless, cost-free profit — which is exactly why this repo bakes in an OOS split with a ≥30-trade gate, a named no-skill benchmark, and costs-on-by-default: they're EMH's joint-hypothesis and free-lunch logic turned into engineering rules.
  • Real-world episode: McLean & Pontiff (2016) found 97 published stock-return predictors earned about 58% less after publication (26% less in the gap before it), as investors traded them; and this desk's own "priced in" tests, where conditioning on what yields did into a release scored 49.3% against a shuffled placebo's 51.5%.

There's an old joke. An economist walks past a twenty-dollar bill on the pavement and says it can't be real, or someone would have picked it up.

That joke is the efficient market hypothesis. And on a pavement as crowded as a market, it is mostly right. A free edge in plain sight gets traded until it is gone.

So prices are always right?

No. The claim is narrower. Prices already reflect what is cheap and available to know, so you cannot use that information to predict the next surprise. Your best guess for tomorrow's price is today's price.

How would I test that?

Ask whether yesterday's return helps predict today's. Take five hundred daily returns. If there is no real link, the correlation you measure still wobbles by about point zero four five, just from noise. That is one over the square root of five hundred.

And if I measure point zero three?

Less than one standard error. Routine noise. Building a strategy on it would be a mistake.

What about point one five?

That is more than three standard errors. A real statistical signal in that sample. But it is the start of a research question, not an edge. You still don't know if it holds out of sample, if it survives costs, or why it would exist at all.

Why the last one?

Because of the joint-hypothesis problem. To call a return abnormal you need a model of normal. If you find excess returns, either the market is inefficient or your model missed a risk you are being paid to carry. No test separates the two.

Does anyone actually watch edges disappear?

Yes. David McLean and Jeffrey Pontiff followed ninety-seven published stock-return predictors. Returns were about twenty-six percent lower in the gap before publication, and about fifty-eight percent lower after it, as investors read the papers and traded them.

So the bill gets picked up once someone prints the map.

Exactly. And what was left sat in stocks that are expensive to trade. The bill lingers where bending down costs money.

So I can never make money?

You can. Efficiency forbids a free lunch, not getting paid for risk others won't carry, like carry or illiquidity. The question for any edge is who is paying you, and why.

One-line version?

Treat the market as efficient until you can show, out of sample and after costs, why the twenty-dollar bill is still there.

Section 01

Why this matters

Every lesson after this one in the Theory Lab describes a way to look at price data and try to extract an edge — a momentum effect, a mean-reversion pattern, a volatility risk premium, a macro-driven signal. Before any of that is useful, you need to understand the thing all of it is arguing against: the Efficient Market Hypothesis (EMH). EMH is not a rule you either "believe in" or "reject." It's the default assumption — the null hypothesis — that a price already incorporates all relevant, available information, so there is no free, riskless way to profit from information you already have access to. Nobody in quantitative finance treats EMH as literally, perfectly true. But almost everyone treats it as the thing you have to argue your way past, with evidence, before you trust a new strategy. That's the posture this entire repo is built around, and this lesson is where that posture comes from.

💡
Plain-English intuition

If a $20 bill is lying on a busy sidewalk, why is it still there?

This is the classic "efficient markets" joke, and it's worth taking seriously rather than dismissing as glib. If someone really did drop a $20 bill on a crowded sidewalk, the honest answer is: it probably wouldn't stay there long. Someone would pick it up within seconds. The same logic applies to financial markets, which are about as crowded and well-incentivized a sidewalk as exists — thousands of professional traders, each financially rewarded for spotting free money, are staring at the same prices you are. If a pattern in price data reliably predicted future returns and cost nothing to exploit, it would already be traded away: the buying or selling pressure from people exploiting it would push the price to the point where the edge disappears. EMH is the formal statement of that intuition — not "the market is always right about fundamental value," but "the market has already priced in everything that's cheap and available to know, so a costless edge sitting in plain sight is unlikely to survive." The interesting question — and the subject of the rest of this course — is what happens when exploiting the edge is not free: when it requires capital, patience, leverage, career risk, or tolerance for a kind of risk other participants are structurally unwilling to bear. Those frictions are exactly where real, persistent effects tend to live, and exactly why EMH is a starting prior rather than a wall.

Illustrative, not real market data. If today's return told you anything reliable about tomorrow's, this scatter would show a visible slope — a diagonal cloud instead of a shapeless one. Weak-form efficiency predicts exactly this shapeless cloud; consistently finding a slope here (net of costs, out of sample) is what a real anomaly would look like. Nothing about the process changed mid-path; the divergence is purely the accumulation of unpredictable, zero-mean shocks (\(\epsilon_t\)) compounding step by step. There was no single "true path" anyone could have forecast from the starting point — under EMH's random-walk implication, identical starting information still produces genuinely different outcomes, which is the practical, visual meaning of unpredictability.

The math, step by step

Watch the formula build
From "price plus a surprise" to a testable noise band, one piece at a time
\[ P_t = P_{t-1} + \color{#fbbf24}{\epsilon_t} \]

Today's price is yesterday's price plus a surprise. Everything the model says lives in what we assume about that surprise.

\[ P_t = P_{t-1} + \epsilon_t, \qquad E[\epsilon_t \mid \color{#fbbf24}{\mathcal{F}_{t-1}}] = 0 \]

Given everything known yesterday, your best forecast of the surprise is zero. Its size can be forecast; its direction cannot.

\[ E[P_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}] = \color{#fbbf24}{P_{t-1}} \]

Take expectations of the first line: the best forecast of tomorrow's price is today's price. That is a martingale, the object EMH actually predicts.

\[ \rho_1 = \mathrm{Corr}(r_t,\, r_{t-1}) = \color{#fbbf24}{0} \]

The checkable consequence: yesterday's return should not predict today's. The true lag-1 autocorrelation is zero.

\[ \mathrm{SE}(\hat\rho_1) \approx \color{#10b981}{\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}} \]

A sample estimate still wobbles around zero by about \(1/\sqrt{n}\). With 500 returns that is 0.045, so \(\hat\rho_1 = 0.03\) is noise and \(\hat\rho_1 = 0.15\), more than three standard errors out, is a signal worth investigating.

EMH itself is a qualitative claim about information, but it has a precise, testable mathematical consequence: if a market is efficient, prices should behave like a random walk (or more generally, a martingale) — strictly, after allowing for whatever normal expected return the pricing model permits, which is where the joint-hypothesis problem below comes from. Let's build that up term by term.

Let \(P_t\) denote the price of an asset at time \(t\) (say, today's closing price). Consider the simplest possible model of how price evolves from one period to the next:

This says: conditional on everything you knew at time \(t-1\) — including the entire history of past prices — your best forecast of tomorrow's shock is zero. Not "the shock is always exactly zero" (it isn't; that's exactly why prices move and risk exists) but "you cannot use what you already know to predict its direction." A process with this property is called a martingale: formally, \(E[P_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}] = P_{t-1}\) — your best forecast of tomorrow's price, given everything you know today, is simply today's price. Notice a martingale is a slightly weaker and more general statement than "pure random walk with i.i.d. shocks" — it only pins down the conditional mean, and says nothing about the variance of \(\epsilon_t\) needing to be constant over time (in real markets it isn't: this is exactly what volatility clustering and GARCH-type models capture, and it's why this repo's own volatility research exists as a separate, honest piece of machinery from any directional signal). A martingale in price is the object EMH actually predicts; "random walk" is the popular, slightly stronger shorthand for it.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

A market being a "martingale" just means your best guess for tomorrow's price is today's price — not because nothing will happen, but because you have no way to know yet which direction the next surprise will push it. It's like a coin that's landed heads ten times in a row: the honest guess for the next flip is still 50/50, because the coin has no memory, even though the running tally of heads keeps climbing around.

Why does this matter for trading? Because it has a sharp, checkable implication for autocorrelation — whether today's price change helps predict tomorrow's. Define the one-period return as \(r_t = P_t - P_{t-1} = \epsilon_t\) (in this simple additive model; in practice you'd typically use log returns, \(r_t = \ln P_t - \ln P_{t-1}\), for reasons covered in a later lesson on return construction). The lag-1 autocorrelation of returns is:

\[ \rho_1 = \mathrm{Corr}(r_t,\, r_{t-1}) \]
ρ₁lag-1 autocorrelation — does yesterday's return predict today's?
r_t, r_{t-1}today's and yesterday's returns

If the weak-form martingale property holds, \(\rho_1\) (and every \(\rho_k\) for \(k \ge 1\)) should be statistically indistinguishable from zero — knowing yesterday's return tells you nothing about today's. This is directly testable: you can compute the sample autocorrelation of a return series and run a formal test (a Ljung–Box test, a variance-ratio test, or simply a t-test on \(\hat\rho_1\)) to check whether it differs from zero by more than sampling noise would produce. This is precisely the kind of test that underlies decades of empirical market-efficiency research, and it's the mathematical core of the "weak form" described below.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Autocorrelation is just asking "does knowing yesterday's move help you guess today's move at all?" If a market is efficient, the honest answer is no — yesterday's up day tells you nothing useful about whether today will be up or down, the same way knowing a fair coin landed heads yesterday tells you nothing about today's flip.

The three forms of EMH

Eugene Fama's original 1970 formulation split the hypothesis into three nested forms, distinguished by what information is assumed to already be reflected in price. Each form is a stronger claim than the one before it, and each rules out a different category of strategy:

📈

Weak form

Current prices reflect all information in the history of past prices and volumes. If true, this rules out technical analysis — chart patterns, moving-average crossovers, momentum signals built purely from price history — as a source of riskless, cost-free edge, because any predictable pattern in past prices would already be arbitraged away. This is the form the random-walk math above directly tests.

📰

Semi-strong form

Current prices reflect all publicly available information — earnings reports, macro releases, news, analyst research, everything anyone can legally read. If true, this rules out fundamental analysis on public data as a source of riskless edge. Tested classically via event studies, checking how fast and completely prices adjust after a public information release.

🔒

Strong form

Current prices reflect all information, public and private. If true, even corporate insiders trading on undisclosed information could not systematically outperform. Essentially never taken as literally true — well-documented profitable insider trading shows private information has value the market hasn't priced in. Included mainly as the logical extreme; that private information pays is exactly what insider-trading law targets.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Think of it as three levels of a game being "unbeatable." Weak form says you can't win using only the scoreboard from past games. Semi-strong says you can't win even if you also read every public news report about the players. Strong form says you couldn't win even with secret information nobody else has — which is basically nobody's serious real-world claim, since insider trading really does make money, which is exactly why it's illegal.

The joint-hypothesis problem

Here is the single most important epistemological point in this lesson, and the reason EMH is unusually hard to pin down scientifically. Fama himself pointed this out (in his 1970 review and again explicitly in his 1991 update, "Efficient Capital Markets II"): you cannot test market efficiency in isolation. To test whether "prices reflect available information," you first need a model of what the price should be given that information — an asset-pricing or equilibrium model (CAPM, a multi-factor model, a discounted-cash-flow model, whatever). Every test of EMH is therefore secretly a joint test of two things at once:

The joint-hypothesis problem: every test of EMH is secretly a test of two things bundled together — "is the market efficient" and "is the pricing model used to define 'normal' correct." An anomalous result can never tell you which one failed.
  1. Is the market efficient (given the assumed pricing model)?
  2. Is the assumed pricing model itself correct?

If you run a test and find "abnormal" returns — returns your model didn't predict — you cannot tell which of the two failed. Maybe the market really is inefficient and you found a genuine mispricing. Or maybe the market is fine and your pricing model is simply missing a risk factor, so what looks like "alpha" is actually compensation for a risk your model didn't account for (this is exactly how the size and value factors were eventually absorbed into asset-pricing models that started as single-factor CAPM and grew into multi-factor models). There is no experiment that cleanly separates these two possibilities — you're always testing a bundle. This is why professional researchers are reluctant to declare "EMH is false" from a single anomaly: the anomaly might just mean the pricing model used to define "normal" was wrong, and a better model would swallow it.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine checking whether a bathroom scale is accurate, but the only way to test it is against a second scale that might itself be off. If the two disagree, you can't tell which one is wrong — maybe the first, maybe the second, maybe both a little. Testing "is the market efficient" has the same problem: you can only test it against a pricing model that might itself be wrong, so a surprising result never proves the market messed up — it might just mean your ruler was bent.

Worked example

A back-of-envelope autocorrelation check

Suppose you have 500 daily returns for a currency pair and you compute the lag-1 sample autocorrelation and get \(\hat\rho_1 = 0.03\). Is that evidence against weak-form efficiency? By itself, no — you need to know if 0.03 is distinguishable from the noise you'd expect even if the true \(\rho_1\) were exactly zero. Under the null of no autocorrelation, the standard error of a sample autocorrelation with \(n\) observations is approximately \(1/\sqrt{n}\). With \(n = 500\), that's \(1/\sqrt{500} \approx 0.045\). Your estimate of 0.03 is smaller than one standard error — nowhere near the roughly two-standard-error threshold usually used for "statistically significant" — so this is exactly the kind of small, noisy wobble a genuinely efficient, unpredictable series produces routinely. It would be a mistake to build a strategy on it.

Now suppose instead \(\hat\rho_1 = 0.15\) on the same 500 observations — more than three standard errors from zero. That's a real statistical signal: past returns do appear to help predict future ones, in this sample. But notice what you still don't have: you don't know if it will hold out-of-sample, you don't know if it survives realistic transaction costs and slippage, and — per the joint-hypothesis problem above — you don't know if it reflects a genuine informational inefficiency or just compensation for a risk factor your simple model of "normal" didn't include. A statistically significant autocorrelation is the start of a research question, not the end of one. This is exactly the gap between "found a pattern in historical data" and "found an edge" that the rest of this Theory Lab, and this repo's backtest discipline, exists to close.

Section 03

It actually happened: published, then priced

The $20-bill box in Section 01 says a costless edge in plain sight should not survive. Here is what that looks like when someone prints the map to the bill, and what happened when this desk tested the trader's everyday version of efficiency, "it's priced in".

It actually happened

McLean & Pontiff: what happens to a $20 bill once it is in a journal

Academic finance spent decades documenting stock-return anomalies: characteristics such as past returns, accounting ratios or share issuance that predicted which stocks would do better. If real, each one was a bill on the sidewalk. David McLean and Jeffrey Pontiff asked the next question: what happens to the bill after someone publishes a map to it?

In "Does Academic Research Destroy Stock Return Predictability?" (Journal of Finance, 2016) they followed 97 published predictors through three periods: the original study's sample, the stretch after that sample ended but before the paper appeared, and the years after publication. Returns were about 26% lower in the stretch before publication, which they treat as an upper bound on what data mining alone explains: part of the in-sample result was the luck of the original search. After publication returns were about 58% lower. The difference, roughly 32%, is the part they attribute to investors reading the research and trading on it.

Two details make it this lesson rather than a footnote. The post-publication decline was larger for the predictors with the biggest in-sample returns: the most visible bills went first. And predictor returns were higher in stocks that are expensive to arbitrage, the illiquid ones and those with high idiosyncratic risk. The bill gets picked up where bending down is cheap, and it lingers where bending down costs money.

This desk: "it's priced in", tested two ways

Traders use "priced in" as a forecast, so the desk tested both versions it usually carries: does a decision the front end has already repriced move markets less on the day, and does the run-up into a release say which way the outcome pushes? Neither held up; the two boxes further down give the numbers.

Read through this lesson, the direction result is the semi-strong form passing a test. The run-up into a release is public information and already in the price, so it should tell you nothing about which way the surprise pushes, and it told the desk less than nothing. "Priced in" is true in the efficient-markets sense and useless as a trade: the prior repricing did not make the day smaller, and it did not say which way the day would go.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Once someone posts a map to the $20 bill online, lots of people go and look, and soon it's gone. That's what happened to published stock tricks: after the papers came out, they paid a lot less. And knowing that "everyone already expected this" didn't help the desk guess which way prices would jump. A shuffled, random version of the guess did slightly better.

Where the textbook version breaks

The textbook EMH is a switch: information is either in the price or it isn't, and it gets there at once. The McLean–Pontiff evidence is a dial. Publication roughly halved the returns rather than erasing them, over years rather than instantly, and what remained sat where arbitrage is costly: the limits-to-arbitrage and Adaptive Markets reading from the pitfalls box below, not the frictionless one. It is also the joint-hypothesis problem in the wild: the 26% that vanished before publication is, at most, the original search's luck rather than an inefficiency (McLean and Pontiff treat it as an upper bound on data mining).

Section 04

How practitioners actually use this

Nobody at a real trading desk spends their day debating whether EMH is "true." It's used as a working tool in three concrete ways:

  • As a prior, not a verdict. When a new pattern shows up in a backtest, the default assumption is that it's noise, overfitting, or a known risk premium in disguise — not a genuine new discovery — until proven otherwise. The burden of proof sits on the new idea, not on the market's efficiency. This inverts how a naive researcher instinctively treats a backtest: "if the equity curve goes up, it works" gets replaced with "if the equity curve goes up, what would have to be true about market structure for this to be real, and can I find supporting evidence for that beyond the curve itself?"
  • As a filter for economic plausibility. A durable inefficiency generally needs a reason it survives being arbitraged away — some friction that stops the "$20 bill" from being picked up instantly. Common survival reasons: funding constraints (an edge requires leverage or capital that most participants can't or won't deploy), information asymmetry (genuinely hard-to-observe or hard-to-interpret data), structural/forced flows (rebalancing flows, hedging flows, central bank operations that aren't profit-seeking and therefore don't get arbitraged away by pure profit-seekers), regulatory constraints (position limits, capital requirements that stop certain players from taking the other side), or a genuine risk premium (the "edge" is really compensation for bearing a risk — tail risk, illiquidity, career risk — that most participants avoid). If you can't articulate why the effect you found should exist and persist, that's a reason for skepticism, not a decisive kill — but it raises the bar for what evidence would convince you.
  • As the benchmark, always. Any claimed edge is judged against what a no-skill, EMH-consistent baseline would have done — usually buy-and-hold, or a simple fixed exposure. "Beat a bad strategy" and "beat efficient-market pricing" are different bars; only the second one is meaningful.
⚠️
Common pitfalls

Ways people misuse or misunderstand EMH

  • Treating EMH as "no strategy can ever make money." It doesn't say that. It says riskless, information-free profit shouldn't persist. Strategies that earn a return by bearing a genuine, priced risk (equity risk premium, carry, illiquidity) are entirely consistent with efficient markets — that's compensation for risk, not a free lunch. Confusing "EMH forbids all profitable strategies" with what it actually claims is one of the most common misreadings.
    🧒 Explain like I'm 5

    EMH doesn't say nobody can ever make money — it says nobody can make money for free, with zero risk attached. Getting paid extra for driving a truck full of fireworks isn't "beating the system," it's fair payment for taking on real danger other drivers won't. A lot of trading profit works the same way: it's the market paying you to hold a risk most other people don't want, not a secret trick.

  • Treating a single backtest anomaly as falsification. Because of the joint-hypothesis problem, one profitable-looking backtest can never single-handedly refute market efficiency — it's equally consistent with "the pricing model used to judge 'abnormal' was incomplete." Refuting EMH convincingly requires an effect that's robust across time periods, markets, and specifications, and ideally has a plausible economic mechanism.
  • Conflating "efficient" with "correctly priced" or "rational." EMH says prices reflect available information quickly — it does not require that the information itself, or the participants processing it, are rational, unbiased, or free of behavioral quirks. A market can be highly efficient at incorporating information and still occasionally price things at levels that look absurd in hindsight (bubbles), if the information available at the time didn't clearly distinguish the bubble from a justified re-rating.
  • Ignoring Andrew Lo's Adaptive Markets refinement. A newer, less absolutist view — the Adaptive Markets Hypothesis — reframes efficiency as something that varies with the competitive/ecological conditions of a market rather than a fixed on/off property: markets are "more or less efficient" depending on the population of participants, how much capital is chasing a given effect, and how the environment is changing. This doesn't overturn the core "no easy free lunch" logic, but it explains why some inefficiencies can appear, get arbitraged away, and reappear as conditions shift — rather than treating EMH as a single permanent verdict.
  • Forgetting weak-form and semi-strong-form violations are different claims. A violation of the weak form means past prices alone predict future prices — a much narrower, more surprising claim than a semi-strong violation, which just means some public information (an earnings report, a macro release) takes time to be fully priced in. Confusing the two overstates how strong a given piece of evidence actually is.
Scenario drill · an illustrative research meeting

A strong anomaly, freshly published

A journal has just published a stock-return predictor with an impressive record over decades of data in the original study. A colleague wants to start trading it next week, sized on the paper's historical returns. What do you recommend?

  1. Trade it at full size. Decades of significant returns cannot be luck.
    The in-sample record is the most optimistic number you will ever see. Part of it is the luck of the original search, and once a predictor is published investors trade it. Sizing on the paper's returns oversizes the trade.
  2. Assume much of the edge has gone, test it on data from after the study's sample, with costs, and ask what friction would let it survive.
    This is the evidence and the logic of the lesson together. Returns on published predictors fell by about 58% on average after publication, and an edge needs a reason it is not arbitraged away. If it survives that test, size it on the out-of-sample result.
  3. Trade it only in illiquid, hard-to-arbitrage stocks, where the effect held up better.
    That matches McLean and Pontiff: predictor returns were higher where arbitrage is costly. But the same costs that protect the effect also eat into your returns, so it needs a cost-inclusive test before any money goes in.
  4. Ignore it. Efficient markets mean no strategy can ever make money.
    That misreads the hypothesis. It forbids a riskless free lunch, not every profit. On average published predictors lost a large share of their returns, not all of them, and a priced risk can still pay.

What the research shows. This is an illustrative situation, not a dated event. The evidence behind the answer is real: McLean and Pontiff (Journal of Finance, 2016) followed 97 published predictors and found returns about 26% lower in the gap between the end of the study's sample and publication, and about 58% lower after publication. The decline was larger for the predictors with the biggest in-sample returns, and returns held up better where arbitrage is expensive.

Section 05

Key Takeaways

✓EMH's testable core is a martingale. \(E[\epsilon_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}] = 0\) — return autocorrelation should be statistically indistinguishable from zero if the weak form holds.
✓Fama's three nested forms rule out different strategies. Weak (past prices) rules out technical analysis, semi-strong (all public info) rules out fundamental analysis on public data, strong (public + private) would rule out even insider trading.
✓The joint-hypothesis problem is unavoidable. You can never test market efficiency in isolation — only "efficiency + the assumed pricing model" together.
⚠A statistically significant autocorrelation is a start, not an edge. Clearing ~3 standard errors (like \(\hat\rho_1=0.15\) on 500 observations) says nothing yet about OOS survival, costs, or mechanism.
✓EMH forbids the free lunch, not all profit. Genuine risk premia — carry, illiquidity, tail risk — are entirely compatible with efficient markets.
✓This repo's validation discipline is EMH turned into engineering. The OOS split, the ≥30-trade gate, a named no-skill benchmark, and costs-on-by-default are the joint-hypothesis and free-lunch logic made into hard rules.
⚠Adaptive Markets reframes efficiency as variable, not fixed. Inefficiencies can appear, get arbitraged away, and reappear as the competitive landscape shifts — don't treat EMH as a single permanent verdict.
✗"Priced in" is not a trade (priced-in-direction and priced-in, both NULL). Conditioning on what yields did into a release scored 49.3% against a shuffled placebo's 51.5%, and FOMC days ran ≈1.3 ATR however much had been repriced beforehand. Published edges decay too: about 58% lower after publication (McLean & Pontiff).
Section 06

Self-test

Q1. Why can't EMH ever be conclusively falsified by a single empirical test?
Because of the joint-hypothesis problem: testing whether prices reflect available information requires assuming some model of what the "correctly reflected" price should be. Any test that finds abnormal returns can't distinguish between "the market is inefficient" and "the assumed pricing model is wrong." Both explanations are always available, so no single test result can cleanly refute market efficiency on its own — you're always testing the pair together.
Q2. Does EMH forbid all profitable trading strategies?
No. EMH forbids riskless, information-free profit that persists after costs — a genuine free lunch. It does not forbid earning returns by bearing real, priced risk (market risk, carry risk, illiquidity risk, tail risk) or by having a genuine informational or structural advantage that isn't available to the market as a whole. Strategies that work because they're compensated for risk are entirely compatible with efficient markets.
Q3. What's the difference between a weak-form violation and a semi-strong-form violation?
A weak-form violation means future price movements can be predicted from past price/volume history alone — e.g. a reliable technical pattern. A semi-strong-form violation means prices don't fully and immediately incorporate publicly available non-price information — e.g. an earnings report or macro release that keeps moving the price for days after release rather than being priced in instantly. Semi-strong violations are the broader, more commonly-documented category (post-earnings-announcement drift is a well-known example); weak-form violations are the narrower and, if genuinely found, more surprising claim, since price history is the cheapest, most universally available data there is.
Q4. If a backtest shows a strategy with a statistically significant positive Sharpe ratio in-sample, has it beaten EMH?
Not by itself. In-sample significance only shows the rule fits the specific historical sample it was built or tuned on — which is exactly what curve-fitting to noise would also produce. It says nothing about whether the pattern generalizes to data the rule never saw, whether it survives realistic transaction costs, or whether — per the joint-hypothesis problem — it reflects a real inefficiency versus an incomplete benchmark model. Only a genuinely out-of-sample result, with costs, evaluated against a stated no-skill benchmark, and ideally with a plausible economic reason to persist, counts as real evidence.
Q5. What kinds of frictions or structural features let a market inefficiency persist despite profit-seeking arbitrageurs?
Common candidates: funding/capital constraints (the trade needs leverage or capital most participants can't deploy), information asymmetry (the relevant data is hard to obtain or interpret), structural or forced flows (rebalancing, hedging, or policy-driven flows that aren't profit-motivated and therefore don't get arbitraged away), regulatory limits (position caps, capital rules that keep certain players from taking the other side), and genuine risk premia (what looks like "alpha" is compensation for a risk — illiquidity, tail risk, career risk — that most capital avoids bearing). An apparent edge with none of these plausible is more likely to be overfitting or noise than a real, durable finding.
Q6. How does the Adaptive Markets Hypothesis (Andrew Lo) differ from the classical EMH framing?
Classical EMH treats efficiency as close to a fixed property a market either has or lacks. Adaptive Markets reframes efficiency as a variable, ecological outcome: how efficient a market is at any moment depends on the population of participants, how much capital and competition is chasing a given effect, and how conditions are changing (an evolutionary/competitive analogy rather than a physics-style equilibrium one). This explains, without abandoning the "no easy free lunch" core logic, why effects can appear, attract capital, get arbitraged away, and later reappear as the competitive landscape shifts.
