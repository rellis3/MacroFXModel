Execution Algorithms: TWAP, VWAP, POV, IS

Once you accept that a big order moves the price against you the more of it you dump at once, "how do I actually place it" stops being an afterthought and becomes its own decision — four standard schedules, and the honest cost accounting (Perold's Implementation Shortfall) that tells you which one you actually needed.

CONCEPT — not built or tested in this repo ~17 min read Prereqs: market-impact-almgren-chriss.html (Market Impact Models & Almgren-Chriss)
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • TWAP spreads an order evenly across time — simple and predictable, but blind to the fact that trading volume itself is never evenly spread through the day.
  • VWAP instead matches the order's schedule to the historical intraday volume profile, trading more during the heavy open/close and less during the thin midday lull, so the order participates roughly in proportion to how much everyone else is trading.
  • POV (Percentage of Volume) drops the pre-set schedule entirely and instead trades at a fixed percentage of real-time observed volume, so it naturally speeds up when the market gets busy and slows down when it goes quiet.
  • Implementation Shortfall (Perold, 1988) is the honest scorecard: the gap between the "paper" decision price and what you actually paid, split into delay cost (drift before you started), market impact cost (the Almgren-Chriss-style concession from your own trading), and opportunity cost (the price of leaving part of the order unfilled).
  • Algorithm choice is a real tradeoff, not a preference: urgency (fear of the price drifting away before you're done) pulls toward TWAP/POV/aggressive execution; impact concern (fear of moving the price yourself) pulls toward a slower VWAP-style schedule — the same risk-aversion tradeoff the Almgren-Chriss lesson formalizes with a single parameter \(\lambda\).
  • The worked example computes a 4-bucket VWAP-matching share schedule for a 40,000-share order, then separately walks a small trade through all three Implementation Shortfall components in dollars: \$450 delay + \$750 impact + \$300 opportunity = \$1,500 total, on a \$500,000 order.
Section 01

Why this matters

The Market Impact Models & Almgren-Chriss lesson in this series establishes why a large order should be broken up over time in the first place: trading it all at once pays a large, avoidable temporary-impact cost, while spreading it out too slowly leaves it exposed to price risk for longer. That lesson solves for the theoretically optimal trading trajectory given a risk-aversion parameter. This lesson is about what happens next — the concrete, named schedules real desks actually type into an order-management system, and the accounting framework used to judge, after the fact, whether a given execution was actually any good.

Four names come up constantly in any conversation about how an order was actually worked: TWAP, VWAP, POV, and Implementation Shortfall. The first three are execution styles — different rules for pacing the same total order over time. The fourth, Implementation Shortfall, is not a pacing style at all — it is the measurement framework used to score any of the other three (or any other execution approach) against what the trade decision was actually worth before execution began.

💡
Plain-English intuition

You've decided to buy 40,000 shares. Now what?

Suppose a portfolio manager decides, at 9:30am, to buy 40,000 shares of a stock. Nothing about that decision says how to actually place the order. Dump it all in at once as a single market order, and — per the Kyle's Lambda logic from earlier in this series — you push the price against yourself immediately and pay a large, unnecessary impact cost on top of whatever the shares were "really" worth at 9:30am. Spread it out, and you avoid that self-inflicted cost, but now you're exposed to a new risk: the price might simply drift away from you — up, if you're buying — before you finish, for reasons that have nothing to do with your own trading at all.

The simplest possible answer to "how do I spread it out" is: evenly, by the clock. Trade 1/8th of the order every half hour across a 4-hour window. That's TWAP — Time-Weighted Average Price — and it's a completely reasonable first idea. Its flaw is equally simple: real markets don't trade the same amount every half hour. Volume is almost always heaviest right at the open and right at the close, and thinnest around midday. An evenly-spread TWAP order is, without meaning to, trading a disproportionately large share of its size during the thin midday lull relative to how much natural liquidity is actually available then — exactly the condition that (as the earlier Market Microstructure lesson's Kyle's Lambda scenario showed) makes the same order size cost more in impact. VWAP fixes this by matching the order's pace to the market's own historical volume pattern instead of the plain clock, and POV goes one step further and reacts to volume as it happens in real time, rather than trusting a historical pattern at all. All three are just different answers to the same one question: given that you must spread the order out somehow, exactly what should "somehow" mean?

Section 02

The math, step by step

Three scheduling rules first, in increasing order of sophistication, then the Implementation Shortfall accounting framework used to judge any of them after the fact.

1. TWAP — Time-Weighted Average Price

Split the total order \(X\) into \(K\) equal time intervals and trade an equal fraction in each:

\[ x_k = \frac{X}{K} \quad \text{for every interval } k=1,\dots,K \]
Xtotal order size to execute
Knumber of equal time intervals the execution window is chopped into
x_kshares traded in interval k — identical for every interval, by construction

TWAP's entire appeal is its simplicity and predictability — a counterparty (or a compliance reviewer) can verify exactly what should have traded in any given interval with nothing more than a calculator. Its weakness is exactly what the intuition above described: it implicitly assumes trading volume is uniform through the day, which is essentially never true. TWAP trades too large a fraction of its order, relative to natural liquidity, during quiet periods, and too small a fraction during the heaviest, most liquidity-rich windows.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

TWAP is like deciding to drink exactly one eighth of a bottle of water every half hour no matter what — simple to plan, but it ignores that you're actually much thirstier right after waking up and right before bed than in the middle of the day. It's an easy rule, just not a smart one about when demand is naturally higher or lower.

2. VWAP — Volume-Weighted Average Price

VWAP fixes exactly that blind spot by scheduling participation proportional to a historical intraday volume profile — the empirically typical fraction of a full day's volume that trades in each interval, estimated from recent past sessions in the same name:

\[ x_k = X \cdot w_k, \qquad \sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k = 1 \]
w_kthe historical share of total daily volume that typically trades in interval k
Σw_k = 1the weights across all K intervals must sum to exactly the whole order

Interval \(k\)'s weight \(w_k\) is not fixed at \(1/K\) like TWAP — it is read off (or forecast from) that name's own historical volume curve, which in most liquid equity markets is famously "U-shaped": heavy at the open, heavy again at the close, and lighter through the middle of the day. Trading in proportion to \(w_k\) means the order participates at roughly the same rate as the rest of the market throughout the day — heavier when the market is naturally heavier, lighter when it's naturally lighter — which, by the same Kyle's-Lambda logic as before, tends to produce less impact per share than TWAP's flat schedule, because the order is never forced to represent an outsized fraction of a thin period's volume.

The benchmark this schedule targets, the actual VWAP price for the day, is itself just the volume-weighted average traded price across the whole session:

\[ \text{VWAP} = \frac{\sum_{k} P_k \cdot \text{Vol}_k}{\sum_{k} \text{Vol}_k} \]
P_kthe traded price in interval k
Vol_kthe total market volume traded in interval k

An order executed exactly along the historical weights \(w_k\), at each interval's prevailing price, will tend to land close to this VWAP benchmark — which is precisely why "VWAP" is used both as the name of the scheduling algorithm and as the name of the performance benchmark that algorithm (and, informally, almost any execution) gets measured against.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

VWAP is the same water-drinking idea as TWAP, but smarter: drink more of the bottle during the times you're actually thirstiest (morning and night) and less during the quiet middle stretch, matching your pace to real demand instead of the plain clock.

3. POV — Percentage of Volume

TWAP and VWAP both commit, in advance, to a fixed schedule — \(x_k\) is decided before the trading day even starts. POV drops that commitment entirely and instead targets a fixed participation rate, \(p\), against whatever volume is actually observed in real time:

\[ x_k = p \cdot \text{Vol}_k^{\text{observed}} \]
pthe target participation rate — e.g. 10% means "trade 10% of whatever volume actually happens"
Vol_k^observedthe real, live volume that traded in interval k — not a historical forecast

Because \(p\) is applied to observed volume rather than a pre-committed historical weight, POV automatically speeds up when the market is unexpectedly busy (a news headline, a large print from someone else) and automatically slows down when it's unexpectedly quiet — it adapts to the actual session as it unfolds, rather than to what a typical session usually looks like. The tradeoff: a POV order's total completion time is not known in advance the way TWAP's or VWAP's is, since it depends on how much total volume actually materializes; an unexpectedly quiet day can leave a POV order still unfinished well past when a TWAP or VWAP schedule would already be done, which is exactly the "opportunity cost of an unfilled remainder" component of Implementation Shortfall covered next.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

POV throws out the pre-planned drinking schedule entirely and says "drink 10% of however much water everyone else in the room is drinking, whenever they drink it." If the room suddenly gets thirsty, you speed up automatically; if it goes quiet, you slow down automatically. The catch: you no longer know exactly when you'll finish your own bottle, because that now depends on everyone else's pace, not just yours.

TWAP — evenly by the clock

Simple, fully predictable in advance. Ignores that volume isn't uniform, so it over-trades thin periods and under-trades heavy ones relative to natural liquidity.

VWAP — by the historical volume shape

Matches a pre-committed schedule to the typical U-shaped intraday volume curve. Still a fixed, pre-set schedule — vulnerable if today's actual volume pattern doesn't match history.

POV — a fixed % of live volume

Reactive rather than pre-committed — speeds up and slows down with real trading activity as it happens. Completion time is uncertain, since it depends on how much volume actually shows up.

4. Implementation Shortfall — Perold's honest scorecard

André Perold's 1988 framework starts from a single, sharp idea: the real cost of a trade decision is not just the commission and spread paid on the shares that got filled. It is the total gap between the "paper" world — where the entire order executes instantly, at the price that prevailed the moment the decision was made, with zero cost — and the real world, where execution takes time, moves the price, and sometimes doesn't fully complete at all:

\[ \text{IS} = (\bar{P}_{\text{exec}} - P_{\text{decision}})\cdot X_{\text{filled}} \;+\; (P_{\text{cancel}} - P_{\text{decision}})\cdot X_{\text{unfilled}} \]
P_decisionthe "paper" price at the exact moment the trade decision was made
P̄_execthe actual average price achieved on the shares that did get filled
X_filled / X_unfilledhow many shares of the original order were, and weren't, executed
P_cancelthe price used to value the unfilled remainder — e.g. the price when the order was cancelled or the session's close

Read in words: Implementation Shortfall is what you actually paid for the shares you got, above the paper decision price, plus a charge for the shares you never got at all, valued at whatever the price was by the time you gave up on them. This one number is the honest, all-in cost of the entire decision-to-execution process — but a single total dollar figure doesn't say why it was expensive. Perold's framework's real value is splitting that total into causally distinct pieces:

1

Delay cost

Price drift between the decision and the moment trading actually begins — time spent getting the order to a desk, choosing an algorithm, or simply waiting for a chosen entry point, during which the price can move for reasons having nothing to do with your own trading yet:

\[ \text{Delay} = (P_{\text{start}} - P_{\text{decision}}) \cdot X_{\text{filled}} \]
2

Market impact cost

The price concession that comes specifically from your own trading pushing the price as you execute — the Kyle's-Lambda / Almgren-Chriss cost, measured from when trading actually began to the average price you achieved:

\[ \text{Impact} = (\bar{P}_{\text{exec}} - P_{\text{start}}) \cdot X_{\text{filled}} \]
3

Opportunity cost

The cost attached to any portion of the order left unfilled — valued against the decision price, since an incomplete order means the portfolio manager's original decision was never fully carried out at all:

\[ \text{Opportunity} = (P_{\text{cancel}} - P_{\text{decision}}) \cdot X_{\text{unfilled}} \]
Delay
Cost of hesitation
Drift before your own trading even began — not caused by you
Impact
Cost of your own footprint
The concession from your own trading — this is the Almgren-Chriss term
Opportunity
Cost of giving up
What the unfilled portion of the order "should have" cost, valued at the decision price

Because delay and impact are both measured against the shares that actually filled (\(X_\text{filled}\)), and opportunity cost is measured against exactly the shares that didn't (\(X_\text{unfilled}\)), the three components add up cleanly back to the total: Delay + Impact for the filled shares reduces to \((\bar P_\text{exec}-P_\text{decision})\cdot X_\text{filled}\), and adding the Opportunity cost term for the unfilled shares reproduces the full Implementation Shortfall formula above exactly — nothing is double-counted, and nothing is left out.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Implementation Shortfall answers "what did it really cost me to actually get this done, start to finish?" not just "what price did I pay for the shares I got." It breaks that real cost into three honest buckets: how much the price drifted before you even started (not your fault), how much your own buying pushed the price up while you traded (your footprint), and how much it cost you to simply never finish the order at all (the price of giving up).

\(X\)Total order size the decision called for.
\(K\)Number of scheduling intervals an order is broken into (TWAP/VWAP).
\(x_k\)Shares scheduled to trade in interval \(k\).
\(w_k\)Historical fraction of a typical day's volume occurring in interval \(k\) — the VWAP schedule's weights.
\(p\)Target participation rate for a POV order — the fixed fraction of real-time volume to trade.
\(\text{Vol}_k\)Total market volume traded in interval \(k\) (historical for VWAP's \(w_k\), live for POV).

\(P_\text{start}\)The price when actual trading began, after any delay.
\(\bar P_\text{exec}\)The volume-weighted average price actually achieved across all filled shares.
\(P_\text{cancel}\)The price used to value any unfilled remainder (e.g. cancellation price or session close).
\(X_\text{filled}\), \(X_\text{unfilled}\)Shares of the original order that were, and were not, executed.

Illustrative, a stylized "U-shape" typical of many liquid equity markets — real profiles vary by name and by market. A VWAP schedule's bars (amber) are proportional to the historical volume bars (blue) in every bucket: heavier at the open and close, lighter through the middle of the day. A TWAP schedule, by contrast, would draw four bars of exactly equal height regardless of this shape.

Worked example

Part 1 — building a VWAP schedule; Part 2 — computing Implementation Shortfall

Part 1. A desk needs to buy \(X=40{,}000\) shares and wants to match the historical intraday volume profile shown in the chart above: Open 35%, Midday 1 15%, Midday 2 15%, Close 35% (\(\sum w_k = 1.00\)). The VWAP schedule is simply \(x_k = X\cdot w_k\) in each bucket:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Open: } & 40{,}000 \times 0.35 = 14{,}000 \text{ shares}\\ \text{Midday 1: } & 40{,}000 \times 0.15 = 6{,}000 \text{ shares}\\ \text{Midday 2: } & 40{,}000 \times 0.15 = 6{,}000 \text{ shares}\\ \text{Close: } & 40{,}000 \times 0.35 = 14{,}000 \text{ shares} \end{aligned} \]

Check: \(14{,}000+6{,}000+6{,}000+14{,}000 = 40{,}000\) — the full order, allocated in exactly the same proportions as the market's own historical volume shape. A TWAP schedule on the same order would instead trade a flat 10,000 shares in every one of the four buckets.

Part 2. A separate, small trade: a portfolio manager decides at 9:30am to buy 10,000 shares at a decision price \(P_\text{decision}=\$50.00\). By the time the desk actually starts trading, the price has drifted to \(P_\text{start}=\$50.05\). The order fills in two clips — 6,000 shares at \$50.10 and 3,000 shares at \$50.20 — leaving 1,000 shares unfilled at the end of the day, valued at the closing price \(P_\text{cancel}=\$50.30\).

1

Average execution price on the filled shares

\[ \bar{P}_{\text{exec}} = \frac{6{,}000(50.10) + 3{,}000(50.20)}{9{,}000} = \frac{300{,}600+150{,}600}{9{,}000} = \frac{451{,}200}{9{,}000} \approx \$50.1333 \]
2

Delay cost — drift before trading began

\[ \text{Delay} = (50.05 - 50.00)\times 9{,}000 = 0.05 \times 9{,}000 = \$450 \]

Measured over the 9,000 shares that ultimately got filled — the unfilled 1,000 shares are accounted for separately, in the opportunity cost term, so no shares get charged for delay twice.

3

Market impact cost — the price concession from your own trading

\[ \text{Impact} = (50.1333 - 50.05)\times 9{,}000 = 0.0833\times 9{,}000 = \$750 \]
4

Opportunity cost — the unfilled 1,000 shares

\[ \text{Opportunity} = (50.30 - 50.00)\times 1{,}000 = 0.30\times 1{,}000 = \$300 \]
5

Total Implementation Shortfall

\[ \text{IS} = \$450 + \$750 + \$300 = \$1{,}500 \]

On a \(10{,}000\)-share order at a \$50.00 decision price (\$500,000 of notional), \$1,500 of total shortfall is 30 basis points — and the breakdown says exactly where it came from: half of it (\$750) is genuine market impact from the desk's own trading, \$450 is drift that happened before a single share traded, and \$300 is the price of never finishing the order at all. A single "\$1,500, or 30bps" headline number would hide all three of those very different root causes.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

Algorithm selection on a real execution desk is a genuine decision, made trade by trade, that comes down to weighing two competing fears against each other — exactly the risk-aversion tradeoff the Almgren-Chriss lesson formalizes with a single parameter, now applied as a practical choice between named, off-the-shelf schedules:

⏳

Urgency — fear of the price drifting away

High conviction, a fast-moving name, or information that could leak or become stale pushes toward finishing quickly — accepting more impact cost in exchange for less exposure to adverse price drift while the order is still open.

🌊

Impact concern — fear of moving the price yourself

A large order relative to typical daily volume, or a thinly-traded name, pushes toward a patient, low-footprint schedule — accepting more price-drift risk in exchange for a smaller self-inflicted impact cost.

🧭

The practical resolution

Low urgency and high impact concern favors VWAP; high urgency with real impact concern favors POV's adaptive pacing; genuinely low stakes on both fronts favors plain TWAP for its simplicity; high urgency with low impact concern favors an aggressive, fast-completing schedule that accepts impact cost as the price of certainty.

Implementation Shortfall is the after-the-fact discipline that keeps this honest: a desk that always claims "we chose the schedule the situation called for" should be able to show, trade after trade, that its delay, impact, and opportunity costs actually land where that logic predicts — low impact cost on patient VWAP trades, low delay/opportunity cost on urgent aggressive trades — rather than simply asserting it. Transaction cost analysis (TCA) desks exist specifically to run this Implementation Shortfall decomposition on every trade, systematically, rather than trusting a single blended average cost number.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where execution-algorithm reasoning goes wrong

Treating "VWAP" as automatically synonymous with "good execution." Matching the VWAP benchmark only means you traded in proportion to how the whole market traded that day — if the price trended strongly against you throughout the session, a perfectly-executed VWAP schedule still pays that trend in full. VWAP is a participation schedule and a benchmark, not a guarantee of a good price.

Ignoring that VWAP's schedule is built on a historical, not a guaranteed, volume profile. If the actual day's volume shape differs meaningfully from history (an unscheduled news event concentrates volume somewhere the historical profile didn't expect), a pre-committed VWAP schedule can end up badly out of step with real liquidity, exactly where POV's live-volume adaptivity would have done better.

Treating Implementation Shortfall's "decision price" as arbitrary or movable after the fact. The whole framework's honesty depends on fixing \(P_\text{decision}\) at the true moment the decision was made, before any information about how execution actually went. Quietly redefining the decision price to a more favorable moment after seeing the results turns an honest cost-accounting tool into a way to flatter a bad execution.

Comparing Implementation Shortfall totals across trades of very different urgency without the decomposition. A low total IS on a low-urgency VWAP trade and a low total IS on a high-urgency aggressive trade do not represent the same quality of execution decision-making — the components behind an equal headline number can look completely different, which is exactly why the breakdown, not just the total, is the useful output.

Forgetting the opportunity cost term entirely. An execution that never finishes the order can look artificially cheap if only the cost on the filled shares is reported — Perold's framework exists specifically because a merely "good average price on the shares that traded" can hide a decision that was never actually carried out in full.

Section 04

Key Takeaways

✓TWAP is the simple baseline, not the sophisticated choice. Equal shares in equal time intervals is predictable but ignores that real trading volume is never uniform through the day.
✓VWAP schedules by the historical volume shape. \(x_k = X\cdot w_k\) matches participation to a typically U-shaped intraday volume curve — heavier at the open/close, lighter at midday.
✓POV reacts to real, live volume instead of a historical forecast. \(x_k = p\cdot\text{Vol}_k^\text{observed}\) speeds up and slows down with actual market activity, at the cost of an uncertain completion time.
✓Implementation Shortfall is the honest, all-in scorecard. It splits the gap between decision price and actual cost into delay, market impact, and opportunity cost — three different root causes, not one opaque number.
✓Algorithm choice is a real tradeoff, not a habit. Urgency (fear of drift) pulls toward faster, more aggressive execution; impact concern (fear of your own footprint) pulls toward a slower, VWAP-style schedule — the same risk-aversion axis Almgren-Chriss formalizes mathematically.
⚠Matching the VWAP benchmark is not the same as a good price. A perfectly on-schedule VWAP execution still pays in full for any adverse trend that ran through the whole session — the benchmark measures participation quality, not price quality.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. What specific blind spot does VWAP fix relative to TWAP?
TWAP assumes trading volume is uniform across the execution window and splits the order into equal-sized clips per time interval regardless. Real intraday volume is essentially never uniform — it is typically heaviest at the open and close and lightest around midday. TWAP therefore ends up trading a disproportionately large share of its order, relative to natural liquidity, during thin periods (worse impact, by Kyle's-Lambda logic) and a disproportionately small share during heavy periods. VWAP fixes this by weighting each interval's order size to the historical fraction of a typical day's volume that trades then, so the order participates roughly in proportion to actual market activity.
Q2. Why can a POV order's total completion time not be known in advance, unlike TWAP or VWAP?
TWAP and VWAP both pre-commit to a fixed schedule over a fixed time window before trading starts, so their duration is known up front. POV instead targets a fixed percentage \(p\) of whatever volume actually trades in real time, \(x_k = p\cdot\text{Vol}_k^\text{observed}\) — the total time to complete the order therefore depends on how much real volume actually shows up, which is not known in advance. An unexpectedly quiet session can leave a POV order unfinished well past when a TWAP or VWAP schedule targeting the same size would already be done.
Q3. In Perold's Implementation Shortfall framework, why is delay cost measured against \(X_\text{filled}\) rather than the full original order size?
Because the unfilled portion of the order already has its own, separate cost term — opportunity cost, \((P_\text{cancel}-P_\text{decision})\times X_\text{unfilled}\) — measured against the decision price directly. Charging delay cost against the full original order size (filled plus unfilled) would double-count the drift on the unfilled shares once inside delay cost and again inside opportunity cost. Measuring delay only against the shares that were ultimately filled keeps the three components (delay, impact, opportunity) summing back exactly to the total shortfall with no shares counted twice.
Q4. A 20,000-share buy order has a decision price of \$100.00. Trading begins at \$100.02 and fills completely at an average price of \$100.10. Nothing is left unfilled. What is the delay cost, the market impact cost, and the total Implementation Shortfall?
Delay cost \(= (100.02-100.00)\times 20{,}000 = 0.02\times20{,}000 = \$400\). Market impact cost \(= (100.10-100.02)\times 20{,}000 = 0.08\times20{,}000 = \$1{,}600\). There is no unfilled quantity, so opportunity cost is \$0. Total Implementation Shortfall \(= \$400+\$1{,}600+\$0 = \$2{,}000\), which also equals the direct check \((100.10-100.00)\times20{,}000=\$2{,}000\) — consistent, since with zero unfilled shares the whole shortfall is just the filled-shares term.
Q5. Why does high "impact concern" push a desk toward VWAP or a similarly patient schedule, while high "urgency" pushes toward a fast, aggressive execution?
Impact concern is the fear that your own trading will move the price against you — by \(\Delta p=\lambda Q\), spreading the same size out over more time and matching it to natural liquidity (VWAP's approach) reduces how much of a footprint you leave per unit of time, lowering total impact cost. Urgency is the opposite fear — that the price will drift away from you, for reasons unrelated to your own trading, the longer the order stays open. A fast, aggressive execution minimizes that exposure window at the cost of accepting more impact. These pull in opposite directions on the exact same underlying risk-aversion axis the Almgren-Chriss framework formalizes: patience trades lower expected impact cost for more price-drift risk, and speed trades the reverse.
Q6. Why is it considered dishonest, in the Implementation Shortfall framework, to redefine the "decision price" after seeing how an execution turned out?
The entire framework's value comes from anchoring the benchmark to a fixed, objective moment — the instant the trade decision was actually made — that is chosen independently of, and before, anything about how the subsequent execution goes. If the decision price could be shifted after the fact to whatever moment happens to make the resulting shortfall look smaller, the "cost" reported would no longer reflect the real gap between the decision and its execution; it would just be cherry-picked to flatter the result. This is the same discipline as fixing a backtest's in-sample/out-of-sample split before looking at results — the benchmark has to be pinned down before you know the answer, or it stops measuring anything honest.
