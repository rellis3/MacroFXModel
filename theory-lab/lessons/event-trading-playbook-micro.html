How Markets Talk to Each Other · 6 of 6

Payrolls, CPI and Central Banks, or The Move Is Priced Before the Number

Scheduled releases are the one kind of big news whose timing everyone knows. Before the number, the options market prices how far price should move. After it, price moves on the surprise, not the number. This deck is the arithmetic and the discipline in between.

The whole idea
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

Payrolls are on Friday. I think the number will be strong. Should I just get long the dollar now?

First ask what the market already expects. Not the number. The size of the move. The options market tells you that before the release.

How?

Take two option expiries on euro dollar. One expires Thursday, before the release. One expires Friday, after it. Say Thursday's is priced at seven percent volatility and Friday's at nine.

Nine doesn't sound much bigger than seven.

That's the trick. Volatility doesn't add up across days, but variance does. Square each one and multiply by the days. Five times nine squared, minus four times seven squared. What's left is the Friday on its own, and it comes out at about fourteen and a half percent.

And in pips?

Divide by about sixteen to turn a yearly number into a daily one. With euro dollar at one oh eight, that's about ninety-eight pips for one standard deviation. An ordinary day is about forty-eight. So the market expects payrolls day to move about twice as much.

So a straddle over the release is a bet that the move beats that.

Right. Its breakeven is about eight tenths of that standard deviation, roughly seventy-eight pips. And once the number is out, that extra volatility vanishes. If the move is small, the straddle buyer loses even though something happened.

Now direction. A strong number means a stronger dollar, surely.

Only if it's stronger than expected. Price reacts to the surprise: the actual minus the consensus. A strong number that everyone expected is already in the price.

Has that ever been really obvious?

The tenth of November, twenty twenty-two. US inflation came in at seven point seven percent. That's still nearly four times the Fed's target. But the forecast was about seven point nine. The S and P five hundred rose five and a half percent, its best day in two and a half years, and the dollar fell hard.

Good news that was really just less bad news.

Exactly. And the first few seconds are the worst time to trade. Spreads widen, quotes vanish, and the first move often reverses when people read the revisions or hear the press conference. That's the second move.

So what about my stop?

A stop is an order, not a guarantee. Price can jump straight through it. Size the position so a move of two or three event standard deviations still costs you an amount you can live with.

One-line version?

Know the priced move before the number, trade the surprise not the level, and size for the gap, not the stop.

Section 1

What makes a scheduled event different

You can't know the number. You can know almost everything else.

🕣

The time

US payrolls and CPI at 8:30 a.m. New York; the FOMC statement at 2:00 p.m., the press conference at 2:30.

📊

The consensus

The median economist forecast. It is already in the price, so only a miss can move it.

💰

The priced move

Options that span the release cost more than options that don't. The difference is a quote for the size of the move.

🫧

The liquidity

Market makers widen or pull quotes just before. Spreads are at their widest when the number hits.

Before the release: what size is priced? At the release: was it a surprise? After: did the details change the story?
Section 2

A straddle is a price for the size of the move

Buy a call and a put at today's price. You win if price moves far enough either way, so the cost is the market's bet on size, not direction.

Straddle ≈ 0.8 × σ × √T × S
σ · implied vol, yearly
√T · square root of time, in years
S · spot price
0.8 ≈ √(2/π) · average size of a normal move

An at-the-money call is worth about 0.4σ√T·S (Brenner and Subrahmanyam, 1988), so a straddle is about 0.8. That 0.8 is the average absolute size of a normal move in standard deviations. So the straddle's cost is the expected move, and its breakeven is exactly that far from the strike.

Where this needs qualifying

It's an average, not a cap: about four moves in ten end up bigger than the straddle's breakeven. And once the number is out, the extra volatility it was charging for is gone.

Section 3

Variance adds up, so the event can be backed out

One expiry ends before payrolls (Thursday, 4 days, 7%). One spans it (Friday, 5 days, 9%). Volatility doesn't add across days; variance does.

Follow it through
Thu: 0.07² × 4 = 0.0196
Fri: 0.09² × 5 = 0.0405
Each expiry's total variance is its vol squared times its days. The Friday total contains the event; the Thursday total doesn't.
σₑ² = σ₂²T₂ − σ₁²T₁ − σ_b²(T₂−T₁−1)
0.0405 − 0.0196 = 0.0209
Take away the days both expiries share. Here they are one day apart, so the last term is zero and what's left is the event day alone: 52% of the week's variance in one day.
σₑ = √0.0209 = 14.5%
→
÷ √252 → 0.91% → ±98 pips at 1.0800
An ordinary day at 7% is ±48 pips. Payrolls are priced at about twice the move, or 4.3 ordinary days of variance squeezed into one. The straddle breakeven is about 0.8 × 98 ≈ 78 pips.
Where the textbook version breaks

Event vol is a small difference of two big numbers. Quote the 5-day at 8% instead of 9% and the event vol drops from 14.5% to 11.1%. Use fresh mid quotes on the same day-count clock, and read the answer as a range.

Section 6

The first move and the second

The pattern Fleming and Remolona (1999) documented in Treasuries, and traders see everywhere.

Before: quotes pulled, spreads wide
→
First move: a fast jump on the headline, thin volume
→
Second move: volume surges as the details are read
→
After: implied vol crushed
🔁

Revisions

Payrolls revise the prior two months every time. +50k on the headline with −80k of revisions is net weaker news.

🧩

The rest of the report

Unemployment rate, hourly earnings, core versus headline CPI. When they disagree, the market follows what the central bank watches.

🎙️

The press conference

The statement drives the first move; the Chair's answers 30 minutes later drive the second.

Section 7

Central banks: a surprise against the priced path

For a rate decision, "consensus" is what futures and swaps already price. A fully priced hike moves little; a hold when a cut was priced is a hawkish surprise.

The action
  • The rate decision itself, against what futures priced
  • Kuttner (2001): yields barely move on the expected part of a Fed change, strongly on the unexpected part
The words
  • The signal about the future path, in the statement and the press conference
  • Gürkaynak, Sack and Swanson (2005): this "path" surprise moved longer yields more than the decision did
It actually happened

3 December 2015: the ECB cut its deposit rate 10bp to −0.30% and extended bond buying to March 2017 at an unchanged €60bn a month. Markets had priced more. The euro rose about 3% against the dollar, its biggest one-day rally since 2009 on Bloomberg's figures. Easing, and the currency went up.

Section 8

Size for the gap, not the stop

$500 of risk on a $100,000 account, EUR/USD at $10 a pip per 100,000. The event is priced at ±98 pips for one standard deviation.

Sized off a 30-pip stop
  • $500 ÷ (30 × $10) ≈ 1.7 lots
  • The stop sits well inside the priced move
  • A 150-pip jump through it costs about $2,500
Sized for 3 event sd
  • 3 × 98 ≈ 295 pips
  • $500 ÷ (295 × $10) ≈ 0.17 lots
  • A gap is survivable; you can still trade the second move
Where the textbook version breaks

Real event moves are fatter-tailed than normal ones, and a stop fills at the next available price. On 15 January 2015 the SNB scrapped its 1.20 EUR/CHF floor without warning; the euro fell far below 1.00 francs within minutes, and stops under 1.20 filled hundreds of pips away. FXCM's clients ended up owing it about $225 million.

Section 9

Blackouts and the pre-event checklist

Decide before 8:30, not at it.

🏛️

The central bank's blackout

Fed officials stay quiet from the second Saturday before an FOMC meeting until midnight on the day after it. The ECB keeps a quiet week. No last-minute guidance is coming.

⛔

Your own blackout

For example: no new positions from 15 minutes before a tier-one release until spreads are normal again. The window matters less than fixing it in advance.

📋

Before every release

What's out and when · consensus and usual surprise · the priced move in pips · which figure the bank watches · cost of a 3 sd move on what you hold · a plan for hot, in line, soft · when your blackout ends.

Section 10 · It actually happened

US CPI, 10 November 2022: 7.7%, and a rally

It actually happened

October CPI rose 0.4% in the month and 7.7% over the year, against about 0.6% and 7.9% expected; core rose 6.3% against about 6.5%. Inflation was still nearly four times the Fed's target. The S&P 500 closed up 5.5%, its best day since April 2020; the Nasdaq rose 7.4%; the 2-year yield fell from 4.62% to 4.32%; the dollar fell broadly. The next day the rally extended, and in December the Fed slowed to a 50bp hike.

Section 11

Quick check

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

A 4-day expiry is at 7% and a 5-day expiry spanning payrolls is at 9%. What is the event-day vol?

Variance adds, vol doesn't: √(0.09² × 5 − 0.07² × 4) = √0.0209 = 14.5%. The 9% averages the event with four quiet days.
Question 2

CPI prints at 7.7%, a very high level, against 7.9% expected. Which way should rate-sensitive assets move?

The level was priced; the surprise wasn't. On 10 November 2022 the 2-year yield fell 30bp and the S&P 500 rose 5.5% on exactly this miss.
Question 3

You buy the event straddle (breakeven about 78 pips). The pair moves 60 pips on the release. What happens?

A straddle is a bet that the move beats what was priced. Once the number is out, the event's variance is spent and short-dated vol collapses.
Question 4

Payrolls beat by 50k, but the prior two months are revised down by 80k. What's the net news?

Revisions are part of the same release and are surprise too. The first move trades the headline; the second trades the sum.
Question 5

Why is a tight stop poor protection through a tier-one release?

A stop is an order, not a guarantee. Size so a move of two or three event standard deviations is affordable instead.
Your call
Scenario drill · 10 November 2022

Long dollars into a CPI release, with an ordinary-day stop

It is 8:20 a.m. in New York. You are long US dollars against the euro and the yen, betting that October's CPI will be hot and push the Fed to keep hiking by 75 basis points. Your position is sized for an ordinary day, with stops about one ordinary day's move away. The consensus is about 0.6% for the month and 7.9% for the year. CPI has been the most market-moving release of the year. What do you do in the next ten minutes?

  1. Hold. The stops limit the damage if you're wrong.
    Your stops sit inside the move the options market is pricing for this release. A stop is an order to trade at the next available price, and in the first seconds after a big surprise there may be little liquidity between your level and where price lands. You have sized for a stop that may not fill where you set it.
  2. Add to the position. Inflation has been running hot all year, so the number will be high.
    That is a view on the level, and the level is already in the consensus and the price. Only a hotter-than-7.9% print helps you. Adding doubles your exposure to a coin flip on the surprise, at the widest spreads of the day.
  3. Cut the position to a size where a move of two or three event standard deviations is affordable, or go flat, and decide again after the number is out.
    This sizes for the gap, not the stop. You keep the option to trade the second move once you know the surprise and spreads have normalised. If CPI is hot, you can re-enter with information; if it is soft, a small position limits the damage.
  4. Keep the position but buy options that pay if the dollar falls.
    Defensible: the protection caps the loss at its premium. But you are buying it at event vol, the most expensive moment, and you are still carrying a full-size bet on the surprise. Cutting size does the same job more cheaply.

What happened. CPI rose 0.4% in October and 7.7% over the year; core rose 0.3% and 6.3%. All were below consensus. The 2-year Treasury yield fell from 4.62% to 4.32%, the dollar fell broadly, with the yen's biggest one-day gain against it since 2008, and the S&P 500 rose 5.5%. A long-dollar position sized for an ordinary day took a move far larger than an ordinary day in the first minutes. The next month, the Fed slowed to a 50 basis-point hike.

Section 12

What to carry forward

01

The size of the move is priced before the release: an at-the-money straddle costs about 0.8σ√T·S, the expected absolute move.

02

Variance adds across days, so σₑ² = σ₂²T₂ − σ₁²T₁ − σ_b²(T₂−T₁−1). 7% over 4 days and 9% over 5 price payrolls at 14.5%: ±98 pips at 1.0800.

03

Price reacts to the surprise, actual minus consensus, not the level. For central banks, the consensus is the path futures already price.

04

The first move is a fast jump in thin liquidity; the second comes from revisions, the rest of the report and the press conference.

05

Size for the gap, not the stop. Fix a blackout and run the checklist before every release.

06

It actually happened, 10 November 2022: CPI at 7.7% against about 7.9% expected sent the S&P 500 up 5.5% and the 2-year yield down 30bp.

What this now lets you do

Read two option quotes and say how many pips a release is priced for; size a position so the gap is survivable; and judge a print by its surprise, then wait for the details before trusting the first move.
