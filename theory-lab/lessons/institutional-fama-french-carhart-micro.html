Fama-French & Carhart, Beyond the Single Factor

CAPM says one number — market beta — should explain a stock's expected return. It doesn't, not fully: small stocks and cheap "value" stocks both beat what their beta predicts, decade after decade. Fama and French's fix wasn't a better beta. It was two more zero-cost, long-short portfolios, measured directly and handed to the regression as their own factors — and this deck ends on the most distinctive part: currency markets have the exact same story, and this repo already tried building one.

Section 1

Two anomalies CAPM's beta can't touch

📉

The size effect

Sort stocks by market cap. The smallest ones earn more, on average, than their CAPM beta says they should — a persistent, replicated pattern a single factor has no room to describe.

💰

The value effect

Sort stocks by book-to-market instead. Cheap ("value") stocks beat their beta-predicted return; richly priced ("growth") stocks fall short of theirs. Same story, different sort.

Both patterns survive controlling for beta. Both are too large and too persistent to be noise. CAPM's single factor simply isn't enough — and the fix Fama & French (1993) reached for wasn't a smarter beta, it was two more measured, tradeable portfolios sitting right next to it.
Section 2

Two more zero-cost portfolios, added as regressors

The three-factor model
R − R_f
=
α + βMKT(Rm−Rf)
+
βSMB·SMB
+
βHML·HML
The market term (amber) is CAPM, unchanged. SMB (green) and HML (purple) are new — and each one is a real, tradeable strategy first, a "factor" second.

SMB (Small Minus Big): the return of a diversified small-cap portfolio minus a diversified large-cap portfolio, every period. HML (High Minus Low): the return of a high book-to-market ("value") portfolio minus a low book-to-market ("growth") portfolio. A stock's βSMB or βHML just measures how much its own return moves with each of those spreads — regardless of whether the stock is technically small or cheap itself.

The two sorts intersect into 6 value-weighted portfolios, rebalanced annually — using book-value data lagged from the prior fiscal year-end, so no portfolio ever uses accounting data the market couldn't have known yet.
SMB = avg(3 small row) − avg(3 big row)
HML = avg(2 high col) − avg(2 low col)
Averaging across the OTHER dimension on each side is what purges the tilt — SMB is a clean size spread with no value tilt riding along, and HML a clean value spread with no size tilt.
Same discipline as elsewhere in this curriculum

The annual rebalance's built-in accounting lag is exactly the point-in-time-computability rule this curriculum's own Feature Engineering, Leakage & Reproducibility lesson insists on — a factor built from book value the market couldn't have known yet isn't measuring a tradeable strategy at all.

Section 5

Carhart's fourth factor — momentum earns its own line

Value — HML's logic

Buy things that got cheap, on the bet they revert. A high book-to-market ratio is the signature.

Momentum — UMD's logic

Buy things that are already going up, on the bet the trend continues a while longer. Rank by trailing 12-month return, skipping the most recent month to dodge short-term reversal.

These aren't the same idea at two speeds — they're genuinely different bets, and at times they've been negatively correlated: a stock can screen as cheap (high HML exposure) while still falling (negative UMD exposure). Carhart (1997) gave momentum its own factor line specifically because it survives even after size and value are already controlled for.

Why the negative correlation is a feature

Two factors that don't move in lockstep combine into a smoother combined exposure than either alone — the same diversification logic behind holding two low-covariance assets in one portfolio, applied to factors instead of stocks.

Section 6

The 2015 extension, briefly

RMW

Robust Minus Weak

Long high-operating-profitability firms, short low-profitability ones. More expected profit, holding price fixed, should mean higher expected return.

CMA

Conservative Minus Aggressive

Long low-investment firms, short high-investment ones. Aggressive investment today competes with cash that could otherwise be paid out or compounded.

Both come from a dividend-discount-model argument, not a pure anomaly hunt. The five-factor model is the modern academic standard — but the 3- and 4-factor versions are still quoted constantly in practice, chosen by context rather than one strictly replacing the others.
Section 7

This curriculum already taught you a factor model

The FX carry trade, from this curriculum's own Interest Rate Parity lesson, is the identical structure.

Equity factor
  • MKT — return of the whole market
  • HML — long cheap, short expensive stocks
  • UMD — long winners, short losers
Direct FX analog
  • "Dollar factor" — average return across a currency basket vs. USD
  • FX carry factor — long high-rate currencies, short low-rate funding currencies
  • FX momentum — Menkhoff, Sarno, Schmeling & Schrimpf (2012), built the same way UMD is

The FX carry trade exists precisely because UIP's prediction — that a high-yielder should depreciate to offset its extra interest — fails, on average, across decades. That's HML's risk-premium logic, transplanted: a persistent long-short spread, compensated for real, debated risk (crash risk, or consumption risk — Lustig & Verdelhan, 2007), not a free lunch.

Section 8

This repo actually tried it — and got an honest null

Verified against this repo's actual code

forge/xsect.py builds a genuine cross-sectional long-short basket across 26 FX/gold instruments, with both a "momentum" (follow the leaders) and a "reversion" (fade the extremes) sign as pre-registered, walk-forward-tested alternatives — the same construction move as UMD, applied to a distance-from-weekly-open score. Result, 10 years, 6 folds: OOS t = 0.44, inside a shuffled-score null's own range (−2.05 to +1.68). A clean null, not a validated edge. Separately, forge/yield_basket.py builds a genuine rate-differential-based FX cross-sectional basket on the live YieldSpreadBot engine — written, but currently unrun, blocked on FRED API access.

No literal SMB, HML, or UMD exists anywhere in this codebase — this repo trades FX and gold, not equities, so that's expected. What's genuinely present is the same methodological move, tested honestly, with a result that didn't clear its own noise floor.

Section 11

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

What two anomalies does CAPM's single beta leave unexplained?

Small stocks and high book-to-market ("value") stocks both earn more than their CAPM beta predicts — the exact patterns SMB and HML were built to measure directly.
Question 2

How is SMB actually computed from the six Fama-French portfolios?

Averaging across all three book-to-market buckets on each side purges any value/growth tilt, isolating a clean size spread. (That second option describes HML, not SMB.)
Question 3

Why does Carhart's UMD get its own factor instead of folding into HML?

Value and momentum have shown periods of negative correlation — a stock can be cheap (high HML) while still falling (negative UMD) — which is exactly why UMD adds genuinely new information rather than restating HML.
Question 4

What is the direct FX-market analog of Carhart's UMD factor?

FX momentum is a real, published, direct analog — not just a loose comparison — built by the identical rank-and-spread recipe as UMD.
Question 5

What did this repo's own forge/xsect.py cross-sectional FX basket actually find, walk-forward tested?

The construction is genuine and the test is honest, but the result doesn't clear its own null — a documented academic factor existing elsewhere is not the same claim as this repo's own signal clearing an OOS bar.
Section 12

What to carry forward

01

CAPM's single beta leaves the size and value effects unexplained; Fama & French (1993) add SMB and HML — real, tradeable long-short portfolios — as two more regressors.

02

SMB and HML have a precise recipe: independent 2×3 sorts (size median split, B/M 30/40/30) into six value-weighted portfolios, rebalanced annually with a deliberate accounting lag.

03

Carhart (1997) adds UMD/momentum — a genuinely distinct, sometimes negatively-correlated-with-value continuation effect, not a redundant restatement.

04

Fama-French (2015) adds RMW and CMA via a dividend-discount-model argument; it's the modern standard, but 3- and 4-factor versions remain in routine use.

05

The FX carry trade is the identical methodological move in a currency-market costume, with direct published analogs for MKT, HML, and UMD alike.

06

This repo's own honest attempt at the FX analog (forge/xsect.py) came back a clean null — a documented factor elsewhere is not validated edge here.

What this now lets you do

Read a manager's or a strategy's factor loadings as a style profile, not a skill certificate. Recognize the FX carry and momentum trades for what they structurally are — priced factors compensated for real risk, not free lunches. And keep "documented in the literature" and "validated on this repo's own data" as two entirely separate claims, exactly as forge/xsect.py's own null result illustrates.
