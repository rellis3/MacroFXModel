ARIMA & Vector Autoregression (VAR)

AR(1) told you one series pulls back toward one mean. Real data is rarely that clean — shocks echo forward after the level has already reverted, raw price levels need differencing before "mean reversion" even applies, and in FX and macro almost nothing moves in isolation. ARIMA and VAR are the two directions single-series mean reversion generalizes in.

CONCEPT — not yet built in this repo ~17 min read Prereqs: Stationarity, ACF/PACF & the AR(1) signature; Ornstein-Uhlenbeck & Mean Reversion
TL;DR
  • ARIMA generalizes single-series AR(1)/OU mean reversion two ways: more lags of the level (AR(p)), a second kind of memory for past shocks rather than past levels (MA(q)), and differencing (the "I") to make a non-stationary series like a raw price level stationary before fitting.
  • The order (p,d,q) is read off the data via the Box-Jenkins loop — ACF/PACF cutoff patterns, a unit-root check for d, Ljung-Box residual diagnostics — not guessed or assumed to be (1,1,0).
  • VAR is the orthogonal generalization: instead of one series on its own lags, it regresses a whole system of series (e.g. the DE–US yield spread and EUR/USD) each on lagged values of itself and every other series, so it can reveal bidirectional feedback a single "Y on lagged X" regression can never see.
  • Granger causality tests whether one series's lags significantly improve forecasting another beyond its own history — it is explicitly a statistical/predictive claim, not true structural causation, since an unobserved common driver can produce the same result.
  • The lesson's running example — does the yield spread lead EUR/USD, or the reverse — is this repo's own prior lead-lag case study formalized as a testable VAR/Granger question; no such fit has actually been run in this repo yet.
Section 01

Why this matters

The OU/AR(1) lesson gave you one number, \(\phi\), and one behavior: a series either gets pulled back toward a mean \(\mu\) at speed \(\theta = -\ln(\phi)/\Delta t\), or (if \(\phi \geq 1\)) it doesn't. That's already a genuinely useful model — but it has two hard limits baked in. First, it only remembers one lag: today depends on yesterday and nothing further back. Second, it only describes one series in isolation — there is no second variable in the equation for anything to lead or lag.

Both limits show up directly in work already sitting in this repo. This codebase's own macro research notes (Lessons 2–3) build a detailed, dated, qualitative case that the DE–US yield spread leads EUR/USD by roughly a trading session — but the argument stops at "the spread moved first, then FX followed," a narrative reading of a chart, not a fitted model. This project's own lead-lag tooling in this repo does the same kind of thing for a credit-spread/vol pair. Neither one has a model with two variables in it that could answer, formally, "how much does the spread's own past explain FX beyond what FX's own history already explains, and does the reverse hold too?" AR(1)/OU literally cannot represent that question — it has no second series.

ARIMA is the generalization that stays inside the single-series world but relaxes the "only one lag, only remembers levels" restriction: more lags (AR(p)), a second kind of memory for past shocks rather than past levels (MA(q)), and a systematic recipe, differencing, for handling series that aren't stationary to begin with (the "I"). VAR is the orthogonal generalization: instead of widening how many of one series's own lags you use, it widens how many series you model jointly, so each variable's forecast can depend on lagged values of every variable in the system — itself included. This lesson builds ARIMA first, since it slots directly on top of the AR(1)/OU lesson, then VAR — the tool this repo's yield-spread/FX question actually needs.

💡
Plain-English intuition

More memory, and more posts to be pulled toward

Recall the OU rubber-band ball: pulled back toward a fixed post, jostled by random noise. ARIMA adds two extra pieces of physics to that same ball. First, give it a fading echo: a push applied a few instants ago can still be nudging the ball right now, even though the push itself is long gone and the ball has already drifted back near the post — that's the MA term, a memory of past forces, distinct from the AR term's memory of past positions. Second, sometimes the post itself isn't standing still — it's slowly sliding across the floor. No amount of rubber-band physics makes sense against a moving post; you first have to track the post's own drift and subtract it out (difference the series) before "pull back toward a fixed point" is even the right description. That's the "I" — make the series stationary first, then do the OU/AR(1)-style physics on what's left.

VAR asks a different kind of question. Forget one ball on one post — now picture two balls, each tied to its own post, but also connected to each other by a second elastic band. Tug ball X, and a moment later ball Y feels a pull too, through that connecting band. Tug Y instead, and X may or may not feel anything back — the connection can run one way, both ways, or (if the band is slack) not really at all. You cannot tell which of those is true by watching either ball on its own; you have to model both balls, and both bands, together. That is exactly what a VAR does, and exactly what a single-equation regression of "Y on lagged X" alone cannot do — it can only ever look for the one arrow it was told to look for.

A single-equation regression can only see one arrow — VAR estimates the whole system
X's past → Y (a21) Y's past → X (a12) own-lag / noise
Four coefficients, estimated jointly from one system, not four separate regressions run in isolation. A single-equation regression of "FX on lagged spread" can only ever report on the blue arrow, because that is the only arrow it was set up to look for — it has no way to also check whether the red arrow exists. A VAR estimates both directions at once from the same data, which is exactly what's needed to answer "does the spread lead FX, does FX lead the spread, or both" rather than assuming the answer before you look.
Section 02

The math, step by step

From AR(1) to a general AR(p)

Recall the discrete-time AR(1) form from the OU lesson: \(X_t = c + \phi X_{t-1} + \epsilon_t\) — today's value depends on yesterday's value and nothing further back. The natural generalization is to let today depend on several past values, each with its own coefficient:

\[ X_t = c + \phi_1 X_{t-1} + \phi_2 X_{t-2} + \cdots + \phi_p X_{t-p} + \epsilon_t \]
Xₜtoday's value
φᵢweight on the level i periods back
phow many past lags enter the equation
εₜthis period's white-noise shock

This is AR(p), an autoregressive model of order p. Each \(\phi_i\) measures how much of the value from \(i\) periods ago still explains today's value, after accounting for everything the intervening lags already explain — it is not simply "does yesterday correlate with \(i\) days ago," it is "does \(i\) days ago add anything once you already know all the lags in between." That distinction is exactly why you don't eyeball raw autocorrelations to choose \(p\); you use the partial autocorrelation function (PACF) instead. The Stationarity/ACF-PACF lesson's AR(1) signature — a single significant spike in the PACF at lag 1, then a sharp drop to ~zero, alongside an ACF that decays smoothly — generalizes directly: for a general AR(p), the PACF shows significant spikes at lags \(1,\ldots,p\) and then cuts off sharply, while the ACF keeps decaying gradually (geometrically, or as a mix of decaying terms) well past lag \(p\). That PACF-cutoff / ACF-decay contrast is the data-driven way to read off \(p\), rather than assuming \(p=1\) because that's the case you happen to already know.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

AR(1) only remembers what happened yesterday. AR(p) has a longer memory — it can look back several days and ask how much each of those days still matters today, after already accounting for the days in between. The trick for figuring out how far back to look is a chart called the PACF: it keeps showing "yes, this lag still matters" until it suddenly stops, and wherever it stops is roughly how far back the model needs to remember.

The other kind of memory: MA(q)

AR(p) only ever uses past levels of \(X\). But some series carry a second, different kind of memory: a shock hits, and even once whatever caused it has fully dissipated from the level of \(X\), the shock's effect keeps echoing through the error structure for a few more periods. Moving-average models formalize exactly that:

\[ X_t = \epsilon_t + \theta_1 \epsilon_{t-1} + \cdots + \theta_q \epsilon_{t-q} \]
Xₜtoday's value
θⱼweight on the shock j periods back
qhow many past shocks enter the equation

Read the distinction carefully, because it's the crux of the whole ARMA idea: an AR term says "today depends on where \(X\) was \(i\) days ago" — a memory of past levels, the rubber-band-to-a-post mechanism from the OU lesson. An MA term says "today depends on how large the surprise was \(j\) days ago" — a memory of past shocks, which can persist even after the level itself has fully reverted. Concretely: a news shock that fully passes through price by the next bar, but whose noisy aftereffects (a temporary bid/ask imbalance, a liquidity gap slowly refilling) keep nudging returns for one or two more bars, is MA-like behavior. A price that sits persistently away from fair value and slowly closes that gap over many days is AR-like behavior. Real series can carry both mechanisms at once, which is exactly the motivation for combining them rather than picking only one.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Think of an AR term as "the level itself is still off" — like a room that's slow to cool down after the heater's been on. An MA term is more like an echo — the heater already turned off and the room is back to normal temperature, but a loud noise it made while running is still bouncing around the walls for a moment. Both are ways the past keeps affecting right now, just through a different channel — one through the level, one through the leftover ripple of a shock.

The identification signature flips for MA(q): its ACF cuts off sharply after lag \(q\) (spikes at \(1,\ldots,q\), then ~zero), while its PACF decays gradually. Put the four canonical shapes side by side, and that four-way pattern-match, applied to real ACF/PACF plots, is the "identification" step of the Box-Jenkins methodology described next.

Combining: ARMA(p,q)

\[ X_t = c + \sum_{i=1}^{p} \phi_i X_{t-i} \; + \; \epsilon_t + \sum_{j=1}^{q} \theta_j \epsilon_{t-j} \]
Σφᵢ Xₜ₋ᵢthe AR piece — memory of past levels
Σθⱼ εₜ₋ⱼthe MA piece — memory of past shocks

ARMA blends both memories in one equation. The practical reason to allow mixed models at all, rather than always fitting a pure AR or a pure MA, is parsimony: a series that would need a high-order pure AR(10) or MA(10) to fit well is often described just as accurately by a low-order ARMA(1,1) — fewer parameters estimated from the same amount of data, and fewer free parameters is directly less overfitting risk, the exact concern this repo's minimal-DOF-first discipline is built around. Box and Jenkins allowing \(q>0\) at all, rather than restricting attention to ever-larger pure-AR models, is precisely this parsimony trade.

Handling non-stationary series: the "I" in ARIMA

Everything above — AR, MA, ARMA — assumes \(X_t\) is already stationary: constant mean, constant variance, autocorrelation that depends only on lag, not on calendar time (per the Stationarity lesson). Most raw financial series fail that outright: a raw price level trends over time with no fixed mean to revert to; in AR(1)/OU terms that's the \(\phi \geq 1\) boundary case the OU lesson's Q4 flags as "the model doesn't apply here." Box and Jenkins's fix is not to force a mean-reverting model onto a series that structurally has no fixed mean — it's to difference the series until it does:

\[ \Delta X_t = X_t - X_{t-1} \qquad (\text{first difference}, \ d=1); \quad \text{repeat} \to \Delta^d X_t \ \text{if needed} \]
ΔXₜone differencing pass — this period minus last
dhow many differencing passes until it's stationary
The trap: identifying \(p\) and \(q\) off the ACF/PACF of a raw, non-stationary price level — before checking stationarity or differencing — produces a meaningless, slowly-decaying ACF at every lag (the "everything looks significant forever" signature) rather than a real cutoff pattern. Always difference (or check via a unit-root test) first.

"Differencing \(d\) times until stationary" is meant literally and iteratively: check the raw series for stationarity (informally via ACF decay behavior, formally via a unit-root test like the Augmented Dickey-Fuller test — per the Stationarity lesson); if it fails, take one difference and check again; repeat until it passes. Most daily financial series need only \(d=1\) — returns/changes are typically far closer to stationary than raw levels. \(d=2\) is uncommon and is usually a sign something is off in the transformation choice rather than a genuine property of the data.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine trying to describe how a hot air balloon "bounces" while it's steadily climbing thousands of feet — the climb drowns out the bounce and the description just becomes "it went up." Look instead at the balloon's altitude change from minute to minute, and the actual bouncing pattern becomes visible. Differencing does exactly that to a trending price series: subtracting each value from the one before strips out the steady climb (or fall) so whatever real up-and-down pattern is left underneath can actually be seen and modeled.

Once the differenced series passes the stationarity check, you fit an ordinary ARMA(p,q) to that differenced series — this is precisely what ARIMA(\(p,d,q\)) means: Integrated (differenced \(d\) times), AutoRegressive (order \(p\)), Moving Average (order \(q\)):

\[ \Delta^d X_t = c + \sum_{i=1}^{p} \phi_i\,\Delta^d X_{t-i} \; + \; \epsilon_t + \sum_{j=1}^{q} \theta_j \epsilon_{t-j} \]
p
AR order
How many past levels enter — read off the PACF cutoff
d
Differencing order
How many times to difference until stationary — from a unit-root check
q
MA order
How many past shocks enter — read off the ACF cutoff

Crucially, the "identify \(p\) and \(q\) from the ACF/PACF shape" step described above is always applied to the differenced series, never the raw one. Running that identification on a non-stationary raw series produces a meaningless, slowly-decaying ACF at every single lag — the classic "everything looks significant forever" signature of non-stationarity — rather than one of the clean cutoff/decay patterns described above. Skipping this step and reading ACF/PACF shapes off a trending raw price series is one of the most common ways an ARIMA fit goes wrong before it even starts.

\(p\)AR order — how many past levels (lags of \(X\)) enter the equation. Identified from where the PACF of the (differenced) series cuts off.
\(d\)Differencing (integration) order — how many times the raw series must be differenced before it's stationary. Identified via a stationarity check (ACF decay / unit-root test), not guessed.
\(q\)MA order — how many past shocks (lags of \(\epsilon\)) enter the equation. Identified from where the ACF of the (differenced) series cuts off.
\(\phi_i\)AR coefficients — weight on the level \(i\) periods back.
\(\theta_j\)MA coefficients — weight on the shock \(j\) periods back.
\(\epsilon_t\)The white-noise innovation at time \(t\) — mean zero, uncorrelated with its own past, the same conceptual "unpredictable shock" as \(dW_t\) in the OU lesson, in discrete time.

Put together, this is the Box-Jenkins methodology, a three-step iterative recipe rather than a single formula: (1) identification — difference until stationary, then pattern-match the ACF/PACF shapes to propose candidate \(p,q\); (2) estimation — fit the candidate model's coefficients (by maximum likelihood or conditional sum of squares); (3) diagnostic checking — the fitted model's residuals should look like white noise, no leftover autocorrelation; a Ljung-Box test on the residuals checks this formally. If diagnostics still show structure, you go back to step 1 and revise \(p\) or \(q\) — the loop repeats until the residuals are clean. ARIMA is the model; Box-Jenkins is the recipe for choosing \(p,d,q\) from the data rather than guessing.

🧮
Worked example

Walking the Box-Jenkins identification loop

Suppose you take a candidate FX-related series and, following the recipe above, first check it for stationarity and find it needs one difference (\(d=1\)) — you work from daily changes, not raw levels, from here on. You plot the ACF and PACF of that differenced series. Suppose the PACF shows a single significant spike at lag 1 and then drops to ~zero, while the ACF decays smoothly across many lags — that is exactly the AR(1) signature already familiar from the OU lesson, so the provisional model is ARIMA(1,1,0): one AR lag, one difference, no MA term.

Now suppose, on a different candidate series, the PACF instead shows significant spikes at both lag 1 and lag 2 before cutting off to ~zero at lag 3, with the ACF again decaying smoothly. That's the AR(2) signature — the provisional model becomes ARIMA(2,1,0). You estimate it, then run diagnostic checking: a Ljung-Box test on the residuals still flags statistically significant leftover autocorrelation at lag 3. That's the model telling you it isn't done — you go back to step 1, and either extend to AR(3) or consider a mixed ARMA term to explain the lag-3 residual structure more parsimoniously, then re-estimate and re-check. This loop — identify, estimate, check, revise — not a single one-shot fit, is what "doing Box-Jenkins properly" means in practice.

Vector Autoregression (VAR): the multivariate extension

Everything above still describes one series' own history. But the question this repo's macro notes actually pose — does the yield spread lead EUR/USD, does EUR/USD lead the spread, or both — is inherently about two series and how each one's past predicts the other. VAR is the natural generalization: instead of regressing one series on its own lags, you regress a whole system of \(k\) series, each one on lagged values of itself and every other series in the system. Written out explicitly for the two-variable VAR(1) case (one lag, two series \(X\) and \(Y\) — say, the yield spread and EUR/USD):

\[ X_t = a_{11}X_{t-1} + a_{12}Y_{t-1} + \epsilon_{1,t} \] \[ Y_t = a_{21}X_{t-1} + a_{22}Y_{t-1} + \epsilon_{2,t} \]
a₁₁, a₂₂each series' own AR(1) coefficient
a₁₂Y's past → X (cross-effect)
a₂₁X's past → Y (cross-effect)

Read each coefficient by what it connects: \(a_{11}\) is \(X\)'s own AR(1) coefficient (does \(X\)'s own past predict \(X\)), \(a_{22}\) is the same for \(Y\). \(a_{12}\) is the cross-effect of \(Y\)'s past on \(X\) — "does yesterday's FX help predict today's spread." \(a_{21}\) is the cross-effect of \(X\)'s past on \(Y\) — "does yesterday's spread help predict today's FX." All four coefficients (plus the two intercepts, omitted above for clarity) are estimated jointly, from the same two-equation system, which is exactly what lets a VAR reveal bidirectional feedback — \(a_{12}\) and \(a_{21}\) both nonzero — that a single regression equation, by construction, can never see, because it was only ever set up to look for one direction. The general VAR(\(p\)) with \(k\) series extends this the same way AR(1) extended to AR(p): each series regressed on \(p\) lags of every series in the system, with a coefficient matrix per lag rather than a single scalar \(\phi\).

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

A regular regression can only ever check "does yesterday's spread predict today's FX" because that's the one question it was set up to ask — it has no way to also check the reverse. A VAR fits both questions at once, from the same data, in one system: does yesterday's spread predict today's FX, AND does yesterday's FX predict today's spread. That's the only honest way to find out whether the influence runs one way, both ways, or not really at all.

This is exactly the right tool for the question this repo's Interest Rate Parity lesson and this project's own prior macro research notes' Transatlantic Yield Spread lesson both raise but never formally test: does the yield spread lead FX, does FX lead the spread, or does the influence run both ways? A VAR fit on the two series doesn't just answer "are they correlated" — it answers, coefficient by coefficient, which lags of which series carry genuine incremental predictive power over the other.

The classic diagnostic built on top of a fitted VAR is Granger causality testing. Concretely: to test whether \(X\) "Granger-causes" \(Y\), you compare two regressions of \(Y\) — one using only \(Y\)'s own lags (the restricted model), one adding \(X\)'s lags too (the unrestricted model) — and run an F-test on whether the added \(X\) lags improve the fit by a statistically significant amount. If they do, \(X\) is said to "Granger-cause" \(Y\). It is essential to be precise about what that phrase means and doesn't mean: Granger causality is a strictly statistical, predictive notion — "does knowing \(X\)'s past significantly improve forecasting \(Y\), beyond \(Y\)'s own history" — and is explicitly not a claim of true, structural, mechanistic causation. Clive Granger, who introduced the test, was careful about exactly this distinction; a third, unobserved common driver hitting both series with slightly different timing can produce a "Granger-causes" result with no direct causal link between them at all. It's a real and useful diagnostic — just a narrower claim than the word "causality" in its name suggests.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

"Granger-causes" doesn't mean "X makes Y happen" — despite the name, it only means "knowing X's recent history genuinely helps you guess Y's next move, better than just watching Y alone." Roosters crowing right before sunrise "Granger-causes" the sunrise by this narrow definition, even though everyone knows the rooster isn't actually causing the sun to rise — something else (the Earth's rotation) is driving both on its own schedule.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

ARIMA is the standard classical baseline for single-series forecasting in macro and finance — still the model quant desks fit first and compare fancier approaches against. Per this repo's "name the benchmark before claiming improvement" discipline, if a more elaborate model (an ML forecaster, a regime-switching model) can't beat a properly Box-Jenkins-fit ARIMA at the same horizon, the elaborate model hasn't yet demonstrated it adds anything — ARIMA is the honest floor to clear, not a strawman to wave past.

VAR's home turf is macroeconomics, where it displaced an older generation of large simultaneous-equation models built on many hard-to-defend structural assumptions about which variables affect which others and how. Christopher Sims's "Macroeconomics and Reality" (1980) made exactly this case: let a comparatively unrestricted VAR, estimated straight from the data, reveal the dynamic relationships among a handful of macro variables (output, prices, interest rates) via its fitted coefficients and impulse response functions, rather than assuming the structure up front. That paper is part of the body of work behind Sims's 2011 Nobel Memorial Prize. In trading and macro research specifically, two-or-more-variable VARs are the standard tool for exactly the lead-lag questions this repo already raises informally: a policy-rate differential against a currency pair, a credit spread against an equity index (this repo's own This project's own lead-lag tooling measures a lead-lag empirically without a VAR/Granger structure underneath it yet), a term spread against a growth proxy.

🎯
Real-world trading scenario

Does the yield spread lead EUR/USD, or is it the other way around?

This is the exact question this project's own prior macro research notes' Transatlantic Yield Spread lesson poses and answers only qualitatively: its Lesson 3 case study walks through a dated example (17–18 Feb 2026) where the DE–US yield spread fell steadily on the Tuesday while EUR/USD stayed range-bound, then EUR/USD sold off hard on the Wednesday — read as "the spread led FX by about 24 hours." That's a real, useful observation, but it's a single narrated instance, not a tested relationship with a known lag and known direction.

A researcher wanting a genuine answer would fit a VAR(2) system on daily changes in the 2-year DE–US yield spread and EUR/USD log returns (both differenced/returned first, per the stationarity discussion above), then run Granger causality tests in both directions: does adding two days of lagged spread changes significantly improve forecasting EUR/USD returns beyond EUR/USD's own history, and separately, does adding two days of lagged EUR/USD returns significantly improve forecasting the spread beyond its own history? Suppose the result comes back asymmetric — the spread Granger-causes FX at a 2-day lag, but the reverse test is not significant. That is a materially different, more useful finding than "the spread sometimes seems to move first": it says the lead-lag relationship has a specific direction and a specific horizon, which would directly justify treating the spread as a genuine leading indicator with an explicit ~2-day timing window — size a signal off the spread and expect its FX effect to show up over the following two trading days — rather than as a same-day coincident confirmation check, changing exactly how a signal built on this relationship should be timed and evaluated.

To be clear about what this is: the 2-day, one-directional result above is an illustration of what a VAR + Granger causality test could show, chosen to make the payoff concrete — not a result already produced in this repo. No such fit has been run here yet.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where ARIMA/VAR fitting goes wrong in practice

Spurious regression on non-stationary series. Fitting AR/VAR-family models directly to raw, trending price levels rather than differenced/stationary series can produce impressively high \(R^2\) and significant-looking coefficients that are pure statistical mirage — the classic Granger & Newbold (1974) "spurious regression" result. The "I" step in ARIMA, and the equivalent stationarity check before fitting a VAR, exists precisely to prevent this. Skipping it is the single most common way this family of models goes wrong before estimation even starts.

The curse of dimensionality in VAR. A VAR's parameter count grows roughly as \(k^2 \times p\) (\(k\) series, \(p\) lags): a VAR(4) across 5 series already needs on the order of 100 coefficients (\(5 \times 5 \times 4\)). On daily FX/macro data, where a few thousand usable observations is generous, that's a lot of parameters chasing not much data — a direct overfitting risk this repo's minimal-DOF-first discipline exists to catch. Adding more series to a VAR "just in case" is not free; each one squares the parameter growth, not adds to it linearly.

Granger causality is not true causation — restated precisely. The test measures whether lagged \(X\) significantly improves forecasting \(Y\) beyond \(Y\)'s own history — a statistical, predictive-incremental-value claim, not a structural or mechanistic one. A textbook failure mode: an unobserved common driver (say, a shared macro-regime shock) hits both series with slightly different delays, producing a clean "X Granger-causes Y" result with no direct causal link between them at all. Treat a Granger result as "X's history has genuine forecasting content for Y" — a real and useful finding — never as "X makes Y happen."

Cointegrated series need a VECM, not a differenced VAR. Two series that are each individually non-stationary but move together in the long run (cointegrated — see the Cointegration lesson) lose that long-run relationship if you naively difference both before fitting a plain VAR. The correct tool there is a Vector Error-Correction Model (VECM), which keeps the long-run equilibrium relationship as an explicit term rather than differencing it away. Picking a plain differenced VAR when the pair is actually cointegrated throws away real information the data contains.

Regime dependence / parameter instability. Exactly the caution this project's own prior macro research itself gives (§2.7's breakdown modes — risk-off flights to safety, central-bank intervention, extreme positioning, geopolitical shocks) applies directly to any VAR/Granger fit: a lead-lag relationship estimated over one regime is not guaranteed to hold in the next, and a single full-sample coefficient or Granger-test result can hide a regime-dependent reversal exactly the way a pooled null can hide a subset effect elsewhere in this repo's validation discipline.

Section 05

Self-test

Q1. What's the practical difference between an AR term and an MA term in an ARMA model?
An AR term ties today's value to past levels of the series itself — a memory of where \(X\) has been, the same rubber-band-to-a-post mechanism as OU/AR(1). An MA term ties today's value to past shocks (innovations) — a memory of how large recent surprises were, which can keep echoing through the current value even after the level itself has already fully reverted. Practically, a persistently mispriced series slowly closing a gap looks AR-like; a one-off shock whose noisy aftereffects (a liquidity gap, a bid/ask imbalance) linger for a bar or two after the level has already normalized looks MA-like. The two mechanisms are structurally different and a real series can carry both at once, which is why ARMA allows combining them.
Q2. Why does ARIMA add a differencing step (the "I") that plain ARMA doesn't have, and how do you know how many times (d) to difference?
AR, MA, and ARMA all assume the series is already stationary — constant mean, constant variance, lag-dependent-only autocorrelation. Most raw financial levels (a raw price, a raw log-price) fail that outright: they have no fixed mean to revert to, the AR(1)/OU boundary case of \(\phi \geq 1\). Differencing (\(X_t - X_{t-1}\), repeated \(d\) times) removes that trend/non-stationarity so the ARMA machinery becomes valid on what's left. You don't guess \(d\) — you check stationarity on the raw series (ACF decay behavior, or formally a unit-root test like ADF); if it fails, difference once and re-check; repeat until it passes. Most daily financial series need only \(d=1\).
Q3. You plot the ACF and PACF of a (already-differenced) series: the PACF shows two significant spikes and then cuts off to ~zero, while the ACF decays smoothly across many lags. What ARIMA order does that suggest, and why?
That's the AR(2) signature: for a pure AR(p) process, the PACF shows significant spikes at exactly lags \(1,\ldots,p\) and then cuts off sharply, while the ACF decays gradually rather than cutting off — here \(p=2\). Since the series was already differenced once (\(d=1\) assumed from the setup) and there's no evidence of the MA ACF-cuts-off-sharply signature, the provisional model is ARIMA(2,1,0) — two AR lags, one difference, no MA term. This would still be checked with diagnostic residual testing (e.g. Ljung-Box) before being accepted.
Q4. In the two-variable VAR(1) system \(X_t = a_{11}X_{t-1}+a_{12}Y_{t-1}+\epsilon_{1,t}\), \(Y_t=a_{21}X_{t-1}+a_{22}Y_{t-1}+\epsilon_{2,t}\), what would it mean if \(a_{12}\) were estimated as essentially zero but \(a_{21}\) were large and statistically significant?
\(a_{12}\) near zero means \(Y\)'s past has no meaningful predictive power for \(X\) — knowing yesterday's \(Y\) doesn't help forecast today's \(X\) beyond what \(X\)'s own past already explains. \(a_{21}\) large and significant means \(X\)'s past strongly predicts \(Y\) — knowing yesterday's \(X\) meaningfully improves forecasting today's \(Y\). Together, that's a one-directional lead-lag relationship: \(X\) leads \(Y\), not the reverse, and there's no feedback loop — exactly the pattern the yield-spread scenario above illustrates (spread leads FX, not the other way around).
Q5. Why is "the yield spread Granger-causes EUR/USD" not the same statement as "the yield spread causes EUR/USD to move"?
Granger causality is defined purely in predictive/statistical terms: adding lagged spread values to a regression of EUR/USD significantly improves the forecast beyond EUR/USD's own history. That is an incremental-forecasting-value claim, not a claim about mechanism. It's entirely possible for X to Granger-cause Y with no direct causal link at all — a third, unobserved common driver (a shared macro shock, a regime change) could hit both series with slightly different timing and produce exactly this statistical signature. Granger causality is a real and useful diagnostic for "does X's history carry information about Y's future," but the word "causes" in its name is doing narrower work than it sounds like — it says nothing about the true underlying mechanism.
Q6. Why is a VAR fit across many lags and many series a bigger overfitting risk than a single-series AR(p) with the same number of lags?
A single-series AR(p) has \(p\) coefficients (plus an intercept) — parameter count scales linearly with the number of lags. A VAR with \(k\) series and \(p\) lags needs a full \(k \times k\) coefficient matrix per lag, so parameter count scales as \(k^2 \times p\) — quadratically in the number of series. Adding a third or fourth series to "see more of the system" therefore multiplies the parameter count far faster than the data available to estimate it grows, making a many-variable VAR a much faster route to overfitting than simply adding more lags to one series.
