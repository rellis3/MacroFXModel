Reading the Statement Like a Model Would

A central bank statement, a news headline, an earnings-call transcript — none of it is a number, and every trading model built so far in this curriculum only eats numbers. This lesson closes that gap: how raw text gets turned into a numeric feature, why "the committee remains attentive to inflation risks" is a loaded phrase a machine can now be taught to weigh correctly, and why the clock on a text-based signal is far easier to get quietly, catastrophically wrong than the clock on a price series ever is.

The whole idea
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

When the Fed statement drops, I read it and get a feel: more hawkish than last time, or less. Can a model do that?

That is exactly what this lesson builds. Turn that feel into a number a system can use.

Where do you start?

With words. The old way just counts them, and to a counter, inflation is no closer to risk than to coffee. Word2Vec, from 2013, fixes that. It learns a short list of numbers for each word from the company it keeps.

So hawkish ends up near tighten and raise.

Right, without anyone labeling them. But it has a flaw. Each word gets one fixed vector. Hold, in "the Fed will hold rates", gets the same vector as hold in "hold my coffee".

And BERT fixes that?

Yes. BERT, from 2019, reads the whole sentence at once, in both directions, so each word is read in its context. FinBERT is BERT trained further on financial text, so it knows that "attentive to inflation risks" is a loaded phrase, not filler.

Give me a number.

The lesson sketches three statements. "Inflation has moderated" scores about plus zero point one on a hawkish scale. "Highly attentive to inflation risks" jumps to plus zero point five five. "Further tightening will depend on incoming data" slips back to plus zero point three five.

So I'd trade the jump?

Careful. One reading is noisy. Models trip on sarcasm and hedges, and zero point five five is not a fifty-five percent chance of anything. The trend across meetings is the useful part.

Has text ever actually worked as a signal?

The famous case says be careful. In 2010 a paper turned nearly ten million tweets into seven mood series. One mood, "Calm", called the Dow's daily direction with eighty-seven point six percent accuracy.

That sounds incredible.

It was tested on a few weeks of December 2008, after trying seven series at several lags. A fund launched to trade it closed within about a year, and a 2017 replication could not find the effect.

One line?

Turning text into a number always works. Whether that number predicts anything is a separate claim, and only an out-of-sample test can settle it.

Section 2

The information is real. The format is the problem.

None of these arrive as a clean numeric feed — and a purely numeric model never sees them.

🏦

Central bank statements

The rate decision is a number. Whether the guidance is "firm" or "data-dependent," and whether the tone shifted from last meeting, is not — and often moves price more than the decision itself.

📰

News headlines & wire articles

"Beats estimates," "misses on guidance," "unexpected escalation" — the market-relevant content is in the wording, not in any field a numeric feed exposes.

🎙

Earnings-call transcripts

The reported EPS number is one line. A CFO hedging on forward guidance for three paragraphs is a different signal entirely, and it's not in the EPS line.

A trained human analyst reads all three fluently, by eye, and forms a fast holistic impression — "this reads more hawkish than last time." This lesson's whole arc is making that impression explicit and reproducible: first teach a model what individual words mean, then teach it to read a whole sentence at once the way a human does, then specialize that reading skill for finance specifically.

Section 3

Step one: give every word a location, not a label

A word embedding is a dense vector — a few hundred real numbers — positioned so words used in similar contexts end up near each other.

Before and after
one-hot: [0,0,1,0,…,0]
→
embedding: [0.31, −0.08, …]
A one-hot vector says only "this is word #4,812" — every word is equally, meaninglessly far from every other. A trained embedding says "this word tends to keep company with these other words" — and that company is exactly what similarity ends up meaning.

Nobody hand-labels these two neighborhoods. They fall out purely from which words tend to appear near which other words, across a huge body of ordinary text — a training signal that needs no human annotation at all, which is exactly why this technique scales.

Section 4

Two ways to train the same idea

Word2Vec's two objectives are mirror images of each other — both trained on nothing but raw, unlabeled text.

Try it
target: "hawkish"
predicts →
tighten, rates, inflation
Given one target word, predict the words likely to surround it. Fed "hawkish" repeatedly, the model learns to expect "tighten" and "inflation" nearby far more than unrelated words — and that repeated pull is what drags "hawkish" close to them in vector space.
"the committee remains ___ about inflation risks"
predicts →
attentive
The mirror image: given the surrounding context, predict the single missing word in the middle. "Attentive" or "concerned" fit that slot far better than an unrelated word — again, purely from how often each candidate actually appears in that company.

A well-known side effect of this geometry: vector arithmetic starts capturing relationships, not just similarity — the classic example is vec(king) − vec(man) + vec(woman) landing close to vec(queen). The space encodes "royal" and "gender" as roughly consistent directions, entirely as a byproduct of the prediction task.

Section 5

One fixed vector per word is the limitation — attention is the fix

"Hold" gets the same embedding whether it means "the Fed will hold rates" or "hold my coffee." That's the exact problem the previous lesson's architecture solves.

↔

Bidirectional

A word's representation is built from both what comes before it and what comes after it, at once — not read strictly left-to-right.

🎯

Attention — straight from lesson 2

The same mechanism that let a time-series transformer weigh which past bars matter now lets a language model weigh which other words in the sentence matter to this one.

📚

Pre-training

Trained first on a huge general corpus — Wikipedia, books — before ever seeing a single finance-specific example. This general-purpose model is BERT.

This is the payoff for having built the attention mechanism first. A transformer reading a sentence and a transformer reading a price series are the same underlying trick, pointed at two different kinds of sequence — every position attending to every other position, weighted by learned relevance, instead of information having to survive being carried forward one step at a time.
Section 6

FinBERT: the same reader, after finance school

FinBERT is stated plainly: BERT, further trained (fine-tuned) on financial text specifically.

BERT — general reader

Pre-trained on broad text. Reads "bearish," "guidance cut," and "beat estimates" the way general English would — missing the precise financial connotation each phrase actually carries on a desk.

FinBERT — finance specialist

Same architecture, continued training on finance-domain text and labeled financial-sentiment examples. Now "the committee remains highly attentive to inflation risks" is read as the loaded, specific hedge phrase it actually is — not a bland aside.

The output is a sentiment score produced directly from raw text — a probability over positive/negative/neutral, or, framed for a central-bank use case, a hawkish/dovish score — with no human hand-scoring that specific sentence. It's the direct computational analogue of an analyst's fluent, context-aware read.

Section 7

Context-blind vs. context-aware, on the same sentence

The whole reason FinBERT-style scoring replaced simple word-counting: negation.

Try it — score "the outlook is not bad, but hardly reassuring"
sees: "bad" → negative word found
=
score: −0.60 (negative)
A word-count scorer matches "bad" against a negative-word list and scores the sentence negative — it has no mechanism to notice "not" sitting immediately in front of it, or "hardly" qualifying "reassuring." Every word is scored in isolation.
reads the whole sentence at once, via attention
=
score: +0.10 (mildly, hedged, positive)
A transformer's representation of "bad" is built using every other word in the sentence — including "not," directly before it. It correctly reads the negation, and also picks up that "hardly reassuring" pulls the mild positive back down — a hedged, muted read, not a strongly positive or strongly negative one.

This is not a contrived edge case. Hedge phrases, qualified statements, and negation are exactly the register central bank statements and earnings-call answers are written in — a bag-of-words scorer is structurally blind to the part of the sentence that usually carries the real signal.

Section 8

The timestamp on the article is not when the market saw it

This is the trap that's specific to text — a price bar's timestamp is rarely in question; an article's is, constantly.

✍

Backfilled or corrected articles

A wire service can revise a story and keep the original publish timestamp — the version your dataset scraped may only have existed, in full, several minutes after that stamp claims.

⏱

Central bank release time, to the second

An embargo lifting at 14:00:00.000 ET versus a dataset recording 14:00 flat, or the previous minute, is a subtle, easy-to-miss error — and it sits exactly where the biggest price move happens.

Either error quietly leaks information from after the true decision moment into a backtest that thinks it's trading strictly on the "claimed" timestamp. The backtest never sees an obviously impossible input — it just silently gets to react a little earlier than any real trader ever could have.

Section 11

The same warning, wearing a new outfit

This is exactly the look-ahead-bias problem from the feature-engineering lesson much earlier in this curriculum, back for one more round: a feature is only usable at the moment it was actually, verifiably knowable — not the moment a convenient field in a dataset claims. Text-based signals just make the violation easier to miss, because the "obviously wrong" version (a feature literally computed from the future) rarely happens; what actually happens is a timestamp that's subtly, plausibly, quietly off by a few minutes.

📉

Trend over single reading

One document's sentiment score is a noisy, high-variance read. Averaged across many documents about the same event, or tracked meeting-over-meeting, the per-document noise partially cancels.

🎯

Not a calibrated probability

A FinBERT output like "0.72 hawkish" is a model's internal confidence, useful for ranking documents against each other — not a 72% real-world chance of anything.

Section 12 · It actually happened

"Twitter mood predicts the stock market"

It actually happened

Bollen, Mao and Zeng (Journal of Computational Science, 2011) turned about 9.8 million tweets from 2008 into daily sentiment series: a positive/negative score and six "moods". Positive/negative didn't help. One mood, "Calm", seemed to lead the Dow by a few days, and a neural network built on it called the Dow's daily direction with 87.6% accuracy, on a test window of a few weeks in December 2008. A London fund launched on Twitter sentiment in 2011 closed within about a year. In 2017 Lachanski and Pav (Econ Journal Watch) could not reproduce the result.

Section 13

Where the textbook breaks, and what this desk found

Desk test: nothing found cb-tone-direction: priced in half an hour, then gone. N=82 FOMC meetings. First-30-minute reaction vs next-day dollar: sign agreement 46%, t = 0.32. Lexicon Δhawkishness vs next-day: t = −0.75, though it IS priced inside 30 minutes (t = 2.04). |Δhawkishness| vs next-day size: t = 0.54. Don't carry the tone forward as a lean.
Where the textbook version breaks

The pipeline assumes the score measures something stable and is tested on data nobody looked at while choosing the model. In the Twitter paper the moods, lags and network were all chosen with results in view, on a test window of weeks. And text that matters is read by many machines at once, so even a real effect is priced within minutes.

Section 14

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

What does a word embedding actually encode, that a one-hot vector cannot?

A one-hot vector treats every word as equally, meaninglessly distant from every other. An embedding is trained so that words appearing in similar contexts end up near each other — a computable notion of similarity that was never hand-labeled.
Question 2

What specific limitation of Word2Vec does a transformer's bidirectional attention fix?

"Hold" gets the same Word2Vec vector whether it means "hold rates" or "hold my coffee." A transformer builds each word's representation from every other word in the sentence, so the same word gets a different, context-appropriate representation each time.
Question 3

Why does a bag-of-words scorer fail on "not bad"?

A word-count scorer matches "bad" against a negative-term list and stops there — it has no way to notice "not" sitting directly in front of it. A transformer's attention lets "bad" be read together with "not," in context.
Question 4

Why is a text-based signal's timestamp riskier than a price bar's?

A wire correction or backfill can leave an article's stated timestamp earlier than when the finalized text actually existed — silently letting a backtest "know" something before it really could have.
Question 5

Why does the lesson recommend tracking a sentiment score's trend across many documents, not trusting one reading?

One misread hedge phrase or one instance of sarcasm can swing a single score meaningfully. The trend across many documents or successive meetings is a far steadier, more informative read.
Your call
Scenario drill · 2011

A fund wants to trade Twitter mood

A widely reported paper says one mood series built from about 9.8 million tweets, "Calm", called the Dow's daily direction with 87.6% accuracy. The test window was a few weeks of trading days in December 2008, after seven sentiment series were tried at several lags. A new London fund will trade the signal and asks you to allocate. What do you do?

  1. Allocate. 87.6% is far above chance, and the paper is everywhere.
    Seven series, several lags and a test window of weeks give many chances to find something by luck. Press coverage and citations measure attention, not out-of-sample evidence.
  2. Ask for the signal's record on data the researchers never saw, with the number of series and lags tried counted against it, before committing anything.
    This is the step the lesson keeps returning to. Turning text into a score always works; whether the score adds anything has to be shown out of sample, with the search that found it taken into account.
  3. Allocate a small pilot and watch the live results.
    A small live test limits the damage and does produce genuinely out-of-sample evidence. But it pays to run a test that a fresh historical check could have done first, and a few months of daily direction calls is itself a noisy sample.
  4. Build your own version with FinBERT and more mood series, and keep whichever works best.
    A better text model does not fix the problem. Adding more series and keeping the winner widens the search that produced the original result, making a lucky fit more likely, not less.

What happened. The London hedge fund launched in 2011 to trade Twitter sentiment closed within about a year. In 2017 Lachanski and Pav tried to reproduce the original result and could not recover its predictive power. The text-to-number step worked as advertised. The claim that the number predicted the Dow did not survive.

Sources: Bollen, Mao and Zeng, "Twitter mood predicts the stock market", Journal of Computational Science 2(1), 2011 (arXiv preprint: 9,853,498 tweets, test period 1 to 19 December 2008); Lachanski and Pav, "Shy of the Character Limit", Econ Journal Watch 14(3), September 2017; HedgeCo News on the Derwent Capital Markets fund closing, 29 May 2012.

Section 15

What to carry forward

01

Word embeddings turn words into dense vectors positioned so similar-context words sit close together — trained on raw text alone, no labels needed.

02

Word2Vec's skip-gram and CBOW objectives are mirror images of the same co-occurrence idea, but each word still gets exactly one fixed vector.

03

Transformers (the same attention mechanism from lesson 2) fix that: bidirectional, context-aware reading, where the same word means different things in different sentences.

04

FinBERT is BERT, further trained on financial text — a sentiment score computed directly from raw text, context-aware in a way bag-of-words scoring structurally cannot be.

05

A text signal's timestamp is not automatically trustworthy — backfilled articles and to-the-second release times can leak post-decision information into a backtest that thinks it's using the claimed time.

06

Trust the trend across many documents, never a single reading — and remember a sentiment score is a model's internal confidence, not a calibrated real-world probability.

07

"Twitter mood predicts the stock market" (2011) reported 87.6% on weeks of test data and did not replicate. On this desk, FOMC statement tone is priced inside 30 minutes and says nothing about the next day (cb-tone-direction, NULL).
