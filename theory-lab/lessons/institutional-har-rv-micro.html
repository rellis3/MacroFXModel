HAR-RV, Three Horizons and an Ordinary Regression

The previous lesson built GARCH out of a single noisy number per day — one squared return. This lesson replaces that number with realized variance, a far better-measured daily estimate built from many intraday returns — and shows that forecasting it well takes nothing more exotic than an ordinary linear regression on three horizons: today, this week, and this month.

Section 1

One squared return is a coin flip. Many of them aren't.

1

The old way — one observation per day

GARCH's entire picture of "yesterday's variance" comes from a single squared close-to-close return, ε²ₜ₋₁. Two days with identical true variance can produce very different squared returns just from where the close happened to land.

Σ

Realized variance — many observations, summed

RVₜ = Σ r²ₜ,ⱼ over every intraday interval j in day t (5-minute bars are typical). Instead of one noisy squared return, you're averaging roughly 78 of them — the same statistical trick behind every "more data, less noise" result you've ever relied on.

∞

What it converges to

As the sampling interval shrinks (subject to microstructure noise in practice), RVₜ converges to the quadratic variation of the price path over that day — the formal object measuring exactly how much a continuous price process moved, jumps included.

🔗

Same object, next lesson

Bipower Variation, next in this category, splits this exact quantity into a continuous part and a jump part. Same underlying object, two different lessons asking different questions of it.

If you wanted to know how bumpy a car ride was, you could ask "how big was the single worst bump?" — one noisy number. Or you could add up every jolt felt the entire ride — realized variance. It uses far more information about what actually happened.
Section 2

Why volatility "remembers" longer than GARCH thinks it should

A genuine RV series decays far more slowly than GARCH's clean geometric persistence implies — the "long memory" stylized fact. Corsi's fix doesn't need fractional calculus.

🌅

The day trader

Cares almost entirely about today and yesterday — the horizon their positions actually live on.

📅

The weekly rebalancer

Cares about how choppy the past week has been on average — one wild day matters less than the week's overall character.

🗓

The monthly allocator

Cares about the past month's average character — a single day or week barely moves their picture of "normal."

✳

Corsi's move

Don't pick one horizon — regress on all three at once, as three ordinary linear terms. The blend of simple pieces approximates genuine long memory almost as well as fitting a fractionally-integrated model directly.

Where this needs a name

This is the Heterogeneous Market Hypothesis (Müller et al., 1997) turned into a forecasting model — "Heterogeneous Autoregressive" is literally what the H and the AR in HAR-RV stand for.

Section 4

The model itself — three terms, added together

RVₜ₊₁ = β₀ + β_d·RVₜ + β_w·RVₜ⁽ʷ⁾ + β_m·RVₜ⁽ᵐ⁾ + εₜ₊₁

β₀

Constant

A baseline term, same units as RV.

βd

Daily term — RVₜ

Today's own realized variance — the fastest-moving, most reactive horizon.

βw

Weekly term — RVₜ⁽ʷ⁾

Average RV over the past 5 trading days including today.

βm

Monthly term — RVₜ⁽ᵐ⁾

Average RV over the past 22 trading days including today — the slowest-moving anchor.

Every term on the right-hand side is a number you already have the moment day t closes — no latent recursive state the way GARCH's σ²ₜ₋₁ is. Fitting β₀, β_d, β_w, β_m is literally an ordinary least-squares regression on three engineered features. Nothing about the estimation is volatility-specific; all the cleverness is in which three features to regress on.
Section 5

A 22-day worked example, drawn

Days 10–14 carry a volatility cluster. By day 22 the daily figure has decayed back near baseline — but the weekly and especially monthly averages haven't fully forgotten it.

Visualize it
Monthly (purple) runs highest because it's the only window still fully containing the cluster's peak. The forecast (green) blends all three by their fitted weights (β_d=0.35, β_w=0.41, β_m=0.20, plus β₀=0.03) — landing above the daily figure alone precisely because the longer horizons remember what yesterday already has forgotten.

Section 8

Is this actually built here?

Verified against this repo's actual code

Yes — genuinely, and running live every day, though not (yet) as any asset class's primary σ. js/volForecastBench.js's harRvPred() is Corsi's exact model, fit by a strictly causal, expanding-window walk-forward OLS. js/volForecastScheduler.js's daily runVolForecast() job computes a full HAR-RV shadow forecast (f.har) for every one of ~28 instruments, every trading day. js/volHorseRaceEngine.js races it live against 7 other estimators (RW, EWMA, HV, Yang-Zhang, GARCH, and two combination models) by out-of-sample QLIKE, via a live endpoint. And js/indexRvHar.js runs a genuine-intraday-RV variant for equity indices — built specifically because an earlier range-based HAR shadow understated SPX's true volatility by roughly half (≈9.5% read versus a true ≈19%).

What it is not, per this project's own PLATFORM_MODEL mapping: the estimator actually setting live trading bands today is Yang-Zhang for FX, GARCH(1,1) for indices, and rolling HV for commodities. HAR-RV sits beside all three as an actively-benchmarked challenger — purely additive, never touching the primary numbers — not as a replacement for any of them. The horse race is a genuinely live computation over current data, not a fixed verdict baked into the code.

Section 9

Where HAR-RV quietly stops being HAR-RV

No genuine intraday data
  • HAR-RV's entire edge rests on RV being well-measured — many observations, not one
  • Only daily OHLC bars available? True RV can't be computed at all
  • Running the same equation on a single-observation daily proxy isn't a free upgrade — it's an untested estimator wearing Corsi's formula
  • This repo's own indexRvHar.js exists because that exact substitution understated index vol by roughly half
Collinearity + smoothing lag
  • Daily, weekly, monthly RV averages move together far more than "independent" features normally would
  • Can make individual β estimates unstable on a short window — a small ridge penalty (used in this repo) is the standard fix
  • A blended, multi-horizon forecast structurally reads below reality right as a sharp new shock hits
  • Documented in this project's own gold research: HAR sat ~15% under a plain close-to-close estimate in an elevated regime
"HAR-RV beats GARCH" is an empirical finding conditional on well-measured, high-frequency data — not a universal law. This project's own horse race exists precisely to re-check that ranking per instrument, not to assume it from the math.
Section 10

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Why is realized variance (RV) a better estimate of a day's true variance than one squared close-to-close return?

RV = Σ r²ₜ,ⱼ over every intraday interval — averaging roughly 78 observations per day instead of trusting one squared close-to-close return.
Question 2

What are the three regressors in the HAR-RV equation?

RVₜ₊₁ = β₀ + β_d·RVₜ + β_w·RVₜ⁽ʷ⁾ + β_m·RVₜ⁽ᵐ⁾ + εₜ₊₁ — the daily figure plus a weekly (5-day) and monthly (22-day) rolling average of it.
Question 3

What was Corsi's key argument for why three linear regressors can mimic genuine long-memory behavior?

Daily traders, weekly rebalancers, and monthly allocators each contribute their own simpler persistence — layering one linear term per horizon approximates the complicated aggregate almost as well as fitting a fractionally-integrated model directly.
Question 4

When HAR-RV outperforms GARCH, what is usually the honest reason?

HAR-RV is actually the simpler model — plain OLS on three linear terms — versus GARCH's nonlinear recursive maximum-likelihood filter. The typical edge comes from better-measured input, not a fancier model.
Question 5

What does this repo's own code reveal about HAR-RV's actual status here?

Corsi's exact model runs daily for every instrument (js/volForecastScheduler.js, js/volForecastBench.js) and is raced against 7 alternatives (js/volHorseRaceEngine.js) — but the platform's live trading bands still come from Yang-Zhang (FX), GARCH (indices), or HV (commodities), per PLATFORM_MODEL.
Section 11

What to carry forward

01

Realized variance sums many intraday squared returns instead of relying on one squared daily return — a large-sample average, not a single noisy draw.

02

HAR-RV is literally an OLS regression on three engineered features — today's RV, and its 5-day and 22-day rolling averages.

03

Corsi's insight: heterogeneous trading horizons, layered as three simple linear terms, approximate genuine long-memory behavior without fractional-differencing machinery.

04

HAR-RV's typical edge over GARCH comes from a better-measured input, not a fancier model — it's the simpler model of the two.

05

This repo genuinely runs Corsi's exact model live, daily, for every instrument, benchmarked against 7 alternatives — as a challenger, not (yet) any asset class's primary σ.

06

HAR-RV needs genuine intraday data to be the model the literature validated — fed a single-observation daily proxy instead, it quietly becomes something else.

What this now lets you do

Recognize why a better-measured input can beat a fancier recursive model, and why that lesson generalizes well past volatility forecasting. Read a multi-horizon regression like HAR-RV for what it actually is — three plain averages, fit by OLS — rather than assuming "long memory" requires exotic machinery. Know exactly where this repo's own HAR-RV sits today: real, live, actively tested, and honestly not yet in the driver's seat for any asset class.
