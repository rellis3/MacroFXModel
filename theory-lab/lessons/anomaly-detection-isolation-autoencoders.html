Anomaly Detection: Isolation Forests & Autoencoders

Two ways to let a model find "this doesn't look like the rest" on its own, across many variables at once — without ever writing down a threshold rule by hand.

CONCEPT — not built or tested anywhere in this repo ~17 min read Prereqs: Data Cleaning: Outliers, Bad Ticks & Missing Data helpful for the classical baseline this lesson contrasts against
TL;DR
  • Isolation Forest (Liu, Ting & Zhou, 2008) builds an ensemble of random trees that split the data on random features at random thresholds; anomalies are "few and different," so they get carved off in fewer splits than normal points — a shorter average path length across the ensemble is the anomaly score.
  • Autoencoders are neural networks trained to reconstruct their own input through a narrow bottleneck layer; the reconstruction only holds up well for patterns the network saw a lot of during training. The reconstruction error — how far the output is from the original input — is the anomaly score.
  • Both methods generalize the classical, univariate rules in Data Cleaning: Outliers, Bad Ticks & Missing Data (rolling median/MAD thresholds) to multivariate anomalies — a point that looks completely normal on every single variable in isolation, but whose combination of variables has never occurred before.
  • Anomaly detection is the pointwise, cross-sectional cousin of Change-Point Detection's sequential framing: change-point tools ask "has the process shifted, over time"; anomaly detectors ask "is this individual observation unusual, right now, relative to everything else."
  • Mandatory honest framing: an anomaly score flags "this is statistically unusual" — never "this is wrong," and never "this is a trading opportunity." What counts as unusual always needs a human decision about context, and a model trained on one regime will flag an entirely normal but different regime as one long anomaly.
Section 01

Why this matters

Data Cleaning: Outliers, Bad Ticks & Missing Data already covers the classical toolkit for catching a bad value: a rolling median/MAD filter, or a maximum-plausible-return-per-tick bound. Both of those are deliberately simple, deliberately univariate rules — they look at one series at a time and ask "is this single number too far from its recent neighbors." That simplicity is a feature, not a limitation, for the fat-finger-print and stale-quote problems that lesson targets. But a large class of real anomalies are invisible to any univariate rule, because no single variable ever leaves its normal range — it's the combination that's wrong. A spread that's perfectly normal, paired with a volume that's perfectly normal, paired with a bid-ask width that's perfectly normal, can still be an anomaly if that specific combination of spread, volume, and width has never co-occurred before. Catching that requires a model that can reason about many variables jointly — which is exactly what Isolation Forest and autoencoders are built to do, at the cost of being harder to explain in a single sentence than "more than five MADs from the median."

💡
Plain-English intuition

Two very different ways to notice "this one's odd"

Isolation Forest's idea: if you're trying to separate one weird kid out of a crowded classroom by asking random yes/no questions ("taller than the person next to you?", "wearing blue?"), the weird kid — who stands out on several axes at once — gets isolated in just a couple of questions, while an ordinary kid buried in a dense cluster of similar classmates takes many more questions to separate from everyone else. Isolation Forest does exactly this with random trees: it doesn't need to know what makes a point unusual ahead of time, only that unusual points are quicker to isolate through random splitting.

Autoencoders take a completely different route: train a network to compress its input down to a small bottleneck and then rebuild it from that compressed version, using mostly normal data. A network that's only ever practiced compressing-and-rebuilding normal patterns gets good at that specific job and stays bad at anything unfamiliar — so when a genuinely unusual input comes through, the reconstruction comes out noticeably wrong. The size of that mistake is the anomaly score.

Section 02

The math, step by step

Isolation Forest — anomalies are few and different

Fei Tony Liu, Kai Ming Ting & Zhi-Hua Zhou introduced Isolation Forest in "Isolation Forest," Proceedings of the 8th IEEE International Conference on Data Mining (ICDM), 2008 (cited in full below), built around one precise structural insight: anomalies are "few and different" — rare, and sitting apart from the dense region where most of the data lives. That structural property, rather than any distance or density calculation, is what the algorithm exploits directly.

An isolation tree is built by recursively splitting the data: at each node, pick a feature at random, then pick a split value at random between that feature's observed minimum and maximum in the current node's data, and partition the points into two child nodes. Repeat until every point is alone in its own leaf (or a maximum tree depth is reached). Because anomalies are few and sit apart from the dense normal region, a random split is disproportionately likely to separate an anomaly from everything else almost immediately — it takes only a handful of random cuts to wall off a point that's already far from the crowd, while a normal point buried in a dense cluster requires many more cuts before it ends up alone.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine a game of "20 Questions," but the questions are all random guesses instead of clever ones — "is it bigger than a shoebox?", "is it red?" If you're thinking of something totally ordinary, like "a regular apple," random questions take forever to pin it down exactly, because there are a million things it could still be confused with. But if you're thinking of something bizarre — "a flaming purple bicycle" — even random, dumb questions narrow it down fast, because almost nothing else fits that weird combination. Isolation Forest is that game played automatically: things that get pinned down in very few random questions are the weird ones.

A single tree's path length for one point can be noisy — one particular sequence of random splits might get lucky or unlucky. Isolation Forest averages over an ensemble of many independently built random trees to smooth this out, and converts the average path length into a bounded anomaly score using Liu, Ting & Zhou's normalization:

\[ s(x, n) = 2^{-\, \frac{E[h(x)]}{c(n)}} \]
E[h(x)]the average path length for point x, across every tree in the ensemble
c(n)the average path length of an unsuccessful search in a Binary Search Tree of n points — the expected path length for an ordinary point, used to normalize the score
s(x, n)the anomaly score — bounded between 0 and 1

Why a shorter average path length signals an anomaly, made explicit through the formula: as \(E[h(x)]\) gets smaller relative to \(c(n)\), the exponent \(-E[h(x)]/c(n)\) moves toward 0, which pushes \(s(x,n)\) toward 1 — the maximum possible score. As \(E[h(x)]\) approaches \(c(n)\) (an ordinary path length, the same as a typical point in an unstructured tree), \(s(x,n)\) settles around 0.5. Scores well below 0.5 correspond to path lengths longer than typical, meaning that point took extra splits to isolate — consistent with sitting inside a dense, ordinary region. In practice: scores close to 1 flag likely anomalies, scores close to 0.5 or below flag likely normal points, and a threshold (often the top 1-5% of scores) turns the continuous score into a binary flag when one is needed.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Think of the score as "how fast did we catch this one?" measured on a 0-to-1 scale. Caught almost instantly (score near 1) means it stuck out a mile. Took forever to corner (score near 0) means it blended right into the crowd. Averaging across a whole forest of random-question games, instead of trusting just one game, keeps a single lucky or unlucky round of questions from fooling the answer.

Autoencoders — reconstruction error as the anomaly score

An autoencoder is a neural network with two halves: an encoder that compresses the input \(x\) down into a lower-dimensional latent (or "bottleneck") vector \(z\), and a decoder that expands \(z\) back out to reconstruct an approximation \(\hat{x}\) of the original input. The network is trained end-to-end with no labels at all, purely to minimize the gap between its own output and its own input:

\[ z = f_{\text{enc}}(x), \qquad \hat{x} = f_{\text{dec}}(z), \qquad \mathcal{L}(x) = \lVert x - \hat{x} \rVert^2 \]
zthe compressed latent representation — forced through a bottleneck narrower than the raw input, so the network can't just memorize the identity function
x̂the network's reconstruction — its best attempt to rebuild x from only the compressed z
ℒ(x)the reconstruction loss — the squared distance between original and reconstruction, the exact quantity minimized during training

Because the bottleneck is narrower than the input, the network cannot simply pass every input straight through unchanged — it is forced to learn which patterns in the training data are common enough to be worth encoding efficiently, discarding whatever it can safely reconstruct approximately instead of exactly. Trained on data that is mostly normal, the network becomes good specifically at compressing and rebuilding normal patterns, and stays comparatively bad at anything that looks unfamiliar — the network was never given the chance to practice a pattern it rarely or never saw.

The reconstruction-error-as-anomaly-score idea, stated precisely: at inference time, run any new point \(x\) through the trained encoder-decoder and measure \(\lVert x - \hat{x} \rVert^2\) again. A normal point, resembling what the network trained on, reconstructs well and produces a small error. An anomalous point — one that doesn't fit the patterns the network learned — reconstructs poorly, and that reconstruction gap is itself the anomaly score. No separate scoring mechanism is bolted on; the training objective and the anomaly score are the same quantity, just evaluated on new data instead of training data.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

An autoencoder is like someone who's only ever seen golden retrievers, trying to sketch a cat from memory right after glancing at it. They compress what they saw down to a rough mental note ("four legs, medium size, fur"), then try to redraw it from that note. Because their entire practiced vocabulary is "golden retriever," the sketch comes out looking suspiciously dog-shaped — pointier ears than a real dog, but still not a convincing cat. The sketch isn't wrong because they're bad at drawing; it's wrong in a very specific, informative way that reveals the cat never fit the pattern they'd practiced. That gap between the real cat and the dog-shaped sketch is exactly the reconstruction error.

🧮
Worked example

Isolation Forest score from path lengths, across a small ensemble

Take an isolation forest of \(t = 100\) trees built on \(n = 256\) points, giving a normalization constant of roughly \(c(256) \approx 10.24\) (the standard \(c(n) = 2H(n-1) - 2(n-1)/n\), using the harmonic-number approximation \(H(m) \approx \ln(m) + 0.5772\)). Suppose a candidate point's path lengths, averaged across all 100 trees, come out to \(E[h(x)] = 3.2\) — much shorter than the ~10.24 an ordinary point in this dataset would need:

\[ s(x, 256) = 2^{-\frac{3.2}{10.24}} = 2^{-0.3125} \approx 0.805 \]

A score of roughly 0.805 sits well above 0.5, flagging this point as a likely anomaly. Contrast that with a second point whose average path length comes out to \(E[h(x)] = 9.8\) — close to the ordinary \(c(256)\approx 10.24\) baseline: \(s(x,256) = 2^{-9.8/10.24} \approx 2^{-0.957} \approx 0.516\), landing right around 0.5, the score of a typical, unremarkable point that took roughly the expected number of splits to isolate.

🎯
A named scenario

A multivariate anomaly a MAD filter would sail straight past

Consider a single FX cross's tick, described by three variables at once: the bid-ask spread, the trade volume, and the quote-update frequency in the preceding minute. On this particular tick, the spread sits at the 60th percentile of its own recent history, volume sits at the 55th percentile of its own recent history, and quote-update frequency sits at the 65th percentile of its own recent history — every single variable, taken on its own, is unremarkable. A rolling MAD filter run separately on each of the three series (the classical approach from Data Cleaning: Outliers, Bad Ticks & Missing Data) would flag none of them; nothing crosses a few-MADs-from-the-median threshold on any single axis.

But this exact combination — moderately wide spread, moderately elevated volume, and moderately elevated quote frequency occurring together — has essentially never happened in this pair's recent history; ordinarily, wide spreads and heavy quote-update activity go together with either genuinely high or genuinely low volume, not a bland middle value. An Isolation Forest or an autoencoder trained on the joint distribution of these three variables would isolate this point quickly (few splits needed to wall it off from the "normal" joint clusters) or reconstruct it poorly (the network never practiced this specific combination), flagging it as unusual precisely because the multivariate structure — not any one variable — is what's off. This is presented purely to illustrate why a multivariate detector catches something a univariate rule structurally cannot; it is not a claim that this scenario, or any anomaly detector built on it, has been built, backtested, or validated anywhere in this repo.

Worth remembering: an anomaly score answers "how statistically unusual is this," full stop. It never answers "is this an error," and it never answers "is this a trading opportunity" — both of those require a human decision about what "unusual" means in this specific context, made after the score comes back, not baked into the score itself. And a model trained on one regime has no way to distinguish "genuinely wrong" from "a different, but entirely legitimate, market regime it simply hadn't seen before" — both look identical to it: unfamiliar.
Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

Isolation Forest is the workhorse default for tabular, multivariate anomaly detection in practice: it trains fast (no gradient descent, just building random trees), needs very little hyperparameter tuning, and scales well to datasets with many features — a natural fit for a data-quality layer that needs to watch dozens of fields on every incoming record at once, well beyond what a handful of separate univariate rules could practically cover. Autoencoders are reached for when the data has structure a tree-based method handles less naturally — long sequences, images, or very high-dimensional embeddings — or when the practitioner wants a smoothly differentiable score that can itself be inspected (which input dimensions contributed most to the reconstruction error, a form of built-in diagnostic that a tree ensemble's score doesn't offer as directly).

In both cases, the standard workflow is the same: fit the model on a large window of data assumed to be mostly normal (both methods tolerate a small contamination fraction of undetected anomalies in the training set without much degradation), score new observations against that fitted model, and treat the resulting score as one input into a human review process — not as an automated accept/reject gate on its own, especially for anything consequential like flagging a trade or halting a feed.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where anomaly detection gets over-trusted

  • Reading "anomalous" as "wrong." The score measures statistical rarity relative to training data, not correctness. A genuinely valid but rare event (a real, sharp news-driven move) scores identically to a genuine data error — the score alone cannot distinguish them.
  • Reading "anomalous" as "an opportunity." Nothing about a high anomaly score implies the unusual pattern is profitable, predictable, or repeatable — it is a description of rarity relative to history, not a forecast of future edge.
  • Training on one regime and scoring another. Both methods learn "normal" from whatever data they were fit on. A model fit during a calm regime will flag an entire subsequent volatile-but-legitimate regime as one long anomaly, because it never saw anything like it before — not because anything is actually wrong.
  • Treating the score as calibrated probability. Isolation Forest's \(s(x,n)\) and an autoencoder's reconstruction error are both relative, model-specific quantities, not probabilities that a point is "truly" anomalous with some percent confidence.
  • Skipping the human-judgment step. What counts as "unusual enough to act on" is a threshold decision that depends entirely on context (what asset, what regime, what the downstream consequence of a false positive is) — the model surfaces a ranked list of candidates, it does not make that call.

Key Takeaways

✓Isolation Forest's core insight: anomalies are "few and different." Random recursive splitting isolates rare, separated points in fewer cuts than points buried in a dense normal cluster — the average path length across an ensemble of random trees is the anomaly score.
✓A shorter average path length signals an anomaly. \(s(x,n)=2^{-E[h(x)]/c(n)}\) pushes toward 1 as the path length shrinks relative to the ordinary baseline \(c(n)\) — few splits needed means the point sits apart from the crowd.
✓Autoencoders score anomalies by how badly they reconstruct. Trained mostly on normal data through a compressed bottleneck, the network reconstructs familiar patterns well and unfamiliar ones poorly — that reconstruction gap is the score.
✓Both are genuinely multivariate. They catch a bad combination of otherwise-individually-normal variables, closing a structural gap a per-series MAD threshold cannot.
✗An anomaly score is never "this is wrong" or "this is an opportunity." It is a measure of statistical rarity relative to training data, full stop — every downstream judgment about what "unusual" means here requires a human.

Section 05

Self-test

Q1. State Isolation Forest's core insight in one sentence, and explain why it means anomalies get isolated in fewer splits.
Anomalies are "few and different" — rare, and sitting apart from the dense region where most of the data lives. Because a random split is disproportionately likely to fall between an isolated point and the crowd, it takes only a handful of random cuts to wall off a point that's already separated from the normal cluster, while a normal point buried deep inside a dense group of similar points needs many more cuts before the whole surrounding crowd has been separated away from it.
Q2. Why does a shorter average path length E[h(x)] push Isolation Forest's score s(x,n) toward 1 rather than toward 0?
The score formula is \(s(x,n) = 2^{-E[h(x)]/c(n)}\). As \(E[h(x)]\) shrinks relative to the ordinary baseline \(c(n)\), the exponent \(-E[h(x)]/c(n)\) moves toward 0 (from a more negative number), and \(2^0=1\) is the maximum of that function — so a shorter-than-typical path length pushes the score up toward 1, the anomaly end of the scale. A path length close to \(c(n)\) (typical for an ordinary point) pushes the exponent toward \(-1\), giving a score around 0.5.
Q3. What forces an autoencoder to learn something other than the identity function, and why does that matter for anomaly detection?
The bottleneck layer is deliberately narrower (lower-dimensional) than the input, so the network physically cannot pass every input through unchanged — it has to choose which patterns to encode efficiently and which to approximate. Trained mostly on normal data, it becomes specifically good at compressing and rebuilding the patterns common in that training data, and stays comparatively bad at anything unfamiliar. That's exactly why reconstruction error on a new point is informative: it directly measures how well that point fits the patterns the bottleneck learned to prioritize.
Q4. Give a concrete example of a multivariate anomaly that a rolling MAD filter, applied separately to each variable, would completely miss.
A tick where the bid-ask spread, trade volume, and quote-update frequency each sit at an unremarkable percentile individually (say, 55th-65th) but whose specific joint combination has essentially never occurred together before — ordinarily wide spreads and heavy quote activity co-occur with clearly high or clearly low volume, not a bland middle value. Since no single variable crosses a per-series MAD threshold, a univariate filter run on each series separately flags nothing, even though the joint pattern is highly unusual — exactly the gap Isolation Forest and autoencoders are built to close.
Q5. Why can a model trained during a calm market regime flag an entire subsequent volatile-but-legitimate period as anomalous, and why isn't that necessarily a bug?
Both Isolation Forest and an autoencoder learn "normal" entirely from whatever data they were trained on. If that training window was calm, the model has never seen the joint patterns that show up in a genuinely different, more volatile — but entirely legitimate — regime, so those patterns look statistically rare to it, producing high anomaly scores throughout. This isn't a flaw in the arithmetic; it's the honest consequence of the method only ever being able to describe "unusual relative to what it was shown," which is exactly why the mandatory framing of this lesson insists a high score means "unusual," never "wrong."
