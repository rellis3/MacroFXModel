HAR-RV & Realized Volatility Forecasting

A single squared daily return is one noisy sample of that day's true variance. Realized variance — the sum of hundreds of intraday squared returns — is not. Corsi's HAR-RV model shows that forecasting it well takes nothing more exotic than an ordinary regression on three horizons: yesterday, the past week, and the past month.

CONCEPT — genuinely implemented and running live in this repo daily (as a challenger forecast), not yet the primary σ for any asset class — see Section 06 ~15 min read Prereqs: GARCH & Volatility Clustering and Garman-Klass & Yang-Zhang: OHLC Volatility Estimators
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • Realized variance (RV) sums many intraday squared returns over a day instead of relying on one squared close-to-close return — a large-sample average of a day's true variance instead of a single noisy draw of it.
  • HAR-RV (Corsi, 2009): \(RV_{t+1} = \beta_0 + \beta_d RV_t + \beta_w RV_t^{(w)} + \beta_m RV_t^{(m)} + \epsilon_{t+1}\) — RV's own average over the past day, week, and month, fit by plain OLS. No fractional-differencing machinery required.
  • Corsi's insight: traders operating at different horizons (daily, weekly, monthly) each contribute their own persistence to the aggregate market, and layering three ordinary linear regressors — one per horizon — approximates the market's genuine long-memory behavior almost as well as an actual fractionally-integrated model.
  • Contrast with the previous lesson: GARCH(1,1) forecasts variance from one noisy observation (yesterday's squared return) fed through a recursive filter; HAR-RV forecasts it directly from realized variance itself — a well-measured quantity, used as both target and regressor. When genuine intraday data exists, that's usually a meaningfully better forecast — not because the model is fancier (it's simpler: OLS versus GARCH's nonlinear MLE), but because the input is measured better.
  • This repo genuinely runs Corsi's exact daily/weekly/monthly OLS regression, live, every day, for every tracked instrument — as a shadow/challenger forecast that is actively benchmarked against the incumbent estimators, not (yet) the estimator that sets any asset class's live trading bands. The full finding, with file and function names, is in Section 06.
  • HAR-RV is not a free upgrade over GARCH or a range-based estimator: it needs a genuine intraday-derived RV series to be the model the literature actually validated. Feed it a single-observation daily proxy instead and you've quietly rebuilt something else.
Section 01

Why this matters

The previous lesson built GARCH(1,1) entirely out of daily closing prices: one return per day, squared, fed into a recursive filter. That's a real constraint, not a stylistic choice — a single squared return \(\epsilon_{t-1}^2\) is an extremely noisy way to estimate "how much variance did the market actually generate yesterday." Two trading days with identical true underlying variance can easily produce squared returns that differ by a factor of three or four purely from the luck of where the close happened to land relative to the day's actual path. GARCH's entire recursive structure — the floor, the reaction term, the inertia term — is built on top of that one noisy number, every single day.

If you have access to intraday prices, there's a direct fix, and it doesn't require a smarter model — it requires a better-measured target. Instead of one squared return per day, sum up hundreds of small squared intraday returns sampled throughout the session. Averaging many noisy observations of the same underlying quantity is one of the oldest tricks in statistics, and it works exactly as well here as anywhere else: the resulting daily variance estimate — realized variance (RV) — is dramatically less noisy than a single squared close-to-close return, for the same underlying day.

That improvement creates a new problem worth having: once you have a genuinely well-measured daily RV series, it turns out to behave differently than a single-return-based variance series does. Its autocorrelation decays far more slowly — a volatility shock's fingerprint lingers for a genuinely long time, longer than GARCH's clean geometric decay (persistence \(\alpha+\beta\), covered in the previous lesson) implies. This "long memory" pattern is one of the most robust empirical regularities in realized-volatility research, and modeling it precisely, the textbook way, calls for fractionally-integrated time-series models — genuinely more machinery than anything covered so far in this curriculum. Fischer Corsi's 2009 contribution was showing you don't actually need any of that: an ordinary linear regression on three cleverly chosen horizons reproduces the behavior almost as well, with nothing more exotic than OLS.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Why a day trader, a weekly rebalancer, and a monthly allocator disagree about "normal"

Picture three different desks watching the same market. A day trader cares almost entirely about how choppy today and yesterday have been — that's the horizon their positions actually live on. A portfolio manager who rebalances weekly cares more about how choppy the past week has been on average — a single wild day matters less to them than the week's overall character. A macro allocator who repositions monthly cares about the past month's average character more than any single day or week within it. None of these three is "wrong" about what volatility currently looks like — they're each answering the question at their own horizon, and all three answers are simultaneously true and simultaneously informative about where volatility is heading next.

Corsi's insight was to stop treating this as a nuisance and start treating it as the model. Instead of picking one horizon and fitting a single decay rate to it (which is what GARCH's \(\alpha+\beta\) does), HAR-RV just includes all three horizons as separate inputs to an ordinary regression, and lets the data decide how much weight each one deserves. The surprising empirical result is that this crude-sounding approach — three flat averages, no exotic math — tracks real markets' slow-decaying volatility memory about as well as models built specifically to have that property from the ground up.

Section 02

The math, step by step

Step 1 — realized variance, defined

Let day \(t\) be split into \(M\) equal intraday intervals (5-minute bars are a common choice — fine enough to capture most of a session's genuine movement, coarse enough that bid-ask bounce and other microstructure noise doesn't dominate the sum), and let \(r_{t,j}\) be the log return over the \(j\)-th of those intervals. Realized variance for day \(t\) is simply the sum of their squares:

\[ RV_t = \sum_{j=1}^{M} r_{t,j}^2 \]
RV_tday t's realized variance — a many-observation estimate, not a single squared return
r_{t,j}the j-th intraday log return within day t (e.g. one 5-minute bar's return)
Mnumber of intraday intervals per day (≈78 for 5-minute bars over a 6.5-hour session)

Compare this directly to what feeds a close-to-close variance estimate (GARCH's \(\epsilon_{t-1}^2\), or a rolling HV window): exactly one squared observation per day. \(RV_t\) sums \(M\) of them. This is not a different kind of estimator with different assumptions — it's the same idea (squared returns measure variance) applied at a much finer sampling rate, averaged up. As \(M\to\infty\) (subject to microstructure noise swamping the finest samples in practice — nobody actually pushes this to tick-by-tick), \(RV_t\) converges to the quadratic variation of the underlying price process over day \(t\): the formal object that measures exactly how much a continuous-time price path moved, jumps included. (The next lesson in this category, Bipower Variation, revisits this exact quantity and shows how to split it into a continuous part and a jump part — worth knowing the two lessons are talking about the same underlying object from different angles.)

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

If you wanted to know how bumpy a car ride was, you could ask "how big was the single biggest bump?" — that's one noisy number, and a smooth ride with one pothole would look identical to a bumpy ride that happened to have its worst moment measured badly. Or you could add up every little jolt felt the entire ride, second by second — that's realized variance. It uses far more information about the actual ride, so it's a much more honest answer to "how bumpy was today."

Step 2 — the long-memory puzzle

Once you build a genuine \(RV_t\) series from intraday data and look at its autocorrelation function, a distinctive pattern shows up: the correlation between \(RV_t\) and \(RV_{t-k}\) decays extremely slowly as the lag \(k\) grows — far more slowly than the clean geometric decay \((\alpha+\beta)^k\) a GARCH(1,1) process implies (the previous lesson's persistence and half-life derivation). This slow, hyperbolic-looking decay is the signature of long memory, and the textbook way to model it directly is a fractionally-integrated process (ARFIMA), which requires estimating a fractional differencing parameter \(d\) — a real, working piece of machinery, but meaningfully harder to estimate robustly and far less intuitive to explain than anything else in this curriculum so far.

Corsi's 2009 contribution, building on the earlier heterogeneous-market literature (Müller et al., 1997), is that you don't need to fit that fractional parameter at all to get most of the practical benefit. His argument: the reason realized volatility looks long-memoried in aggregate is that it's the superposition of genuinely different participants operating at genuinely different horizons — short-horizon traders, weekly rebalancers, monthly allocators — each contributing their own, much simpler persistence to the pooled market. Model each horizon explicitly with an ordinary linear regressor, and the sum of three simple pieces approximates the complicated aggregate almost as well as fitting the aggregate's own fractional parameter directly.

Step 3 — the HAR-RV model itself

Define the two rolling averages the model needs — the past week's and past month's average daily RV, each including the current day \(t\):

\[ RV_t^{(w)} = \frac{1}{5}\sum_{i=0}^{4} RV_{t-i}, \qquad RV_t^{(m)} = \frac{1}{22}\sum_{i=0}^{21} RV_{t-i} \]
RV_t^{(w)}average daily RV over the past 5 trading days (a trading week), including today
RV_t^{(m)}average daily RV over the past 22 trading days (a trading month), including today

The Heterogeneous Autoregressive model of Realized Volatility is then an ordinary linear regression of tomorrow's RV on today's value and both of these rolling averages:

\[ RV_{t+1} = \beta_0 + \beta_d RV_t + \beta_w RV_t^{(w)} + \beta_m RV_t^{(m)} + \epsilon_{t+1} \]
β_d · RV_t

β_w · RV_t^(w)

\(RV_t\)Today's realized variance — the daily/"fast" regressor.
\(RV_t^{(w)}\)Average RV over the past 5 trading days (including today) — the weekly regressor.
\(RV_t^{(m)}\)Average RV over the past 22 trading days (including today) — the monthly regressor.
\(\beta_0\)Constant/intercept term, in the same units as RV.
\(\beta_d, \beta_w, \beta_m\)The three OLS-fitted weights on the daily, weekly, and monthly regressors respectively.
\(\epsilon_{t+1}\)The regression residual — whatever tomorrow's RV isn't explained by the three horizon averages.

Read every term on the right-hand side carefully, because the entire point of the model is how ordinary they are: \(RV_t\), \(RV_t^{(w)}\), and \(RV_t^{(m)}\) are all directly observed historical averages — numbers you already have sitting in a spreadsheet the moment day \(t\) closes. There is no latent recursive state the way GARCH's \(\sigma_{t-1}^2\) term is (itself a forecast, feeding into the next forecast). Fitting \(\beta_0,\beta_d,\beta_w,\beta_m\) is literally an ordinary least-squares regression on three engineered features — the exact same estimation machinery used for any linear regression anywhere else in this curriculum. Nothing about fitting HAR-RV is volatility-specific; all the cleverness is in which three features to regress on.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

HAR-RV is like predicting tomorrow's mood by asking three questions: how was today, how has this whole week been, and how has this whole month been — then taking a weighted blend of the three answers. No fancy math is needed to combine three plain averages; you're just deciding how much to trust "today" versus "this week" versus "this month," and a regression is the standard tool for finding those weights from data.

Section 03

HAR-RV versus GARCH — same job, genuinely different inputs

Both models answer the same question — "how large should tomorrow's variance be?" — and both are fit to historical data and used to produce a one-step-ahead forecast. But they differ in exactly what they're allowed to look at, and that difference is the entire reason one typically outperforms the other when the choice is available:

GARCH(1,1) — one noisy observation, recursively filtered

\(\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha\epsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta\sigma_{t-1}^2\). The only piece of genuinely new information entering the forecast each day is \(\epsilon_{t-1}^2\) — a single squared close-to-close return. GARCH never looks inside the day; its entire picture of "yesterday's variance" is built from that one number, plus its own prior forecast. The model is mathematically elegant (a clean recursive filter, a well-understood stationarity condition, a closed- form long-run mean) — but its raw material each day is thin.

HAR-RV — a well-measured target, used as its own regressor

\(RV_{t+1} = \beta_0 + \beta_d RV_t + \beta_w RV_t^{(w)} + \beta_m RV_t^{(m)}\). Both the target being forecast and the regressors doing the forecasting are realized variance — a quantity built, when genuine intraday data exists, from hundreds of observations per day rather than one. The model itself is plainer than GARCH (three linear terms, fit by OLS, no recursion, no nonlinear likelihood) — the sophistication moved from the model to the input.

The improvement is in the measurement, not the machinery. When genuine high-frequency data is available, HAR-RV typically forecasts a given day's variance meaningfully better than GARCH(1,1) — but the honest reason is that its raw material is better-measured, not that its model is more sophisticated. It's the opposite: GARCH is the more mathematically intricate of the two (a nonlinear recursive filter fit by maximum likelihood), and HAR-RV is literally an OLS regression on three engineered features. Better data, simpler model, usually a better forecast — a genuinely useful lesson that "more sophisticated model" and "better forecast" are not the same axis.

One more structural contrast worth noting explicitly, since it will matter again in the pitfalls section: \(\beta_d+\beta_w+\beta_m\) plays a loose visual echo of GARCH's persistence \(\alpha+\beta\) — both are sums of coefficients on lagged variance-like terms, and both being comfortably below 1 (illustrated below at 0.96) keeps the model's response to a persistent elevated regime bounded rather than extrapolating forever. But it is not the same object mathematically: GARCH's \(\alpha+\beta<1\) is a formal stationarity condition derived from the model's own recursive definition (Section 02 of the previous lesson); HAR-RV is a static linear regression on already-realized values with no recursion at all, so nothing forces \(\beta_d+\beta_w+\beta_m\) below any particular threshold — it's simply a useful sanity check on a fitted (or hand-set) set of coefficients, not a mathematical requirement the way it is for GARCH.

Worked example

Forecasting day 23's realized variance from 22 days of history

Suppose you have 22 trading days of daily realized volatility \(\sigma_t\) (percent, so \(RV_t = \sigma_t^2\) is in units of percent-squared) — each day could plausibly have come from genuine intraday RV, or from the previous lesson's Garman-Klass/Yang-Zhang range-based estimators standing in as a daily variance proxy when only OHLC bars are available (a substitution the pitfall below returns to). Days 10–14 carry a volatility cluster — a plausible shock and its partial decay, exactly the pattern the GARCH lesson's worked example traced with a single event. The baseline \(\sigma\approx0.60\%\) annualizes to about 9.5%; the cluster's peak \(\sigma=1.20\%\) annualizes to about 19% — the same order-of-magnitude gap this project's own research (Section 06) found between an understated range-based proxy and a true intraday-measured realized volatility for an equity index.

The weekly term — average of days 18–22

Summing all 22 RV values gives 13.1158; \(RV_t^{(m)} = 13.1158/22 = 0.5962\) — the highest of the three, because the monthly window still fully contains the cluster's peak (days 11–13), which the 5-day window has already left behind.

4

Combine with illustrative Corsi-style coefficients

Using commonly-cited ballpark published coefficients — \(\beta_0=0.03\), \(\beta_d=0.35\), \(\beta_w=0.41\), \(\beta_m=0.20\) (illustrative figures in the spirit of Corsi's 2009 estimates, not fitted to this 22-day toy series, which is far too short to fit four parameters reliably):

\[ RV_{23} = 0.03 + 0.35(0.3969) + 0.41(0.4387) + 0.20(0.5962) = 0.03+0.1389+0.1799+0.1192 = 0.4680 \]

\(\sigma_{23} = \sqrt{0.4680} \approx 0.684\%\) daily, or about \(0.684\%\times\sqrt{252}\approx10.86\%\) annualized.

Compare this to the naive "same as yesterday" forecast, which simply carries \(RV_t=0.3969\) forward: \(\sigma\approx0.63\%\), about 10.00% annualized. HAR-RV forecasts noticeably higher — 10.86% versus 10.00% — because it hasn't forgotten what the naive forecast has: the weekly and especially the monthly average are still elevated from the shock, even though the single most recent day already looks calm again. That's the entire practical case for HAR-RV in one number: a naive forecast that only looks at yesterday can be fooled by one calm day right after a cluster; HAR-RV's longer-horizon terms keep that memory alive for longer, exactly matching the slow-decay behavior Section 02 described.

Section 06

Is HAR-RV actually built here? Yes — as a live daily challenger, not (yet) the primary

Before writing this lesson's status badge, this repo's actual volatility codebase was read directly rather than assumed. The finding corrects the default this curriculum otherwise applies to a freshly-covered model ("concept — not built or tested anywhere in this repo"): HAR-RV is genuinely implemented, and it runs live, every day, for every tracked instrument — but as a research challenger sitting alongside the platform's actual live estimators, not as the estimator driving anyone's trading bands.

📐

The exact regression, implemented

js/volForecastBench.js's harRvPred() is Corsi's model verbatim: predict \(RV_i\) from \([1, RV_{i-1}, \text{mean}(RV_{i-5..i-1}), \text{mean}(RV_{i-22..i-1})]\), fit by an expanding-window walk-forward OLS (a strictly causal 4×4 normal-equations solve, solve4()) — training pairs are folded in only after each target day completes, so no forecast ever sees its own outcome. A second, near-identical implementation lives in js/volEstimatorAB.js's harSigmaSeries(), used to A/B HAR-RV against the platform's Yang-Zhang estimator directly on real intraday data.

🔁

Computed live, every trading day

js/volForecastScheduler.js's daily runVolForecast() job — the scheduler that produces this project's actual daily vol forecast — computes a full HAR-RV shadow forecast (σ, plus the same High-Low/Open-Close bands the primary forecast gets) for every one of its ~28 instruments and stores it as f.har alongside the primary numbers, via harShadowFields() in js/forecastExport.js. It even has its own export format (buildExportHarText(), "VOL & RANGE FORECAST — HAR-RV").

🏁

Raced against 7 other estimators

js/volHorseRaceEngine.js's volHorseRace(), wired to a live endpoint (/api/vol-horse-race/run in server.js), scores HAR-RV against Random Walk, EWMA, rolling HV, Yang-Zhang, GARCH(1,1), and two combination models (a fixed 50/50 blend and a Granger-Ramanathan-optimal blend) by out-of-sample QLIKE on real intraday data — and explicitly tracks, per instrument, whether HAR-RV beats whatever estimator that asset class currently uses live (harBeatsPlatform).

🔬

A genuine-intraday-RV variant for indices

js/indexRvHar.js's rvHarSigma() runs the same forward-HAR machinery on realized variance built from the true 5-minute intraday path (not a range-based proxy), specifically because the earlier range-based HAR shadow understated index volatility by roughly half. It's wired to a live, daily-cached endpoint (/api/vol-forecast/rv-har) that feeds the index chart export.

What "live" does not mean here: HAR-RV is not the platform's primary σ for any asset class. Per volHorseRaceEngine.js's own PLATFORM_MODEL mapping, the estimator that actually sets live trading bands today is Yang-Zhang for FX, GARCH(1,1) for equity indices, and rolling close-to-close HV for commodities/gold/BTC — HAR-RV sits beside all three as an actively-tested challenger (f.har, additive, never touching the primary numbers) rather than replacing any of them. The horse race itself is a genuinely live computation over current market data, not a hardcoded verdict frozen into the code — this lesson deliberately does not report a fixed "HAR-RV wins N% of the time" number, because no such fixed number is stored anywhere in the codebase; the engine is built to answer that question fresh, instrument by instrument, exactly the discipline the rest of this curriculum insists on for any specific-signal claim.

Two concrete, documented findings from this project's own code are worth citing directly, because both are exactly the kind of nuance a purely theoretical treatment of HAR-RV would miss: indexRvHar.js's own header records that the earlier Garman-Klass-range-based HAR shadow "assumes no gap," missing overnight gaps and the intraday path for equity indices — understating SPX's true volatility by roughly half (about 9.5% read versus a true reading near 19%), which is precisely why the genuine-intraday-RV variant above was built. Separately, js/ccHvSigma.js's header notes that in gold's regime, HAR-RV — "a smoothed forward forecast that reads low in an elevated regime" — sat roughly 15% under a simpler close-to-close estimator during one comparison. Neither finding says HAR-RV is broken; both say exactly what Section 03 above already implied: HAR-RV's quality is only as good as the RV series feeding it, and a smoothed, averaged forecast will structurally read low right as a fresh regime shift is still under way — the same lag-versus-reactivity tradeoff the GARCH lesson raised about flat trailing windows, one level up the sophistication ladder.

Section 07

How practitioners actually use this

In production, HAR-RV is virtually always re-fit walk-forward on an expanding or rolling window — never fit once on a historical sample and frozen, since the relationship between the three horizons genuinely drifts as market structure changes. Because the three regressors are strongly collinear (a calm month rarely contains a wild week, and vice versa), naive OLS on a short or ill-conditioned window can produce unstable individual coefficients even when the combined forecast is fine — this project's own harSigmaSeries() (in js/volEstimatorAB.js) adds a small relative Tikhonov ridge specifically to keep that normal-equations solve well-behaved, a detail worth knowing if you ever implement this yourself rather than assuming plain OLS is the whole story.

HAR-RV also composes naturally with other information. A well-known extension, HAR-IV, adds a forward-looking implied-volatility regressor (from a listed vol index like the VIX or GVZ) alongside the three realized-variance terms — implied vol embeds a variance risk premium and genuinely helps forecast realized vol on top of HAR's own terms, an idea this repo's own harIvPred() (also in volForecastBench.js) implements directly for gold and equity indices. And rather than picking a single winning estimator outright, it's common to blend forecasts — a fixed 50/50 average of HAR-RV and GARCH, or an OLS-learned optimal blend (Granger-Ramanathan regression of realized vol on both forecasts, using only past data) — exactly the "Combo-5050" and "Combo-optimal" entries this repo's own horse race scores alongside the individual models.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where HAR-RV quietly stops being HAR-RV

It needs genuine intraday data to be the model the literature validated. HAR-RV's entire advantage over GARCH rests on RV being a well-measured, many-observation quantity. If all you have is daily OHLC bars, you cannot compute true realized variance — you're back to needing a range-based estimator (Parkinson, Garman-Klass, Yang-Zhang — the previous lesson) or a recursive model like GARCH instead. Running the exact same three-regressor HAR regression on a single-observation daily proxy isn't a free upgrade; it's a different, untested estimator that happens to share Corsi's equation, and this repo's own indexRvHar.js exists specifically because that substitution understated index volatility by roughly half.

The three horizon regressors are strongly collinear. A calm week rarely sits inside a wild month, and vice versa — the daily, weekly, and monthly RV averages move together far more than three "independent" features normally would. This doesn't necessarily hurt the combined forecast much, but it can make the individual \(\beta_d,\beta_w,\beta_m\) estimates unstable or counter-intuitive on a short or noisy fitting window; a small ridge penalty (as this project's own implementation uses) is a standard, pragmatic fix.

A smoothed average forecast structurally lags a sharp regime change. Because HAR-RV blends three different lookback windows, it will, by construction, read below a simpler current-only estimator right as volatility is spiking, and can read below reality for a while as an elevated regime persists — the documented ~15% gap against a plain close-to-close estimate in this project's own gold research (Section 06) is exactly that effect, not a bug. A smoothing model buys stability by trading away some day-one reactivity; know which one your use case actually needs.

"HAR-RV beats GARCH" is not a universal law. It's an empirical finding that holds when genuine high-frequency data is available and well-behaved — this project's own horse race exists precisely because that ranking is an empirical question to re-check per instrument and asset class, not an assumption to bake in from the math alone. A strong general result about one dataset or asset class is not automatically a strong result everywhere else.

Section 09

Self-test

Q1. Why is realized variance (RV) a meaningfully better estimate of a given day's true variance than a single squared close-to-close return?
A single squared daily return is one noisy observation of that day's variance — two days with identical true variance can produce very different squared returns purely from where the close happened to land. RV sums \(M\) squared intraday returns sampled throughout the day, so it averages many observations of the same underlying quantity instead of relying on one — the standard statistical benefit of a larger sample, applied to variance estimation. As sampling frequency increases, RV converges toward the quadratic variation of the price process over that day.
Q2. Write the HAR-RV regression equation and define every term.
\(RV_{t+1} = \beta_0 + \beta_d RV_t + \beta_w RV_t^{(w)} + \beta_m RV_t^{(m)} + \epsilon_{t+1}\). \(RV_{t+1}\) is the forecast target (next day's realized variance); \(RV_t\) is today's own RV; \(RV_t^{(w)}\) is the average RV over the past 5 trading days including today; \(RV_t^{(m)}\) is the average RV over the past 22 trading days including today; \(\beta_0\) is a constant; \(\beta_d,\beta_w,\beta_m\) are the OLS-fitted weights on the three horizons; \(\epsilon_{t+1}\) is the regression residual.
Q3. What was Corsi's key argument for why three ordinary linear regressors can approximate genuine long-memory behavior, without fitting a fractional-differencing parameter?
Different classes of market participants operate at genuinely different horizons — daily traders, weekly rebalancers, monthly allocators — and each contributes its own, comparatively simple persistence to the aggregate market. Modeling each horizon explicitly with its own linear regressor and summing them approximates the complicated slow-decaying aggregate almost as well as fitting a fractionally-integrated model's own \(d\) parameter directly, without needing that harder-to-estimate machinery.
Q4. GARCH(1,1) and HAR-RV both forecast next-period variance. What is the key structural difference in what each model is allowed to see?
GARCH(1,1) only ever sees one new piece of information per day — yesterday's squared close-to-close return, \(\epsilon_{t-1}^2\) — combined recursively with its own prior forecast \(\sigma_{t-1}^2\); it never looks inside the trading day. HAR-RV instead uses realized variance — built, when genuine intraday data exists, from hundreds of observations per day — as both the forecast target and the regressors. HAR-RV's typical edge comes from that better-measured input, not from a more sophisticated model; the HAR-RV regression itself (plain OLS on three linear terms) is simpler than GARCH's nonlinear recursive filter.
Q5. In the worked example, why does HAR-RV's forecast (10.86% annualized) come out higher than a naive "same as yesterday" forecast (10.00% annualized), even though the single most recent day's RV had already reverted close to the pre-shock baseline?
The naive forecast only looks at \(RV_t\), which by day 22 had already decayed back near baseline. HAR-RV's weekly and especially monthly averages, however, still fully or partially contain the shock cluster from days 10–14, pulling the blended forecast higher. This is exactly the practical benefit of the longer horizons: they keep the memory of an elevated regime alive for longer than looking only at the most recent single day would, matching the slow-decay ("long memory") behavior real realized-volatility series exhibit.
Q6. What has to be true of your data before HAR-RV is a legitimate upgrade over GARCH or a range-based estimator, and what happens when it isn't?
You need genuine intraday (high-frequency) price data to compute true realized variance in the first place — HAR-RV's advantage rests entirely on RV being a well-measured, many-observation quantity. With only daily OHLC bars available, true RV can't be computed at all; running the same three-regressor equation on a single-observation daily proxy (like a squared close-to-close return, or naively treating a range-based estimator's output as RV) is not the model the literature validated — this project's own code documents exactly that failure mode understating index volatility by roughly half before a genuine-intraday-RV variant was built to fix it.
