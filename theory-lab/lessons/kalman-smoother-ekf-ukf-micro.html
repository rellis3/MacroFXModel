Hindsight, and Curves the Straight Math Can't See

The plain Kalman filter runs one way only — forward, using data up through right now, never anything after it. Two upgrades sit on top of that same machinery. The first, a smoother, waits until an entire dataset exists and then sharpens every past estimate using data the live filter never had. The second and third, the Extended and Unscented Kalman filters, handle a state-space model that isn't linear — one by drawing a tangent line at every step, the other by sampling the true curve directly and skipping the tangent line altogether.

Section 1

The filter's blind spot: it can't look ahead

Nothing is wrong with the plain Kalman filter — it's doing exactly what a live system should. But "live" has a cost.

→

Forward-only, by construction

The estimate at time t is the best guess using data through t only — the predict/update recursion never runs backward.

🔒

Every past estimate is locked in

Once the filter moves from day 5 to day 6, day 5's number is final. It's never revisited, even if day 6 through day 200 later make it look slightly off.

✓

Exactly right for a live system

A trader acting today genuinely doesn't have tomorrow's data yet. Forward-only isn't a flaw here — it's the only honest option.

⏳

Needlessly conservative for history

For a dataset that's already fully recorded, refusing to use the later rows to improve an earlier estimate throws away information that's sitting right there on disk.

Picture a boat's position, tracked once per radar ping, live, through the night. The crew's 2am estimate could only use pings received by 2am — that's the filter. But a logbook written the next morning has the whole night's pings sitting in front of it. If the 2am position looks inconsistent with where the boat clearly had to be by 4am, the 2am entry deserves revising — using data the crew never had, in hindsight.
Section 2

The smoother's fix: run the filter forward, then walk it back

The RTS (Rauch–Tung–Striebel) smoother adds one backward pass after the forward filter has already finished.

Two passes, in order
Forward filter
t = 1 → T
then, once T is fully known →
Backward smoother
t = T−1 → 1
The forward pass stores both the filtered values (x, P at t|t) and the one-step-ahead predicted values (x, P at t+1|t) at every step — the backward pass needs both to run.
C

C — the smoother gain

C(t) = P(t|t) ÷ P(t+1|t). A ratio of how uncertain this step's filtered estimate was to how uncertain the very next step's predicted estimate was — it controls how strongly this step gets pulled toward what the future revealed.

x

x(t|T) — the smoothed estimate

Uses all T observations, not just the first t. Computed backward, one step at a time, using the already-smoothed value one step ahead of it.

P

P(t|T) — the smoothed variance

Always ≤ the filtered variance P(t|t). More data bearing on the same past moment can only sharpen an estimate, never make it worse.

The recursion starts from a simple boundary condition: the very last time step has no future left to fold in, so its smoothed estimate is identical to its filtered one — x(T|T). Every step before that pulls partway toward the already-smoothed step just ahead of it, by an amount set by C.

By hand: revising yesterday, using what today revealed

Reusing the plain Kalman filter lesson's own two-day hedge-ratio track, unmodified.

x(1|2) = 1.321 + 0.899 × (1.311 − 1.321) ≈ 1.312.   P(1|2) = 0.0080 + 0.899² × (0.0047 − 0.0089) ≈ 0.0046.

The filtered Day 1 estimate, 1.321, was the best guess a live trader actually had at the end of Day 1 — using only Day 1's data. The smoothed Day 1 estimate, 1.312, is slightly lower, because Day 2's observation (1.30, below Day 1's estimate) retroactively pulls the Day 1 read down a little once it's known. The smoothed uncertainty (≈0.0046) is also noticeably lower than the filtered uncertainty (0.0080) — Day 1 really is better known in hindsight.

Where this needs qualifying

Neither of the smoothed numbers above was available to a live trader at the end of Day 1 — both require Day 2's observation. A smoothed estimate must never be treated as something a live system could have produced at the time, in a backtest or anywhere else.

Section 5

EKF: draw a tangent line, then run the same linear math

The Extended Kalman Filter keeps the plain filter's entire predict/update skeleton — it only swaps in a local linear stand-in for a nonlinear f or h.

✂

Linearize at every single step

The Jacobian — in one dimension, just an ordinary derivative — is the slope of the true curve, evaluated fresh at the current best estimate, every step.

=

The mean stays exact

The predicted state and predicted observation both run through the true, unaltered nonlinear f and h — no approximation there at all.

≈

Only the uncertainty is approximated

P is propagated as if the system were linear with the Jacobian's slope — accurate right at the touch point, worse the further the true curve strays from it.

$

Cheap: one derivative per step

That mismatch — exact mean, approximate covariance — is the entire source of the EKF's known weakness: overconfidence when the curve bends away faster than P is narrow.

Imagine predicting a hilly trail using only a ruler laid flat against the ground exactly where you're standing. Right at your feet, the ruler matches the trail perfectly. A few steps ahead, the trail curves away from that flat ruler — maybe it climbs a hill the ruler has no idea is coming. The EKF re-lays that ruler fresh at every single step, so it's never wildly wrong for long — but it can still misjudge how steep the trail gets between one step and the next.
Section 6

UKF: send scouts down the real curve instead

The Unscented Kalman Filter keeps the same skeleton too — but replaces the tangent-line step entirely.

•

A few deterministic sigma points

For a scalar state (L=1), just three: the current estimate itself, and one point pushed to either side by an amount set by the variance and a chosen scaling constant λ.

→

Pushed through the TRUE function

Every sigma point walks the real, unaltered nonlinear h — no straight-line approximation anywhere in this step.

Σ

Reconstruct mean/variance from where they land

A weighted average of the transformed points rebuilds the predicted observation and its variance — no Jacobian required, ever.

$$

Costs more, tolerates more curvature

2L+1 function evaluations per step instead of one, plus λ (and its relatives α, β, κ in the fuller formulation) to choose — but generally more accurate once curvature is strong.

Instead of trusting one ruler laid down at your feet, send out three actual scouts to walk the real trail — one right where you're standing, one a bit ahead, one a bit behind — and let each report exactly what they found, hills and all, with no ruler involved. Then average their honest reports. It costs more legwork than reading one ruler, but nobody had to pretend the trail was flat anywhere.

Section 9

When to reach for each

①

EKF — the default first move

Cheapest: one Jacobian per step. Fine when the nonlinearity is mild and f/h are smooth. Can fail — badly, and silently, as an understated P — when curvature is strong relative to the uncertainty band, or when f/h isn't even differentiable (a kink, a discontinuity).

②

UKF — reach here next

No Jacobian required at all, and generally more accurate once a single tangent line stops being a trustworthy local stand-in. Costs 2L+1 function evaluations per step instead of one, plus a scaling constant to choose.

③

Particle filter — the fallback

From the previous lesson. Reach for it when the nonlinearity is severe enough that neither a tangent line nor a handful of sigma points capture it, or when the noise itself is genuinely non-Gaussian or multimodal — a shape neither EKF nor UKF's Gaussian-style reconstruction can represent.

Escalate in that order. Each step up trades more compute for more fidelity to the true, ugly shape of the problem. Reaching for a full particle swarm to solve a mildly curved, single-humped problem is over-engineering; reaching for one tangent line to solve a genuinely multimodal one will just be wrong.
Section 10

Where this actually stands

65

RTS — Rauch, Tung & Striebel, 1965

The standard tool whenever a state-space model is fit to historical data for reporting or research, rather than run live.

🚀

EKF flew to the moon

Part of the Apollo program's guidance computer in the 1960s. Still the default nonlinear Kalman extension across robotics and aerospace today.

97

UKF — Julier & Uhlmann, 1997

Reached for once the EKF's linearization visibly degrades performance, or when a Jacobian isn't convenient to derive in closed form.

Section 11

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Why can't a live trading system produce a smoothed estimate for "right now"?

A smoothed estimate x(t|T) is defined using data through T, which is after t. A live system at time t simply doesn't have that data yet — it's not a limitation of the math, it's a limitation of time itself.
Question 2

In the worked example, why is the smoothed Day 1 variance (≈0.0046) lower than the filtered Day 1 variance (0.0080)?

The smoothed variance additionally incorporates Day 2's observation — and extra information about the same underlying moment can only reduce uncertainty about it, which is exactly why P(t|T) ≤ P(t|t) always.
Question 3

What exactly does the EKF linearize, and what does it leave exact?

The predicted state and observation both use the true nonlinear functions directly — no approximation there. Only P is propagated as if the system were linear with the Jacobian's slope, which is the entire source of the EKF's known failure mode.
Question 4

Why does the UKF need no Jacobian at all?

Every sigma point walks the real function with no approximation at all — the only approximation left is reconstructing a mean/variance from a handful of exact points, which needs no derivative anywhere.
Question 5

A relationship is severely nonlinear AND the noise is genuinely multimodal. Which tool from this curriculum fits best?

Both EKF and UKF reconstruct a single Gaussian-shaped belief at every step. A genuinely multimodal posterior needs the particle filter's full weighted swarm to represent it at all.
Section 12

What to carry forward

01

The plain Kalman filter is forward-only — each estimate uses only data through that point in time, never anything after it.

02

The RTS smoother adds one backward pass, after the forward filter finishes, using the whole dataset to sharpen every past estimate — never valid as a live, real-time signal.

03

The EKF linearizes f and h via a Jacobian at every step, then runs the exact same linear predict/update math — exact mean propagation, approximate covariance propagation.

04

The UKF skips linearization entirely — it pushes a few deterministic sigma points through the true nonlinear function and reconstructs the mean/variance from where they land.

05

EKF is cheaper and the default first move for mild, smooth nonlinearity; UKF costs more but tolerates stronger curvature and needs no Jacobian at all.

06

When nonlinearity is severe, or the noise is genuinely non-Gaussian or multimodal, escalate further still — to the particle filter from the previous lesson.

What this now lets you do

Recognize the difference between a live filtered estimate and a hindsight-informed smoothed one, and never mistake one for the other in a backtest or research note. Reach for the EKF as a cheap first step against mild nonlinearity, escalate to the UKF once a single tangent line stops being trustworthy, and know when even that isn't enough — and the particle filter is the honest next step.
