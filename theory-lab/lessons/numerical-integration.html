Numerical Integration

When a price is genuinely an area under a curve that can't be found in closed form — the deterministic grid methods that nail it in one or two dimensions, and the reframe that reveals Monte Carlo was secretly doing the same job all along.

CONCEPT — not built or tested in this repo ~16 min read Prereqs: Linear & Quadratic Programming (loosely — mainly needs calculus prerequisites)
TL;DR
  • Many prices and expectations that matter in finance are, mathematically, an area under a curve — and plenty of those curves have no closed-form antiderivative, so the area has to be estimated numerically instead of solved for algebraically.
  • The trapezoidal rule approximates the area as a sum of trapezoids under straight-line segments; its error shrinks with the square of the step size, \(O(h^2)\).
  • Simpson's rule fits a parabola through every three consecutive points instead of a straight line — for smooth functions, its error shrinks with the step size's fourth power, \(O(h^4)\), a dramatically better rate for the same number of points.
  • Monte Carlo integration is a genuine reframe, not a different idea: instead of a deterministic grid, sample points randomly and average the function's value at them — it estimates the same area under the same curve, just by a different route.
  • Deterministic quadrature (trapezoidal, Simpson's) is far more accurate in 1-2 dimensions, but its cost explodes exponentially as dimensions are added — the "curse of dimensionality." Monte Carlo's error shrinks at a fixed \(1/\sqrt{N}\) rate regardless of dimension, which is exactly why high-dimensional option pricing uses simulation, not a grid.
  • The worked example integrates the standard normal PDF from 0 to 1 with the trapezoidal rule using only 4 intervals, and checks the answer against the exact value \(\Phi(1)-\Phi(0)\approx0.3413\).
Section 01

Why this matters

A price, a probability, or an expected value is very often, underneath the finance vocabulary, exactly the same calculus object: the area under some curve, or equivalently an integral. "What's the probability this variable lands between two values" is the area under its density curve between those two points. "What's the fair value of this option" is, in the risk-neutral pricing framework, an expectation — an integral of the payoff against a probability density. When that density or that payoff is a well-behaved standard shape (lognormal, and a vanilla European option payoff), calculus gives a closed-form answer directly, which is exactly why the Black-Scholes formula, elsewhere in this curriculum, is a clean algebraic expression rather than a number a computer has to grind out. The moment either piece stops being that simple — a genuinely exotic payoff, a distribution with no closed-form CDF — the integral has no algebraic shortcut left, and has to be estimated numerically instead. This lesson covers the two families of tool that do that: deterministic quadrature, which lays down a fixed grid and adds up the pieces, and Monte Carlo, which samples randomly instead — and are, on inspection, both answering the exact same kind of question.

Plain-English intuition

Slicing the area under a curve into shapes you already know how to measure

An integral — the area under a curve between two points — is easy for a shape with straight edges (a rectangle, a triangle, a trapezoid) and genuinely hard for a curve with no algebraic shortcut. The oldest trick in numerical integration is to stop trying to measure the curved area directly and instead chop it into thin vertical strips, each one narrow enough that the curve barely bends within it — narrow enough that a straight line (or, better, a gentle parabola) drawn across the top of each strip is a very good stand-in for the true curve there. Measuring the area of a strip with a straight or parabolic top is ordinary geometry; add up all the strips' areas and you have a very good approximation to the true area under the whole curve — provably better the narrower the strips get. That's the entire idea behind the trapezoidal rule and Simpson's rule: replace one hard problem (the exact curved area) with many easy ones (lots of straight-edged or parabola-topped pieces) and add them up.

Monte Carlo integration throws out the grid of strips entirely and replaces it with something that sounds, at first, like a completely different idea: throw random darts at the region under the curve (or, more precisely, sample random points along the horizontal axis and measure the curve's height at each one), and average what you get. It turns out this is mathematically doing exactly the same job — estimating an average height, then multiplying by a width, is precisely what an integral is — just replacing an evenly-spaced, deterministic set of sample points with a randomly-chosen one. The payoff for that randomness, covered below, is that its accuracy doesn't care how many dimensions the problem has — a genuinely different, and often decisive, practical advantage over a fixed grid.

Section 02

The math, step by step

The trapezoidal rule

To approximate \(\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\), split \([a,b]\) into \(n\) equal strips of width \(h = (b-a)/n\), at points \(x_0=a, x_1, \dots, x_n=b\). Within each strip, replace the true curve with the straight line connecting \((x_i, f(x_i))\) to \((x_{i+1}, f(x_{i+1}))\) — the area of that strip is then exactly the area of a trapezoid, average height times width:

\[ \text{Area}_i \approx \frac{f(x_i) + f(x_{i+1})}{2} \cdot h \]

Summing every strip and noting each interior point is shared by two adjacent trapezoids (so it's counted twice, while the two endpoints are counted once) gives the composite trapezoidal rule:

\[ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx \;\approx\; \frac{h}{2}\Big[f(x_0) + 2f(x_1) + 2f(x_2) + \cdots + 2f(x_{n-1}) + f(x_n)\Big] \]
h=(b−a)/nthe step size — narrower strips, smaller h, better approximation
f(x₀), f(xₙ)the two endpoints — counted once, not doubled
2f(xᵢ)every interior point — shared by two neighboring trapezoids, so doubled

The trapezoidal rule's error — how far the approximation sits from the true integral — shrinks proportionally to \(h^2\) (equivalently, proportionally to \(1/n^2\)): halving the step size, i.e. doubling the number of strips, cuts the error to roughly a quarter of what it was. This follows from how well a straight line approximates a smooth curve over a short interval — the same tangent-line-approximation logic used for Newton-Raphson's quadratic convergence in an earlier lesson in this category, applied here to an interval instead of a single point.

Simpson's rule — fitting parabolas instead of straight lines

A straight line is the simplest possible curve to fit through two points. Simpson's rule uses one more point per piece and fits a parabola through every three consecutive points instead — since a parabola can bend to match a genuinely curving function far better than a straight line can, this typically gives a dramatically more accurate approximation for the same number of evaluation points, at essentially no extra conceptual cost. Requiring an even number of strips \(n\) (so points can be grouped in consecutive pairs, each pair plus the shared endpoint forming one parabola-fitted panel), the composite rule is:

\[ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx \;\approx\; \frac{h}{3}\Big[f(x_0) + 4f(x_1) + 2f(x_2) + 4f(x_3) + \cdots + 4f(x_{n-1}) + f(x_n)\Big] \]
h/3Simpson's rule's own leading constant, replacing the trapezoidal rule's h/2
4f(x_odd)points at odd-numbered positions get weight 4
2f(x_even)interior points at even-numbered positions get weight 2

The alternating 4-2-4-2…-4 weighting pattern (with the two endpoints weighted 1) is exactly what falls out of fitting and integrating a parabola through each successive triple of points; it isn't an arbitrary convention. The payoff for the extra structure is a much better error rate: Simpson's rule's error shrinks proportionally to \(h^4\) — halving the step size cuts the error to roughly a sixteenth of what it was, a dramatically faster improvement than the trapezoidal rule's \(h^2\) rate for smooth, well-behaved functions (a function with a kink or a discontinuity inside the interval loses much of this advantage, since the parabola fit assumes local smoothness that a kink violates).

Trapezoidal rule

Straight lines between consecutive points — error shrinks with h² as the strips narrow.

Simpson's rule

Parabolas fitted through each triple of points hug the true curve far more closely — error shrinks with h⁴ for smooth functions.

Monte Carlo integration — the same job, sampled instead of gridded

Recall that an expectation of a function \(g\) of a random variable \(X\) with density \(p(x)\) is itself an integral:

\[ E[g(X)] = \int g(x)\, p(x)\, dx \]

The law of large numbers (from the Probability & Random Variables primer elsewhere in this curriculum) says that averaging many independent draws of \(g(X)\) converges to this same expectation as the number of draws grows. That gives an entirely different-looking recipe for estimating the same integral: draw \(N\) independent samples \(x_1, \dots, x_N\) from the distribution \(p\), evaluate \(g\) at each, and average:

\[ \int g(x)\,p(x)\,dx \;\approx\; \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} g(x_i) \]
xᵢ ~ pN independent random draws from the distribution, not an evenly spaced grid
g(xᵢ)the function evaluated at each random draw
(1/N)Σg(xᵢ)the sample average — the Monte Carlo estimate of the true integral

This is the exact same machinery as the Monte Carlo simulation covered in the Bootstrapping & Monte Carlo lesson elsewhere in this curriculum — generate many synthetic draws and average a quantity of interest across them — reframed here as a general-purpose numerical integration method in its own right, not tied to any particular financial simulation. Whether you call it "simulating an option's expected discounted payoff" or "Monte Carlo integrating the payoff against the risk-neutral density" is purely a matter of vocabulary; the arithmetic is identical.

The error of a Monte Carlo estimate — by the Central Limit Theorem, again from the Probability primer — shrinks proportionally to \(1/\sqrt{N}\), regardless of the dimension of \(x\). That single fact is the crux of the whole comparison with deterministic quadrature, developed fully next.

The curse of dimensionality

Trapezoidal and Simpson's rule, as derived above, are one-dimensional recipes: lay down \(n\) points along a single axis. Extending either method to \(d\) dimensions (needed the moment a payoff depends on more than one underlying) means laying down a grid — roughly \(n\) points along every dimension, for a total of \(n^d\) function evaluations to cover the whole space. That exponential blow-up in the exponent \(d\) is the curse of dimensionality:

A basket option on ten correlated underlyings, or a portfolio-level risk calculation across ten risk factors, is a genuinely ten-dimensional integral — and a grid fine enough to be useful in one dimension (10 points) becomes ten billion evaluations in ten dimensions, utterly infeasible to compute. Monte Carlo's \(1/\sqrt{N}\) error rate, by contrast, does not carry a hidden dependence on \(d\) at all — drawing \(N\) random points in ten dimensions costs exactly the same \(N\) function evaluations as drawing \(N\) random points in one dimension (the points themselves simply have more coordinates; generating and evaluating them isn't intrinsically harder). This is precisely why every high-dimensional pricing problem in practice — basket options, multi-asset portfolio risk, path-dependent payoffs sampled at many time steps (each time step effectively adding a dimension) — is priced with Monte Carlo simulation, never a deterministic grid, while genuinely low-dimensional problems (pricing a single vanilla option under a nonstandard 1D distribution, say) are usually better served by quadrature, which is both more accurate and far cheaper per unit of accuracy in that regime.

Worked example

Integrating the standard normal PDF from 0 to 1 by hand, with 4 strips

Estimate \(\int_0^1 \varphi(z)\,dz = \Phi(1)-\Phi(0)\), where \(\varphi(z) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-z^2/2}\), using the trapezoidal rule with \(n=4\) strips, so \(h = (1-0)/4 = 0.25\).

1

Evaluate φ at the 5 grid points

\[ \begin{aligned} \varphi(0.00) &= 0.39894 \\ \varphi(0.25) &= 0.38667 \\ \varphi(0.50) &= 0.35207 \\ \varphi(0.75) &= 0.30114 \\ \varphi(1.00) &= 0.24197 \end{aligned} \]
2

Apply the composite trapezoidal formula

\[ \int_0^1 \varphi(z)\,dz \approx \frac{0.25}{2}\Big[0.39894 + 2(0.38667) + 2(0.35207) + 2(0.30114) + 0.24197\Big] \]
\[ = 0.125 \times \big[0.39894 + 0.77334 + 0.70414 + 0.60228 + 0.24197\big] = 0.125 \times 2.72067 = 0.34008 \]
3

Compare to the true value

The known true value is \(\Phi(1)-\Phi(0) \approx 0.84134 - 0.50000 = 0.34134\). The 4-strip trapezoidal estimate, \(0.34008\), is off by only about \(0.00126\) — a relative error under 0.4%, from just 5 function evaluations and no calculus beyond arithmetic. Per the \(O(h^2)\) error rate, doubling to 8 strips (halving \(h\) to 0.125) would be expected to cut this error to roughly a quarter of its current size, to well under 0.001 — exactly the behavior the interactive chart above lets you confirm directly by dragging the slider.

Real-world trading scenario

Pricing a 7-underlying basket option — why the desk reaches for simulation, not a grid

A structuring desk needs to price a basket call option whose payoff depends on the weighted average of 7 correlated equity underlyings at expiry — a genuinely 7-dimensional pricing integral (the expectation of the payoff against the joint risk-neutral density of all 7 assets' terminal prices). A junior quant, having just learned Simpson's rule, suggests laying down a grid of even 15 points per dimension for high accuracy: \(15^7\), on the order of 170 billion evaluations — not a slow computation, an outright impossible one on any available hardware within a workable timeframe, however this hypothetical case is scaled up in accuracy or precision.

Monte Carlo sidesteps the exponential blow-up entirely: draw, say, 1 million joint samples of the 7 underlyings' terminal prices (from a correlated multivariate model, calibrated the same way any of this curriculum's covariance-matrix machinery would build one), evaluate the basket payoff on each of the 1 million draws, and average. The cost is exactly 1 million evaluations regardless of the fact that there are 7 dimensions involved — order-of-magnitude cheaper than any deterministic grid could ever be at that dimension, at the cost of a \(1/\sqrt{N}\) convergence rate rather than quadrature's much faster per-point convergence in low dimensions. Halving the Monte Carlo error requires quadrupling \(N\) (from 1 million to 4 million draws) — a real, known cost, but a polynomial one, utterly unlike the grid method's exponential wall.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

Quant researchers reach for deterministic quadrature constantly in contexts that are easy to overlook precisely because they aren't the headline "pricing an option" calculation: computing a normalizing constant for a likelihood function during model calibration, evaluating a characteristic-function-based pricing formula (the Fourier-transform approach used by models like Heston, elsewhere in this curriculum, reduces option pricing to a one-dimensional integral of a characteristic function — routinely evaluated with Simpson's rule or a specialized quadrature rule like Gauss-Legendre, never with Monte Carlo, precisely because it's low-dimensional and quadrature is both faster and more accurate there), or computing a risk metric that's a 1D or 2D integral of a fitted density. Any time a problem is genuinely one- or two-dimensional and the integrand is reasonably smooth, quadrature is the default, unglamorous, highly accurate workhorse — reaching for Monte Carlo there would be strictly worse on both speed and accuracy.

Monte Carlo takes over decisively the moment genuine high dimensionality enters: pricing path-dependent derivatives (each monitoring date along the path effectively adds a dimension), multi-asset basket and rainbow options, and portfolio-level risk measures (Value-at-Risk, Expected Shortfall) computed jointly across many risk factors are all standard Monte Carlo territory in practice, for exactly the curse-of-dimensionality reason developed above. In between the extremes, quasi-Monte Carlo methods (using deterministic, carefully-structured low-discrepancy point sequences — Sobol or Halton sequences — instead of genuinely random draws) are a real, widely used middle ground: they retain Monte Carlo's dimension-independent cost structure while often converging faster than \(1/\sqrt{N}\) in practice for moderately high-dimensional, reasonably smooth integrands, at the cost of losing the clean probabilistic error bars a true random Monte Carlo estimate comes with.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where this goes wrong in practice

Applying Simpson's rule to a function with a kink or a discontinuity inside the interval. Both quadrature rules assume the integrand is smooth enough for a local straight-line or parabolic fit to be a good approximation. A payoff with a kink (a digital or barrier feature, say) sitting inside one of the fitted panels breaks that assumption, and the impressive \(O(h^4)\) Simpson's-rule error rate can silently degrade back toward — or worse than — the plain trapezoidal rate. Splitting the integration range at any known kink is the standard fix.

Underestimating the curse of dimensionality until it's too late. A grid method that works comfortably in 1 or 2 dimensions can look like it should "just scale up" to 3 or 4 — the exponential cost only becomes obviously infeasible once someone tries to actually run it, by which point real development time may already be sunk into a deterministic-quadrature implementation that has to be thrown out in favor of simulation.

Treating a Monte Carlo estimate's standard error as the whole story. The \(1/\sqrt{N}\) rate is an average-case statement about repeated sampling — a single Monte Carlo run, for a fixed finite \(N\), still has genuine sampling noise, and reporting a point estimate without its standard error (or without enough paths to make that standard error small relative to the quantity being estimated) can overstate precision. This connects directly to the Bootstrapping & Monte Carlo lesson's own house discipline: Monte Carlo's convergence is about repeated resampling from a fixed, already-specified model, and says nothing about whether that model itself is correctly specified.

Confusing "more accurate per point" with "cheaper overall." Simpson's rule is more accurate than the trapezoidal rule per function evaluation in low dimensions — but in a genuinely high-dimensional setting, that per-point accuracy advantage is irrelevant next to the sheer number of points a grid needs, which is the entire point of the dimensionality comparison above. Picking the "better" quadrature rule is the wrong optimization once dimensionality, not per-point accuracy, is the binding constraint.

Section 04

Key Takeaways

✓The trapezoidal rule sums trapezoid-shaped strips under straight-line fits. Its error shrinks with the square of the step size, O(h²).
✓Simpson's rule fits parabolas through triples of points instead. A much better O(h⁴) error rate for smooth functions, at essentially no extra conceptual cost.
✓Monte Carlo integration is the same job, sampled instead of gridded. Averaging random draws of a function is mathematically estimating the same integral as a deterministic quadrature rule.
⚠Deterministic quadrature's cost explodes exponentially with dimension. The curse of dimensionality — roughly nᵈ evaluations for a d-dimensional grid — makes it infeasible past 2-3 dimensions.
✓Monte Carlo's 1/√N error rate doesn't depend on dimension at all. The exact reason high-dimensional option pricing (baskets, path-dependent payoffs) always uses simulation, never a grid.
⚠Neither method validates the model being integrated against. Quadrature and Monte Carlo both faithfully compute the integral of whatever density or payoff they're given — garbage in, precisely-computed garbage out.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. Why does Simpson's rule generally beat the trapezoidal rule's accuracy for the same number of evaluation points, on a smooth function?
The trapezoidal rule approximates the curve locally with a straight line, which cannot bend to match any actual curvature in the function — its error comes entirely from that mismatch, and shrinks proportionally to h². Simpson's rule fits a parabola through each set of three consecutive points instead, which can match a smooth function's local curvature far more closely than a straight line can, leaving a much smaller residual error that shrinks proportionally to h⁴ — a much faster improvement as the step size shrinks, for the same underlying grid of points, as long as the function is smooth enough for the local parabolic fit to be accurate (a kink or discontinuity breaks this advantage).
Q2. In what precise sense is Monte Carlo integration "the same thing" as the trapezoidal rule or Simpson's rule, rather than a fundamentally different technique?
All three are estimating the same object — the area under a curve, or equivalently an expectation, which is exactly what an integral is. The trapezoidal rule and Simpson's rule do this with an evenly-spaced, deterministic set of sample points and a specific weighting scheme derived from fitting straight lines or parabolas. Monte Carlo does it by drawing points randomly instead of on a fixed grid and taking a simple average — a different sampling scheme for choosing where to evaluate the function, but mechanically still "evaluate the function at a set of points, combine those values into a single number that estimates the area/expectation."
Q3. Why does a 7-dimensional basket-option pricing problem make deterministic quadrature infeasible but leave Monte Carlo's cost basically unaffected?
A deterministic grid needs roughly n evaluation points per dimension, so covering d dimensions costs on the order of n^d total evaluations — exponential in the number of dimensions. At 7 dimensions, even a modest per-dimension grid density becomes tens or hundreds of billions of evaluations, computationally infeasible. Monte Carlo instead draws N joint samples (each one a full 7-dimensional point) and evaluates the function once per sample — the cost is simply N evaluations, with no exponential dependence on d baked in at all; drawing a 7-dimensional random point costs essentially the same as drawing a 1-dimensional one. This is exactly why Monte Carlo, not a grid, is the standard tool for genuinely high-dimensional pricing problems.
Q4. A Monte Carlo estimate uses 1 million paths. Roughly how many paths would be needed to cut its standard error in half, and why?
Monte Carlo's error shrinks proportionally to 1/√N. To cut the error in half, N needs to increase by a factor of 4 (since √4 = 2) — so roughly 4 million paths, not 2 million. This quartic-in-accuracy relationship (each halving of error costs 4× the computation) is a real, genuine cost of Monte Carlo, but it's a fixed, dimension-independent relationship — unlike deterministic quadrature, where the cost of improving accuracy can interact with dimensionality in a much less forgiving, exponential way.
Q5. Why would a quant researcher choose Simpson's rule over Monte Carlo simulation to price a vanilla option under a nonstandard, but still one-dimensional, terminal distribution?
For a genuinely one-dimensional integral (a single underlying's terminal-price distribution), deterministic quadrature like Simpson's rule converges far faster and more accurately than Monte Carlo for a given amount of computation — its O(h⁴) error rate for a smooth integrand beats Monte Carlo's fixed 1/√N rate decisively at low dimension, and it requires no random-number generation or repeated resampling to reduce noise. Monte Carlo's real advantage — a cost that doesn't explode with dimension — is irrelevant here, since there's only one dimension to begin with; reaching for Monte Carlo in this case would be strictly slower and less accurate than the deterministic alternative for no compensating benefit.
