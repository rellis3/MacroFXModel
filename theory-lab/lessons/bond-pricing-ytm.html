Bond Pricing & Yield to Maturity

A bond's price is nothing more than the present value of a known stream of future cash — the same discounting arithmetic as any other asset, applied to coupons and a face value instead of dividends. Yield to maturity flips the question around: given the price the market is actually charging, what single discount rate explains it?

CONCEPT — not built or tested in this repo ~16 min read Prereqs: basic compounding / present value (covered in this curriculum's returns & compounding primer)
TL;DR
  • A bond's price is the present value of every cash flow it pays: each coupon \(C\) discounted back from the period it arrives in, plus the face value \(F\) discounted back from maturity — \(P = \sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{C}{(1+y)^t} + \frac{F}{(1+y)^n}\).
  • Yield to maturity (YTM) is the single discount rate \(y\) that makes that formula's output equal the bond's actual observed market price. It's implicit — you plug in a price and solve for \(y\), not the other way around — and for more than a couple of periods there is no closed-form algebraic solution, only an iterative numerical search.
  • Price and yield move in opposite directions, always: discount the same fixed cash flows at a higher rate and every term in the sum shrinks, so price falls when yield rises, and vice versa.
  • A bond trades at par when its coupon rate equals its YTM, at a premium (above face value) when the coupon rate exceeds YTM, and at a discount (below face value) when YTM exceeds the coupon rate.
  • The worked example prices a 5-year, 4% annual-coupon, $1,000 face bond at a 5% YTM and gets \(P \approx \$956.71\) — a discount bond, because the 4% coupon sits below the 5% YTM — then sets up a Newton-Raphson-style iterative search for the YTM implied by an observed market price.
  • The interactive chart drags a YTM slider along the price-yield curve for that same bond — the curve visibly bends rather than running straight, the convexity this lesson's follow-up (Duration & Convexity) builds on directly.
Section 01

Why this matters

Every fixed-income instrument — a government bond, a corporate bond, the fixed leg of an interest-rate swap — reduces to the same question: a known, contractually fixed set of future cash payments, and a price today. Bond pricing is just present-value arithmetic applied to that stream, and yield to maturity is the single number the market uses to summarize an entire bond's return in one figure, the way a "price-per-share" summarizes an equity. Once you can price a bond and back out its yield, you have the building block underneath a rates desk's entire vocabulary: yield curves, duration, spread trades, and the sensitivity analysis that governs how every fixed-income (and, transitively, most FX and macro) portfolio manages risk.

This lesson also sets up the next two in this category directly. Duration and convexity (Lesson 2) are, respectively, the first- and second-order sensitivity of exactly the pricing formula built here to a change in yield. Bootstrapping the yield curve (Lesson 3) exists precisely because a single bond's YTM is a blended average across all its cash flows' maturities, not a clean per-maturity rate — a limitation this lesson surfaces but doesn't yet solve.

💡
Plain-English intuition

A bond is just a stack of IOUs, priced the way you'd price any of them

Imagine a friend offers you a deal: give me $1,000 today, and I'll pay you $40 a year for the next five years, then hand back your $1,000 at the end. That's a bond — a 4% coupon on $1,000 face value, five-year maturity. Whether $1,000 is a fair price for that promise depends on one thing: what else you could do with your money instead. If bank accounts pay 5% right now, your friend's deal is worse than the alternative, so you shouldn't pay a full $1,000 for it — you'd only pay some smaller amount that makes the deal competitive with the 5% you could get elsewhere. Bond pricing is exactly that comparison, made precise: discount every future payment back to today at whatever rate the market currently demands, and add them up.

Yield to maturity flips the same idea around. Instead of "here's the market's going rate, what should I pay," it asks "given what the bond is actually trading for right now, what rate does that price imply I'm earning if I hold it to maturity?" It's a single, back-solved number that lets you compare a 5-year bond against a 10-year bond, or a corporate bond against a government one, on the same scale — much like a z-score lets you compare unrelated series on the same ruler.

Section 02

The math, step by step

A coupon-paying bond promises two kinds of cash flow: a fixed coupon payment \(C\) at the end of each period until maturity, and a single lump-sum face value \(F\) (also called par value or principal) returned alongside the final coupon at maturity. If the bond has \(n\) periods remaining and the market's required discount rate per period is \(y\), the bond's price is the present value of that entire cash-flow stream:

\[ P = \sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{C}{(1+y)^t} + \frac{F}{(1+y)^n} \]

Each term is ordinary present-value discounting, the same arithmetic behind any multi-period cash flow: a dollar received \(t\) periods from now is worth \(1/(1+y)^t\) dollars today, because a dollar today could instead be invested at rate \(y\) and grow into \((1+y)^t\) dollars by then. The bond formula is simply that discounting applied once per coupon, plus once more for the face value.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

A dollar promised to you next year is worth less than a dollar in your hand today, because you could take today's dollar, put it in the bank, and have more than a dollar by next year. A bond's price is just adding up every single future payment it promises, each one shrunk down to reflect how long you have to wait for it.

Yield to maturity — the same formula, solved backwards

In the pricing formula above, \(y\) is an input: you're told the going market rate and asked to compute \(P\). In practice it usually runs the other way. A bond is already trading somewhere — you observe its market price \(P_{mkt}\) — and the question is: what single, constant discount rate \(y\), applied uniformly to every cash flow, makes the formula's output equal that observed price? That rate is the yield to maturity:

\[ P_{mkt} = \sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{C}{(1+\text{YTM})^t} + \frac{F}{(1+\text{YTM})^n} \]
P_mktthe price actually observed in the market — the known input now
YTMthe single unknown rate being solved for

YTM is best understood as the internal rate of return an investor earns by buying the bond at \(P_{mkt}\), holding it to maturity, collecting every coupon, and reinvesting each coupon at that same rate \(y\) (an assumption worth flagging up front — reinvestment risk is exactly the gap between YTM's assumption and what actually happens if rates move, covered further in the pitfalls below). It compresses an entire multi-year cash-flow schedule into one number that can be quoted, compared across bonds of different maturities and coupons, and tracked over time — which is precisely why "the 10-year yield" is the headline number a macro desk watches, not "the 10-year bond's price."

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Yield to maturity is running the pricing math backwards. Instead of "here's the interest rate, tell me the price," it asks "here's the price everyone's actually paying, so what interest rate would make that price fair?" It's one tidy number that lets you compare a five-year bond to a ten-year bond the same way a speed limit sign lets you compare a car and a bike on the same scale.

Why there's no clean formula for \(y\): for a single-period bond, solving for \(y\) is simple algebra: \(P=(C+F)/(1+y)\) rearranges directly to \(y=(C+F)/P - 1\). For a two-period bond it's a quadratic, at least still solvable by a closed-form formula. But for three or more periods, \(y\) appears raised to three or more different powers in the same equation simultaneously, and there is no general algebraic formula (analogous to the quadratic formula) that isolates it. Every real bond with more than a couple of coupons left needs an iterative numerical search — an initial guess for \(y\), refined step by step until the formula's price output matches \(P_{mkt}\) closely enough. This curriculum's Numerical Methods material covers exactly this kind of root-finding in depth via Newton-Raphson; the worked example below shows the first few iterations applied to this specific problem.

The inverse price-yield relationship

Look again at the pricing formula: every single cash flow is divided by \((1+y)\) raised to some positive power. Raise \(y\), and every one of those denominators grows, so every term in the sum shrinks — with no exception, because every term has the identical structure. There is no scenario in the plain fixed-coupon bond formula where raising \(y\) increases any individual term. This gives the single most important qualitative fact in fixed income:

\[ y \uparrow \;\Rightarrow\; P \downarrow \qquad\qquad y \downarrow \;\Rightarrow\; P \uparrow \]

Bond prices and yields move in strictly opposite directions. This isn't an empirical tendency that occasionally breaks — it falls directly out of the algebra of discounting a fixed cash-flow stream, and it holds for every plain-vanilla fixed-coupon bond, always. It is also, note, not a straight-line (linear) relationship — the curve connecting price to yield bends, an effect called convexity that the interactive chart below makes visible and that Lesson 2 quantifies precisely.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Think of the bond's future payments as fixed amounts of cash sitting at fixed dates in the future. Turning up the discount rate is like turning up how much you shrink every one of those future payments to bring them back to today. Shrink them more, and the total you're willing to pay right now goes down — every single time, with no exceptions.

Par, premium, and discount bonds

Comparing a bond's fixed coupon rate (the coupon divided by face value) against its YTM tells you immediately whether the bond trades above, below, or exactly at face value:

Par bond — coupon rate = YTM

The bond's price equals its face value exactly, \(P=F\). This happens precisely when the coupon the bond pays matches the return the market currently demands — there's no gap to compensate for either way.

Premium bond — coupon rate > YTM

The bond pays a coupon richer than what the market currently requires, so investors bid its price above face value, \(P>F\), until the higher price brings the effective return back down to the market's YTM.

Discount bond — coupon rate < YTM

The coupon is stingier than the going rate, so the price falls below face value, \(P<F\), compensating the buyer with capital gain (buy below face, redeem at full face) for the below-market coupon.

All three cases are the exact same pricing formula — nothing changes structurally, only the relationship between \(C/F\) (the coupon rate) and \(y\) (the YTM). At maturity, every one of these bonds still pays back exactly \(F\), regardless of what it traded for along the way; a premium bond's price is guaranteed to decay down toward \(F\) as maturity approaches (a fact used directly in the "pull to par" framing practitioners use, covered below).

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

If a bond's coupon happens to match exactly what the market currently wants, it sells for face value — a fair trade, nothing to make up for. If its coupon is more generous than what's on offer elsewhere, people will pay extra to get it, bidding the price above face value. If its coupon is stingier, buyers only take it at a discount, so the cheap purchase price makes up for the weak coupon.

Clean price, dirty price, and accrued interest (briefly)

The pricing formula above assumes you're valuing the bond exactly on a coupon date — no partial period in between. In reality, bonds trade on every business day, most of which fall between coupon dates. The dirty price (also called the "full" or "invoice" price) is what a buyer actually pays: the bond's theoretical present value plus accrued interest — the portion of the next coupon the seller has already "earned" by holding the bond since the last coupon date, and is owed as compensation for handing that accrued claim to the buyer. The clean price is the dirty price minus that accrued interest — it's the price quoted on trading screens, precisely because it doesn't jump by the coupon amount on every coupon date and so moves smoothly with genuine changes in yield, while the dirty price is what actually settles. This lesson's pricing formula and worked example both assume valuation on a coupon date, where clean price and dirty price coincide.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine buying a house mid-month from someone who's been paying rent to live there. If you also owe them for the days of rent-value they've already "earned" before handing over the keys, that extra owed amount is like accrued interest. The clean price is the sticker price everyone quotes; the dirty price is the sticker price plus that little extra owed chunk — the amount that actually changes hands.

Worked example

Pricing a 5-year, 4% coupon bond at a 5% YTM, then reversing the problem

A bond has $1,000 face value, pays a 4% annual coupon (\(C = \$40\) per year), matures in exactly \(n=5\) years, and the market's required YTM is 5% (\(y=0.05\)). Discount every cash flow back to today.

1

Discount each of the five $40 coupons

\[ \frac{40}{1.05^1}=38.10,\quad \frac{40}{1.05^2}=36.28,\quad \frac{40}{1.05^3}=34.56,\quad \frac{40}{1.05^4}=32.91,\quad \frac{40}{1.05^5}=31.34 \]
2

Sum the discounted coupons

\[ 38.10+36.28+34.56+32.91+31.34 = 173.19 \]
3

Discount the $1,000 face value from year 5 only

\[ \frac{1000}{1.05^5} = \frac{1000}{1.27628} \approx 783.53 \]
4

Add the two pieces

\[ P = 173.19 + 783.53 = \$956.71 \]

The bond prices at $956.71 — a discount to its $1,000 face value, exactly as expected, since its 4% coupon rate sits below the 5% YTM the market demands. This matches the discount-bond case from the comparison above.

Now reverse it: given a market price, find the YTM

Suppose instead you observe this same bond trading at \(P_{mkt} = \$1{,}021.00\) and want to know what YTM the market is implying. There's no algebraic shortcut (per the box above) — set up an iterative search instead. Newton-Raphson refines a guess \(y_k\) using the pricing function \(f(y) = P(y) - P_{mkt}\) (the gap between the model price at a trial yield and the observed price) and its derivative \(f'(y)\) (which, not coincidentally, is (the negative of) modified duration times price — the subject of the very next lesson):

\[ y_{k+1} = y_k - \frac{f(y_k)}{f'(y_k)} \]
f(y)model price at trial yield y, minus the observed market price
f'(y)the pricing function's slope — how fast price moves per unit of y
1

Start with a reasonable guess, \(y_0 = 4.00\%\)

The coupon rate is a common, cheap starting guess. At \(y_0=0.04\), plugging into the pricing formula gives \(P(0.04) = \$1{,}000.00\) exactly (a 4% coupon bond priced at a 4% YTM is, by definition, a par bond). So \(f(y_0) = 1000.00 - 1021.00 = -21.00\) — the model price is too low, meaning the trial yield is too high (recall: yield up, price down) relative to what a $1,021 market price implies.

2

Estimate the local slope \(f'(y_0)\) and take a step

A quick numerical estimate (evaluating the pricing formula at \(y=3.9\%\) and \(y=4.1\%\) — prices of \$1,004.48 and \$995.57 respectively — and taking the difference) gives a slope of roughly \(f'(y_0) \approx -4{,}459\) dollars per unit change in \(y\) — consistent with duration-driven price sensitivity, formalized in Lesson 2. The Newton step is:

\[ y_1 = 0.0400 - \frac{-21.00}{-4459} \approx 0.0400 - 0.0047 \approx 0.0353 \]
3

Repeat until \(f(y_k)\) is close enough to zero

Plugging \(y_1 \approx 3.53\%\) back into the pricing formula gives a price much closer to \$1,021, and \(f(y_1)\) shrinks to only a small fraction of the original $21 gap. One or two more iterations of the same step converge to the bond's true YTM to well beyond any practical precision. This is exactly the Newton-Raphson procedure covered in depth in this curriculum's Numerical Methods material — bond YTM solving is one of its textbook applications.

Real-world trading scenario

A rate-sensitive fund watches "the 10-year" reprice overnight

A fixed-income portfolio manager holds a large position in 10-year Treasury notes. Overnight, a stronger-than-expected inflation print hits the wire. Within minutes, headlines say "the 10-year yield jumped 15 basis points." No one on the desk needs to re-run a pricing model to know what happened to the bond's price — the inverse price-yield relationship from this lesson says it immediately and unambiguously: yield up means price down, full stop, for every plain fixed-coupon bond in the book.

The manager's actual work starts one level deeper: how much did the price fall, and does that match what the position's risk model predicted? A bond priced near par with a longer maturity will have moved by a noticeably larger dollar amount than a bond near maturity with only a few coupons left, even though both are described by the exact same "yield up, price down" direction. Untangling "how much" from "which direction" is precisely the job duration and convexity do — this scenario is the reason Lesson 2 exists immediately after this one, not an isolated curiosity.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

Rates desks and macro funds live inside this formula every day, mostly in its "solve for YTM" direction rather than its "solve for price" direction — market prices are observed continuously; the yield is the derived, comparable summary statistic. A trader quoting a bond says "it's trading at a yield of 4.32%," not "it's trading at $978.40" — the yield is what's comparable across different coupons, maturities, and issuers, which is exactly why it's the quoted convention.

Pull to par is a standard piece of desk intuition built directly on this lesson's par/premium/discount framework: as a bond approaches maturity, holding its yield fixed, a premium bond's price decays smoothly down toward face value and a discount bond's price rises smoothly up toward face value, since at maturity every bond redeems at exactly \(F\) regardless of what it traded for along the way. Traders holding premium bonds near maturity account for this expected price decay explicitly rather than mistaking it for a market move.

Yield-based relative value is the standard way desks compare bonds that differ in coupon, maturity, and issuer all at once — a 3% coupon bond and a 6% coupon bond of the same maturity aren't directly comparable by price, but their YTMs are, on the same scale, the same way a z-score makes otherwise-incomparable series comparable. Spread trades (buying one bond, selling another, on the difference between their yields) are built entirely on this comparability.

Solving for YTM in production systems is virtually never done by hand — every pricing library and terminal runs a Newton-Raphson (or a similar bisection/secant) root-finder against the exact pricing equation shown above, purely because no closed form exists for more than a couple of coupon periods. The iterative worked example above is the literal algorithm, at toy scale, behind every "YTM" field a Bloomberg terminal or pricing library displays.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where this goes wrong in practice

Treating YTM as a guaranteed realized return. YTM's derivation implicitly assumes every coupon is reinvested at that exact same rate \(y\) for the life of the bond, and that the bond is held to maturity without default. In reality, rates move around, so coupons actually get reinvested at whatever rate prevails when each one is paid — this gap is called reinvestment risk, and it means the return an investor actually realizes over the holding period can differ meaningfully from the quoted YTM, especially for longer-maturity, higher-coupon bonds where more of the total return comes from reinvested coupons rather than price appreciation.

Confusing coupon rate with yield. The coupon rate is fixed at issuance and never changes for the life of the bond; YTM moves every time the market price moves. A bond's coupon rate tells you what it was worth relative to rates at issuance — its YTM tells you what it's worth relative to rates right now. These only coincide for a par bond.

Ignoring clean vs. dirty price when comparing quotes. Prices quoted between coupon dates are almost always clean prices; forgetting to add accrued interest back before comparing to a settlement amount, or comparing a clean-price quote against a formula that assumes valuation exactly on a coupon date, introduces a systematic (if usually small) error.

Assuming a single starting guess always converges cleanly. Newton-Raphson's iteration can fail to converge, oscillate, or converge to an economically meaningless root (e.g. a negative yield far from any sensible value) if the initial guess is poor or the pricing function's local slope is unusual — real implementations bound the search to a sensible yield range and fall back to a slower but more robust method (like bisection) if Newton-Raphson misbehaves.

Section 04

Key Takeaways

✓A bond's price is just present value. \(P = \sum \frac{C}{(1+y)^t} + \frac{F}{(1+y)^n}\) — discount every coupon from the period it lands in, discount the face value from maturity, and add.
✓YTM is the same formula solved backwards. Given an observed market price, YTM is the single discount rate that reproduces it — implicit, and (beyond 1-2 periods) solvable only iteratively, e.g. via Newton-Raphson.
✓Price and yield move in strictly opposite directions. This falls directly out of the discounting algebra — no exceptions for a plain fixed-coupon bond.
✓Coupon rate vs. YTM tells you par/premium/discount. Coupon = YTM → par; coupon > YTM → premium; coupon < YTM → discount.
✓The price-yield relationship is curved, not straight. That curvature (convexity) is why a duration-only estimate of a price move is an approximation, not exact — the subject of the very next lesson.
⚠YTM assumes coupons are reinvested at YTM itself. Real reinvestment happens at whatever rate prevails later — the realized return on a held bond can differ from its quoted YTM.
⚠Clean price ≠ dirty price between coupon dates. The formula here assumes valuation exactly on a coupon date; real settlement amounts include accrued interest.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. Why can't you generally solve the bond pricing formula for \(y\) algebraically once there are more than a couple of coupon periods?
Because \(y\) appears raised to a different power in every term of the sum simultaneously (\((1+y)^1, (1+y)^2, \dots, (1+y)^n\)), and there is no general algebraic formula — analogous to the quadratic formula for degree-2 polynomials — that isolates a single variable appearing at three or more different powers in the same equation. For \(n=1\) it's linear (solvable directly); for \(n=2\) it's a quadratic (still solvable by formula); for \(n \ge 3\) there is, in general, no closed form, so YTM must be found by an iterative numerical search such as Newton-Raphson.
Q2. A bond's coupon rate is 6% and its YTM is 4.5%. Does it trade above, below, or at face value, and why?
Above face value — a premium bond. Its coupon (6%) is richer than the return the market currently requires (4.5%), so investors are willing to pay more than face value for the bond, up to the point where the higher purchase price brings the bond's effective return back down to the market's 4.5% YTM. This is the "coupon rate > YTM ⇒ premium" rule from the par/premium/discount comparison above.
Q3. If market yields rise, what happens to bond prices, and why is this true without exception for a plain fixed-coupon bond?
Prices fall. Every cash flow in the pricing formula is divided by \((1+y)\) raised to some positive power; raising \(y\) makes every one of those denominators larger, so every term in the sum shrinks with no exception, since every term shares that exact structure. There is no configuration of a plain fixed-coupon bond where a yield increase raises any individual discounted cash flow, so the sum — the price — must fall.
Q4. What does yield to maturity actually assume about reinvestment, and why does that matter for the return an investor actually realizes?
YTM is derived assuming every coupon received along the way is reinvested at that exact same rate, \(y\), until maturity, and that the bond is held to maturity without default. In reality, market rates change over time, so coupons actually get reinvested at whatever rate happens to prevail when each one is paid — not necessarily the original YTM. This gap, called reinvestment risk, means the actual realized return on a held bond can differ from its quoted YTM, especially for bonds with more coupons and a longer time to maturity, where reinvested-coupon income makes up a larger share of the total return.
Q5. What is the difference between a clean price and a dirty price, and why do trading screens quote the clean price?
The dirty (full/invoice) price is what a buyer actually pays: the bond's present value plus accrued interest — the portion of the next coupon the seller has already earned since the last coupon date and is owed for transferring that claim. The clean price is the dirty price minus that accrued interest. Screens quote the clean price because it moves smoothly with genuine changes in yield, while the dirty price jumps mechanically by the coupon amount on every coupon date — a jump that has nothing to do with a change in valuation and would make the quoted price much harder to read at a glance.
