Lesson 10 · Cross-Asset & Structure

PCA & Factor Models, Finding the One Trade Hiding in Many

A basket of currency pairs looks like several separate bets. Most days it's really one bet — "risk-on" or "risk-off" — wearing several costumes. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is the tool that measures exactly how true that is: how many genuinely independent risks a basket actually contains, versus how many instruments it happens to be split across.

Section 1

One basket, one bet in disguise

Open any multi-pair FX dashboard on a day the market moves and a familiar pattern shows up: EURUSD up, GBPUSD up, AUDUSD up, USDJPY down, USDCHF down — six or seven "different" pairs that all just told you the same thing, dollar weakness or strength, at the same time.

If you hold positions in all of them thinking you have seven independent views, you don't. You have one large, leveraged bet on a single shared factor, plus some small residual noise around it.

The opposite can also be true: most days the basket looks coordinated, and then one pair — with its own domestic story, a central-bank surprise, a commodity shock — decouples and moves on its own reasons. Knowing which situation you're actually in, quantitatively rather than by eyeballing a heatmap, is the entire point of this lesson.

Section 2

Finding the camera angle that shows the most motion

The plain-English version, before any notation.

Imagine filming a swarm of fireflies at night from many possible camera angles. Most angles show a confusing tangle of dots moving every which way. But there's usually one angle — one line of sight — from which the swarm's motion looks biggest and clearest: maybe the fireflies are mostly drifting left-to-right together, and pointing the camera straight down that axis captures almost all of the apparent motion in a single dimension.

PCA is the procedure for finding that best camera angle automatically — except instead of fireflies, it works on a cloud of daily returns across several instruments. It finds the direction through that cloud where the data is most spread out (the first principal component, PC1), then the next-best direction perpendicular to the first (PC2), and so on. "Spread out" here is just a stand-in for variance: a direction the cloud is spread wide along explains a lot of day-to-day movement; a direction it's thin along barely matters.

The upshot

If most of a basket's spread lives along one single direction, most of what's "moving" is really one underlying thing, viewed from several currency pairs — several angles — at once.

Section 3

Step 1 — how two series really move together

Covariance is a running check: was each series above or below its own normal, on the same day?

Try it
Above-normal on pair A tends to land on the same day as above-normal on pair B. Most products of deviations are positive, so covariance comes out positive — they move as a team.
Above-normal on one tends to land on days the other is below-normal. Products of deviations are mostly negative, so covariance comes out negative — they pull in opposite directions.
No consistent pattern — positive and negative products roughly cancel out, so covariance lands near zero. Neither predicts the other.
Cov(x,y) = average of (xₜ − x̄)(yₜ − ȳ) across every day t. Its units are messy — return-of-x times return-of-y — which is why correlation (covariance rescaled into [−1, 1]) is usually what gets quoted instead.

For a basket of N instruments, computing this pairwise number for every combination and arranging the results in an N×N grid gives the covariance matrix Σ — its diagonal holds each instrument's own variance, its off-diagonal entries the pairwise covariances. This is the exact same object this project's own correlation heatmap already renders for you. PCA is the next step after you have that grid, not a replacement for it.

Section 4

Step 2 — the one piece of linear algebra you need

A matrix like Σ is a machine: feed it a direction, it stretches, squishes, or rotates that direction. Almost every direction comes out pointing somewhere new.

Illustration
Left: v is special — Σ only lengthens it, it never leaves v's own line. Right: u is ordinary — Σ rotates it onto a completely different line.
v

Eigenvector

A direction Σ only stretches or shrinks — it never rotates it off its own line. For a covariance matrix, these directions are the principal components.

λ

Eigenvalue

The amount of stretching along that direction — how much variance lies there. Bigger λ, more important direction. Formally: Σv = λv.

⊥

Mutually perpendicular

An N×N covariance matrix always has exactly N eigenvectors, and they are guaranteed mutually orthogonal — a complete, non-overlapping set of directions.

That perpendicularity is what makes eigenvectors useful as "factors": directions at 90° to each other can't share variance by definition. Principal components are uncorrelated because of the geometry, not because you found something in the data.

Section 6

Step 3 & 4 — ranking components, then scoring them

Sort the N eigenvectors by their eigenvalue, largest to smallest: λ₁ ≥ λ₂ ≥ … ≥ λ_N. The eigenvector with the largest eigenvalue is PC1 — the single direction along which the basket's daily returns vary the most. PC2 is next-largest, perpendicular to PC1, capturing most of whatever PC1 didn't. And so on, down to PC_N, which by the time you get there is usually mopping up scraps of noise.

The eigenvalues themselves tell you exactly how much variance each component owns, because the sum of all N eigenvalues equals the basket's total variance. The explained variance ratio of component i is simply its share of that total:

Section 8

Worked example — three FX pairs, one shared driver and one holdout

EURUSD, GBPUSD, and AUDJPY, with a correlation matrix showing EURUSD/GBPUSD strongly linked (0.75) and AUDJPY only weakly tied to either (0.20, 0.15). Running PCA on this gives eigenvalues summing to 3 (the trace of a 3×3 correlation matrix): λ₁ ≈ 1.85, λ₂ ≈ 0.95, λ₃ ≈ 0.20, so EVR₁ ≈ 62%.

Shared factor present

PC1's loadings come out roughly (0.63, 0.61, 0.48) — EURUSD and GBPUSD load almost equally and strongly, since they drive the shared factor. AUDJPY loads in the same direction but noticeably smaller — some, not all, of its variance is shared with the other two.

If AUDJPY were fully idiosyncratic

With correlations of 0.02 and −0.03 instead, PC1 would load almost entirely on EURUSD/GBPUSD alone — roughly (0.71, 0.70, 0.05) — with EVR₁ closer to 50%, and a separate component would load almost entirely on AUDJPY by itself: a fully idiosyncratic instrument needs its own dimension.

That contrast is the whole diagnostic in miniature: a high loading on PC1 for every instrument, with a high EVR₁, says "this basket is really one trade in several costumes." A pair sitting near-zero on PC1 and picking up its own later component says "this one is actually doing something different."
Section 9

How practitioners actually use this

Running PCA across the G10 majors very reliably produces a PC1 readable as a broad "dollar factor" or "risk-sentiment factor" — most major pairs load onto it with the same sign relative to USD strength or weakness. On interest-rate curves, an even older, extremely well-documented result: the first three principal components of a yield curve's covariance matrix map almost exactly onto level (parallel shifts), slope (steepening/flattening), and curvature (butterfly) — stable enough that many desks quote curve risk directly in those units rather than tenor-by-tenor exposure.

Risk aggregation

Total portfolio risk decomposes into "how much exposure to PC1 (the common factor) versus PC2, PC3, …" — a cleaner view of concentration than a pairwise correlation grid, which can't tell you whether medium-to-high numbers mean one dominant factor or several moderate ones. This is exactly the motivation behind an "effective number of bets" metric.

Regime detection

Watching each day's PC1 score, and how much of that day's variance PC1 alone explained, flags unusual days — an instrument that normally loads heavily on PC1 suddenly decoupling, or a normally-idiosyncratic instrument suddenly co-moving tightly with the pack.

The caveat

PCA is descriptive, not a forecast — it tells you what the historical covariance structure looked like over the window you fed it, not what tomorrow's structure will be. Correlations, and EVR₁, famously spike toward 1 in a crisis — the well-known "correlations go to 1 in a crash" effect.

Section 10

What's actually built in this repo, right now

This project already computes the eigenvalues of a correlation matrix — via a cyclic Jacobi rotation — to build an "effective number of bets" metric used in this project's own diversification research. That part is real and already shipped.

What's still missing

The current implementation deliberately returns only the eigenvalues, not the eigenvectors — so it can tell you "your book is concentrated" (a single summary number) but not "concentrated in EURUSD and GBPUSD specifically." The loadings this lesson covers — which instruments actually make up the dominant factor — are a documented, unbuilt extension, not something to assume already exists on a live dashboard.

The eigenvectors fall out of the same Jacobi-rotation algorithm as its accumulated rotation matrix, so extracting them would be an extension of existing code rather than a new algorithm — but until that's built, "effective number of bets" answers how concentrated, not concentrated around what.

Section 11

Where PCA on a market basket goes wrong

⚠

Treating loadings as fixed

Σ is estimated over whatever history window you feed it, and correlation structure genuinely shifts across regimes. A PCA on the last two years can describe a materially different factor structure than one on the last two months.

⚠

Raw covariance, mixed volatility

An instrument with much larger absolute daily moves mechanically dominates a raw-covariance PCA regardless of how correlated it actually is. Running PCA on the correlation matrix — standardizing every series first — is the standard fix, and the one used throughout this lesson.

⚠

"Uncorrelated" ≠ "economically independent"

PC2, PC3, … are uncorrelated with PC1 by geometry, over the exact sample fitted. That's not evidence of a clean economic story — later components increasingly mix real idiosyncratic signal with plain estimation noise.

⚠

Small N, unstable eigenvectors

With only 3-5 instruments and a modest sample, eigenvector directions — especially the smaller, later ones — can be noisy. Eigenvalues are comparatively more stable than eigenvectors under sampling noise.

And the broadest caveat: PCA is not causal. A high loading tells you an instrument co-moves strongly with the dominant statistical direction — it does not tell you why, and any trading rule built on top still needs its own honest out-of-sample validation.
Section 12

Quick check

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

PC1 explains 70% of variance across a 6-pair basket. What does that imply about the "effective number of bets" in that basket?

70% of what moves the combined book on any given day is one common factor moving through all 6 positions at once — not 6 independent sources of risk. A shock to that factor hits most of the book in the same direction simultaneously.
Question 2

Why are principal components always uncorrelated with each other, by construction rather than by empirical luck?

Perpendicular directions in a data cloud can't share variance with each other by geometric definition — a structural consequence of how the components were built, not a pattern discovered in the data.
Question 3

For Σ = [[2, 0], [0, 1]] (a = 2, b = 0, d = 1), what are the two eigenvalues?

λ = (a+d)/2 ± √(((a-d)/2)² + b²) = 1.5 ± √(0.25 + 0) = 1.5 ± 0.5 = 2 and 1. With zero covariance the eigenvalues are just the two variances themselves.
Question 4

What's the difference between an eigenvalue and a loading?

λᵢ answers "how much total variance does this component capture." A loading — one entry of the eigenvector vᵢ — answers "how much does this specific instrument contribute to this specific component." A component can have a large eigenvalue while one instrument still loads near-zero on it.
Question 5

Why run PCA on the correlation matrix rather than raw covariance, across a mixed basket of a stable G10 pair and a volatile EM cross?

An instrument with larger absolute daily moves has a mechanically bigger variance term. PCA on raw covariance lets that instrument dominate simply because it's loudest, not because it shares the most structure. Standardizing to correlation first removes that scale effect.
Section 13

What to carry forward

01

A correlated basket often has far fewer "real" independent risks than it has instruments — PCA measures exactly how many, and how much of today's move is the shared one.

02

An eigenvector of Σ is a direction only stretched, never rotated (Σv = λv); the eigenvalue λ is how much variance lies along it. Principal components are eigenvectors ranked by eigenvalue, largest first.

03

Explained variance ratio, EVRᵢ = λᵢ / Σⱼλⱼ, turns eigenvalues into a headline percentage — "PC1 explains 72% of the basket's variance."

04

Loadings (the eigenvector's entries) tell you which instruments actually make up a factor — the diagnostic eigenvalues alone can't give you.

05

This repo computes eigenvalues today (feeding "effective number of bets") but not eigenvectors/loadings yet — know which claim you can actually back with a live number here versus which one needs the full lesson's math done by hand.

06

PCA is descriptive, not causal or predictive — correlation structure shifts across regimes, spiking toward 1 in a crisis, so anything built on top still needs its own out-of-sample validation.

What this now lets you do

Read a correlation heatmap and ask the sharper question underneath it: not just "how correlated," but "how many genuinely independent bets is this basket actually," and estimate a 2×2 case's eigenvalues and explained variance by hand. Recognize level/slope/curvature and "risk-on/risk-off" factors the moment they reappear later, under different names.
