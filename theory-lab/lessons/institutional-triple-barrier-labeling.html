Triple-Barrier Labelling & Meta-Labelling

The obvious way to label training data for a trading model — "was the return over the next fixed N bars positive?" — describes a trade nobody actually places. A real position exits at a stop-loss, a take-profit, or a time limit, whichever comes first, and a label built on the wrong exit teaches a model the wrong payoff shape entirely.

TL;DR
  • Labeling a bar as "up" or "down" based on the return over the next fixed \(N\) bars ignores the fact that a real position is closed by whichever of a stop-loss, a take-profit, or a time limit is hit first — the label and the actual trade payoff can quietly disagree.
  • Triple-barrier labelling (López de Prado, 2018) sets three barriers at every entry — an upper take-profit, a lower stop-loss, and a vertical time limit — and walks forward bar by bar; the label is whichever barrier is touched first: \(+1\), \(-1\), or a small/zero label at the time limit.
  • Barrier widths scale with a volatility estimate rather than a fixed pip or dollar distance, so the same barrier logic behaves sensibly whether the market is calm or violent — see GARCH & Volatility Clustering and Garman-Klass & Yang-Zhang Volatility Estimators.
  • Meta-labelling is a second, distinct idea: keep a primary model or rule for direction only — it can be weak and noisy — and train a separate secondary ("meta") model whose only job is predicting the probability that a signal which already fired will actually win, using the same triple-barrier labels restricted to instances where the primary signal fired.
  • Filtering (or sizing) trades by the meta-model's predicted probability raises realized precision on the trades actually taken, at the unavoidable cost of taking fewer of them — and meta-labelling can only filter an existing signal, never manufacture edge that was never there to begin with.
  • This repo's own bot/modules/ml_confidence.py is a genuine instance of this exact pattern, verified line-by-line below — not just a lesson that references the idea in the abstract.
Section 01

Why this matters

Every earlier lesson on model validity — The Research Pipeline, Feature Engineering, Leakage & Reproducibility, and White's Reality Check & Hansen's SPA Test — asks some version of "does this backtest reflect something real?" This lesson asks a question that comes before any of those: for a supervised trading model, what exactly is the training label supposed to mean? The most common answer in practice is a fixed-horizon return label — pick a lookahead window of \(N\) bars, and label the entry \(+1\) if the price is higher \(N\) bars later, \(-1\) otherwise (or a regression target, the raw \(N\)-bar return itself). It is simple, and it is also a description of a trade nobody trading with any risk control actually places.

A real position is not held blindly for exactly \(N\) bars and then closed regardless of what happened in between. It is closed by whichever comes first: price hits a take-profit, price hits a stop-loss, or a maximum holding time is reached. A fixed-horizon label can be flatly wrong about the trade's actual outcome — it might label a bar \(+1\) because the price happened to be higher \(N\) bars later, even though the path getting there blew straight through where a real stop-loss would have sat; conversely it can label a bar \(-1\) even though the path touched a real take-profit well before bar \(N\) and a real position would have already been closed, profitably, long before the horizon the label is measuring. Training a classifier on labels that don't describe the payoff structure of the strategy that will actually use them is a subtle, structural form of the same problem Feature Engineering, Leakage & Reproducibility covers for input features: the label itself can silently misrepresent the thing it claims to measure, well before any leakage or multiple-testing question from the lessons around this one even enters the picture.

💡
Plain-English intuition

A label should describe the trade you'd actually place

Imagine grading a trader's decision to enter a position by checking the market price exactly 10 days later, no matter what the trader's own stop-loss or take-profit orders would have done in between. That grade can be completely disconnected from what actually happened to the trader's position — maybe their stop got hit on day 3 and they were flat (and safely out) for the remaining seven days; maybe their take-profit filled on day 2 and they locked in a gain that the day-10 price, by coincidence, later gave back. Either way, "the price 10 days later" is not "what this trade actually earned."

Triple-barrier labelling fixes this by defining the label the same way a real trade defines its own exit: walk forward from the entry and stop at the first of three things that happens — hit the profit target, hit the stop, or run out of time. Whatever fires first is the label. It is not a cleverer statistical trick; it is simply asking the label to describe the trade that would actually have been taken, rather than an arbitrary snapshot of price at a fixed distance in the future.

Section 02

The triple-barrier method, precisely

Fix an entry time \(t_0\) with entry price \(P_0\), and a volatility estimate \(\hat\sigma_{t_0}\) available at (or before) \(t_0\) — a GARCH conditional volatility, an EWMA, a Garman-Klass/Yang-Zhang range estimator, or any other point-in-time volatility measure. Three barriers are set around the entry:

\[ U = P_0\big(1 + k_u\,\hat\sigma_{t_0}\big), \qquad L = P_0\big(1 - k_l\,\hat\sigma_{t_0}\big), \qquad V = t_0 + h \]
Uthe upper barrier — a take-profit, a multiple \(k_u\) of the volatility estimate above entry
Lthe lower barrier — a stop-loss, a multiple \(k_l\) of the volatility estimate below entry
Vthe vertical barrier — a hard time limit, \(h\) bars after entry
k_u, k_lvolatility multiples, not necessarily equal — set separately for take-profit and stop-loss

From \(t_0\), walk forward bar by bar and define the first-touch time \(\tau\) — the first bar at which price reaches \(U\), reaches \(L\), or the vertical barrier \(V\) is reached without either horizontal barrier having fired first:

\[ \tau = \min\big(\tau_U,\ \tau_L,\ V\big), \qquad y = \begin{cases} +1 & \text{if } \tau = \tau_U \text{ (upper touched first)} \\ -1 & \text{if } \tau = \tau_L \text{ (lower touched first)} \\ 0 \text{ or } r_\tau & \text{if } \tau = V \text{ (time limit reached first)} \end{cases} \]
τ_U, τ_Lthe first bar index at or after \(t_0\) at which price touches \(U\), respectively \(L\)
ythe resulting label — the entire point of the exercise
r_τthe small realized return actually banked at the vertical barrier, an alternative to a flat 0 label
1

Pick an entry and a volatility estimate

Entry price \(P_0\) at time \(t_0\), and a volatility estimate \(\hat\sigma_{t_0}\) computed using only information available at or before \(t_0\) — never a look-ahead estimate that peeks past the entry.

2

Set the three barriers

Upper \(U\) and lower \(L\) as volatility-scaled multiples above and below \(P_0\); vertical \(V\) as a fixed number of bars \(h\) ahead — all three chosen before looking at how the path actually unfolds.

3

Walk forward, bar by bar

Starting at \(t_0+1\), check each bar's price against \(U\) and \(L\) in the order the bars actually occurred — exactly the path a real open position would have experienced, nothing more.

4

Stop at the first touch

The moment either \(U\) or \(L\) is reached, stop — that bar is \(\tau\), and the barrier touched decides the label. If neither is reached by \(V\), stop at \(V\) instead.

5

Assign the label

\(+1\) for an upper touch, \(-1\) for a lower touch, and \(0\) (or the small realized return \(r_\tau\)) for a vertical-barrier timeout — a deliberate design choice for whichever downstream use the label is meant for.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine you're watching a ball roll down a hallway with a finish-line tape stretched high up on the left wall, another stretched low down on the right wall, and a clock on the far wall. You don't care which one the ball is "closest to" after some fixed number of seconds — you just watch, and the very first tape the ball actually touches, or the clock running out first, is what decides the story. That's the whole triple-barrier rule: watch the real path, and stop at whichever of the three things genuinely happens first.

The classic triple-barrier diagram — entry 1.0850, worked example from Section 03
The price path (blue) climbs and wobbles for six bars, staying well clear of both barriers, then closes above the upper barrier at bar 7 — the walk stops there. The vertical barrier at bar 10 is drawn for reference but never comes into play: the horizontal touch happened first.
\(P_0\)Entry price at time \(t_0\).
\(\hat\sigma_{t_0}\)Volatility estimate available at or before entry — GARCH, EWMA, or a range-based (Garman-Klass/Yang-Zhang) estimator.
\(U, L\)Upper (take-profit) and lower (stop-loss) barriers, each a volatility multiple above/below \(P_0\).
\(k_u, k_l\)Volatility multiples setting the width of the upper and lower barriers — need not be equal.
\(V\)Vertical barrier — the entry time plus a fixed maximum holding period \(h\).
\(\tau\)First-touch time — the earliest bar at which any one of the three barriers is reached.
\(y\)The resulting label: \(+1\), \(-1\), or a time-limit outcome (0 or the realized return \(r_\tau\)).

Worked example: walking one entry to its label

🧮
Worked example

Entry 1.0850, upper 1.0870, lower 1.0835, vertical 10 bars

A volatility estimate of \(\hat\sigma_{t_0}=5\) pips per bar (0.0005), with \(k_u=4\) and \(k_l=3\), gives \(U = 1.0850 + 4(0.0005) = 1.0870\) and \(L = 1.0850 - 3(0.0005) = 1.0835\) — an asymmetric barrier, a wider take-profit than stop-loss, a deliberate and common design choice, not a requirement of the method. A toy 7-bar price path (illustrative, not real market data): 

Bar    Price     vs. U=1.0870   vs. L=1.0835
0      1.0850    entry           entry
1      1.0855    below           above
2      1.0848    below           above
3      1.0857    below           above
4      1.0862    below           above
5      1.0850    below           above
6      1.0865    below           above
7      1.0872    TOUCHED ←       (never touched)
1

Check the lower barrier across every bar first

The lowest price reached in bars 1–7 is 1.0848 at bar 2 — still 13 pips clear of \(L=1.0835\). The lower barrier is never touched on this path.

2

Check the upper barrier, bar by bar, in order

Price stays below 1.0870 through bar 6 (highest so far: 1.0865). At bar 7, price reaches 1.0872 — at or above \(U=1.0870\) for the first time.

3

Compare against the vertical barrier

The vertical barrier sits at bar 10. Bar 7 is well before bar 10, so the horizontal touch wins the race — the vertical barrier never comes into play on this path.

4

Assign the label

\(\tau = 7\), and the barrier touched at \(\tau\) is the upper one — the label is \(y=+1\). Had the path instead first dipped to 1.0834 at some bar before bar 7, the lower barrier would have fired first and the label would be \(-1\), regardless of anything the price did afterward.

Notice what the label does not depend on: it does not care what price does at bar 8, 9, or 10 — those bars are irrelevant once bar 7 resolves the trade, exactly as they would be irrelevant to a real position that had already been closed by its take-profit order.

Section 04

Meta-labelling: a second, separate idea

Triple-barrier labelling answers "how should one entry's outcome be labeled?" Meta-labelling answers a completely different question: given a signal that already fired, should this specific trade be taken at all, or how big should it be? The two ideas compose — meta-labelling is trained on triple-barrier labels — but they solve different problems, and it is worth being precise about the split.

The primary model — direction only

Some rule or model decides long, short, or no signal. It can be a simple technical rule, a hand-built heuristic, an existing rule-based system, or even a genuinely weak, noisy model — meta-labelling's whole value proposition is that the primary signal does not need to be good on its own. Its only job is to fire on candidate opportunities and get direction right often enough to be worth filtering, not to already be a profitable, standalone strategy.

The secondary ("meta") model — confidence only

A separate model, trained only on the instances where the primary signal actually fired, predicts the probability that this specific trade hits its take-profit before its stop-loss — using the exact same triple-barrier labels from Sections 02–03, restricted to primary-signal instances. It never proposes direction; direction was already decided. Its one output is a probability, used to filter low-confidence signals below some threshold and/or size positions larger when confidence is higher.

Why this split is powerful rather than just a re-organization of the same problem: "find a model that identifies genuine alpha from raw market data" is a hard, mostly-unsolved problem. "Given that a specific signal already flagged this instance as a candidate, is it more or less likely than the average flagged instance to actually win?" is a narrower, easier, and completely separable problem — the meta-model is never asked to find opportunities from scratch, only to rank ones a primary source has already surfaced. A primary signal that is barely better than a coin flip on its own can still hand the meta-model real, exploitable structure to separate winners from losers within its own flagged instances — turning a signal that alone couldn't be traded profitably into a usable, filtered system.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine a friend who is a mediocre stock picker — right about 52% of the time, not good enough to trust blindly. But suppose you also notice that whenever your friend sounds especially confident, they're right much more often than when they sound unsure. You wouldn't ask your friend to become a better stock picker; you'd just start only listening when they sound confident, and ignoring them the rest of the time. The friend is the primary signal. Learning when to trust their confidence is the meta-model. Neither job requires the other to become good at picking stocks from scratch.

Precision on the trades actually taken rose from 50.0% to 75.0% — but the number of trades taken fell from 10 to 4, a 60% reduction in trade count. The meta-model did not create six new winners; it identified which four of the original ten were the better bets and left the rest on the table.

Section 05

Where this quietly goes wrong

Triple-barrier and meta-labelling are both, at their core, disciplined ways of asking "what would a real trade have actually experienced?" — which means both inherit, rather than avoid, the leakage discipline this curriculum already builds elsewhere. Two failure modes are specific enough to this lesson's material to name directly.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Look-ahead in the label itself, and asking meta-labelling to do the impossible

The volatility estimate that sets barrier widths must itself be point-in-time. This is the exact same discipline Feature Engineering, Leakage & Reproducibility spends an entire lesson on: a barrier width computed from a full-sample or centered volatility estimate — one that implicitly uses information from after the entry — leaks knowledge of how volatile the future actually turned out to be into where the barriers get placed today. The path-walking step itself is inherently leakage-safe (it only ever uses real future prices exactly as a live position would experience them, in order); the risk sits entirely in how \(\hat\sigma_{t_0}\) gets computed before the walk begins.

Meta-labelling can only improve precision — it cannot manufacture alpha from nothing. Meta-labelling's entire mechanism is filtering an existing set of flagged instances into a higher-precision subset. If the primary signal genuinely carries zero information content — its fired instances are, in truth, indistinguishable from random entries — there is no real structure in the "win vs. lose" pattern of those instances for a meta-model to find. A meta-model trained against pure noise can and will still report an apparently improved in-sample win rate at some threshold (with only 10 or even a few hundred instances, a model can always find some historical split that separates them), but that split is fit to the specific noise realization in the training sample, not a genuine relationship — it collapses out-of-sample. This is precisely the honest-backtest discipline of White's Reality Check & Hansen's SPA Test, applied one layer deeper: an apparent improvement from filtering needs the same genuine out-of-sample scrutiny as any other backtested result, not a free pass because "meta-labelling" is the mechanism doing the filtering.

✅
Verified against this repo's actual code

A genuine two-stage primary → secondary architecture, and a genuine triple-barrier walker — both real, both worth reading precisely

Triple-barrier labeling: pylego/barrier_race.py's _first_touch() walks a running cumulative max of highs and cumulative min of lows forward from an entry bar, finds the first bar index at which a take-profit price is touched (tp_arr) and the first at which a stop-loss price is touched (sl_arr), and returns whichever comes first — falling back to 'timeout' at max_bars_ahead (the vertical barrier) if neither fires — exactly the upper/lower/vertical first-touch race this lesson derives. It is not a one-off: race_trades() and race_grid() both call it, and it is reused by AnalogML/ml_walkforward.py (whose own docstring calls it "pylego.barrier_race's triple barrier"), the Volatility Bot's check_barriers() in pylego/broker/paper.py, pylego/strategy/volatility.py's trade_spec(), and the range-line/Asia-range engines in js/rangeLineAnalyser.js and js/asiaRangeEngine.js.

Meta-labelling: bot/modules/ml_confidence.py's MLConfidenceModule is a genuine two-stage architecture, not just similar vocabulary. Direction is inherited from upstream primary modules — _inherited_direction() reads whichever of gold_macro, macro_regime, or vol_gate already produced a LONG/SHORT signal — and a separately-trained secondary ensemble (an averaged XGBoost + LightGBM pair, trained by bot/scripts/train_gold_model.py on closed-trade outcome_hit_tp / outcome_hit_sl columns — the same triple-barrier-style "which barrier fired" framing this lesson builds from scratch) predicts a win-probability used only to scale position size. The module's own docstring names this explicitly ("meta-labelling confidence layer," citing what it calls "the Chapter 17 meta-labelling pattern" — worth a note: in the actual López de Prado (2018) text, triple-barrier labeling and meta-labelling are Chapters 3–4, cited in Further Reading below; the module's own comment may simply be referencing a different internal chapter numbering). One honest divergence from the textbook version covered in this lesson: this implementation is deliberately more conservative than a pure filter — passed is hard-coded True and the module documents itself as "advisory-only... it NEVER blocks a trade," only ever scaling size down (never up) when the predicted win-probability sits below 0.5, down to a floor of 0.4×. Classic meta-labelling can also skip a low-confidence trade outright; this module chooses to size it down instead and never skip it — a real, named design decision, not an oversight.

A last note, since this is the final lesson of this category. This lesson's labeling discipline and the previous lesson's statistical honesty — White's Reality Check & Hansen's SPA Test — are answers to the same underlying question, asked at two different points in the research process. That lesson asks whether an apparent backtested edge, once you account for having tried many strategies, actually reflects something real rather than the best-looking result of a big enough search. This lesson asks whether the training label a model learned from ever described a real, tradable payoff in the first place. Both exist because a backtest can look convincing for reasons that have nothing to do with a genuine, persistent market relationship — one from testing too many things, the other from measuring the wrong thing to begin with.

Section 06

Key Takeaways

✗A fixed-horizon return label describes a trade nobody with real risk controls actually places. A real position exits at whichever of a stop-loss, take-profit, or time limit comes first — not at a fixed bar count regardless of path.
✓Triple-barrier labelling sets an upper, lower, and vertical barrier and labels by whichever is touched first — walking the real forward path exactly as a live position would experience it.
✓Barrier widths scale with a point-in-time volatility estimate, not a fixed pip/dollar distance, so the same barrier logic adapts sensibly across calm and violent regimes.
✓Meta-labelling separates "which direction" from "how confident," letting a weak, noisy primary signal be filtered or sized by a secondary model trained only on the same triple-barrier labels, restricted to fired instances.
⚠Filtering by meta-probability raises precision by taking fewer trades — it is a tradeoff, not a free lunch, and it can only rank an existing signal's instances, never manufacture edge from a signal with zero real information content.
✓This repo genuinely implements both ideas — pylego/barrier_race.py's first-touch walker and bot/modules/ml_confidence.py's primary→secondary architecture — worth reading directly, not taking on faith.
Section 07

Self-test

Q1. Why can a fixed-horizon return label (e.g., "return over the next 20 bars") disagree with what a real trading position actually earned?
A real position with risk controls is closed by whichever of a stop-loss, a take-profit, or a maximum holding time is reached first — not held blindly for a fixed number of bars regardless of the path in between. The price 20 bars later can be higher even though the path first blew through where a real stop-loss would have closed the position, and it can be lower even though the path first touched a real take-profit well before bar 20. The fixed-horizon label measures a snapshot; a real position's outcome is determined by the first barrier its own exit rules would have hit.
Q2. State the triple-barrier labeling rule precisely: what determines the label, and what are the three possible outcomes?
Set an upper barrier \(U\) (take-profit), a lower barrier \(L\) (stop-loss), and a vertical barrier \(V\) (a time limit \(h\) bars after entry). Walk forward from entry; the label is \(+1\) if \(U\) is touched first, \(-1\) if \(L\) is touched first, and either \(0\) or the small realized return \(r_\tau\) if the vertical barrier \(V\) is reached without either horizontal barrier having fired first. The label is decided entirely by which of the three is touched earliest — a genuine race, not a comparison of all three at some fixed later point.
Q3. Why scale the upper and lower barrier widths to a volatility estimate rather than using a fixed pip or dollar distance for every entry?
A fixed-width barrier doesn't adapt to changing volatility regimes: the same 20-pip stop that is comfortably wide during a calm period can be trivially touched by ordinary noise during a volatile one, and the same 20-pip target that's easily reached in a volatile period can be unrealistically far in a calm one. Scaling both barriers to a volatility estimate — a GARCH conditional variance, or a Garman-Klass/Yang-Zhang range-based estimate — keeps the barrier's real difficulty roughly comparable across regimes, which keeps the resulting labels meaningfully comparable across time as well.
Q4. In meta-labelling, what exactly is the primary model's job, and what exactly is the secondary (meta) model's job? Why does splitting the problem this way help?
The primary model or rule decides direction only — long, short, or no signal — and can be weak or noisy; it does not need to be profitable on its own. The secondary (meta) model is trained only on instances where the primary signal fired, using the same triple-barrier labels, and predicts the probability that this specific fired instance wins. Splitting the problem helps because "find genuine alpha from raw market data" is a hard, largely unsolved problem, while "given that a signal already flagged this instance, is it more or less likely than average to win?" is a narrower, separable, and often much easier problem — the meta-model never has to find opportunities from scratch, only rank ones already surfaced.
Q5. In the worked example, filtering the 10 fired signals to only those with meta-probability above 0.60 raised the win rate from 50.0% to 75.0%, while taking only 4 of the original 10 signals. What does this demonstrate, and what is the necessary tradeoff?
It demonstrates that the meta-model's predicted probability carried real information about which fired signals were more likely to win — filtering on it genuinely raised the precision of the trades actually taken. The necessary tradeoff is trade count: raising precision this way removed six of the ten original opportunities entirely. The meta-model did not create new winners out of the discarded six; it separated the original ten into a smaller, higher-quality subset and a larger, lower-quality one that gets skipped.
Q6. Suppose a primary signal's fired instances are, in truth, no better than random entries — literally zero information content. Can a meta-model still produce a genuinely profitable filtered subset out-of-sample?
No. Meta-labelling can only filter and re-rank structure that already exists in the primary signal's fired instances; it cannot manufacture information that isn't there. A meta-model trained against a primary signal with zero real information content can still report an apparently improved in-sample win rate at some threshold — with a finite, often small sample, some split that looks like it separates winners from losers can usually be found — but that split fits the specific noise realization in the training sample, not a genuine relationship, and it will not survive genuine out-of-sample testing.
