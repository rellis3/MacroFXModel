Gamma Exposure & Dealer Hedging Flows

Gamma is a second derivative of an option-pricing formula — but aggregated across an entire open-interest book, it becomes a real, mechanical forecast of how much buying or selling pressure dealer hedging will inject into a market, and where that pressure runs out.

CONCEPT — not built or tested in this repo ~17 min read Prereqs: Black-Scholes-Merton Option Pricing
TL;DR
  • Dealer gamma exposure (GEX) at a strike is \(\Gamma \times \text{OI} \times 100 \times S^2 \times 0.01\) — gamma converted from "delta change per $1 move" into "dollar delta change per 1% move," scaled up by the contract multiplier and the open interest sitting at that strike.
  • A call and a put at the same strike and expiry have identical gamma (a direct consequence of put-call parity) — so it's the dealer's own long/short sign, not whether the option is a call or a put, that decides whether hedging flow amplifies or dampens a price move.
  • The standard simplifying assumption — dealers are net short most of the OI they wrote to a public that's net long options — makes dealer GEX negative at most strikes; that sign is an assumption baked into the convention, not something directly observable from open interest alone.
  • Short dealer gamma means hedging flow has the same sign as the price move (buy as it rises, sell as it falls) — destabilizing. Long dealer gamma means hedging flow has the opposite sign — stabilizing, mean-reverting.
  • The worked example shows ATM gamma roughly doubling from 30 to 7 days to expiry, then more than doubling again into the final day — the numeric substance behind "gamma effects intensify near expiry."
  • A "wall" isn't sellers pushing back — it's the point where the hedging demand that was fuelling a move disappears because the hedge is already complete. When a wall breaks instead, that same mechanism reverses into a squeeze.
Section 01

Why this matters

The Black-Scholes-Merton lesson derives delta (\(\Delta = \partial C/\partial S\)) and gamma (\(\Gamma = \partial^2 C/\partial S^2\)) as partial derivatives of the option-pricing formula, and shows why gamma for an at-the-money option rises as time to expiry shrinks. That lesson stops at the level of a single option. This lesson picks up exactly where it left off and asks a different question: what happens when you sum gamma across an entire open-interest book — every outstanding contract at every strike — held by the dealers who wrote those options to the public?

The answer is a real, mechanical source of market structure. A dealer who sold options doesn't just hold a static hedge; the hedge has to be rebalanced continuously as price moves, and gamma is precisely the rate at which that rebalancing has to happen. Add up gamma times open interest across a strike ladder and you get a genuine, formula-derived estimate of how much forced, price-insensitive buying or selling a given move in the underlying will trigger from the dealer community — not a chart pattern, not folklore, a direct consequence of the calculus in the previous lesson. This is the mechanism behind the "walls," "magnets," and "squeezes" that show up on any sufficiently liquid options-underlying chart, and this lesson formalizes it: the dealer gamma exposure (GEX) formula, the rate at which hedging flow changes, the sign logic that decides whether that flow calms a market or accelerates it, and the "gamma-flip" level some practitioners watch as a structural pivot.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Walls don't push back — they run out of fuel

The single most counterintuitive fact in this whole subject: a "wall" is not a wall of sellers lying in wait, ready to slam price back down the moment it arrives. If that were true, you'd expect to see visible selling pressure building as price approaches the level. What actually happens is closer to the opposite — dealer hedging fuels the move on the way in, and then that fuel simply runs out.

Picture a large concentration of call open interest sitting above the current price. As price grinds up toward that strike, the calls' delta rises — they behave more and more like owning the stock outright — and the dealers who sold those calls have to keep buying the underlying to stay hedged. That buying itself adds upward pressure, which pushes price further toward the strike, which raises delta further, which forces more buying. It's a self-reinforcing loop, and from the outside it looks exactly like a rally with real conviction behind it.

Then price actually reaches the strike. The calls are now deep enough in the money that their delta has saturated near 1.0 — they behave almost exactly like the stock itself, and there's no more "coming due" delta left to hedge. The dealer's hedge is complete. The buying that was propping up the rally simply stops — not because anyone stepped in to sell, but because the mechanical demand that had been pushing price higher has nothing left to do. Price stalls with no obvious trigger, which is exactly why it's so often misread as "sellers defending a level" when the real mechanism is an absence of further buying, not the appearance of selling.

Section 02

The math, step by step

Three pieces build on top of the Black-Scholes Greeks: a formula for a single strike's dealer gamma exposure, a rule for the sign of that exposure, and the rate at which it forces hedging flow as price moves. Take them in order.

Dealer gamma exposure (GEX) — the standard formula

For a single strike \(k\), with the underlying currently at \(S\), the (unsigned) dollar gamma exposure carried by the open interest at that strike is:

\[ \text{GEX}_k = \Gamma_k \times \text{OI}_k \times 100 \times S^2 \times 0.01 \]

The \(S^2 \times 0.01\) term is worth deriving rather than memorizing, because otherwise it looks arbitrary. Start from \(\Gamma_k \times \text{OI}_k \times 100\): this is "how many shares of the underlying the dealer's aggregate hedge position changes by, per $1 move in \(S\)" — a share count, not a dollar figure. Multiply by \(S\) (the price of one share) and it becomes "how many dollars the hedge position's value changes by, per $1 move" — this is sometimes called dollar gamma on its own. Options-flow analytics conventionally quote GEX not per $1 move but per 1% move of the underlying, because a $1 move means something completely different for a $20 stock than for a $2,000 one. A 1% move in dollar terms is \(S \times 0.01\). So multiplying dollar gamma by \(S \times 0.01\) instead of by 1 rescales the whole quantity from "per $1 move" to "per 1% move":

\[ \underbrace{\Gamma_k\,\text{OI}_k\,100}_{\text{shares per \$1 move}} \times \underbrace{S}_{\text{\$ per share}} \times \underbrace{(S \times 0.01)}_{\text{\$ representing a 1\% move}} = \Gamma_k\,\text{OI}_k\,100\,S^2\,(0.01) \]
ΓₖOIₖ100the raw hedge-flow rate, in shares per $1 move
× Sconverts shares to dollars
× (S × 0.01)rescales from "per $1" to "per 1%"
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

GEX just asks: "if this stock moves 1%, how many dollars' worth of stock will dealers be forced to buy or sell to stay hedged?" Gamma alone tells you how fast a dealer's hedge has to change; multiplying it out by how many contracts exist and how big the stock's price is turns that into a real dollar number you can compare across strikes and across entirely different stocks.

Why calls and puts carry the same gamma

A genuinely useful fact falls straight out of put-call parity, the no-arbitrage relationship between a call and a put on the same strike and expiry (referenced but not derived in the Black-Scholes lesson):

\[ C - P = S - Ke^{-rT} \]

Differentiate both sides twice with respect to \(S\). The right-hand side, \(S - Ke^{-rT}\), is linear in \(S\) — its second derivative is exactly zero, since \(K\), \(e^{-rT}\), and the "1" from differentiating \(S\) once are all constants that vanish on the second pass. So:

\[ \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial S^2} - \frac{\partial^2 P}{\partial S^2} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Gamma_{\text{call}} = \Gamma_{\text{put}} \]
Γcall = Γputidentical at the same strike and expiry, always — not an approximation

This matters because it means \(\Gamma_k\) in the GEX formula above is the same number whether the open interest at strike \(k\) is calls, puts, or a mix of both. What differs between a call and a put is only delta — a long call and a long put have opposite-signed delta, but identical, positive gamma. The consequence: the sign of dealer GEX at a strike is determined entirely by who's on which side of the trade (is the dealer long or short that open interest), never by whether the contract happens to be a call or a put.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

A call and a put at the same strike need the exact same amount of re-hedging effort as price moves — that part is fixed by math, no exceptions. What actually decides whether that hedging pushes price up or down is simply whether the dealer bought or sold the option in the first place, the same way it matters whether you're the person who owes money or the person who's owed it, regardless of what the debt is called.

The dealer's sign — an assumption, not an observation

Any long option position (call or put) has positive gamma. A dealer who wrote (sold) an option to a customer holds the opposite position — short the option — and a short option position has negative gamma, since shorting simply flips the sign of every Greek. The standard simplifying assumption used across options-flow analytics is that dealers exist primarily to accommodate customer demand, and in aggregate, retail and institutional customers are net buyers of both calls and puts — buying calls for upside speculation, buying puts for downside protection or speculation. Under that assumption, dealers are net sellers (writers) at most strikes, which makes dealer gamma negative for both the call side and the put side of the book — not because calls and puts behave differently (they don't; their gamma is identical), but because the dealer is short both.

State this plainly: open interest tells you how many contracts are outstanding at a strike. It does not tell you who holds the long side and who holds the short side. "Dealers are net short this strike" is a standard, usually-reasonable, but genuinely unverifiable assumption layered on top of the raw OI number — not a fact read directly off the data. Every GEX estimate inherits this assumption, and different data providers who assume differently will disagree on the sign of individual strikes even while agreeing on the raw open interest.
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Knowing how many raincoats were sold in a town tells you nothing about who's wearing them versus who's giving them away as gifts. Open interest is the same: it counts contracts outstanding, not who's on the buying end and who's on the selling end. "Dealers are net short" is everyone's best guess about who's on which side, not something you can actually read off the raw count.

Writing the sign explicitly, let \(s_k \in \{-1, +1\}\) denote the assumed sign of the dealer's net position at strike \(k\) (\(-1\) for the standard "dealer net short" case, \(+1\) for the less common case where dealers are assumed net long, e.g. specific strikes where a dealer's own hedging created a long position). The signed dealer GEX at strike \(k\) is:

\[ \text{DealerGEX}_k = s_k \times \Gamma_k \times \text{OI}_k \times 100 \times S^2 \times 0.01 \]
sₖ = −1the typical case — dealer wrote the options, is net short
sₖ = +1the atypical case — dealer ended up net long that strike

A total, book-wide dealer GEX simply sums this signed quantity across every strike (and both option types, since \(\Gamma_{\text{call}} = \Gamma_{\text{put}}\) makes it natural to combine call and put OI at a strike into one term): \(\text{TotalDealerGEX} = \sum_k \text{DealerGEX}_k\). Under the standard assumption (\(s_k = -1\) everywhere), this total is negative — dealers are net short gamma across the board — which is the commonly cited "dealers are usually short gamma" claim, restated precisely rather than asserted.

The delta-hedging flow rate

Gamma isn't only useful for computing a static dollar figure — it directly gives the rate at which a dealer's required hedge position changes as price moves, which is the real mechanical content behind "gamma tells you how powerful the hedging flow is." Consider the open interest at one strike, all assumed held short by the dealer (the standard case). Each contract's delta is \(\Delta\) (identical in magnitude for the corresponding long position, whether that's a call or a put), so the customers collectively hold \(\Delta \times \text{OI} \times 100\) shares' worth of delta, and the dealer — the opposite side — must hold that many shares long to stay flat. Differentiate that required hedge position with respect to \(S\):

\[ \frac{d(\text{hedge shares})}{dS} = \frac{d}{dS}\Big[\Delta \times \text{OI} \times 100\Big] = \frac{d\Delta}{dS} \times \text{OI} \times 100 = \Gamma \times \text{OI} \times 100 \]
Δ×OI×100the dealer's required long-hedge position, in shares
dΔ/dS = Γgamma is exactly the derivative that matters
Γ×OI×100shares the dealer must buy per $1 further move — always positive when short this OI

This is always positive (since \(\Gamma > 0\) and \(\text{OI} \ge 0\)): for OI the dealer is short, a $1 further rise in \(S\) always demands more buying, regardless of whether the underlying options are calls or puts. What flips the direction of the resulting market effect isn't this formula — it's whether the dealer is short (needs to chase the move, buying into strength) or long (needs to fade the move, selling into strength) that OI in the first place, covered next.

Long vs. short gamma — deriving the regime table

Put the sign logic together. If the dealer is short the OI at a strike (the standard case, \(s_k=-1\)), the dealer's own gamma is negative, and — as just derived — the dealer must buy as price rises and (by the mirror-image argument, selling as delta falls) sell as price falls. That hedging flow has the same sign as the price move itself: buying while price is already rising, selling while it's already falling. It adds fuel to whatever direction the market is already moving in — destabilizing, trend-amplifying flow.

If the dealer is instead long that OI (\(s_k=+1\)), the dealer's own gamma is positive, and the required hedge trade flips: as price rises the dealer's own delta grows on its own, so the dealer needs to sell stock to stay flat; as price falls, the dealer needs to buy. This hedging flow has the opposite sign to the price move — selling into strength, buying into weakness. It dampens the move — stabilizing, mean-reverting flow.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Picture a dealer standing on a see-saw, forced to keep re-balancing it every time price shifts. If they're "short gamma," they have to keep piling weight onto whichever side is already going down — making the see-saw swing harder and faster, like adding fuel to a fire. If they're "long gamma," they do the opposite: they pull weight off the side that's dropping, which calms the see-saw back toward level. Same see-saw, opposite instincts, depending on which side of the trade the dealer is on.

Short dealer gamma (Γ_dealer < 0) — the typical case

Price rises → dealer buys. Price falls → dealer sells. Hedging flow has the same sign as the move.

Market effect: amplifying / destabilizing. Moves tend to accelerate once they start.

Long dealer gamma (Γ_dealer > 0)

Price rises → dealer sells. Price falls → dealer buys. Hedging flow has the opposite sign to the move.

Market effect: dampening / stabilizing. Moves tend to get faded back toward where they started.

The gamma-flip level

Since \(\text{TotalDealerGEX}\) sums \(s_k \Gamma_k(S)\,\text{OI}_k\, 100\,S^2\,0.01\) across every strike, and \(\Gamma_k(S)\) itself (from the Black-Scholes formula) depends on where the current spot \(S\) sits relative to each strike \(k\) — peaking when \(S \approx k\) and decaying as they move apart — the total is a genuine function of \(S\), not a fixed number. As a thought experiment, evaluate that total at a range of hypothetical spot levels: some strikes' contributions shrink as spot moves away from them, others grow as spot approaches them, and (if the signed \(s_k\) values aren't uniform, or the OI distribution is lopsided enough) the running total can cross zero at some particular price. That crossing point is the gamma-flip level: on one side of it, aggregate dealer gamma is net positive (stabilizing hedging flow dominates); on the other, net negative (destabilizing flow dominates).

Two honest caveats belong here, not as an afterthought. First, the flip level is estimated from the current OI distribution and the assumed \(s_k\) signs — it is not a fixed constant, and it shifts every time OI at any strike changes (a new position opens, an old one closes) or every time the front-month expiry rolls. Second, it inherits the same unverifiable-sign problem as any single strike's dealer GEX: if the true (unobservable) mix of who's long and who's short differs from the standard assumption, the true flip level differs from the estimated one too.

Worked example

ATM gamma at three expiries, then a toy three-strike GEX aggregation

Fix \(S = 100\), \(\sigma = 20\%\) throughout, using the at-the-money approximation \(\Gamma_{\text{ATM}} \approx \phi(0)/(S\sigma\sqrt{T}) = 0.3989/(S\sigma\sqrt{T})\), with \(\phi(0)=1/\sqrt{2\pi}\approx 0.3989\) — this is exactly the approximation the Black-Scholes lesson's own gamma formula reduces to when \(d_1 \approx 0\) (at the money).

1

30 days to expiry

\[ T=\tfrac{30}{365}=0.0822,\quad \sigma\sqrt{T}=0.20\times0.2867=0.0573 \] \[ \Gamma_{\text{ATM}} = \frac{0.3989}{100 \times 0.0573} = \frac{0.3989}{5.734} \approx 0.0696 \]
2

7 days to expiry

\[ T=\tfrac{7}{365}=0.0192,\quad \sigma\sqrt{T}=0.20\times0.1385=0.0277 \] \[ \Gamma_{\text{ATM}} = \frac{0.3989}{100 \times 0.0277} = \frac{0.3989}{2.770} \approx 0.1440 \]

Roughly 2.1× the 30-day value — for the same open interest, the hedging flow rate has already doubled.

3

1 day to expiry

\[ T=\tfrac{1}{365}=0.00274,\quad \sigma\sqrt{T}=0.20\times0.0523=0.0105 \] \[ \Gamma_{\text{ATM}} = \frac{0.3989}{100 \times 0.0105} = \frac{0.3989}{1.047} \approx 0.3811 \]

Another 2.6× jump from the 7-day figure — and roughly 5.5× the 30-day figure. This is the \(1/\sqrt{T}\) relationship doing its work: gamma doesn't rise linearly as expiry approaches, it accelerates, which is exactly why the final trading day before expiry behaves so differently from the two weeks before it.

Real-world trading scenario

The wall becomes fuel — a gamma squeeze

A heavily traded index future sits at 4,480, grinding higher through a quiet session. The nearest-expiry options chain shows a large call open-interest concentration at the 4,500 strike — several multiples the size of neighboring strikes, the kind of outlier that gets flagged as a "call wall." Dealers are assumed net short that OI (the standard case).

As price grinds from 4,480 toward 4,500, those calls' delta rises steadily, and per the hedge-flow-rate formula above, dealers must keep buying futures to stay hedged — buying that itself nudges price higher, exactly the self-reinforcing loop from the intuition box. Ordinarily this would stall right at 4,500: delta saturates near 1.0, the hedge completes, buying demand evaporates, and the move loses its fuel — the textbook "wall holds" outcome.

Instead, a stronger-than-expected data release hits mid-session and price rips straight through 4,500 with real momentum. The calls that were sitting comfortably at-the-money are now suddenly in-the-money and gaining delta faster than the dealers' existing hedge accounted for — gamma near that strike was near its local peak, so the delta jump per additional point of price is large. Dealers who thought their hedge was nearly complete now need meaningfully more long exposure, immediately, and the resulting catch-up buying accelerates the move well past 4,500 before it finds the next level of resistance. The exact same open interest that created resistance on the way up becomes the fuel for the breakout once it's breached — a gamma squeeze.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

Nobody trades dealer GEX as a standalone signal in isolation — the sign ambiguity flagged above is too real for that — but a few applications of the mechanism above are common across options-flow-aware trading desks and the retail tools built to mimic them:

📊

Options-flow / dealer-positioning analytics

Several commercial and free data services estimate aggregate dealer gamma from public open-interest data using exactly the sign conventions above, publishing it as a daily positioning read alongside price.

🧭

A regime filter, not an entry signal

The estimated sign of aggregate dealer gamma (net long vs. net short) is used as context for how a given size of order flow should be expected to behave — amplified in a short-gamma regime, dampened in a long-gamma one — layered on top of an existing setup rather than traded on its own.

📐

The gamma-flip level as a structural pivot

Some practitioners track the estimated gamma-flip price as a level worth watching precisely because the character of price action either side of it is theorized to differ — calmer/mean-reverting on one side, choppier/trend-prone on the other — while treating the level itself as an estimate that moves, not a fixed line.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where this gets over-trusted

Open interest is lagged. Public OI figures typically reflect the previous session's close, not a live, intraday feed. A large intraday move can already have changed the underlying positioning well before the next OI update reflects it — any GEX estimate built on it is describing yesterday's book, not this instant's.

Dealer positioning is assumed, not observed. As stressed above, whether dealers are actually net long or net short any given strike is inferred from a standard convention about who tends to buy vs. write options — never a directly observable fact from raw OI. Every GEX number, every gamma-flip estimate, inherits this assumption and can be meaningfully wrong if the true positioning differs from it.

"Active pinning" vs. "hedging byproduct" is a genuinely open question. There are two distinct stories for why price sometimes gravitates toward high-OI strikes: one where dealer hedging mechanically produces the effect as a byproduct (the mechanism this lesson derives), and one where market participants actively trade toward those levels because they expect the pattern to hold. Both are discussed in academic and practitioner research on dealer gamma imbalances and their relationship to realized volatility, and the honest state of that discussion is unsettled, not resolved in either direction — treat any confident claim that it's "definitely" one or the other with real skepticism.

Section 05

Self-test

Q1. Why do a call and a put at the same strike and expiry have exactly the same gamma, even though their deltas are different (and opposite in sign)?
Put-call parity states \(C - P = S - Ke^{-rT}\). The right-hand side is a linear function of \(S\) (the strike and discount factor are constants), so its second derivative with respect to \(S\) is exactly zero. Differentiating both sides twice therefore gives \(\partial^2C/\partial S^2 - \partial^2P/\partial S^2 = 0\), i.e. \(\Gamma_{\text{call}} = \Gamma_{\text{put}}\), always, not just approximately. Delta differs because the first derivative of the linear right-hand side is 1, not 0 — \(\Delta_{\text{call}} - \Delta_{\text{put}} = 1\) — so delta shifts by a constant between call and put while gamma (the second derivative) doesn't shift at all.
Q2. Why is "dealers are net short gamma" described in this lesson as an assumption rather than a fact?
Open interest only reports how many contracts are outstanding at a strike — it says nothing about which counterparty (customer or dealer) holds the long side versus the short side. The claim that dealers are net short most strikes rests on a separate, standard but unverifiable assumption: that customers, in aggregate, are net buyers of both calls and puts, making dealers the net writers who absorb that demand. That assumption is usually reasonable but is not something directly observable in the OI data itself, which is exactly why different data providers can disagree about specific strikes' signs.
Q3. Derive why short dealer gamma produces "amplifying" hedging flow while long dealer gamma produces "dampening" flow.
A dealer short an option has negative gamma; a dealer long an option has positive gamma. The required hedge position is the negative of the dealer's own delta (holding stock to offset it), so its rate of change with price is \(-\Gamma_{\text{dealer}}\times\text{OI}\times 100\). If \(\Gamma_{\text{dealer}}<0\) (short gamma), that rate is positive: the dealer must buy more as price rises and sell as it falls — the same direction as the move, which is destabilizing since it adds momentum to whatever's already happening. If \(\Gamma_{\text{dealer}}>0\) (long gamma), the rate is negative: the dealer must sell as price rises and buy as it falls — the opposite direction to the move, which dampens it back toward where it started.
Q4. In the worked example, ATM gamma went from ≈0.070 (30 DTE) to ≈0.144 (7 DTE) to ≈0.381 (1 DTE). Why does the increase accelerate rather than stay proportional to how many days were lost?
Because \(\Gamma_{\text{ATM}} \propto 1/\sqrt{T}\), not \(1/T\). Going from 30 to 7 days removes 23 days but \(\sqrt{T}\) only shrinks from \(\sqrt{30/365}\approx0.287\) to \(\sqrt{7/365}\approx0.138\) — roughly halved, so gamma roughly doubles. Going from 7 to 1 day removes only 6 days, but because \(T\) is already small, that's a much larger relative shrinkage of \(\sqrt{T}\) (down to \(\sqrt{1/365}\approx0.052\), well under half of the 7-day value), so gamma jumps by a larger multiple even though fewer calendar days were lost. The square-root relationship means the same number of remaining days matters far more when there are few of them left than when there are many — which is exactly why the final trading day carries disproportionately more hedging intensity than an equivalent one-day shift three weeks out.
Q5. What is the gamma-flip level, and why does it move even on a day when price itself doesn't?
It's the price level at which the aggregate, signed dealer GEX summed across the whole open-interest ladder crosses zero — net stabilizing (long gamma) on one side, net destabilizing (short gamma) on the other. It's estimated, not fixed, because it depends on the current OI distribution (how much is outstanding at each strike) and the assumed sign at each strike, both of which can change without price moving at all — new option positions opening or closing shift OI, and an expiry rolling to the next contract month changes the whole distribution being summed. A stale gamma-flip estimate from yesterday's OI can already be wrong today even if spot hasn't moved.
Q6. In the "wall becomes fuel" scenario, why does the wall breaking cause acceleration rather than a reversal?
Approaching the wall, dealers (short those calls) are buying to keep up with rising delta, which is what pushed price toward the strike in the first place. Ordinarily that buying would evaporate right at the strike once delta saturates near 1.0 and the hedge is complete — the "wall holds" case. But because gamma is near its local peak right around that strike, a strong enough move can carry price through it fast enough that delta jumps by more than the dealers' existing hedge already accounted for. Instead of the hedge completing gently, the dealers suddenly need more long exposure than they have, forcing urgent catch-up buying — the same mechanical demand that built resistance on the way up becomes forced buying on the way through, which is why a broken wall so often accelerates rather than reverses.
