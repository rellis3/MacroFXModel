Drawdowns, Risk of Ruin, and Why Good Systems Get Abandoned

A system with a real edge still loses eight times in a row, still sits underwater for months, and still makes you doubt it at exactly the wrong moment. This lesson does the arithmetic of the bad stretch — so you can tell "normal" from "broken" before you are inside one.

CONCEPT — explained here; desk verdicts quoted where tested ~20 min read Prereqs: Kelly Criterion (helpful); Monte Carlo (helpful)
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • Losses are asymmetric. To recover a drawdown \(d\) you need a gain of \(d/(1-d)\): −20% needs +25%, −50% needs +100%, −75% needs +300%.
  • Losing streaks are normal. With a 55% win rate, a run of 8 losses has a 1-in-595 chance of starting on any given trade — but it is a 24% chance somewhere in 300 trades and more likely than not by about 750. A run of 6 is more likely than not by 152 trades.
  • The worst drawdown keeps growing with time and with volatility, even for a system that makes money. The longer you trade, the deeper the deepest hole you will have sat in.
  • Bet size, not win rate, decides ruin. The same 55% coin-flip edge is comfortable at 1–2% risk per trade and close to a coin toss for halving your account at full Kelly (10%).
  • The human failure is timing: people judge a system by its recent results, so they abandon it near the bottom of its drawdown — and money tends to arrive after good runs and leave after bad ones.
  • The desk's verdict: "a deep drawdown means the edge is gone" is untested here; the desk's one validated sleeve (63% win, ~109 trades) should expect a run of 4–5 losses; and the honest flip side, FX trend following, tested null — some drawdowns are no edge at all, and only an out-of-sample test can tell you which.
Section 01

Why this matters

The previous lesson, Herding, Narratives and Bubbles, was about how a crowd talks itself into a trade. This one is about how a single person talks themselves out of one. Most good strategies are not killed by the market. They are killed by the person running them, during a losing stretch that was always going to happen, at a moment when the arithmetic says "this is normal" and every instinct says "this is broken".

You will meet three pieces of maths here: the recovery asymmetry, the probability of a losing streak, and how the deepest drawdown grows with time and bet size. They connect directly to the Kelly Criterion (how much to bet), Monte Carlo simulation (how to see a distribution of futures rather than one), and Sharpe ratios (which say nothing about how deep the holes along the way will be).

💡
Plain-English intuition

A loaded coin still lands tails four times in a row

Picture a coin weighted so it lands heads 55 times in 100. You win a pound on heads and lose a pound on tails, so in the long run you win. Flip it a few hundred times and you will still see long stretches of tails. Not because the coin changed — because long sequences of a random thing contain long runs. That is what randomness looks like up close.

Now imagine you cannot see the coin. All you see is your bank balance, which has been falling for a month. Is the coin still loaded, or did someone swap it? The balance alone cannot tell you. This lesson gives you the numbers that say how long a run of tails a loaded coin produces, so you can decide in advance what would actually count as evidence the coin was swapped.

Section 02

The math of the bad stretch

1. Recovery is asymmetric

A drawdown is the fall from the highest point your account has reached (its peak) to where it is now, as a fraction of that peak. If you are down a fraction \(d\), what is left is \(1-d\), and to get back to the peak that remainder has to grow by

\[ g = \frac{1}{1-d} - 1 = \frac{d}{1-d} \]

Small drawdowns are almost symmetric; big ones are not. That is the first reason a trader's real job is to keep drawdowns in the region where the curve is still flat.

You have 10 sweets and lose half: now you have 5. To get back to 10 you need to double your 5 — winning half of 5 only gets you to 7 and a half. Losing a lot is quick; climbing back is slow.

2. Losing streaks: compute them, don't feel them

Suppose each trade is independent, wins with probability \(p\) and loses with probability \(q = 1-p\). The chance that a particular trade starts a run of \(k\) losses is just \(q^k\). With \(p = 0.55\), a run of 8 starting right now has probability \(0.45^8 \approx 0.0017\) — about 1 in 595. That number feels reassuring, and it is the wrong number. You do not care about one particular starting point; you care whether a run of 8 happens anywhere in the trades you will take.

That is answered exactly by counting the sequences with no run of \(k\). Let \(a_n\) be the probability that \(n\) trades contain no losing run of length \(k\) or more. Every such sequence starts with \(j\) losses (\(0 \le j \le k-1\)), then a win, then a shorter sequence that also has no run:

\[ a_n = \sum_{j=0}^{k-1} p\,q^{j}\,a_{n-j-1}, \qquad a_n = 1 \text{ for } n < k, \qquad P(\text{run} \ge k \text{ in } n) = 1 - a_n \]

p q^jj losses in a row, then the win that breaks the run
a(n−j−1)the rest of the sequence must also stay clear of a k-run
1 − aₙthe answer you want: a run of k shows up somewhere

Do anything enough times and the "rare" bad patterns turn up. That is not bad luck being unfair; it is just how lots of rolls behave.

3. The deepest drawdown grows with time and volatility

Let \(V_t\) be the account value and \(M_t = \max_{s \le t} V_s\) its running peak. The drawdown at time \(t\) is \(D_t = 1 - V_t/M_t\), and the maximum drawdown over a period is the worst of these, \(\text{MDD}_T = \max_{t \le T} D_t\).

\[ D_t = 1 - \frac{V_t}{M_t}, \qquad \text{MDD}_T = \max_{t \le T} D_t \]
Vₜaccount value after trade t
Mₜthe highest value reached so far — the peak
Dₜhow far below that peak you are now
MDD_Tthe worst hole over the whole period

Because it is a maximum, MDD can only stay the same or get worse as \(T\) grows. For a process with positive drift \(\mu\) and volatility \(\sigma\), Magdon-Ismail and co-authors (2004) showed that the expected maximum drawdown grows roughly like \(\sqrt{T}\) over short horizons and then keeps growing, more slowly — logarithmically — over long ones, with a scale set by \(\sigma^2/\mu\). Double the volatility at the same drift and the typical worst hole gets much deeper; halve the edge and it does too. A positive edge stops the drawdown from growing without limit; it does not stop it growing.

The simulation below makes this concrete for a fixed-fraction trader: each trade risks a fraction \(r\) of current equity, wins \(b\,r\) with probability \(p\), and loses \(r\) otherwise. With \(p = 0.55\), \(b = 1\) and 2% risk per trade, across 2,000 simulated traders (seed 42):

The same edge, the same size: the further you go, the more certain you are to be up — and the deeper the worst hole you will have sat in along the way. One trader in five is still underwater after 100 trades of a genuinely winning system.

4. Risk of ruin is a bet-size problem

"Ruin" need not mean zero. For a fixed-fraction trader the account never literally hits zero, but a fall to half is, for most people and most mandates, the end: clients leave, limits trip, the trader quits. So define ruin here as equity touching 50% of its starting value at any point. The edge per trade, in units of the amount risked, is the expectancy \(E = p\,b - q\); the growth-maximising bet is the Kelly fraction:

\[ E = p\,b - q, \qquad f^{*} = p - \frac{q}{b} = \frac{E}{b} \]
Eexpected profit per trade, in multiples of the amount risked (R)
bpayoff ratio: average win divided by average loss
f*Kelly fraction: the share of equity to risk that maximises long-run growth

For \(p = 0.55\), \(b = 1\): \(E = 0.10R\) and \(f^{*} = 10\%\). Simulating 300 trades at different bet sizes (2,000 traders each, seed 42) shows how the same edge turns from comfortable to dangerous purely through size:

Full Kelly gives the fastest median growth and roughly a four-in-ten chance of being cut in half along the way (in the continuous-time idealisation, the chance of ever falling to a fraction \(x\) of your starting wealth at full Kelly is \(x\) itself — a 50% chance of halving). Beyond Kelly you take more risk for less growth; at twice Kelly the median trader ends roughly where they started. This is why practitioners bet a fraction of Kelly, and why the Kelly lesson spends so long on estimation error: if your \(p\) is overstated, your "half Kelly" may really be full Kelly.

Section 03

The psychology: abandoning a system at its low

The maths says drawdowns are normal. The person living through one experiences something else. Three well-documented tendencies line up against them:

📉

Recency

We weight the last few outcomes far more than the long record. Ten weeks of losses feel like evidence; ten years of backtest feel like a story.

😣

Loss aversion

Losses hurt roughly twice as much as equal gains please (lesson 1). A drawdown is a long run of the painful kind, and pain asks for action.

🔀

Switching to what just worked

The replacement strategy is usually chosen because it did well recently — just as its own good run may be ending.

🧾

The behaviour gap

Morningstar's long-running "Mind the Gap" studies have repeatedly found that the average dollar invested in funds earns less than the funds themselves, because money tends to arrive after good runs and leave after bad ones.

You plant seeds and water them every day. For two weeks nothing comes up, so you dig them all out and plant different seeds. The first seeds were about to sprout. Most plans that work still have a "nothing is happening" stretch, and the danger is giving up right in the middle of it.

Section 04

It actually happened: sound strategies, long droughts

It actually happened

Value investing's lost decade, roughly 2007–2020

Buying cheap stocks and avoiding expensive ones — the "value" premium — is one of the most studied ideas in finance, documented across decades and countries. Measured by the standard academic value factor (cheap minus expensive stocks, in the Fama–French data), it then went through the longest and deepest drawdown in its recorded history, from around 2007 until late 2020, with the sharpest leg in 2018–2020 as large growth and technology companies kept outrunning everything cheap.

Through that stretch the arguments that "value is dead" multiplied — some of them serious, about intangible assets and low interest rates. Value managers saw years of outflows and some closed. In mid-2020 AQR's Cliff Asness published a long piece asking, in its title, whether systematic value investing was dead, and concluding that the cheapness of cheap stocks had become extreme rather than that the idea had broken.

Then, from November 2020 — the month the first effective COVID-19 vaccine results were announced — value staged a sharp rebound that ran through 2021 and 2022. Investors who had left near the bottom of the drawdown took the full loss and missed the recovery. That is not proof value will always come back; it is a demonstration that a long, deep drawdown is exactly what a real premium can look like from the inside.

It actually happened

March 2000: Tiger Management closes near the turn

Julian Robertson's Tiger Management had been one of the most successful hedge funds of the 1980s and 1990s, built on buying what it judged undervalued and shorting what it judged overvalued. In the late-1990s technology boom it refused to chase internet stocks, lagged badly, and suffered heavy losses and redemptions.

In late March 2000 Robertson announced he was closing the funds and returning outside capital, saying in effect that he no longer understood the market. The Nasdaq had peaked earlier that same month. Over the following years the technology bubble deflated and the kind of cheap, unloved stocks Tiger favoured did far better. The story is usually told as a lesson about bubbles; it is just as much a lesson about how a long drawdown wears down even a legendary investor's conviction at close to the worst possible moment.

Worked example

Is a 9-loss streak at trade 220 a reason to stop?

1

What the record says

Your system's tested record: 55% winners, average win equal to average loss (\(b = 1\)), so \(E = 0.55 - 0.45 = 0.10R\) per trade. You risk 2% per trade and plan 300 trades a year.

2

What a 9-loss streak costs

\(0.98^9 = 0.834\): a 16.6% drawdown, needing \(0.166/0.834 = 19.9\%\) to recover. Painful, but inside the simulated median maximum drawdown of 21.2% for 300 trades.

3

How surprising is it?

From the recursion, a run of 9 or more in 300 trades at 55% has probability 11.5%; within 220 trades it is a little lower. Unusual, not damning: roughly one year in nine you should see one.

4

What would actually be evidence?

Not the streak, but the whole record since launch. After 220 trades at a true 55%, the win count has mean 121 and standard deviation \(\sqrt{220 \times 0.55 \times 0.45} \approx 7.4\). If you have won 115 (52%), that is under one standard deviation low — no evidence of anything. If you had won 99 (45%), you would be about three standard deviations low, and that is the kind of number worth stopping for.

🎯
Your exercise

Write your drawdown rules before you need them

Do this once, on a calm day, and keep it next to your screen. Use the simulation above with your own numbers.

1

Your expected losing streak

Enter your win rate and yearly trade count in the streak calculator. Write down the run length that has a 50% chance of appearing this year. When it shows up, you will recognise it as Tuesday, not a crisis.

2

Your "normal" drawdown

Set the simulator to your win rate, payoff, size and a year of trades. Write down the 90th-percentile maximum drawdown. Anything shallower is normal for your system, by your own numbers.

3

Size so you can live with that number

If the 90th-percentile drawdown is one you would not sit through, the answer is a smaller \(r\), now — not abandoning the system later. Stay at or below half Kelly, and lower if your win rate is estimated from a short record.

4

Define "broken" in advance

Pick a statistical test, not a feeling: for example, "live win rate more than 2.5 standard deviations below the tested rate after at least 100 trades", or "drawdown beyond the 99th percentile of the simulation". Write it down. Only that rule gets to switch the system off.

Section 05

What this desk found when it tested the claims

Untested "A deep drawdown means the edge is gone." Not tested here, and not testable from a drawdown alone: the simulator shows that a system with no edge and a system with a real one produce drawdowns that look the same for hundreds of trades. What can be tested is the live record against the tested record — win rate and expectancy with an interval — and that is the rule the exercise above asks you to write.
Validated The desk's one fully-audited strategy, and the streaks it should expect. A slow FX strategy built on the US-versus-foreign 2-year yield spread: out of sample 2015–2025 with honest publication lags, ~109 trades, 63% win, profit factor 2.2, every out-of-sample year 2022–2026 positive. Apply this lesson's recursion to it: over 109 trades at 63%, a run of 4 losses has a 73.5% chance and a run of 5 a 37.7% chance; the expected longest run is about 4.3. A validated edge with a 63% win rate should still be expected to lose four or five in a row. Backtest evidence, paper-traded since 2026-07 — not forward-proven.
Null The honest flip side: "trend following works on FX". On an FX-only basket the desk found nothing — verified not to be a bug with an independent rebuild; a broad multi-asset version was modest (about 0.3 out of sample), barely beating buy-and-hold. Some long drawdowns are not a premium waiting to pay; they are no edge at all. The desk concluded that from a test, not from how the drawdown felt.
The trap: "stick with it through the drawdown" is only good advice for a system whose edge was established before the drawdown, out of sample, with costs. Applied to a system that never had an edge, the same advice is how small losses become large ones. The discipline is the same in both directions: decide on evidence you wrote down in advance, never on the shape of the last few months.
Section 06

How practitioners actually use this

🎲

Simulate before launch

Run thousands of Monte Carlo or bootstrapped paths from the tested trade list to get the distribution of drawdowns and streaks — not just the backtest's one realised path (see Monte Carlo).

📏

Size from the drawdown back

Choose the drawdown the mandate or your nerves can bear, then pick the risk per trade that keeps the 90th or 95th percentile inside it. Fractional Kelly is the ceiling, not the target.

🚦

Pre-committed review triggers

Many funds set a drawdown level that triggers a review and a deeper one that cuts risk. The review asks whether the live record is statistically different from the tested one — not whether it hurts.

⏳

Judge on enough trades

A strategy that trades rarely needs years to show its edge. Know how many trades it takes for the edge to clear noise — and expect to wait that long (see Sharpe ratios).

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where drawdown thinking goes wrong

Quoting the per-trade odds. "Eight losses in a row is 1 in 595" is true for one starting point and irrelevant to a year of trading.

Treating the backtest's max drawdown as the max. It is one sample from a distribution — and, because MDD grows with time, a longer live run will usually exceed it.

Assuming independence. Real losses cluster in regimes, so real streaks run longer than the formula. Treat the formula as a floor on what to expect.

Sizing at full Kelly on an estimated edge. An overstated win rate turns full Kelly into over-betting, where growth falls and ruin climbs.

Using "it's just a drawdown" to excuse a dead system. Drawdowns do not tell you whether the edge exists. Only the record against a pre-stated test does.

Section 07

Key Takeaways

✓Recovery needs \(d/(1-d)\). −50% needs +100%; −75% needs +300%. Keep drawdowns where the curve is still flat.
✓Streaks are normal — compute them. At 55%, a run of 8 is 1 in 595 from any one trade, 24% in 300 trades and more likely than not by ~750. A run of 6 is more likely than not by 152.
✓Max drawdown only grows with time, and scales with volatility relative to edge. Expect live drawdowns to exceed the backtest's.
✓Bet size decides ruin. Same 55% edge: touching −50% in 300 trades is ~0% at 2% risk, ~9% at half Kelly and ~42% at full Kelly.
⚠Untested here: "a deep drawdown means the edge is gone". A drawdown cannot tell you; a pre-stated test of the live record can.
✗Tested null: trend following on FX. Some drawdowns are no edge at all — "hold through it" is only right for an edge established out of sample.
Section 08

Self-test

Q1. Your account is down 40% from its peak. What gain do you need to get back?
\(g = d/(1-d) = 0.40/0.60 = 66.7\%\). The 60% that is left has to grow by two-thirds.
Q2. A colleague says "a run of 8 losses at a 55% win rate is a 1-in-595 event, so my system must be broken." What's wrong?
\(0.45^8 \approx 1/595\) is the chance that a run of 8 starts at one particular trade. Over many trades there are many starting points. Counted exactly, a run of 8 or more appears somewhere in 300 trades about 24% of the time, and is more likely than not by about 750 trades.
Q3. Why does a backtest's maximum drawdown understate what you should expect live?
It is one realised path out of a wide distribution, and maximum drawdown is a running maximum that can only grow as the period lengthens. A long live run usually sets a new, deeper worst. Simulate the distribution and plan for a high percentile, not the backtest's single number.
Q4. A system wins 60% with b = 1. What is the Kelly fraction, and roughly what happens if you bet twice that?
\(f^{*} = p - q/b = 0.60 - 0.40 = 20\%\). At twice Kelly the expected log-growth falls to about zero: the median trader goes nowhere while suffering enormous drawdowns and a high chance of touching half their starting equity.
Q5. After 200 trades a system tested at 55% has won 104 (52%). Is that evidence the edge has gone?
Expected wins 110, standard deviation \(\sqrt{200 \times 0.55 \times 0.45} \approx 7.0\). 104 is under one standard deviation below — entirely consistent with a 55% system. It is also consistent with a 50% system, which is why short records cannot settle the question in either direction.
Q6. Value investing had a historic drawdown from about 2007 to 2020 and then rebounded sharply. What can — and can't — you conclude?
You can conclude that a long, deep drawdown is compatible with a real premium, and that leaving near the bottom locked in the loss. You cannot conclude that every drawdown recovers: the desk's FX trend-following test is the counter-example, a strategy whose weak results reflected no edge. The difference is evidence established before the drawdown, not the drawdown itself.
