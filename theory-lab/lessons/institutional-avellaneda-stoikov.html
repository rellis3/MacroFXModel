Avellaneda-Stoikov Inventory-Aware Market Making

A market maker earns the spread by quoting both sides — but every fill leaves a position behind, and a position is a bet the market maker didn't necessarily want to make. Avellaneda & Stoikov (2008) built the first widely-used closed-form answer to a genuinely practical question: given a inventory you're already holding, where should your bid and ask actually sit?

TL;DR
  • A market maker who always quotes symmetrically around the mid-price — same distance on both sides, regardless of what they're currently holding — can accumulate a runaway position: every fill on the same side just keeps adding to it, because nothing in a symmetric quote discourages that.
  • Avellaneda & Stoikov's (2008) fix is the reservation price \(r(s,q,t) = s - q\gamma\sigma^2(T-t)\) — NOT the mid-price \(s\), but the mid shifted by an amount proportional to current inventory \(q\), risk aversion \(\gamma\), variance \(\sigma^2\), and time remaining \((T-t)\). A market maker centers their quotes on \(r\), not \(s\).
  • The shift always points toward flat: long inventory (\(q>0\)) pulls \(r\) below the mid, making the ask relatively closer (easier to sell into) and the bid relatively farther (harder to buy more); short inventory does the mirror image. This is inventory skew, and it is the entire mechanism that keeps this model's market maker from accumulating a runaway position the way naive symmetric quoting can.
  • The total optimal spread, \(\delta_b+\delta_a = \gamma\sigma^2(T-t) + \frac{2}{\gamma}\ln\!\left(1+\frac{\gamma}{k}\right)\), splits into two genuinely different components: a pure inventory-risk term that grows with risk aversion, variance, and time left to hold a position, and a pure market-making profit term set by how fast order-arrival intensity decays with quoted distance from the mid (the parameter \(k\)).
  • This is a stylized, finite-horizon, inventory-only model. It does not price in adverse selection — the risk that a fill itself is informative that the market is about to move against the market maker — which is exactly the mechanism the sibling Glosten-Milgrom and Order Flow Imbalance lessons cover instead. A real desk's spread has to compensate for both risks at once; this lesson is honest about covering only one of them.
  • A full worked numeric example and a live six-input calculator below let you compute \(r\), the spread, and the resulting bid/ask for any inputs, and watch the reservation price and both quotes skew as inventory sweeps from short to long.
Section 01

Why this matters

Quoting a market sounds simple in its purest form: post a bid a little below the mid-price, post an ask a little above it, collect the spread every time both sides eventually get hit, and repeat. The trouble is that "both sides eventually get hit" is a statement about the long run, and a market maker doesn't live only in the long run — they live fill by fill, in real time, and fills rarely arrive in neat, offsetting pairs. Sell into the bid, and the market maker is suddenly long. Nobody has to buy from them next; the very next ten fills could all land on the same side too. Every executed trade changes the market maker's inventory, and inventory is not a bookkeeping detail — it's a directional bet the market maker is now carrying, whether they wanted one or not, and the market can move against it at any moment.

A market maker who ignores this and just keeps quoting the same fixed distance on both sides of the mid, forever, has no mechanism at all to push their own inventory back toward flat. If the market happens to be persistently one-sided for a while — more genuine sellers than buyers showing up, say — that symmetric quoter simply keeps buying, level after level, accumulating a larger and larger long position with no internal brake on the process. Avellaneda & Stoikov's 2008 paper, "High- frequency trading in a limit order book," is the seminal closed-form answer to the obvious next question: if a market maker is currently holding some inventory \(q\), how should that change where they quote, not just how eagerly they quote?

💡
Plain-English intuition

A shopkeeper who already has too much of one item

Picture a secondhand bookshop that both buys and sells the same title — offering a lower price to whoever walks in wanting to sell a copy, and a higher price to whoever wants to buy one, pocketing the gap. If the shop already has forty copies sitting on the shelf, unsold, a sensible owner doesn't keep offering the same generous buy price to the next person who walks in wanting to sell a forty-first — they cut the buy price further (less eager to take on even more of what they already have too much of) and sweeten the sell price a little (more eager to finally move some of the pile). Nothing about the book's actual value changed; only the shop's own current position did, and that alone is reason enough to shade both prices.

A market maker holding a long inventory faces exactly this situation, continuously, with a security instead of books. The reservation price is the formal version of "shade both prices toward getting rid of what you already have too much of" — and it is precisely calibrated, not just a vague nudge, using how risk-averse the market maker is, how volatile the security is, and how much time is left before the trading session's clock runs out.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine you're trading stickers with friends: you'll buy a sticker for 2 coins or sell one for 3 coins, all day. If you already have a huge pile of dinosaur stickers, do you really want to keep offering 2 coins for more dinosaur stickers? Probably not — you'd rather pay less for more of them (so you stop piling up) and be happy to sell yours for a little less too, just to get rid of some. That's the whole idea: how many stickers you're already holding should change your prices, not just how good a trader you are.

Section 02

The math, step by step

1. Setting the stage: mid-price dynamics and CARA risk aversion

Avellaneda & Stoikov model the security's mid-price \(s_t\) as a driftless Brownian motion, \(ds_t = \sigma\,dW_t\) — the exact same diffusion term the sibling Ornstein- Uhlenbeck lesson uses, with \(\theta = 0\): "no rubber band pulling it back toward any mean, just random jostling scaled by \(\sigma\)." That lesson's own framing is worth reusing directly here rather than re-deriving separately — a pure random walk in the mid-price is exactly the OU process's degenerate special case, and \(\sigma\) means the identical thing in both lessons: the size of the unpredictable shock per unit time. (Later market-making literature does sometimes substitute a mean-reverting, \(\theta>0\) process for the mid-price instead of a pure random walk — a natural-sounding extension this lesson won't pretend to detail, since the original 2008 paper itself uses the plain driftless case throughout.)

The market maker is assumed to have CARA (constant absolute risk aversion) utility, \(u(x) = -e^{-\gamma x}\), over terminal wealth at a fixed horizon \(T\), where \(\gamma>0\) is the risk-aversion parameter — a higher \(\gamma\) means a market maker who dislikes variance in their terminal wealth more, and who will therefore demand more compensation for holding any given amount of inventory risk. Solving the market maker's optimal-control problem under this setup (Avellaneda & Stoikov do this via a Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation and an asymptotic expansion — a full derivation this lesson doesn't reproduce, since the closed-form result is the genuinely useful part) produces two formulas: where to center the quotes, and how wide to make them.

2. The reservation price — not the mid-price, a shifted indifference price

The central object of the model is the reservation price (also called the indifference price):

\[ r(s,q,t) = s - q\,\gamma\,\sigma^2\,(T-t) \]
s
Mid-price
The market's current best estimate of fair value — where symmetric quoting would center both sides
q
Current inventory
Signed: positive if long, negative if short, zero if flat — this is what makes r differ from s at all
r
Reservation price
Where the market maker actually centers their quotes — the mid, shifted toward flat
γrisk aversion — how much the market maker dislikes holding inventory risk
σ²variance of the mid-price — same σ as the OU lesson's diffusion term, squared
(T−t)time remaining in the trading horizon — how much longer this inventory has left to move against the market maker

This is the single most important thing to get right about this model: \(r\) is not the mid-price. It is the mid, shifted by an amount \(q\gamma\sigma^2(T-t)\) — and the direction of that shift always points toward encouraging the market maker back toward flat inventory. Take \(q>0\) (long): the shift subtracts a positive amount from \(s\), so \(r<s\) — the reservation price sits below the mid. Centering quotes on a below-mid \(r\) makes the ask (posted above \(r\)) closer to the true mid than a symmetric quote would be — more attractive to whoever wants to buy, more likely to get hit, and every hit sells off some of the long position. Simultaneously it makes the bid (posted below \(r\), which is already below \(s\)) farther from the mid than a symmetric quote — less attractive, less likely to add still more inventory the market maker least wants right now. \(q<0\) (short) is the exact mirror image: \(r>s\), the bid becomes relatively more attractive and the ask relatively less so, nudging the position back up toward flat. At \(q=0\), the shift vanishes entirely and \(r=s\) — a flat market maker has no inventory reason to skew away from the mid at all.

Read the sign carefully — it's easy to get backward on a first pass. Long inventory (\(q>0\)) shifts \(r\) down, below the mid. That is what makes the ask look cheap (relative to \(r\)) and the bid look already-paid-up, biasing fills toward selling. It is tempting to guess "long inventory should push my price expectations up," but the reservation price isn't a forecast of where the market is headed — it's the price at which the market maker themselves would be indifferent between their current position and flat, and a market maker already holding a long position that could lose value is, by construction, less eager to hold even more of it, hence a lower indifference price for adding to that position.
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

If you're already holding way too many dinosaur stickers, would you pay the SAME price for one more as someone who has zero? No — you'd only want one more if it were cheaper than usual, because you already have plenty. That "I'd only want more if it's cheaper" price is the reservation price. It's not a guess about whether stickers are about to become more valuable — it's just an honest price for someone who's already holding a lot of them.

3. The optimal spread — two genuinely different components

Around the reservation price, Avellaneda & Stoikov derive the optimal total width between the bid and ask distances, \(\delta_b\) and \(\delta_a\) (measured outward from \(r\), not from \(s\)):

\[ \delta_b + \delta_a = \gamma\sigma^2(T-t) + \frac{2}{\gamma}\ln\!\left(1+\frac{\gamma}{k}\right) \]

The second term's \(k\) comes from a specific model of how likely a quote is to get filled the farther it sits from the mid: order arrivals are modeled as a Poisson process whose intensity decays exponentially in distance,

\[ \lambda(\delta) = A\,e^{-k\delta} \]
Athe baseline fill-arrival rate right at the touch (δ=0)
khow fast that arrival rate decays as the quote moves farther from the mid — a market where traders are very price-sensitive has a LARGE k

In the common simplified/symmetric-around-\(r\) form this lesson uses (matching how the result is most often reproduced in practitioner walkthroughs), the two half-distances are set equal, \(\delta_b=\delta_a= \tfrac{1}{2}(\delta_b+\delta_a)\), so the actual quotes are:

\[ \text{bid} = r - \frac{\delta_b+\delta_a}{2}, \qquad \text{ask} = r + \frac{\delta_b+\delta_a}{2} \]

The two additive terms making up the total spread answer two completely different questions, and it's worth keeping them mentally separate:

1. Inventory-risk term: γσ²(T−t)

The exact same expression that sets how far \(r\) shifts per unit of inventory. It answers: "how much extra margin do I need, just to compensate for the risk of holding any position at all, for the time I have left?" It grows with risk aversion (a jumpier market maker demands more cushion), with variance (a more volatile security is more dangerous to be stuck holding), and with time remaining (more time is more time for the market to move against an open position). Notice this term has nothing to do with getting filled at all — it's pure compensation for risk, and it would still apply even to a market maker who somehow never had to worry about whether their quotes actually traded.

2. Market-making profit term: (2/γ)ln(1+γ/k)

Answers a different question entirely: "given that quoting farther from the mid earns more per fill but gets filled less often (that's what \(k\) controls), how wide should I quote to balance those two forces optimally?" As \(\gamma\to0\) (a risk-neutral market maker), this term converges to \(2/k\) — a pure liquidity-provision spread driven only by how fast fills dry up with distance, with no risk aversion involved at all. For \(\gamma>0\) it's larger than \(2/k\): even the "pure profit-margin" half of the spread widens somewhat as the market maker becomes more risk-averse, because a risk-averse market maker also wants a bigger buffer per individual trade, not just less total inventory risk.

Not a coincidence: the same γσ²(T−t) shows up twice. The exact expression that sets how far the reservation price shifts per unit of inventory is also, unchanged, the inventory-risk half of the total spread. Both come out of the same second-order approximation to the market maker's value function in the paper's derivation — the model is internally consistent about exactly one thing: the "cost" of holding one more unit of inventory for the remaining time \((T-t)\), at this \(\gamma\) and \(\sigma\), is priced identically wherever it shows up.
A clean structural feature worth noticing: the total spread width doesn't depend on inventory at all. \(\delta_b+\delta_a\) has no \(q\) in it anywhere. Whatever inventory the market maker is carrying, the bid-ask distance stays exactly the same width — what changes with inventory is only where that fixed-width window is centered (on \(r\), not \(s\)). Inventory skew in this model is entirely a re-centering effect, not a widening-one-side effect.

4. Inventory skew, end to end

Putting the two formulas together: a market maker who is currently long skews both quotes down — bid and ask both shift lower than a naive symmetric quote around \(s\) would place them, because both are built from \(r<s\). The ask being lower makes it easier to sell (closer to the mid, more likely to attract a buyer); the bid being lower makes it harder to buy more (farther below the mid than a symmetric quote, less likely to attract a seller). Symmetric quoting does neither of these things — it holds both quotes a fixed distance from \(s\) regardless of \(q\), which is exactly why a symmetric quoter has no internal mechanism stopping inventory from drifting arbitrarily far from flat if fills happen to keep landing on the same side for a while. The reservation-price mechanism is the model's entire answer to that problem, and it is the single idea this whole lesson exists to convey.

5. The role of the time horizon T

Every formula above carries \((T-t)\), remaining time until a fixed horizon \(T\). As \((T-t)\to0\), the inventory-shift term \(q\gamma\sigma^2(T-t)\) shrinks to zero regardless of how large \(q\) is, so \(r\to s\) — the reservation price converges back to the plain mid-price right at the horizon. This is deliberate, not an accident of the algebra: the model is built around a market maker who must, by construction, care less and less about holding a position as the clock runs out on the very last moments they'd be exposed to it — there's less and less time left for that inventory to move against them. Note, though, that the spread does not collapse to zero at the same moment: the market-making profit term \(\frac{2}{\gamma}\ln(1+\gamma/k)\) has no \((T-t)\) in it at all, so as \((T-t)\to0\) the total spread converges to that pure profit term, not to zero — quoting stays profitable-width even in the model's very last instant, only the inventory-driven skew disappears.

Be honest about what this modeling choice actually represents: it is a deliberate, standard feature of the original finite-horizon HFT framing — Avellaneda & Stoikov built a model of a market maker who effectively wants to end the trading session flat, and the reservation price mechanically enforces exactly that as \(t\to T\). It is not a realistic description of a desk that never actually "closes the book" — most real market-making operations run continuously, session after session, with no single terminal time at which they're forced to stop caring about inventory. Later literature has developed infinite- horizon and rolling-horizon variants of inventory-aware market making that don't force this end-of-day flattening behavior — this lesson notes that such variants exist honestly, without inventing details of any specific paper's exact formulation, since the original 2008 model is finite-horizon throughout and that's the version derived here.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

If a toy store is closing forever at 5pm today, the owner stops caring about restocking shelves as 5pm gets close — there's no point buying more toys they'll never get to sell. That's the model's "T" — a hard closing time that makes inventory worries fade out right at the end. A real toy store that reopens again tomorrow doesn't actually think this way; it always cares at least a little about what's on the shelves. The model's flat-by-closing-time behavior is a simplification that makes the math clean, not a claim about how every real market maker actually operates.

Worked example

A market maker long 5 units, halfway through the session

Mid-price \(s=100.00\), inventory \(q=+5\) (long), risk aversion \(\gamma=0.1\), volatility \(\sigma=2\) (so \(\sigma^2=4\)), half the trading session remaining (\(T-t=0.5\)), and a market-depth parameter \(k=1.5\). These are illustrative round numbers chosen for clean arithmetic, in generic price units — not tied to any specific instrument or currency pair.

1

Compute the inventory shift

\(q\gamma\sigma^2(T-t) = 5 \times 0.1 \times 4 \times 0.5 = 1.00\).

2

Compute the reservation price

\(r = s - 1.00 = 100.00 - 1.00 = \mathbf{99.00}\). Being long pulled the reservation price a full point below the mid — the market maker's own indifference point for adding to this position sits noticeably cheaper than the market's actual mid.

3

Compute the two spread components separately

Inventory-risk term: \(\gamma\sigma^2(T-t) = 0.1\times4\times0.5 = 0.20\) (identical to the per-unit shift above, since here \(q=1\)-unit sensitivity is exactly this number). Market-making profit term: \(\frac{2}{\gamma}\ln(1+\gamma/k) = \frac{2}{0.1}\ln(1+0.1/1.5) = 20\times\ln(1.0667) \approx 20\times0.06454 \approx 1.2908\).

4

Add them for the total spread

\(\delta_b+\delta_a = 0.20 + 1.2908 \approx \mathbf{1.4908}\). Half of that, \(0.7454\), is added on each side of \(r\).

5

Compute the final bid and ask

Bid \(= r - 0.7454 = 99.00 - 0.7454 \approx \mathbf{98.2546}\). Ask \(= r + 0.7454 = 99.00 + 0.7454 \approx \mathbf{99.7454}\). Compare this to what a naive symmetric quoter, ignoring inventory entirely, would have posted around the plain mid: bid \(99.00-0.7454\approx98.2546\)... using \(s=100\) instead of \(r=99\) gives bid \(\approx99.2546\), ask \(\approx100.7454\) — a full point higher on both sides. The inventory-aware quotes sit a full point cheaper across the board, exactly enough to make selling more attractive and buying more relatively less so.

Section 03

How this connects to the rest of this Theory Lab

This model doesn't stand alone — it deliberately isolates one specific risk (inventory) from two other things this curriculum covers in dedicated lessons of their own, and being precise about that boundary is the honest way to present it.

What this model does NOT price in: adverse selection

Nothing in \(r(s,q,t)\) or the spread formula asks whether the counterparty on the other side of a fill knows something the market maker doesn't. The sibling Glosten-Milgrom lesson shows a spread can exist for a purely risk-neutral market maker, with zero inventory concern at all, purely because some fraction \(\mu\) of arriving flow is informed — a completely different mechanism from this lesson's inventory-holding risk. A real market maker faces both risks simultaneously: this model's fill-intensity function \(\lambda(\delta)=Ae^{-k\delta}\) treats every arriving order as equally likely to be informed or uninformed, which is exactly the simplification a Glosten-Milgrom- style toxicity adjustment would relax.

A related but different real-time signal: Order Flow Imbalance

The sibling Order Flow Imbalance lesson builds real-time signals — OFI and the microprice — from a sequence of book updates, often used in practice to refine a market maker's estimate of the "true" reference price \(s\) that this model simply treats as an exogenous input following \(ds=\sigma\,dW\). This lesson's \(s\) doesn't ask where it comes from; a live desk plugging numbers into this model in practice would very plausibly be feeding it a microprice-adjusted reference rather than a naive best-bid/best-ask midpoint.

And none of this matters unless the quotes this model produces are actual resting limit orders in a real venue. The sibling Limit Order Books & Queue Dynamics lesson covers exactly that mechanical layer — price-time priority, queue position, cancellation and requoting — which this lesson takes entirely for granted. The reservation price and optimal spread tell a market maker where to quote; the limit-order-book lesson covers what actually happens once that quote is sitting in a real queue, competing with everyone else's resting orders at the same price.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

This model is really good at answering "how much of this thing do I already have, and how should that change my prices?" It's not trying to answer a completely different question: "is the person buying from me right now secretly smarter than me about where the price is headed?" That second question has its own lesson (Glosten-Milgrom) with its own completely different answer. A real market maker has to worry about both questions at once — this lesson is honest that it only tackles the first one.

Section 04

How practitioners actually use this

🎛️

A calibration target, not a plug-and-play formula

\(A\) and \(k\) in the Poisson fill-intensity model are not known constants — they must be estimated from a venue's own historical fill data, and a poor fit means the "optimal" spread is optimal for the wrong market.

⚙️

A building block inside larger market-making systems

The reservation-price mechanism is widely used as one input inside more elaborate, production market-making engines — often combined with toxicity filters, position limits, and latency-aware requoting logic this lesson doesn't cover.

📐

A teaching device for "why does inventory matter at all"

Even where the exact closed-form isn't used live, the reservation-price intuition — center on an inventory-shifted price, not the raw mid — is close to universal informal practice among human market makers.

🔁

Rolling re-solves, not a single static answer

In practice \(s\), \(q\), and even the fitted \(A,k\) keep changing tick by tick, so a live implementation re-solves \(r\) and the spread continuously — this lesson's single "worked example" is really one frozen instant of a process meant to run constantly.

🎯
Real-world trading scenario

A desk that just got run over on one side

Imagine a market maker who has been symmetric-quoting a currency pair and, over the last hour, has been repeatedly lifted on their offer as a wave of genuine buying interest passed through — they are now meaningfully long, with no natural mechanism in a naive symmetric strategy to stop the position from growing further if the buying continues. Switching that same desk onto an Avellaneda-Stoikov-style reservation price would immediately compute a negative shift from the mid proportional to that new, larger \(q\): both quotes would drop, the ask closer to the touch (more likely to finally get some of that inventory sold back off) and the bid farther from it (less likely to keep adding to an already-uncomfortable position). This is exactly the mechanical behavior a real inventory-limit system is trying to approximate by hand with hard position caps and manual skew — the formula just makes the skew continuous and quantitatively principled instead of a blunt on/off cutoff.

Nothing above is a claim this repo runs any such system live — see the status badge above for exactly what does and doesn't exist here today. It illustrates the mechanism a desk that did run this model would actually be reacting to, which is the point of the exercise.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where this model quietly stops matching reality

Continuous, free re-quoting is assumed, not modeled as costly. The math treats moving a quote as instantaneous and free. Real venues have latency, and a resting order that gets cancelled and replaced loses its place in the queue entirely — the sibling Limit Order Books lesson's queue-priority mechanics are completely absent from this model, which can make its prescribed "optimal" requoting frequency unrealistic on a venue where queue position itself has real value.

\(\sigma\) is assumed known and constant, and \(\lambda(\delta) =Ae^{-k\delta}\) is assumed to be the right functional form. Both are calibration choices dressed up as fixed model inputs. Real volatility clusters and regime-shifts rather than sitting at one constant value for the whole horizon, and there is no guarantee actual order-arrival intensity truly decays exponentially in distance from the mid rather than in some other shape — a poor fit on either assumption means the formula's numbers are precise but not accurate.

Adverse selection is not in this model at all. As Section 03 above states plainly, every arriving order is treated as equally likely to be informed — this model has no \(\mu\)-style parameter the way Glosten- Milgrom and PIN do. A market maker relying on this model alone in a genuinely toxic-flow environment is pricing one real risk correctly and one real risk not at all.

The finite-horizon "flatten by T" behavior is a modeling simplification, not a universally desirable trading behavior. As Section 02 discussed, this is a deliberate feature of the original framing, appropriate for a desk that genuinely wants to end a session flat — not automatically the right target for a desk that runs continuously across sessions with no single terminal time.

Section 05

Key Takeaways

✓The reservation price \(r(s,q,t)=s-q\gamma\sigma^2(T-t)\) is not the mid-price — it's the mid shifted toward flat by an amount set by inventory, risk aversion, variance, and time remaining.
✓Long inventory shifts r below the mid; short inventory shifts it above. This makes selling (if long) or buying (if short) relatively more attractive, nudging the market maker back toward flat — the entire mechanism symmetric quoting lacks.
✓The total spread splits into an inventory-risk term (γσ²(T−t)) and a market-making profit term ((2/γ)ln(1+γ/k)) that answer genuinely different questions — and the total spread's width doesn't depend on inventory at all; only where it's centered does.
⚠As (T−t)→0, r converges to s but the spread does not converge to zero — it converges to the pure profit term, since only the inventory-driven half of the spread vanishes at the horizon.
✗This model prices inventory risk only — it has no adverse-selection mechanism at all. Glosten-Milgrom and PIN cover the informed-trading risk this model is silent on; a real desk needs both, not either.
✗Free continuous re-quoting, constant known σ, and a specific exponential fill-intensity shape are all simplifying assumptions — each one is a real place the closed-form answer can quietly stop matching the venue it's actually being used on.
Section 06

Self-test

Q1. Is the reservation price r(s,q,t) the same thing as the mid-price s? If not, what determines how far apart they are?
No. \(r(s,q,t) = s - q\gamma\sigma^2(T-t)\) — they're equal only when \(q=0\) or \((T-t)=0\). Otherwise \(r\) is shifted away from \(s\) by an amount proportional to current inventory \(q\), risk aversion \(\gamma\), the mid-price's variance \(\sigma^2\), and remaining time \((T-t)\). The larger any of those four factors, the farther \(r\) drifts from the plain mid.
Q2. A market maker is currently long (q > 0). Does the reservation price shift above or below the mid, and why does that push the market maker's inventory back toward flat?
Below the mid: \(r = s - q\gamma\sigma^2(T-t)\), and with \(q>0\) the subtracted term is positive, so \(r<s\). Centering both quotes on a below-mid \(r\) makes the ask (posted above \(r\)) closer to the true mid — more attractive to a buyer, more likely to sell off some of the long position — while the bid (posted below \(r\), already below \(s\)) sits farther from the mid, less likely to attract another seller and add still more inventory. Short inventory produces the exact mirror image, with \(r\) above the mid.
Q3. What are the two components of the total optimal spread, and what does each one depend on?
\(\delta_b+\delta_a = \underbrace{\gamma\sigma^2(T-t)}_{\text{inventory risk}} + \underbrace{\frac{2}{\gamma}\ln(1+\gamma/k)}_{\text{market- making profit}}\). The first term depends on risk aversion, variance, and time remaining — pure compensation for the risk of holding any position at all, with no reference to how easily quotes get filled. The second depends on risk aversion and the fill-intensity decay rate \(k\) — it balances earning more per fill (quoting farther out) against getting filled less often, and would remain nonzero even for a market maker with zero inventory-holding risk at all.
Q4. What happens to r and to the total spread as (T−t) → 0 (right at the trading horizon)? Are both effects the same?
No — they behave differently. \(r\to s\): the inventory-driven shift \(q\gamma\sigma^2(T-t)\) vanishes as \((T-t)\to0\) regardless of \(q\), so the reservation price converges back to the plain mid. The total spread does not converge to zero, though — only its inventory-risk term \(\gamma\sigma^2(T-t)\) vanishes; the market- making profit term \(\frac{2}{\gamma}\ln(1+\gamma/k)\) has no \((T-t)\) in it and survives unchanged, so the spread converges to that pure profit-margin width, not to zero.
Q5. Does this model account for the risk that the counterparty filling a quote is an informed trader? If not, which lesson(s) in this curriculum do?
No. This is an inventory-only model — its fill-intensity function \(\lambda(\delta)=Ae^{-k\delta}\) treats every arriving order as equally likely to be informed or uninformed, and nothing in \(r(s,q,t)\) or the spread formula depends on the probability of informed trading at all. The Glosten-Milgrom and PIN lessons in this curriculum cover exactly that separate risk (adverse selection) — a real market maker's spread has to compensate for both risks at once, but this model addresses only one of them.
Q6. A market maker with s=100, γ=0.1, σ=2, T−t=0.5, k=1.5 is short 5 units (q=−5) instead of long 5. Compute r, and say which side (bid or ask) becomes relatively more attractive to a counterparty as a result.
\(r = 100 - (-5)(0.1)(4)(0.5) = 100 + 1.00 = 101.00\) — the mirror image of the worked example's long case (which gave \(r=99.00\)). Both quotes are now centered above the mid instead of below it: the bid (posted below \(r=101\)) sits closer to the true mid than a symmetric quote would, making it relatively more attractive to a seller — exactly what a market maker who is short wants, since a fill on the bid buys back some of the short position and moves inventory toward flat.

Further reading
  • Marco Avellaneda & Sasha Stoikov, "High-Frequency Trading in a Limit Order Book," Quantitative Finance, 8(3), 217–224, 2008 — the seminal paper this entire lesson is built on: the reservation price, the optimal spread, and the exponential order-arrival intensity model.
  • Olivier Guéant, Charles-Albert Lehalle & Joaquin Fernandez-Tapia, "Dealing with the Inventory Risk: A Solution to the Market Making Problem," Mathematics and Financial Economics, 7(4), 2013 — a well-known follow-up that generalizes the Avellaneda-Stoikov framework, including asymptotic solutions and inventory-limit considerations beyond the original closed-form approximation.
  • Lawrence R. Glosten & Paul R. Milgrom, "Bid, Ask and Transaction Prices in a Specialist Market with Heterogeneously Informed Traders," Journal of Financial Economics, 14(1), 1985 — the adverse-selection mechanism this lesson explicitly does not model; covered in full in the sibling Glosten-Milgrom lesson.
  • Rama Cont, Arseniy Kukanov & Sasha Stoikov, "The Price Impact of Order Book Events," Journal of Financial Econometrics, 12(1), 2014 — Sasha Stoikov's own later work on real-time order-flow signals, covered in the sibling Order Flow Imbalance lesson.
