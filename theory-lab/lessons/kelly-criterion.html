The Kelly Criterion & Position Sizing

The formula for the bet size that maximizes long-run growth rate — and why betting exactly that size, on purpose, is usually a mistake.

CONCEPT — a sizing framework, only valid once a real edge exists ~16 min read Prereqs: Bayesian Inference
TL;DR
  • For an even-money bet with win probability \(p\), the growth-optimal bet size is \(f^* = 2p - 1\) — bet a fraction of bankroll equal to your edge over 50/50.
  • For a continuous return stream with drift \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\), the same logic gives \(f^* = \mu/\sigma^2\): optimal leverage is edge divided by variance, not volatility.
  • Compounding costs you: the geometric growth rate is approximately \(g \approx \mu - \sigma^2/2\) (volatility drag), which is why a bumpy return stream compounds slower than a steady one with the same average return.
  • Overbetting isn't just riskier, it's provably worse — growth rises up to \(f^*\), then falls, hitting zero exactly at \(2f^*\) and going negative beyond it, even though every individual bet still has positive expected value.
  • Worked example in this lesson: \(p=0.55\) gives full Kelly \(f^*=10\%\) of bankroll; half-Kelly (5%) captures ~75% of full Kelly's growth rate while cutting typical drawdown by roughly half or more.
Section 01

Why this matters

Every strategy in this repo eventually produces the same two outputs: a direction (fade or follow, long or short) and a size. Fourteen lessons in this series — dozens of pages of code — are almost entirely about the first output. This page is about the second, and it deserves the same rigor, because sizing is not a footnote to a strategy — it is a mathematical decision with its own optimum, its own failure modes, and its own literature, independent of whatever entry signal is feeding it.

The question the Kelly criterion answers is deceptively narrow: if you have a bet with a genuine, known edge that you can repeat many times, what fraction of your current bankroll should you risk on each repetition to grow your bankroll as fast as possible over the long run? Not "as fast as possible on this one bet" (that would usually mean betting everything) — as fast as possible compounded over many bets, which turns out to be a different, smaller, and more interesting number. The gap between those two questions — expected value of one bet versus long-run compounded growth — is the entire subject of this lesson, and it is the same gap that separates arithmetic returns from geometric returns anywhere in finance.

This lesson sits under "Risk & Sizing" rather than "Signals" on purpose. Kelly sizing cannot manufacture an edge that isn't there, and used carelessly it will happily size up a fake or overestimated edge into a ruinous bet. It is a formalization of a question this repo's own working philosophy already raises — it states plainly that "volatility-based position sizing, cutting losers and letting winners run are powerful drivers of results — worth building and validating alongside an entry signal." Kelly is the textbook version of the first half of that sentence. Read this lesson as "how to size a real edge correctly," never as "how to make a mediocre signal look better."

💡
Plain-English intuition

Why "always bet everything" is wrong even with a real edge

Suppose someone offers you a coin that lands heads 55% of the time, and pays even money — bet $1, win $1 on heads, lose your $1 on tails. This is a genuine edge: on average you make money every time you play. So how much of your bankroll should you bet each round?

Betting everything, every time, feels aggressive but tempting — more money at risk means more expected profit per round, right? It's also a guaranteed way to go broke. Bet 100% of your bankroll and the very first tails (which happens 45% of the time) wipes you out completely — you have $0, and $0 stays $0 forever no matter how good the coin is. Betting too little is safe but leaves growth on the table: bet 1% per round and you'll basically never go broke, but you'll also barely benefit from a genuinely strong edge. Somewhere between "bet nothing" and "bet everything" there is a size that grows your bankroll the fastest, compounded over many rounds — not the size that maximizes the expected dollar profit of a single round. That size is the Kelly fraction, and the reason it isn't "bet everything" is that a single catastrophic loss doesn't just cost you money — it costs you a percentage of everything you have left to compound with, and there is no coming back from losing 100%. The math below makes this trade-off exact instead of just intuitive.

Section 02

The math, step by step

Setting up the objective — why maximize the log of wealth, not wealth itself. Call your current bankroll \(W_0\). If you bet a fraction \(f\) of it on a favorable coin and win, your new bankroll is \(W_0(1+f)\); if you lose, it's \(W_0(1-f)\). Play this game \(n\) times in a row, and because each round multiplies your bankroll by a factor (rather than adding a fixed amount), your wealth after \(n\) rounds is a product of all those per-round factors, not a sum:

\[ W_n = W_0 \prod_{i=1}^{n} (1 + f X_i) \]
Wₙbankroll after n rounds — a product, not a sum
fthe fraction of current bankroll risked each round
Xᵢround i's outcome: +1 on a win, −1 on a loss

where each \(X_i\) is \(+1\) on a win and \(-1\) on a loss for that round. Maximizing the plain expected value of \(W_n\) is exactly the trap from the intuition box: since each winning round multiplies wealth by \((1+f)\) and the expected value of a product of independent factors grows fastest the larger \(f\) is (up to \(f=1\)), naively maximizing \(E[W_n]\) pushes you toward betting everything, every round — and a strategy that bets everything hits \(f=1\) and a single loss eventually, with probability approaching 1 over enough rounds, which sends \(W_n\) to zero. Expected value doesn't "see" that a single wipeout is catastrophic and irreversible; it just averages over all paths, including the astronomically lucky all-wins path that pulls the average up while contributing nothing to what almost every actual realized path looks like. This is the core reason Kelly's insight had to reach for a different objective function entirely.

Because wealth compounds multiplicatively, the natural quantity to maximize is not wealth itself but the growth rate — and logarithms turn products into sums, which is exactly the tool for analyzing a multiplicative process. Take the log of both sides of the wealth equation:

\[ \ln W_n = \ln W_0 + \sum_{i=1}^{n} \ln(1 + f X_i) \]
ln Wₙlog of ending wealth — turns the product above into a sum
Σ ln(1+fXᵢ)sum of each round's log-growth contribution

Dividing by \(n\), the average per-round log-growth is \(\frac{1}{n}\sum \ln(1+fX_i)\), and by the law of large numbers this converges, as \(n\) grows, to its expected value \(E[\ln(1+fX)]\). This quantity is called the bet's expected log-growth rate, and it is what Kelly (1956) proved is the right thing to maximize if your goal is to grow a repeatedly-compounded bankroll as fast as possible in the long run — not because "log" is an arbitrary preference, but because it is the unique quantity whose expectation, maximized round by round, also maximizes the bankroll's actual long-run compounded growth rate with probability 1. (In economics language: log wealth is the utility function under which maximizing expected utility is provably equivalent to maximizing long-run geometric growth — this is a mathematical fact about compounding, not a subjective preference for log-shaped utility.)

\(W_0, W_n\)Starting bankroll, and bankroll after \(n\) bets.
\(f\)The fraction of current bankroll risked on each bet — the thing we're solving for.
\(X_i\)The outcome of round \(i\): \(+1\) on a win, \(-1\) on a loss (even-money case).
\(p, q\)Win probability and loss probability, \(q = 1-p\).
\(b\)The payoff odds — you win \(b\) units per unit staked on a win (even money is \(b=1\)).
\(f^*\)The Kelly-optimal fraction — the value of \(f\) that maximizes expected log-growth.
\(g(f)\)The expected log-growth rate as a function of \(f\): \(E[\ln(1+fX)]\).
\(\mu, \sigma^2\)Expected return (drift) and variance of a continuous return stream, in the trading form of Kelly.

Deriving the even-money formula. For the simple coin (win probability \(p\), lose probability \(q=1-p\), even-money payoff), the expected log-growth as a function of \(f\) is:

\[ g(f) = p \ln(1+f) + q \ln(1-f) \]
g(f)expected log-growth rate as a function of bet size f
p ln(1+f)win branch — probability p, bankroll multiplies by (1+f)
q ln(1-f)loss branch — probability q, bankroll multiplies by (1-f)

This says: with probability \(p\) your bankroll multiplies by \((1+f)\) and contributes \(\ln(1+f)\) to the expected log-growth; with probability \(q\) it multiplies by \((1-f)\) and contributes \(\ln(1-f)\). To find the \(f\) that maximizes \(g(f)\), take the derivative with respect to \(f\) and set it to zero — the standard calculus move for finding a maximum of a smooth, single-peaked function (and \(g(f)\) is single-peaked here, concave on \((-1,1)\), so this really does find the maximum, not just a flat spot):

\[ g'(f) = \frac{p}{1+f} - \frac{q}{1-f} = 0 \]
g'(f)=0setting the derivative to zero to locate g(f)'s peak

Cross-multiply to clear the denominators — \(p(1-f) = q(1+f)\) — then expand and collect the \(f\) terms:

\[ p - pf = q + qf \quad\Longrightarrow\quad p - q = pf + qf = f(p+q) \]
clearing denominatorscross-multiplying, then collecting the f terms to isolate f

Since \(p+q=1\) by definition (win and lose are the only two outcomes), this collapses immediately to:

\[ f^* = p - q = p - (1-p) = 2p - 1 \]
f*Kelly-optimal fraction of bankroll to bet
pwin probability
qloss probability, q = 1−p
p−qthe edge over 50/50 — how far above breakeven you sit

This is the classic even-money Kelly formula: bet a fraction equal to your edge (\(p-q\), how far your win probability sits above the 50/50 breakeven point). At \(p=0.5\) (no edge), \(f^*=0\) — bet nothing, correctly. As \(p \to 1\), \(f^* \to 1\) — bet everything, also correctly, because a certain win has no downside to protect against.

The general-odds version. Real bets rarely pay even money. If a win pays \(b\) units per unit staked (so \(b=1\) recovers the coin-flip case) and a loss costs the full stake, the same derivative-and-set-to-zero procedure on \(g(f) = p\ln(1+bf) + q\ln(1-f)\) yields:

\[ f^* = \frac{bp - q}{b} = p - \frac{q}{b} \]
f*Kelly-optimal fraction for a non-even-money bet
bpayoff odds — units won per unit staked on a win
bp−qexpected value per unit staked — the bet's true edge

Read this as "edge over odds": the numerator \(bp-q\) is the bet's expected value per unit staked (win \(b\) with probability \(p\), lose 1 with probability \(q\)) — if this is negative, there is no edge and \(f^*\) is negative, meaning don't take the bet at all (or bet the other side, if that's available). Dividing by \(b\) rescales the edge by how big the payoff swings are, which is the bridge to the continuous form below: a bet with a larger payoff-to-risk ratio needs a smaller fraction of bankroll to extract the same growth, because each unit staked already buys more upside.

The continuous-time / Sharpe-ratio form. Markets don't offer discrete win/lose coin flips — they offer a continuous stream of returns with some expected excess return (drift) \(\mu\) and some variance \(\sigma^2\) per period. Applying the same log-growth-maximization logic to a position sized at fraction \(f\) of capital in an asset with return distribution \((\mu, \sigma^2)\), and using a second-order (Taylor) approximation of the log around the mean — valid when per-period returns are not too large, which is standard for a diversified continuous-return setting — gives the continuous-time Kelly fraction:

\[ f^* = \frac{\mu}{\sigma^2} \]
f*Kelly-optimal fraction of capital to allocate
μexpected excess return (drift) per period
σ²variance of the return stream per period
μ
The edge
Expected excess return (drift) — the numerator, your edge in the trade
σ²
The risk
Variance of the return stream — the denominator, penalizing size for risk
f*
The result
Optimal fraction of capital to allocate — edge divided by variance

This is the form actually used in trading and portfolio theory: the optimal leverage is expected excess return divided by variance. It has a direct, important relationship to the Sharpe ratio, \(S = \mu/\sigma\) (excess return divided by standard deviation, not variance). Substitute \(\mu = S\sigma\):

\[ f^* = \frac{S\sigma}{\sigma^2} = \frac{S}{\sigma} \]
SSharpe ratio, μ/σ — return per unit of volatility
σstandard deviation of returns (not variance) — the denominator here
f*=S/σoptimal leverage rises with Sharpe, falls with volatility

In words: the Kelly-optimal leverage on a strategy is its Sharpe ratio divided by its volatility. A higher Sharpe ratio justifies more leverage; a higher-volatility instrument, at the same Sharpe ratio, justifies less leverage, because variance sits in the denominator of the fraction and enters it squared relative to \(\mu\)'s linear term — doubling volatility while holding the edge \(\mu\) fixed cuts the optimal size by a factor of four, not two. This is the same reason a vol-targeting position-sizing rule (scale position size inversely with recent realized volatility, exactly the kind of thing this project's own working philosophy calls out as worth building) is, in substance, a rolling approximation of Kelly sizing even when nobody explicitly names it that.

Volatility drag: why the geometric mean is always below the arithmetic mean. Take that same second-order approximation one step further and it produces one of the most important, most underappreciated facts in return compounding: for a return stream with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\), the long-run compounded (geometric) growth rate is approximately:

\[ g \approx \mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2} \]
ggeometric (compounded) growth rate — what wealth actually does
μarithmetic mean return per period
σ²/2volatility drag — the compounding cost of variance

The arithmetic mean \(\mu\) is what you'd compute by averaging returns directly — it answers "what's the average return per period?" The geometric mean \(g\) is what actually determines how your compounded wealth grows over many periods — it answers "what constant per-period return would have produced the same ending wealth?" These are different numbers whenever there is any variance at all, and the gap between them — the \(\sigma^2/2\) term — is called volatility drag (or variance drag). It is a real, mechanical cost of variance on compounded wealth, not a pessimistic adjustment or a modeling artifact: a return stream that averages +10% per period but swings wildly compounds slower than a steadier stream averaging the same +10%, because a −50% period followed by a +50% period does not net to zero on a compounding base — it nets to a 25% loss (\(0.5 \times 1.5 = 0.75\)). This single fact is why "more leverage always means more expected profit" is true for arithmetic expectation and false for what a levered account actually experiences over time.

Volatility drag is also the mechanism behind full Kelly's most counterintuitive property: overbetting past \(f^*\) doesn't just reduce growth, it can make growth negative even on a genuinely positive-expectancy bet. Position size scales \(\mu\) linearly (bet twice as big, your drift roughly doubles) but scales \(\sigma^2\) quadratically (bet twice as big, your variance roughly quadruples, because variance scales with the square of position size). So the growth-rate function \(g(f) \approx f\mu - \frac{f^2\sigma^2}{2}\) is an inverted parabola in \(f\) — it rises from zero, peaks exactly at \(f^*=\mu/\sigma^2\) (the same formula derived above, from setting this quadratic's derivative to zero), and then falls back down, crossing zero again at \(f = 2f^*\) and going negative for any \(f\) beyond that. At exactly double the Kelly-optimal size, the drag term exactly cancels the drift term — the growth rate that reaches zero even though the underlying bet's edge never changed. Bet larger still and the compounded account shrinks toward zero over time despite every individual bet still having positive expected value. This is the mathematically precise version of "more size is not always better," and it is why the curve's shape — not just its peak — matters for the next topic.

Overbetting isn't just riskier, it's provably worse. Growth rises with bet size only up to \(f^*\). Bet exactly \(2f^*\) — twice the growth-optimal size — and the drag term exactly cancels the drift term, so the long-run growth rate falls to precisely zero. Bet any larger and compounded wealth shrinks toward zero over time, even though every single bet in isolation still has positive expected value. The edge never changed; only the size did.

Fractional Kelly. Because \(g(f)\) is a smooth peaked curve (a parabola in the approximation above, and single-peaked exactly in the discrete derivation too), it is flat near its own peak — a basic property of any smooth maximum, where the first derivative is zero right at \(f^*\). That means betting slightly less than \(f^*\) costs you very little growth rate, because you're moving along a nearly horizontal part of the curve. But your bet-to-bet variance, and therefore your realized drawdowns, keep shrinking roughly linearly as you scale \(f\) down — no flat spot, just steady reduction. That asymmetry — growth given up is small and second-order near the peak, risk given up is large and first-order everywhere — is the entire argument for fractional Kelly: deliberately betting some fraction \(c \in (0,1)\) of the full Kelly size, most commonly \(c=0.5\) ("half-Kelly"). It is the standard practical response across the literature (Thorp's writing on this is the canonical source) precisely because full Kelly is growth-optimal only under the assumption that you know \(p\), \(b\), \(\mu\), and \(\sigma^2\) exactly — and in any real application those are estimated, not given, which means full Kelly is also the size most exposed to estimation error. Sizing down trades a little bit of theoretical best-case growth for a large amount of protection against both realized variance and the near-certainty that your edge estimate is at least somewhat wrong.

Worked example

A 55%-edge coin flip: full Kelly, half-Kelly, and what half-Kelly buys you

Take the coin from the intuition box: win probability \(p=0.55\), even money (\(b=1\)). Using the derived formula:

\[ f^* = 2p - 1 = 2(0.55) - 1 = 0.10 \]
p=0.5555% win probability, even-money payoff
f*=10%full-Kelly stake as a fraction of bankroll

Full Kelly says bet 10% of your current bankroll on each flip. Note "current bankroll," not "starting bankroll" — Kelly sizing is inherently a percentage-of-equity rule, resized after every win and loss, which is itself the reason it can never fully bankrupt you (you can never bet more than 100% of what's currently there, and \(f^*<1\) whenever there's a real chance of losing) but also why a long losing streak still shrinks your stake precipitously in dollar terms, even though it never literally reaches zero.

Half-Kelly is simply \(c=0.5\) applied to \(f^*\):

\[ f_{half} = 0.5 \times 0.10 = 0.05 \]
c=0.5the fraction of full Kelly being taken
5%half-Kelly stake as a fraction of bankroll

Bet 5% instead of 10%. What does that trade away, and what does it buy? Using the quadratic approximation from above, \(g(f) \approx f\mu - f^2\sigma^2/2\), and noting that at the true optimum \(g(f^*) = \mu^2/(2\sigma^2)\), a standard result for this family of problems is that betting a fraction \(c\) of full Kelly captures a fraction \(2c - c^2\) of the maximum achievable growth rate. At \(c=0.5\):

\[ 2(0.5) - (0.5)^2 = 1 - 0.25 = 0.75 \]
2c−c²fraction of full-Kelly growth rate captured at scale c
75%growth retained at half-Kelly (c=0.5)

Half-Kelly captures roughly 75% of full Kelly's growth rate — giving up about a quarter of the theoretical best-case compounding — while cutting bet size, and therefore per-bet variance, in half. Because typical drawdown depth for a Kelly-family betting scheme scales with bet size in a way that's considerably worse than linear (it's driven by the variance of cumulative log-wealth, which scales with \(f^2\)), halving the bet size does not just halve typical drawdown — it is well known in the Kelly literature (Thorp's applied writing on this, and the "Kelly and drawdown" folklore that grew out of it) that it roughly halves it or more, a very favorable trade: give up about a quarter of the best-case growth rate, get back roughly half (or better) of the typical peak-to-trough drawdown pain. That asymmetry — a modest, disciplined haircut to growth in exchange for a large cut to variance and drawdown — is exactly why half-Kelly (or even quarter-Kelly) is the practitioner default rather than a compromise nobody actually uses.

Growth rate rises with bet size only up to f* — betting more than that doesn't just add risk, it actively makes the long-run growth rate worse, hitting zero at exactly 2f* and going negative beyond it, even though every individual bet still has positive expected value. Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

Kelly's original 1956 paper wasn't about gambling or investing at all — John Kelly, working at Bell Labs, was solving a problem in information theory: how fast could a gambler with a private information channel (a "noisy" edge over the true odds) grow their bankroll, framed in terms of the channel's bit rate. Ed Thorp is the figure who carried it from theory into practice twice over: first into blackjack (using card-counting to estimate a real, computable edge and applying fractional-Kelly-style sizing to it — the subject of Beat the Dealer), and later into quantitative investing at Princeton/Newport Partners, where he argued explicitly for fractional Kelly as a practical risk control, not just a theoretical nicety.

In institutional trading and portfolio management today, full Kelly is almost never used directly — it is treated as a theoretical ceiling, not a target. Position sizes at most systematic desks are set well below the Kelly-implied level, both because \(\mu\) and \(\sigma^2\) are estimated with real uncertainty (an overestimated \(\mu\) or underestimated \(\sigma^2\) makes the Kelly formula recommend a size that is too large for the true, unobservable edge) and because institutions face constraints Kelly's math doesn't model at all — mark-to-market drawdown limits, investor redemption risk, margin calls, correlation across positions that a single-bet Kelly derivation ignores entirely. The Sharpe-ratio form \(f^*=\mu/\sigma^2\) is genuinely useful even outside strict Kelly betting, though, because it's the cleanest available answer to "how much leverage does this strategy's own risk-adjusted return justify" — and vol targeting (scaling a position inversely with a rolling estimate of realized volatility, so that dollar risk stays roughly constant as market volatility rises and falls) is the practical, robust cousin of full Kelly sizing that most systematic desks actually run, trading some of Kelly's precision for much greater robustness to estimation error and regime change.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where Kelly sizing quietly goes wrong

Plugging in a backtested win rate or Sharpe ratio as if it were the true, known \(p\) or \(\mu\). The Kelly formula assumes you know your edge exactly. A backtested edge is an estimate, with its own sampling error, and — as this project's own OOS discipline exists specifically to catch — a backtested in-sample number is systematically optimistic relative to what a strategy will actually realize going forward. Kelly amplifies whatever number you feed it into a bet size; feeding it an inflated edge produces an inflated, overconfident bet size, and the resulting overbetting is exactly the regime, derived above, where growth can turn negative even though the true underlying edge (smaller than the backtest suggested, but still real) is positive.

Treating \(f^*\) as a single number for a book, not a single bet. The classic Kelly derivation is for one repeated, independent bet. Real portfolios hold multiple positions simultaneously, often correlated. Sizing each one to its own standalone Kelly fraction, then holding all of them at once, ignores the shared variance between them and can push the combined book's effective risk well past what any single-position Kelly calculation intended — the same "correlated evidence gets double-counted" failure mode this series flags for naive multi-signal confluence scoring shows up here as correlated risk getting double-counted.

Forgetting that "optimal growth rate" is not the same objective as "acceptable drawdown." Full Kelly is growth-optimal by construction, but growth-optimal paths still routinely include drawdowns of 50% or worse purely from the ordinary variance of a winning strategy — this is not a bug, it is what the geometric-growth-maximizing path actually looks like along the way. An investor, a fund with redemption risk, or a trader who will simply stop following the system after a large loss is optimizing for something Kelly's objective function doesn't include at all. Fractional Kelly narrows this gap; it doesn't eliminate the tension between "fastest compounding" and "survivable to a human."

Using variance/volatility inputs that don't reflect the fat tails real return distributions have. Both the derivation of \(f^*=\mu/\sigma^2\) and the volatility-drag approximation \(g\approx\mu-\sigma^2/2\) rest on a Gaussian-like second-order approximation. Real FX and macro returns have fatter tails and occasional regime shocks that a plain historical \(\sigma\) understates — a reason to size meaningfully below whatever the formula outputs, not just to trust the point estimate, and a direct link forward to Extreme Value Theory (the next lesson), which is specifically about quantifying that tail risk the Gaussian approximation here misses.

Section 04

Key Takeaways

✓Maximize expected log growth, not raw expected value. Wealth compounds multiplicatively, so betting everything (which maximizes plain expected value) eventually meets a loss and wipes you out with probability 1 — log-growth is the objective whose maximization actually tracks long-run compounded wealth.
✓\(f^*=2p-1\) (even money) and \(f^*=\mu/\sigma^2\) (continuous) are both "edge over variance." Bet a fraction proportional to how far your true edge sits above breakeven, scaled down by how much variance that edge has to survive.
✓Volatility drag, \(g\approx\mu-\sigma^2/2\), is a real, mechanical cost. The geometric (compounded) growth rate always sits below the arithmetic mean whenever there's variance — a bumpy return stream compounds slower than a steady one with the same average return.
✓Growth goes negative past \(2f^*\), even with positive edge. Drift scales linearly with bet size but variance drag scales quadratically — overbet enough and a genuinely profitable bet compounds your account toward zero.
✓Half-Kelly captures ~75% of full Kelly's growth at much lower drawdown. Because \(g(f)\) is flat near its peak, sizing down costs little growth but cuts variance (and typical drawdown) by roughly half or more — the standard practitioner default.
⚠Kelly needs an honestly out-of-sample edge, not a backtested one. Plugging an in-sample win rate or Sharpe ratio into the formula produces a confidently oversized bet — the formula does exactly what it's told, and an inflated \(p\) or \(\mu\) is exactly the overbetting regime where growth turns negative.
⚠Correlated book-level positions shouldn't each get standalone Kelly sizing. Sizing every position to its own independent \(f^*\) and holding them all at once ignores shared variance across the book — the combined portfolio ends up overbet relative to what any single position's Kelly calculation intended.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. Why does betting exactly full Kelly, repeatedly, still produce large, painful drawdowns even though it's growth-optimal?
Growth-optimal means fastest expected compounded growth over the long run — it says nothing about the smoothness of the path getting there. Kelly sizing still risks a real fraction of bankroll on every bet, so ordinary variance in outcomes (a losing streak, which will happen with a real, computable probability even under a genuine edge) still produces large percentage drawdowns. Full Kelly is simply the largest bet size for which the growth rate is still positive and maximal — it is not a claim that the ride there is gentle, and in fact it's typically one of the roughest growth-optimal sizes you could choose, since any smaller size gives up little expected growth (the curve is flat near the peak) while noticeably reducing variance.
Q2. What happens to the geometric growth rate if you bet exactly 2× the full-Kelly fraction on a genuinely positive-edge bet?
The growth rate falls to zero. In the continuous approximation \(g(f)\approx f\mu - f^2\sigma^2/2\), the drift term scales linearly with \(f\) but the variance-drag term scales with \(f^2\); at \(f=2f^*=2\mu/\sigma^2\) the two terms exactly cancel. Bet any larger than \(2f^*\) and the compounded growth rate goes negative — the account shrinks toward zero over time on average — even though every individual bet still has positive expected value in isolation. This is the sharpest illustration in the lesson of why "more size" is not automatically "more profit" once compounding is accounted for.
Q3. Why is a backtested win rate a risky number to plug directly into the Kelly formula?
The Kelly formula assumes \(p\) (or \(\mu\)) is known exactly; a backtested win rate is an estimate with sampling error, and in-sample backtest performance is systematically optimistic relative to true forward performance — exactly the gap this repo's IS/OOS split exists to expose. Feeding an inflated edge estimate into Kelly produces an inflated, overconfident bet size, which is precisely the overbetting regime where growth rate can turn negative even though the smaller, true edge is still positive.
Q4. What does fractional (e.g. half) Kelly actually trade away, and what does it buy in return?
Because the growth-rate curve \(g(f)\) is flat near its own peak (a basic property of any smooth maximum), betting somewhat below \(f^*\) gives up only a modest amount of theoretical growth rate — half-Kelly captures roughly 75% of full Kelly's growth rate in the standard approximation. In exchange, per-bet variance falls linearly with bet size and typical drawdown depth falls by roughly half or more, since drawdown severity is driven by the variance of cumulative log-wealth, which scales with \(f^2\). The trade is a modest, controlled haircut to best-case growth for a large reduction in realized risk and estimation-error exposure.
Q5. Why does the continuous form \(f^*=\mu/\sigma^2\) imply that a higher-volatility instrument should be traded with less leverage, at the same Sharpe ratio?
Rewrite \(f^*=\mu/\sigma^2\) using the Sharpe ratio \(S=\mu/\sigma\): substituting \(\mu=S\sigma\) gives \(f^*=S/\sigma\). At a fixed Sharpe ratio, \(f^*\) is inversely proportional to volatility \(\sigma\), not to \(\sigma^2\) directly, but the deeper reason is that variance enters the original formula's denominator as \(\sigma^2\) while drift \(\mu\) enters linearly — so doubling volatility while holding the edge itself fixed cuts the safe optimal position size by four, not by two. This is the same logic underlying volatility-targeted position sizing: scale exposure down as realized volatility rises to keep dollar risk roughly constant.
Q6. Why is it wrong to size every position in a multi-instrument book to its own standalone Kelly fraction and simply hold all of them at once?
The Kelly derivation assumes the variance term reflects the actual bet being sized. In a book of several correlated positions, each sized independently, the combined portfolio's true variance is understated by treating each position's risk as if it existed in isolation — correlated positions move together, so the book's realized risk is larger than the sum of each position's standalone-Kelly-implied risk would suggest. Kelly sizing at the portfolio level needs to account for the covariance structure across positions, not just each position's own \((\mu,\sigma^2)\) in isolation — otherwise the combined book ends up overbet relative to what any single position's Kelly calculation intended.

Further reading
  • John L. Kelly Jr., "A New Interpretation of Information Rate", Bell System Technical Journal, 1956 — the original paper, framed in information-theory terms (channel capacity and a gambler's optimal growth rate) rather than as investment advice; the source of the formula's name.
  • Edward O. Thorp, "The Kelly Criterion in Blackjack, Sports Betting, and the Stock Market", in Handbook of Asset and Liability Management, 2008 (widely circulated earlier as a standalone paper) — the applied bridge from Kelly's information-theory result to real betting and investing, including the case for fractional Kelly in practice.
  • Edward O. Thorp, Beat the Dealer, Random House, 1962 — the first rigorous application of a computable edge (blackjack card counting) combined with Kelly-style bankroll management, the origin story for taking this math out of the lab and into practice.
  • William Poundstone, Fortune's Formula, Hill and Wang, 2005 — an accessible narrative history of the Kelly criterion, from Bell Labs through Thorp's blackjack and Wall Street careers; a good on-ramp before returning to the primary sources above.
  • Leonard C. MacLean, Edward O. Thorp, and William T. Ziemba (eds.), The Kelly Capital Growth Investment Criterion: Theory and Practice, World Scientific, 2011 — the comprehensive academic anthology, including the drawdown-versus-fractional-Kelly analysis referenced in the worked example above.
