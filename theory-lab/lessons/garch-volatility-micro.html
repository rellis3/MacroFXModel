GARCH & Volatility Clustering

The direction of tomorrow's move is close to unpredictable. The size of tomorrow's move is not — big days follow big days, calm follows calm, and that regularity is stable enough to build a one-day-ahead forecast on. GARCH is the model that turns "volatility has memory" into a number, and it's one of the sigma estimators this project actually runs.

The whole idea
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

I can't predict which way the market goes tomorrow. So how can anyone forecast volatility?

Because they are different questions. Direction is like a coin flip: it has almost no memory. Size is like the weather: a stormy day makes another stormy day more likely. Big moves, up or down, come in clumps, and calm comes in runs.

Of either sign? A big up day counts too?

Exactly the same. Mandelbrot noticed it in 1963: large changes follow large changes, of either sign. The model squares yesterday's move, so a two and a half percent rally and a two and a half percent fall push tomorrow's forecast up by the same amount.

So what does GARCH actually do?

It builds tomorrow's variance from three pieces. A small floor. A reaction to yesterday's squared move. And inertia, a big share of wherever the forecast already was. Floor, reaction, inertia.

Give me numbers.

Take the lesson's example. The reaction weight is eight hundredths, the inertia weight is ninety hundredths. On a quiet day the model expects a move of about point seven one percent. Then a two and a half percent day hits. The next day's forecast jumps to about point nine eight percent, roughly thirty-eight percent higher.

And a plain twenty-day average?

It moves less, to about point eight nine, because the big day is only one of twenty. Then it holds that for twenty days and, on day twenty-one, drops straight back when the shock leaves the window. Nothing happened that day. It's a ghost.

How fast does GARCH forget?

Add the two weights: point nine eight. That's the persistence. Each calm day keeps ninety-eight percent of the extra variance. Ten days later the forecast is still about point nine four. It takes about thirty-four trading days to lose half the excess.

And it has to stay below one?

Yes. At one or above, the forecast never settles back. That's the first sanity check on any fitted model.

Did this show up when it mattered?

March 2020. The S and P closed at a record high on the nineteenth of February. Then came a cluster of huge days in both directions: down nine and a half percent on the twelfth of March, up over nine on the thirteenth, down about twelve on the sixteenth. The VIX closed at a record eighty-two point six nine.

Did GARCH see it coming?

No. It only learns from past returns, and February was calm. It reacted once the storm started, then kept volatility raised for months as it faded.

So the one-line version?

Size has memory and direction doesn't: GARCH turns yesterday's storm into today's forecast, but it can't see a storm before it starts.

Section 1

Big moves cluster — of either sign

Watch the return series below. Big bars sit next to other big bars; small bars sit next to other small bars.

Mandelbrot noted this back in 1963: "large changes tend to be followed by large changes, of either sign, and small changes tend to be followed by small changes." Notice the phrase of either sign — a big up day and a big down day both raise the odds tomorrow is also big. That's a statement about size, not direction, which is exactly why it's a statement about volatility rather than about where price is headed.

Why this matters on a desk

Any strategy that expresses a level as "price ± some multiple of σ" — a forecast band, an entry-zone confidence score, a regime tag — is only as good as the σ estimate underneath it. An estimate that doesn't know volatility clusters stays too narrow right after a shock and too wide long after one has faded.

Section 2

A flat average always lags the market

Compare two ways of estimating "how big is a typical move right now."

Flat trailing window (e.g. HV20)
  • Every one of the last 20 days gets equal weight — day t−1 counts exactly as much as day t−20
  • No concept of "conditional on what just happened"
  • A shock gets only a fixed 1/20 weight on day one
  • Then the same shock vanishes off a cliff exactly 20 days later — the "ghosting" effect
A model that reacts + decays
  • Reacts to a fresh shock immediately, with weight α, the very next day
  • Fades that shock's influence smoothly afterward, not abruptly
  • Carries a sense of "how nervous the market already was"
  • This is exactly the gap GARCH exists to close

We need one number before any model can be written down: σ²ₜ, the conditional variance — the model's forecast of how large today's shock will be, using only information available through yesterday's close. "Conditional" is the whole point: it's not a historical average, it's allowed to change every single day as new information arrives.

Section 3

Step 1 — ARCH: react to yesterday's squared shock

σ²ₜ
=
ω
+
α · ε²ₜ₋₁

Robert Engle's 1982 ARCH model (AutoRegressive Conditional Heteroskedasticity) is the simplest version of "volatility reacts to what just happened": today's variance is a baseline ω plus α times yesterday's squared shock.

²

Why square the shock?

A shock can be positive or negative, but "how much turbulence does this represent" doesn't care about sign. A −2% day and a +2% day should raise tomorrow's forecast by the same amount. Squaring turns "a shock of either sign" into a magnitude — the thing a variance should actually respond to.

⏚

What ω does

Sets a floor: variance never falls below what a quiet, shock-free market would produce. Requires ω > 0 for the recursion to make sense.

If shocks weren't squared, a small positive day and a small negative day could cancel out in the running average, and the model would learn nothing about the market's actual turbulence — exactly backwards from what a volatility estimate is for.
Section 4

Step 2 — GARCH: give variance its own memory

ARCH(1) has a gap: it only remembers yesterday's shock, and forgets how elevated variance itself has already been running. Tim Bollerslev's 1986 generalization — the "G" in GARCH — adds a second input: yesterday's own variance forecast.

σ²ₜ
=
ω
+
α · ε²ₜ₋₁
+
β · σ²ₜ₋₁

This is GARCH(1,1) — one lag of the squared shock, one lag of the variance itself. It's the version this repo implements.

⏚

ω — the floor

Baseline variance. Anchors the long-run level the forecast reverts to.

⚡

α — the reaction

Weight on yesterday's fresh squared shock. Larger α means the model reacts more sharply to a single big news day. Typical fitted values: roughly 0.03–0.12.

🔁

β — the inertia

Weight carried over from yesterday's variance level itself. Larger β means an elevated regime is stickier, decaying more slowly. Typical fitted values: roughly 0.85–0.95, usually much larger than α.

What β adds that a flat average can't

HV20 re-derives its estimate from scratch out of a raw window every day — it has no explicit sense that the variance process itself has momentum. GARCH's β term encodes that momentum directly into the recursion.

Push α up and the spike gets taller — the model reacts harder to the news itself. Push β up and the curve flattens out, decaying much more slowly — the elevated regime lingers. This is the entire "reaction vs. inertia" split from the formula, made visible.

Section 6

Persistence: α+β must stay under 1

For the recursion to describe a variance that mean-reverts — settling toward a stable long-run level instead of exploding or vanishing — the parameters need α + β < 1. Each day, a fraction α+β of "how far variance is from its long-run average" carries forward into tomorrow. That fraction is called the persistence.

α+β close to 1 (e.g. 0.97–0.99)

A spike decays very slowly — half-life of weeks. Volatility regimes are sticky, and one sharp news day leaves a slowly-fading fingerprint on the forecast.

α+β ≥ 1

At exactly 1 (IGARCH), a shock never decays — it permanently shifts the variance level. Above 1, the recursion isn't mean-reverting at all; forecasts can grow without bound. It is not covariance-stationary: there is no finite long-run variance.

The long-run variance the model reverts to on a quiet day works out to σ̄² = ω / (1 − α − β) — which is only a sane, positive number when α+β < 1. And from a decay rate of α+β per day, the half-life of a shock's excess variance falls straight out: n½ = ln(0.5) / ln(α+β).

Sanity check worth running on any parameter set

α+β is usually the first thing worth checking when someone hands you a fitted GARCH model — it tells you at a glance how sticky the volatility regime the model describes actually is, and whether the model is even valid.

Worked example: one shock day, traced forward

α = 0.08, β = 0.90 (persistence 0.98), ω = 1.0×10⁻⁶.

5.0×10⁻⁵ → σ̄ ≈ 0.71%

On a quiet day with no recent shocks, this model expects roughly a 0.71% daily move. Now suppose yesterday's variance was sitting right at that long-run level, but yesterday's realized shock was a large 2.5% move: ε²ₜ₋₁ = 0.000625.

=

One 2.5% shock day pushed the next-day forecast from 0.71% to 0.98% — roughly 38% higher — immediately, the very next day. A flat HV20 estimate moves less: one 2.5% day in a 20-day equal-weighted window otherwise at the long-run level lifts it only to about 0.89%, and it keeps that fixed weight for the next twenty days before vanishing abruptly. Ten trading days later, with no further shocks, this GARCH forecast is still at roughly 0.94% — the half-life here is about 34 trading days.

Section 9

Where this connects to MacroFXModel

This project runs GARCH(1,1) in production — js/volForecast.js (garch11VolSeries()) and the walk-forward backtester js/volBacktestEngine.js (garchSigmas()) implement exactly this recursion, initialized at the unconditional variance ω/(1−α−β) so the very first forecasts aren't biased by starting at zero.

📊

One of three estimators

GARCH(1,1) is used for the index asset class. In the live forecaster, commodity (gold) has used Yang-Zhang since 2026-06-30 (it was HV20, which the walk-forward backtester still uses) and FX uses Yang-Zhang(30) — a range-based OHLC estimator.

🎛

Its actual fitted parameters

Live index forecaster: α = 0.06 with an interim β = 0.87 since 2026-06-19 — persistence 0.93, half-life ≈ 9.5 days — because β = 0.91 (persistence 0.97, half-life ≈ 23 days) proved too sticky. β = 0.91 stays as the legacy comparison column and in the backtester. Both are less sticky than this lesson's illustrative 0.08 / 0.90 example.

Where this needs qualifying

This is not a claim that GARCH beats the alternatives out-of-sample — it's one of the estimators compared under the same harness, and which one wins is an empirical question the source lesson leaves open, not something assumed from the math.

Why a forecaster cares — and where it breaks

📏

Vol targeting

Scale position size inversely to expected volatility, so a fixed-dollar-risk position shrinks automatically when the market gets choppier.

🛑

Stops & option fair value

Set stop distances and compare against option-implied volatility using the same forecast.

📐

Probabilistic price bands

Set the width of a forecast band around a level — the use case most directly relevant to this repo.

⚠

Basic GARCH(1,1) is symmetric

It responds identically to a same-sized up shock and down shock, missing the empirical "leverage effect" (bad news spooks markets more). EGARCH/GJR-GARCH exist to fix this — this repo uses plain GARCH(1,1).

A single large σₜ jump after a shock is the model doing exactly what it's supposed to — it doesn't mean the market permanently repriced. Check the persistence to see how fast the spike is expected to fade before treating it as a structural shift.
Section 11 · It actually happened

March 2020: clustering at full strength

It actually happened

On 19 February 2020 the S&P 500 closed at a record high with the VIX in the mid-teens. Five weeks later it was about 34% lower. The big days came in clumps, of both signs: −7.6% on 9 March, −9.5% on the 12th, +9.3% on the 13th, −12% on the 16th, +9% on the 24th. Circuit breakers halted trading on four days in two weeks, and on 16 March the VIX closed at a record 82.69. Volatility then stayed elevated for months.

Section 12

What the desk found, and where the textbook breaks

The claim: the pace of the market persists. A fast stretch is followed by more fast stretches.

Desk test: held up tape-speed-persistence: a slow 15-minute approach is followed by a slow hour, a fast one by a fast hour. Next-hour dwell monotonic in 68 of 68 cells across 17 pairs, the same at random prices. A property of the tape. Pace, never direction.
Where the textbook version breaks

GARCH learns only from past returns, so it can't see the first shock coming: February's calm gave a calm forecast. And plain GARCH(1,1) with normal shocks is symmetric and thin-tailed. March's days were too big for normal draws even around a raised σ, which is why desks use Student-t or asymmetric variants.

Section 13

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Why does ARCH use ε²ₜ₋₁ (squared) instead of εₜ₋₁ directly?

An unsquared shock could let a big up-day and a big down-day cancel out — exactly backwards. Squaring converts a sign-carrying shock into a magnitude the variance can sensibly respond to.
Question 2

What does α+β close to 1 (say, 0.97–0.99) imply?

α+β is the day-over-day decay rate of a shock's excess over the long-run level. Close to 1 means a sticky regime with a long half-life — still valid as long as it stays under 1.
Question 3

ω = 0.01, α = 0.08, β = 0.90, prior variance = 0.50, shock = 2.5. What's today's forecast σₜ² (in %²)?

0.01 + 0.08×(2.5²) + 0.90×0.50 = 0.01 + 0.50 + 0.45 = 0.96%² — matching the worked example rescaled into %².
Question 4

Why does GARCH react to a shock faster than a flat HV20 average?

The α·ε²ₜ₋₁ term puts the new shock into tomorrow's forecast immediately, with weight α. HV20 gives it a fixed 1/20 across 20 equally-weighted days, then drops it off a cliff 20 days later.
Question 5

Which asset class does this repo's actual GARCH(1,1) estimator apply to, per the source lesson?

Index uses GARCH(1,1) (α=0.06, interim β=0.87; β=0.91 kept as the legacy comparison); commodity (since 2026-06-30) and FX use Yang-Zhang.
Your call
Scenario drill · 12 March 2020

The S&P has just fallen 9.5%. How big is tomorrow?

It is the close on 12 March 2020. The S&P 500 fell about 7.6% on Monday and 9.5% today, and the market-wide circuit breaker has halted trading on both days. Three weeks ago the index closed at a record high with the VIX in the mid-teens. You size a position so that its expected daily swing in dollars stays fixed, and you need tomorrow's volatility number. Which do you use?

  1. The volatility you fitted on the calm months before February. That is the market's normal level.
    That is the long-run level, and today is nowhere near it. Clustering says yesterday's storm is the best guide to tomorrow's. Sizing off calm-period volatility leaves you several times too big in the wildest days.
  2. A GARCH-style forecast updated with today's close, which has jumped with the two huge days, and cut the size to match.
    This is what the model is for. Each big day enters squared, so the forecast is now far above February's, and a fixed-risk position has to shrink. It also lets the forecast fade gradually rather than snap back.
  3. Keep the size and go long. After a 9.5% fall, a bounce is due.
    GARCH says nothing about direction. A bounce and a further fall are both big days, and big days are exactly what the model expects next. Betting on the sign with full size leaves you exposed to either.
  4. A 20-day average of squared returns, which has risen with the last two days.
    Better than the calm number, and it does see the shock. But it gives the two huge days only one twentieth of the weight each, so it under-reacts now, and it will drop off a cliff three weeks later when they leave the window.

What happened. The next day the S&P 500 rose 9.3%. On 16 March it fell about 12%, the circuit breaker tripped again, and the VIX closed at 82.69, a record. The breaker was triggered once more on 18 March, and the index rose more than 9% on 24 March. Big days kept coming in both directions, so the up days raised the forecast as much as the down days. Volatility stayed raised for months after the worst had passed.

Sources: S&P 500 daily closes (FRED, SP500) (−7.6% on 9 Mar, −9.5% on 12 Mar, +9.3% on 13 Mar, −12.0% on 16 Mar, +9.4% on 24 Mar 2020; record close 19 Feb 2020); Cboe VIX daily history (14.38 on 19 Feb, 82.69 on 16 Mar 2020); Market-Wide Circuit Breaker Working Group, report to the SEC, 2021 (the four halts of 9, 12, 16 and 18 March 2020).

Section 14

What to carry forward

01

Volatility clusters — big moves of either sign follow big moves, calm follows calm — even though the direction of the next move stays close to unpredictable.

02

GARCH(1,1) has three additive pieces: a floor ω, a reaction to yesterday's squared shock α·ε²ₜ₋₁, and inertia from yesterday's own variance β·σ²ₜ₋₁.

03

α+β is the persistence — the day-over-day decay rate of a shock's excess over normal — and it must stay under 1 or the model isn't covariance-stationary.

04

The long-run variance is ω/(1−α−β); the half-life of a shock is ln(0.5)/ln(α+β).

05

This project already runs GARCH(1,1) in production for the index asset class — α=0.06, interim β=0.87 (β=0.91 kept as the legacy comparison) — while commodity and FX use Yang-Zhang.

06

March 2020: a calm February, then ±9–12% S&P days back to back and a record VIX of 82.69. GARCH tracks a storm once it starts; it can't see one coming. Desk test: the pace of the tape persists intraday (tape-speed-persistence, VALIDATED).

What this now lets you do

Read a GARCH(1,1) recursion symbol by symbol, forecast tomorrow's variance from today's shock and yesterday's variance by hand, and sanity-check any fitted or hand-set parameters by checking α+β < 1 before trusting them.
