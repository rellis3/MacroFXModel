GARCH & Volatility Clustering

The direction of tomorrow's move is close to unpredictable. The size of tomorrow's move is not — big days follow big days, calm follows calm, and that regularity is stable enough to build a one-day-ahead forecast on. GARCH is the model that turns "volatility has memory" into a number, and it's one of three sigma estimators this project actually runs.

Section 1

Big moves cluster — of either sign

Watch the return series below. Big bars sit next to other big bars; small bars sit next to other small bars.

Mandelbrot noted this back in 1963: "large changes tend to be followed by large changes, of either sign, and small changes tend to be followed by small changes." Notice the phrase of either sign — a big up day and a big down day both raise the odds tomorrow is also big. That's a statement about size, not direction, which is exactly why it's a statement about volatility rather than about where price is headed.

Why this matters on a desk

Any strategy that expresses a level as "price ± some multiple of σ" — a forecast band, an entry-zone confidence score, a regime tag — is only as good as the σ estimate underneath it. An estimate that doesn't know volatility clusters stays too narrow right after a shock and too wide long after one has faded.

Section 2

A flat average always lags the market

Compare two ways of estimating "how big is a typical move right now."

Flat trailing window (e.g. HV20)
  • Every one of the last 20 days gets equal weight — day t−1 counts exactly as much as day t−20
  • No concept of "conditional on what just happened"
  • A shock barely moves the average on day one
  • Then the same shock vanishes off a cliff exactly 20 days later — the "ghosting" effect
A model that reacts + decays
  • Reacts to a fresh shock immediately, in full, the very next day
  • Fades that shock's influence smoothly afterward, not abruptly
  • Carries a sense of "how nervous the market already was"
  • This is exactly the gap GARCH exists to close

We need one number before any model can be written down: σ²ₜ, the conditional variance — the model's forecast of how large today's shock will be, using only information available through yesterday's close. "Conditional" is the whole point: it's not a historical average, it's allowed to change every single day as new information arrives.

Section 3

Step 1 — ARCH: react to yesterday's squared shock

σ²ₜ
=
ω
+
α · ε²ₜ₋₁

Robert Engle's 1982 ARCH model (AutoRegressive Conditional Heteroskedasticity) is the simplest version of "volatility reacts to what just happened": today's variance is a baseline ω plus α times yesterday's squared shock.

²

Why square the shock?

A shock can be positive or negative, but "how much turbulence does this represent" doesn't care about sign. A −2% day and a +2% day should raise tomorrow's forecast by the same amount. Squaring turns "a shock of either sign" into a magnitude — the thing a variance should actually respond to.

⏚

What ω does

Sets a floor: variance never falls below what a quiet, shock-free market would produce. Requires ω > 0 for the recursion to make sense.

If shocks weren't squared, a small positive day and a small negative day could cancel out in the running average, and the model would learn nothing about the market's actual turbulence — exactly backwards from what a volatility estimate is for.
Section 4

Step 2 — GARCH: give variance its own memory

ARCH(1) has a gap: it only remembers yesterday's shock, and forgets how elevated variance itself has already been running. Tim Bollerslev's 1986 generalization — the "G" in GARCH — adds a second input: yesterday's own variance forecast.

σ²ₜ
=
ω
+
α · ε²ₜ₋₁
+
β · σ²ₜ₋₁

This is GARCH(1,1) — one lag of the squared shock, one lag of the variance itself. It's the version this repo implements.

⏚

ω — the floor

Baseline variance. Anchors the long-run level the forecast reverts to.

⚡

α — the reaction

Weight on yesterday's fresh squared shock. Larger α means the model reacts more sharply to a single big news day. Typical fitted values: roughly 0.03–0.12.

🔁

β — the inertia

Weight carried over from yesterday's variance level itself. Larger β means an elevated regime is stickier, decaying more slowly. Typical fitted values: roughly 0.85–0.95, usually much larger than α.

What β adds that a flat average can't

HV20 re-derives its estimate from scratch out of a raw window every day — it has no explicit sense that the variance process itself has momentum. GARCH's β term encodes that momentum directly into the recursion.

Push α up and the spike gets taller — the model reacts harder to the news itself. Push β up and the curve flattens out, decaying much more slowly — the elevated regime lingers. This is the entire "reaction vs. inertia" split from the formula, made visible.

Section 6

Persistence: α+β must stay under 1

For the recursion to describe a variance that mean-reverts — settling toward a stable long-run level instead of exploding or vanishing — the parameters need α + β < 1. Each day, a fraction α+β of "how far variance is from its long-run average" carries forward into tomorrow. That fraction is called the persistence.

α+β close to 1 (e.g. 0.97–0.99)

A spike decays very slowly — half-life of weeks. Volatility regimes are sticky, and one sharp news day leaves a slowly-fading fingerprint on the forecast.

α+β ≥ 1

At exactly 1 (IGARCH), a shock never decays — it permanently shifts the variance level. Above 1, the recursion isn't mean-reverting at all; forecasts can grow without bound. This is not a valid model.

The long-run variance the model reverts to on a quiet day works out to σ̄² = ω / (1 − α − β) — which is only a sane, positive number when α+β < 1. And from a decay rate of α+β per day, the half-life of a shock's excess variance falls straight out: n½ = ln(0.5) / ln(α+β).

Sanity check worth running on any parameter set

α+β is usually the first thing worth checking when someone hands you a fitted GARCH model — it tells you at a glance how sticky the volatility regime the model describes actually is, and whether the model is even valid.

Section 7

Worked example: one shock day, traced forward

α = 0.08, β = 0.90 (persistence 0.98), ω = 1.0×10⁻⁶.

σ̄² = ω/(1−α−β)
=
5.0×10⁻⁵ → σ̄ ≈ 0.71%

On a quiet day with no recent shocks, this model expects roughly a 0.71% daily move. Now suppose yesterday's variance was sitting right at that long-run level, but yesterday's realized shock was a large 2.5% move: ε²ₜ₋₁ = 0.000625.

σ²ₜ
=
0.000001 + 0.00005 + 0.000045
=
0.000096 → σₜ ≈ 0.98%

One 2.5% shock day pushed the next-day forecast from 0.71% to 0.98% — roughly 38% higher — immediately, in full, the very next day. A flat HV20 estimate would barely move: adding one 2.5% day into a 20-day equal-weighted average nudges the mean only modestly, and it keeps that fixed weight for the next twenty days before vanishing abruptly. Ten trading days later, with no further shocks, this GARCH forecast is still at roughly 0.94% — the half-life here is about 34 trading days.

Section 9

Where this connects to MacroFXModel

This project runs GARCH(1,1) in production — js/volBacktestEngine.js implements exactly this recursion as garchSigmas(), initialized at the unconditional variance ω/(1−α−β) so the very first forecasts aren't biased by starting at zero.

📊

One of three estimators

GARCH(1,1) is used for the index asset class. Commodity uses HV20; FX uses Yang-Zhang(30) — a range-based OHLC estimator. Same walk-forward harness, three different sigma series.

🎛

Its actual fitted parameters

α = 0.06, β = 0.91 — persistence 0.97, half-life ≈ 23 days. Close to, but a bit less sticky than, this lesson's illustrative 0.08 / 0.90 example.

Where this needs qualifying

This is not a claim that GARCH beats the alternatives out-of-sample — it's one of three estimators compared under the same harness, and which one wins is an empirical question the source lesson leaves open, not something assumed from the math.

Scale position size inversely to expected volatility, so a fixed-dollar-risk position shrinks automatically when the market gets choppier.

🛑

Set stop distances and compare against option-implied volatility using the same forecast.

Probabilistic price bands

Set the width of a forecast band around a level — the use case most directly relevant to this repo.

⚠

Basic GARCH(1,1) is symmetric

It responds identically to a same-sized up shock and down shock, missing the empirical "leverage effect" (bad news spooks markets more). EGARCH/GJR-GARCH exist to fix this — this repo uses plain GARCH(1,1).

A single large σₜ jump after a shock is the model doing exactly what it's supposed to — it doesn't mean the market permanently repriced. Check the persistence to see how fast the spike is expected to fade before treating it as a structural shift.
Section 11

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Why does ARCH use ε²ₜ₋₁ (squared) instead of εₜ₋₁ directly?

An unsquared shock could let a big up-day and a big down-day cancel out — exactly backwards. Squaring converts a sign-carrying shock into a magnitude the variance can sensibly respond to.
Question 2

What does α+β close to 1 (say, 0.97–0.99) imply?

α+β is the day-over-day decay rate of a shock's excess over the long-run level. Close to 1 means a sticky regime with a long half-life — still valid as long as it stays under 1.
Question 3

ω = 0.01, α = 0.08, β = 0.90, prior variance = 0.50, shock = 2.5. What's today's forecast σₜ² (in %²)?

0.01 + 0.08×(2.5²) + 0.90×0.50 = 0.01 + 0.50 + 0.45 = 0.96%² — matching the worked example rescaled into %².
Question 4

Why does GARCH react to a shock faster than a flat HV20 average?

The α·ε²ₜ₋₁ term puts the entire new shock into tomorrow's forecast immediately. HV20 spreads it thin across 20 equally-weighted days, then drops it off a cliff 20 days later.
Question 5

Which asset class does this repo's actual GARCH(1,1) estimator apply to, per the source lesson?

Index uses GARCH(1,1) (α=0.06, β=0.91); commodity uses HV20; FX uses Yang-Zhang(30) — three estimators run under the same harness.
Section 12

What to carry forward

01

Volatility clusters — big moves of either sign follow big moves, calm follows calm — even though the direction of the next move stays close to unpredictable.

02

GARCH(1,1) has three additive pieces: a floor ω, a reaction to yesterday's squared shock α·ε²ₜ₋₁, and inertia from yesterday's own variance β·σ²ₜ₋₁.

03

α+β is the persistence — the day-over-day decay rate of a shock's excess over normal — and it must stay under 1 or the model isn't valid.

04

The long-run variance is ω/(1−α−β); the half-life of a shock is ln(0.5)/ln(α+β).

05

This project already runs GARCH(1,1) in production — α=0.06, β=0.91 — as one of three sigma estimators, applied to the index asset class alongside HV20 (commodity) and Yang-Zhang (FX).

What this now lets you do

Read a GARCH(1,1) recursion symbol by symbol, forecast tomorrow's variance from today's shock and yesterday's variance by hand, and sanity-check any fitted or hand-set parameters by checking α+β < 1 before trusting them.
