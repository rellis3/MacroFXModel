Partial Derivatives & Multivariable Calculus

What happens to ordinary calculus once a function depends on more than one thing at once — and the exact vocabulary behind every option "Greek" you've ever seen quoted.

CONCEPT — not built or tested in this repo ~14 min read Prereqs: Complex Numbers; Calculus for Finance (Deeper Foundations) for Taylor series
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • A partial derivative of a multivariable function is an ordinary derivative taken with respect to one variable while every other variable is held fixed, written \(\partial f/\partial x\).
  • The gradient stacks every partial derivative of a function into one vector, \(\nabla f\), which points in the direction the function increases fastest — the direction gradient descent (a later Numerical Methods lesson) walks in reverse to find a minimum.
  • The Hessian stacks every second partial derivative into a matrix — the multivariable version of curvature, used to check whether a stationary point is a genuine minimum, a maximum, or a saddle.
  • An option's Delta, Gamma, Theta, and Vega are not four separate special concepts — they are literally the partial derivatives of the option-value function \(V(S,t,\sigma,\dots)\) with respect to the underlying, time, and volatility, respectively.
  • Worked example: for \(V(S,t) = S e^{-\delta t}\) (a dividend-paying asset's prepaid forward value), \(\partial V/\partial S = e^{-\delta t}\) and \(\partial V/\partial t = -\delta S e^{-\delta t}\) — two different partial derivatives of the exact same function, holding the other variable fixed each time.
  • How it reached trading: the Black–Scholes–Merton model (1973) is a partial differential equation whose hedge is a partial derivative, and options books have been run by these partials, the Greeks, ever since.
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

I learned slopes at school. One input, one output, how steep is the line. What changes when there's more than one input?

Only one thing. You have to say which input you are moving, and you freeze all the others while you do it.

Give me a picture.

A room that is warmer near the radiator and warms up through the day. Ask how fast the temperature changes and the honest answer is: in which direction? Walk towards the radiator and it rises fast. Stand still and wait an hour and it rises slowly. Each of those is a partial derivative.

So it is just the normal slope, one dial at a time.

Exactly. Nothing new to learn about slopes. Treat every other letter as if it were a fixed number, like five.

Show me with money.

Take an asset worth a hundred that pays a three percent dividend yield, and the price today for delivering it in two years. It comes to about ninety-four dollars and eighteen cents, because you miss two years of dividends.

And the two dials are the price and the time.

Right. Freeze the time and nudge the price up a dollar: the value rises by about ninety-four cents. Freeze the price and add a year: the value falls by about two dollars eighty-three. Same function, two different answers, depending which dial you turn.

And if I want both at once?

Stack them into a list, one slope per dial. That list is called the gradient. It points in the direction where the value climbs fastest. Walk the opposite way and you go downhill fastest, which is how computers fit models.

Where do traders actually use this?

On every options screen. The famous option pricing model of nineteen seventy-three is built on partial derivatives, and its hedge is one. The same year the Chicago Board Options Exchange opened, and books came to be run by these slopes: delta for the price, theta for time, vega for volatility.

So the Greeks are just slopes.

Each one is the slope of the same option value, with one dial turned and the rest frozen. Gamma is the curve in the price direction, a slope of a slope.

What's the trap?

Real markets rarely turn one dial at a time. A big price move often comes with a volatility move. Reading delta alone quietly assumes everything else stood still.

The one-line version?

A partial derivative is an ordinary slope with everything else frozen, so always say which dial you turned and what you held still.

Section 01

Why this matters

Every function studied so far in this curriculum's foundation depended on exactly one input variable: \(f(x)\). But almost nothing in real finance is that simple. An option's value depends on the underlying price and time remaining and volatility and the interest rate, all at once. A portfolio's value depends on every position's size at once. A bond's price depends on both yield and time to maturity. The moment a function depends on more than one thing, a plain question like "the derivative of \(f\)" stops being well-defined — the derivative with respect to what, holding what else fixed? Multivariable calculus is the small, precise set of tools that answers exactly that question, and it turns out to already be hiding in plain sight in a piece of vocabulary you may already have seen quoted on a trading screen: the option "Greeks."

Plain-English intuition

The temperature in a room depends on where you stand — and on which direction you're asking about

Picture a room where the temperature isn't the same everywhere — warmer near the radiator, cooler near the window, and it also changes over the course of the day. Temperature here is a function of more than one thing at once: your position in the room (two coordinates, say east-west and north-south) and the time of day. Ask "how fast is the temperature changing?" and the honest answer is: it depends which direction you mean. If you walk directly toward the radiator, it's rising quickly. If you walk parallel to the wall, at the same distance from the radiator the whole way, it might not change much at all. If you stand completely still and just wait an hour, it might rise slowly as the heating warms the whole room.

A partial derivative answers one very specific version of that question: "if I only let one of these variables change — say, my east-west position — and freeze everything else exactly where it is, how fast does the temperature change?" You get a different answer for each variable you could have picked (east-west, north-south, time-of-day), and each one is computed by the exact same ordinary single-variable derivative rules already built in this curriculum's calculus lesson — you're just temporarily treating every other variable as if it were a fixed constant, not a variable at all. The gradient, covered below, is what you get if you stop asking about one direction at a time and instead ask "which single direction, out of every possible direction I could walk, makes the temperature rise the fastest?" — it turns out that question has one clean answer, built directly from the partial derivatives.

How it reached trading

One price, many risks

Partial derivatives as the Greeks · Black, Scholes & Merton, 1973

An option's value depends on the underlying price, time, volatility and rates all at once. Before 1973 traders had no consistent way to say how much of a position's risk came from each source, or how to hedge one without disturbing the others.

The Black–Scholes–Merton model of 1973 is a partial differential equation, and its hedging argument holds the partial derivative with respect to the stock price, the delta, in shares against each option. As listed options grew from the Chicago Board Options Exchange's opening that year, options books came to be hedged and reported by these partials: delta, gamma, theta and vega, the "Greeks".

Today a risk report is a vector of partial derivatives (Greeks, DV01s by tenor, factor betas), and P&L is explained by adding up their contributions. Model calibration and machine-learning training use the same object, the gradient, stepping parameters downhill on the error, a method first described by Cauchy in 1847.

Section 02

The math, step by step

Watch the formula build
Two partial derivatives of one function, then the gradient
\[ V(S,t) = \color{#fbbf24}{S\,e^{-\delta t}} \]

Start with the function: a dividend-paying asset's prepaid forward value. It has two inputs, the spot \(S\) and the horizon \(t\). At \(S = 100\), \(\delta = 0.03\), \(t = 2\) it is 94.18.

\[ \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} = \color{#fbbf24}{e^{-\delta t}} \]

Freeze \(t\). Then \(e^{-\delta t}\) is just a fixed number multiplying \(S\), and the slope of "number times \(S\)" is that number: \(e^{-0.06} = 0.9418\).

\[ \frac{\partial V}{\partial t} = S \cdot \color{#fbbf24}{(-\delta)\,e^{-\delta t}} \]

Now freeze \(S\) instead. \(S\) is the fixed number, and the ordinary rule for \(e^{-\delta t}\) brings down a \(-\delta\): \(-0.03 \times 100 \times 0.9418 = -2.825\).

\[ \color{#10b981}{\nabla V = \left( e^{-\delta t},\ -\delta S e^{-\delta t} \right) = (0.9418,\ -2.825)} \]

Stack the two slopes into one vector: the gradient. One entry per input, each worked out with the others frozen.

Functions of several variables

A function of several variables simply takes more than one input and produces one output — written \(f(x, y)\) for two variables, or \(f(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n)\) for \(n\) of them. In finance, an option's value is a function of the underlying price \(S\), time to expiry \(t\), volatility \(\sigma\), and the interest rate \(r\), all at once: \(V(S, t, \sigma, r)\). Nothing about the idea of "a function" changes — it still just maps inputs to a single output — but the geometric picture changes: instead of a curve in a flat 2-D plane, a two-variable function is a surface in 3-D space (imagine the temperature example above as a bumpy landscape, with height representing temperature).

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine rating a dish not just on how sweet it is, but on sweet AND salty AND crunchy all at once — you need more than one number to describe it fully. A function of several variables is just a rule that looks at several things at the same time and gives back one answer, like a judge scoring a dish on every category together.

Partial derivatives — one variable at a time, everything else frozen

The partial derivative of \(f(x, y)\) with respect to \(x\), written \(\partial f/\partial x\), is defined exactly like the ordinary single-variable derivative from the calculus lesson, except \(y\) is held completely fixed while \(x\) is nudged:

\[ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h,\, y) - f(x,\, y)}{h} \qquad \text{(} y \text{ held fixed)} \]
∂f/∂x"partial f, partial x" — the derivative with respect to x alone
y held fixedthe entire trick — every other variable is temporarily treated as a constant
lim h→0the identical limit machinery from ordinary single-variable calculus

Practically, this means: to compute \(\partial f/\partial x\), apply the ordinary single-variable differentiation rules from the calculus lesson to \(x\), treating every other letter in the expression (\(y\), \(t\), \(\sigma\), whatever else appears) exactly as if it were a plain number like 5 or \(\pi\) — because for the purposes of this one partial derivative, it is being held constant. To get \(\partial f/\partial y\) instead, do the same thing in reverse: differentiate with respect to \(y\), and now treat \(x\) as the fixed constant. Nothing new needs to be learned about differentiation itself — the entire new idea is "differentiate one variable, freeze the rest."

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine baking cookies where both the oven temperature and the baking time change how done they get. A partial derivative answers one question at a time: "if I only turn up the temperature dial and leave the timer exactly where it is, how much more done do the cookies get?" You freeze everything except the one dial you're curious about.

A first, deliberately simple example

Take a two-asset portfolio whose value is a weighted sum of two asset prices, \(V(S_1, S_2) = w_1 S_1 + w_2 S_2\), with fixed weights \(w_1\) and \(w_2\). Differentiating with respect to \(S_1\), treating \(S_2\) (and the constants \(w_1, w_2\)) as fixed:

\[ \frac{\partial V}{\partial S_1} = w_1 \qquad \frac{\partial V}{\partial S_2} = w_2 \]
∂V/∂S₁ = w₁the w₂S₂ term vanishes entirely — it doesn't depend on S₁ at all
∂V/∂S₂ = w₂symmetric — the w₁S₁ term vanishes when differentiating with respect to S₂

This is deliberately the simplest possible case: because \(V\) is linear, the partial derivative with respect to each asset price is just that asset's own weight, and the mechanics amount to noticing that the other term in the sum doesn't involve the variable being differentiated at all, so it contributes exactly zero. The point of starting here is to see the "hold the other variable fixed" rule applied in its most transparent form before moving to a case where the two variables interact.

A richer example: a dividend-paying asset's forward value

Now take a function where the two variables genuinely interact: \(V(S, t) = S\, e^{-\delta t}\) — the prepaid forward value (what you pay today to receive one unit at time \(t\)) of an asset currently worth \(S\), paying a continuous dividend yield \(\delta\): the dividends paid before delivery are lost to you, so the longer the horizon \(t\), the lower the value. Differentiate with respect to \(S\) first, holding \(t\) (and the constant \(\delta\)) fixed — since \(e^{-\delta t}\) doesn't involve \(S\) at all, it's just a fixed multiplying constant from \(S\)'s point of view:

\[ \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} = e^{-\delta t} \]
∂V/∂Streats e^(−δt) as a fixed constant multiplying S
e^(−δt)the discount factor for lost dividends — a plain number once t is frozen

Now differentiate with respect to \(t\) instead, holding \(S\) fixed — here \(S\) is the fixed constant multiplying a function of \(t\), and the ordinary chain rule for \(e^{-\delta t}\) (derivative of \(e^{ku}\) with respect to \(u\) is \(k e^{ku}\), with \(k=-\delta\) here) applies:

\[ \frac{\partial V}{\partial t} = S \cdot (-\delta) e^{-\delta t} = -\delta S e^{-\delta t} \]
∂V/∂ttreats S as a fixed constant multiplying a function of t
−δSe^(−δt)negative — the forward value falls as the horizon lengthens and more dividends are given up

Two genuinely different numbers come out of differentiating the exact same function — one measures sensitivity to the spot price, the other to the passage of time — and each is computed by the identical mechanical rule: differentiate with respect to the variable named, freeze everything else. This is precisely the structure behind an option's Delta (\(\partial V/\partial S\)) and Theta (\(\partial V/\partial t\)), covered explicitly below.

The gradient — every partial derivative, stacked into one vector

For a function of several variables, stacking all of its partial derivatives into a single vector gives the gradient, written \(\nabla f\) (the symbol is called "nabla" or "del"):

\[ \nabla f = \left( \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1},\ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_2},\ \dots,\ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_n} \right) \]
∇f"grad f" — the vector of every partial derivative
(∂f/∂x₁, …, ∂f/∂xₙ)one entry per input variable, each computed by freezing all the others

The gradient is more than bookkeeping — it has a genuine geometric meaning, directly answering the "which direction rises fastest?" question from the temperature intuition above: the gradient vector points in the direction the function increases fastest, and its length (magnitude) tells you exactly how fast the function is increasing in that best direction. Walking in the exact opposite direction, \(-\nabla f\), is the direction the function decreases fastest — which is precisely the "downhill" direction that gradient descent, a later Numerical Methods lesson's core optimization algorithm, repeatedly steps in to hunt for a minimum.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

If you're standing on a hill in the dark and want to find the fastest way to the top, the gradient is like a magic compass that always points in exactly the steepest uphill direction. Walk the opposite way it points, and you're headed downhill as fast as possible instead.

The Hessian — second partial derivatives, and checking convexity

Just as the second derivative \(f''(x)\) of a single-variable function measured curvature (the central idea of this curriculum's Calculus for Finance lesson), a multivariable function has a whole grid of second partial derivatives — one for every pair of variables, including differentiating with respect to the same variable twice. Stacking all of them into a matrix gives the Hessian, \(H\):

\[ H = \begin{pmatrix} \dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2} & \dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x \partial y} \\[8pt] \dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y \partial x} & \dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y^2} \end{pmatrix} \]
∂²f/∂x²curvature in the pure-x direction, y frozen throughout
∂²f/∂x∂ya "mixed" partial — how ∂f/∂x itself changes as y moves
Hthe Hessian — every second partial derivative, in one matrix

Under mild smoothness conditions the two mixed partials are always equal (\(\partial^2 f/\partial x\partial y = \partial^2 f/\partial y\partial x\)), which is why the Hessian above is symmetric — the exact same property the Deeper Foundations linear algebra lesson proved guarantees real eigenvalues and perpendicular eigenvectors. That connection is not decorative: the Hessian's eigenvalues are precisely what a later Numerical Methods lesson's convex-optimization material checks to certify whether a stationary point (where the gradient is zero) is a genuine minimum, a maximum, or a saddle point — all-positive eigenvalues mean every direction curves upward (a minimum), all-negative means every direction curves downward (a maximum), and mixed signs mean a saddle: curving up in some directions and down in others. If any eigenvalue is exactly zero (and none has the opposite sign), this second-order test is inconclusive and higher-order terms decide.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

The Hessian is like checking a bowl from every angle to make sure it's really a bowl, and not secretly shaped like a horse saddle that dips down one way but curves up another. Just because a spot feels flat doesn't mean it's the bottom of a bowl — the Hessian is the tool that checks the shape all around it.

The multivariable chain rule

The ordinary chain rule from the calculus lesson (differentiating a function of a function) has a multivariable version needed whenever several variables all depend, in turn, on some other underlying variable — for instance, an option's value \(V(S, t)\) where \(S\) itself might be modeled as changing with \(t\). If \(f\) depends on \(x\) and \(y\), and both \(x\) and \(y\) depend on some variable \(u\), the total rate of change of \(f\) with respect to \(u\) sums the contribution through each path:

\[ \frac{df}{du} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\frac{dx}{du} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\frac{dy}{du} \]
(∂f/∂x)(dx/du)the part of f's change coming through x
(∂f/∂y)(dy/du)the part of f's change coming through y
sum bothevery path a change can travel through gets added, not chosen between

Read this as: if \(u\) nudges both \(x\) and \(y\), the total effect on \(f\) is the sum of the effect flowing through the \(x\) channel and the effect flowing through the \(y\) channel — you don't pick one path, you add up every path's contribution. This exact rule, extended to a randomly-moving \(x\), is the multivariable machinery Itô's Lemma (built from the Taylor series covered in Calculus for Finance) builds on.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine turning one dial that's wired up to two different machines at once, and each machine changes your final score a bit. The total change in your score is just the change from machine one added to the change from machine two — you add up every path the wiggle traveled through, instead of picking just one.

Worked example

Two portfolio value functions, two sets of partial derivatives

1

The trivial case — a linear two-asset portfolio

\(V(S_1, S_2) = w_1 S_1 + w_2 S_2\), with weights \(w_1 = 0.6\), \(w_2 = 0.4\):

\[ \frac{\partial V}{\partial S_1} = 0.6 \qquad \frac{\partial V}{\partial S_2} = 0.4 \]

Each partial derivative is simply that asset's fixed weight — a $1 move in \(S_1\) alone moves the portfolio by exactly \$0.60, regardless of \(S_2\)'s value, because the two terms don't interact at all in a linear sum.

2

The richer case — a dividend-paying forward value

\(V(S,t) = S e^{-\delta t}\), with \(S = \$100\), \(\delta = 0.03\) (a 3% continuous dividend yield), \(t = 2\) years:

\[ \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} = e^{-\delta t} = e^{-0.03 \times 2} = e^{-0.06} \approx 0.9418 \]
\[ \frac{\partial V}{\partial t} = -\delta S e^{-\delta t} = -0.03 \times 100 \times 0.9418 \approx -2.825 \]

Read plainly: at \(S=100\), \(t=2\), a \$1 rise in the spot price raises the forward value by about \$0.9418 (slightly less than dollar-for-dollar, because two more years of dividends are still being given up); holding the spot price fixed, each additional year of horizon lowers the forward value by about \$2.825, as one more year of dividends is foregone. Two completely different numbers, from differentiating the exact same function with respect to two different variables — the entire mechanical content of this lesson, worked with real numbers.

Real-world trading scenario

The Greeks on a risk screen are partial derivatives, not four unrelated numbers

An option's value is a function of several variables at once, \(V(S, t, \sigma, r)\) — the underlying price, time to expiry, volatility, and interest rate, in the notation the existing Black-Scholes lesson in this curriculum already uses. Every "Greek" quoted on a live option risk screen is, without exception, one partial derivative of that single function, with respect to one of those variables, everything else held fixed:

Delta, \(\Delta = \partial V/\partial S\)

Sensitivity to the underlying price alone — time, volatility, and rates frozen. Exactly the same partial-derivative mechanics as \(\partial V/\partial S\) in the dividend-forward worked example above.

Theta, \(\Theta = \partial V/\partial t\)

Sensitivity to the passage of time alone — the spot price, volatility, and rates frozen. The exact same partial-derivative mechanics as \(\partial V/\partial t\) above, just applied to an option's value function instead of a forward value. (This sign convention takes \(t\) as calendar time; if \(t\) is time remaining to expiry, \(\Theta = -\partial V/\partial t\).)

Vega, \(\text{Vega} = \partial V/\partial \sigma\)

Sensitivity to volatility alone — spot, time, and rates frozen. Not usually written with a Greek letter (vega isn't actually one), but structurally identical: one more partial derivative of the same \(V\).

Gamma, \(\Gamma = \partial^2 V/\partial S^2\)

The curvature of \(V\) with respect to \(S\) — a second partial derivative, one diagonal entry of \(V\)'s Hessian with respect to its variables, exactly the second-order Taylor correction term covered in Calculus for Finance.

A trader reading "Delta 0.60, Gamma 0.04, Theta −0.03, Vega 0.12" off a screen is reading four entries from the same gradient-and-Hessian structure built on this page, evaluated at today's spot, today's time remaining, and today's volatility — not four independent facts about the option that happen to share a Greek-letter naming convention. This is exactly why a full risk report can combine them into a single multivariable Taylor approximation for how the option's value would move under a joint shock to spot, time, and volatility at once — sum each variable's own partial-derivative contribution, precisely the structure the multivariable chain rule above describes for several inputs moving together.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

Multivariable calculus is the load-bearing vocabulary behind three distinct, common desk-level activities, each of which reuses the exact same partial-derivative/gradient/Hessian machinery built above:

🇬🇷

Risk decomposition via the Greeks

A derivatives book's total risk is reported as a set of partial derivatives (Delta, Gamma, Theta, Vega, Rho) with respect to each market input, precisely so a desk can see which single input is driving P&L without repricing the whole book for every scenario.

⛰️

Model fitting via gradient descent

Fitting almost any statistical or machine-learning model reduces to minimizing some error function of many parameters at once — gradient descent repeatedly steps in the direction \(-\nabla f\) (built above) until the gradient is (near) zero.

🔍

Certifying a genuine optimum

Once an optimizer reports a stationary point (gradient ≈ 0), checking the Hessian's eigenvalues there is standard practice to confirm it's actually a minimum, not a saddle point a naive gradient-only check would miss.

📊

Scenario and stress-testing grids

A "what happens if spot moves 2% and volatility jumps 5 points" question is a multivariable question by construction — answered with the multivariable chain rule/Taylor structure, summing each input's own partial contribution.

The habit worth internalizing from real practice: whenever a model or a risk report has more than one input, the honest first question is "sensitivity with respect to which variable, holding what else fixed?" — a single unqualified word like "sensitivity" or "risk" is ambiguous the moment more than one variable is in play, and a partial derivative is precisely the tool that removes that ambiguity by making the "holding what else fixed" part of the question explicit rather than implied.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where this goes wrong in practice

Treating one Greek in isolation when the market moves several things at once. Delta alone describes a move in spot with time and volatility held perfectly still — but a real market move almost never holds those other variables still. A large spot move is often accompanied by a volatility move too (in either direction, depending on the market), and reading Delta alone as "the" answer to "what will this option do" quietly assumes an all-else-equal condition that may not hold at all.

Forgetting that a stationary gradient doesn't guarantee a minimum. \(\nabla f = 0\) only says the function is flat in every direction at that point — it could be a genuine minimum, a maximum, or a saddle point (curving up in some directions, down in others). The Hessian's eigenvalues are what actually distinguish these cases; stopping an optimizer the moment the gradient is small, without checking the Hessian, can silently report a saddle point as if it were a solved minimum.

Mixing up which variables are held fixed. The entire meaning of a partial derivative depends on exactly which other variables are frozen — \(\partial V/\partial S\) computed with volatility held constant is a genuinely different number from a "sensitivity to spot" computed while also letting an implied-volatility surface shift with spot (a real, common practitioner adjustment called a "sticky" convention). Quoting a partial derivative without being explicit about what's held fixed is a common source of confusion when comparing numbers from two different models or desks.

Confusing a mixed partial derivative's symmetry with a claim about causation. \(\partial^2 f/\partial x \partial y = \partial^2 f/\partial y \partial x\) is a mathematical fact about how a smooth function's curvature works, not a statement that changes in \(x\) and \(y\) cause each other symmetrically in the real world — it's purely a property of the function \(f\) being differentiated, not of whatever real-world quantities \(x\) and \(y\) happen to represent.

Scenario drill · an illustrative risk screen

Spot and volatility move together. Where did the money come from?

Your option position shows Delta 0.60, Gamma 0.04, Vega 0.12 per volatility point and Theta −0.03 per day. Overnight, spot rises by 2 and implied volatility rises by 5 points. Your manager asks how much the position made, and why. What do you do?

  1. Use Delta: 0.60 × 2 = +1.20.
    Delta is the slope with volatility and time frozen. Here volatility did not stand still, so Delta alone misses part of the move.
  2. Add up each input's own contribution: Delta 0.60 × 2 = +1.20, Gamma ½ × 0.04 × 2² = +0.08, Vega 0.12 × 5 = +0.60, Theta −0.03 for the day. About +1.85 in total.
    Each Greek is one partial derivative, so each input's move is paid for by its own slope, and the contributions add. You get the total and a clear answer to "why".
  3. Reprice the option from scratch with the new spot, volatility and date.
    That gives an accurate total, and for big moves it is the better number. But it does not say how much came from spot and how much from volatility, which is what was asked. Use it to check the sum.
  4. Average the four Greeks and multiply by the size of the move.
    The Greeks measure sensitivity to different inputs, in different units. Averaging them mixes up dials that have nothing to do with each other.

How it works out. This is an illustrative screen using the Greek values quoted in this lesson, with units added for the example; it is not a real position. Delta alone says +1.20; the sum of the partials says about +1.85, and a third of that came from volatility. This is the multivariable chain rule at work: when several inputs move, add the contribution through every path.

Section 04

Key Takeaways

✓A partial derivative is an ordinary derivative with everything else frozen. \(\partial f/\partial x\) applies the exact same single-variable rules from the calculus lesson, treating every other variable as a fixed constant.
✓The gradient stacks every partial derivative into one vector. \(\nabla f\) points in the direction the function increases fastest — the negative of that direction is what gradient descent walks along.
✓The Hessian is the multivariable version of curvature. Its eigenvalues distinguish a genuine minimum from a maximum or a saddle point at a stationary point — a direct forward-pointer to convex optimization.
✓The option Greeks are literally partial derivatives. Delta, Theta, and Vega are first partial derivatives of the option value with respect to spot, time, and volatility; Gamma is a second partial derivative — not four separate special ideas.
✓The worked forward-value example shows two different partials of one function. \(\partial V/\partial S \approx 0.9418\) and \(\partial V/\partial t \approx -2.825\) for the same \(V(S,t)=Se^{-\delta t}\) — the mechanics only ever differ in which variable is frozen.
⚠A stationary gradient alone doesn't certify a minimum. \(\nabla f = 0\) is consistent with a minimum, a maximum, or a saddle point — the Hessian is what tells them apart.
ℹThe Greeks are partial derivatives. Since Black–Scholes–Merton (1973), options books are hedged and reported by delta, gamma, theta and vega: one sensitivity per input, from one value function.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. In plain language, what is the difference between an ordinary derivative \(f'(x)\) and a partial derivative \(\partial f/\partial x\)?
An ordinary derivative applies to a function of a single variable — there is nothing else to hold fixed, because there is nothing else. A partial derivative applies to a function of several variables, and the entire extra idea is that every variable other than the one you're differentiating with respect to is held completely constant while you compute it. Mechanically, once the other variables are frozen, the actual differentiation step uses the identical rules as ordinary, single-variable calculus — the only new concept is the freezing.
Q2. Why does the gradient \(\nabla f\) point in the direction of steepest increase, rather than, say, the direction of the largest single partial derivative?
The gradient combines every partial derivative into a single vector, and it can be shown (via the multivariable chain rule applied along an arbitrary direction) that the rate of increase of \(f\) along any given direction equals the gradient's length times the cosine of the angle between that direction and the gradient — which is maximized exactly when the direction chosen lines up with the gradient itself (cosine of zero degrees is 1, the largest possible value). No single partial derivative alone captures this, because a partial derivative only reports the rate of change along one specific coordinate axis, not along every possible diagonal direction in between.
Q3. For \(V(S,t) = Se^{-\delta t}\), why is \(\partial V/\partial t\) negative while \(\partial V/\partial S\) is positive?
\(\partial V/\partial S = e^{-\delta t}\) is always positive (it's an exponential of a real number), meaning the forward value always rises when the spot price rises, holding time fixed — an intuitive result, since the forward value is directly proportional to the spot price. \(\partial V/\partial t = -\delta S e^{-\delta t}\) carries a minus sign because a longer horizon means more dividend payments are given up before delivery, which continually erodes the forward value relative to simply holding the spot price itself — holding \(S\) fixed, letting \(t\) grow makes the discount factor \(e^{-\delta t}\) shrink, so the forward value shrinks too.
Q4. Why is it accurate to say an option's Gamma is "a second partial derivative" rather than just "a second derivative"?
Because the option's value function \(V(S,t,\sigma,r)\) depends on several variables at once, and Gamma specifically measures curvature with respect to just one of them — the underlying price \(S\) — while time, volatility, and rates are all held fixed throughout. Calling it simply "a second derivative" would drop that essential detail about which variable is being varied and which are frozen; "partial" is the word that carries exactly that information, distinguishing Gamma (\(\partial^2V/\partial S^2\)) from, say, a hypothetical volatility-curvature term (\(\partial^2V/\partial\sigma^2\)), which is a different, less commonly quoted quantity entirely.
Q5. An optimizer reports that it found a point where the gradient of a loss function is (numerically) zero. Is this enough to conclude the point is a minimum? What else would you check?
No — a zero gradient only means the function is momentarily flat in every direction at that point; it is equally consistent with a genuine minimum, a maximum, or a saddle point (curving upward in some directions and downward in others). To tell these apart, check the Hessian matrix's eigenvalues at that point: all-positive eigenvalues indicate a genuine minimum (every direction curves upward), all-negative indicate a maximum, and a mix of signs indicates a saddle point; a zero eigenvalue (with no opposite sign) leaves the test inconclusive — exactly the check a later Numerical Methods lesson's convex-optimization material formalizes.
