Regularized Regression, Why the Penalty's Shape Matters

Plain OLS regression has no defense against too many predictors, or predictors that overlap with each other — it will happily produce wildly unstable, even nonsensical coefficients while the in-sample fit still looks great. Ridge and LASSO both fix this by penalizing large coefficients, but they penalize them differently — one with a sum of squares, one with a sum of absolute values — and that one change in shape decides whether a weak predictor gets quietly shrunk or deleted outright. This builds directly on the earlier Regression Diagnostics lesson's warnings about collinearity.

Section 1

Why OLS breaks when predictors pile up

Two ways to hand a regression more than it can cleanly untangle.

📈

Too many predictors, too few observations

With many candidate predictors relative to the number of rows, OLS has enormous freedom to fit the specific noise pattern of this sample — and it will, because minimizing squared error is the only thing it's told to do.

🔗

Highly correlated predictors

When two predictors carry nearly the same information, OLS can't tell which one deserves credit for their shared signal — it can assign an inflated weight to one and a compensating, even sign-flipped, negative weight to the other, and both estimates are close to meaningless individually.

⚠

Sometimes it's worse than unstable

Push collinearity far enough — a predictor that's an exact linear combination of others — and the matrix OLS needs to invert stops being invertible at all. The "solution" isn't just unstable; it's mathematically indeterminate.

Where this connects — Regression Diagnostics

The earlier Regression Diagnostics lesson in this curriculum covers what collinearity does to inference — inflated standard errors, unstable t-statistics and p-values, once the residuals misbehave. This lesson picks up one step earlier: what happens to the coefficients themselves, and the two classic fixes for it.

Section 2

The fix: make big coefficients cost something

Plain OLS minimizes only the fit error. Regularization adds a second term that punishes coefficient size.

The shared shape
‖y − Xβ‖²
+
λ · penalty(β)
=
what gets minimized
The first term is the ordinary least-squares loss — how well the model fits the data, unchanged from plain OLS. The second term is new: a penalty on the size of the coefficients, dialed up or down by λ. At λ = 0 this is exactly plain OLS; as λ grows, fitting the data starts costing against keeping coefficients large.
Ridge and LASSO share this exact structure. Every difference between them comes from one thing only: how "penalty(β)" is defined.

This is a deliberate trade: a little bias (the fitted coefficients are no longer the unbiased OLS estimates) bought in exchange for a large reduction in variance (how wildly the fitted coefficients would swing if the fit were re-run on a slightly different sample). That trade is the entire idea underneath both methods — they just pay for it with differently shaped penalties.

Section 3

Ridge — penalize the sum of squares

Hoerl & Kennard, 1970 — written specifically to address correlated ("nonorthogonal") predictors.

The Ridge objective
‖y − Xβ‖²
+
λ Σⱼ βⱼ²
The penalty is the sum of every coefficient squared. Squaring exaggerates large values, so the cheapest way to satisfy the penalty is usually to spread a given amount of "explaining power" across several correlated predictors rather than piling it onto one — exactly the fix for OLS's wild, sign-flipped coefficients under collinearity.
↘

Shrinks everything, smoothly

Every coefficient moves toward zero as λ grows — the ones with real signal, and the weak ones too, in proportion to how little they were carrying.

⊘

Never lands on exactly zero

Shrinking a squared term always leaves a diminishing-returns tail — β approaches zero as λ grows but essentially never reaches it exactly, no matter how large λ gets.

🎚

Shrinks; does not select

Ridge doesn't know a predictor is useless — it only knows its coefficient is small, and shrinks accordingly. Every original predictor stays in the model.

Section 4

LASSO — penalize the sum of absolute values

Tibshirani, 1996 — Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator. The name describes exactly what it does.

The LASSO objective
‖y − Xβ‖²
+
λ Σⱼ |βⱼ|
One change from Ridge: squares become absolute values. It sounds cosmetic. It isn't — that single change is what makes LASSO's solutions regularly land with one or more coefficients at exactly zero, not just small.
✂

Deletes, not just trims

Weak or redundant predictors can be pushed all the way to exactly zero — effectively dropped from the model entirely, as if they were never included.

🧹

Automatic feature selection

The surviving nonzero coefficients form a shorter, more interpretable model — this is a side effect of the penalty's shape, not a separate selection step bolted on afterward.

🎲

Not free of arbitrariness

Among a group of near-duplicate predictors, which one survives can be somewhat arbitrary — a slightly different sample can flip the pick. Section 9 covers Elastic Net's fix for exactly this.

Section 6

The geometry: why a diamond zeroes and a circle doesn't

Both penalties can be rewritten as a constraint: minimize the loss, but keep the penalty inside a budget t. That budget is a shape.

Two constraint regions, same loss contours
The regularized solution is wherever the OLS loss contours (grey/purple ellipses, centered on the unconstrained estimate) first "kiss" the boundary of the allowed region as it expands outward. A circle is smooth everywhere — no special points — so a tilted ellipse touches it at some ordinary edge point, generally off both axes. A diamond's corners sit exactly on the axes; a corner is a sharp change in direction, and a wide range of ellipse shapes will first touch the diamond precisely there rather than sliding onto a flat edge.
That geometric fact — corners are far more likely tangent points than any single point on a smooth curve is — is the entire reason LASSO tends to zero coefficients out and Ridge does not. It isn't a numerical coincidence; it's a direct consequence of the penalty's shape.

Section 9

Elastic Net — splitting the difference

Zou & Hastie, 2005 — built specifically to fix LASSO's weak spot with correlated groups.

LASSO's weak spot
  • Among a group of strongly correlated, genuinely useful predictors, LASSO tends to pick one somewhat arbitrarily and zero out the rest
  • A slightly different sample can flip which member of the group survives
  • Close to a coin flip when predictors are near-duplicates, as x₁/x₂ were above
Elastic Net's fix
  • Blend both penalties: λ(ρ‖β‖₁ + (1−ρ)‖β‖₂²), with ρ ∈ [0,1]
  • ρ = 1 is pure LASSO, ρ = 0 is pure Ridge
  • The L2 piece discourages the all-or-nothing pick — a correlated group tends to survive together, shrunk, instead of one member kept at full strength
Not a free lunch

Elastic Net adds a second tuning parameter, ρ, on top of λ — both usually chosen together by cross-validation. It's the standard answer specifically when the practical concern is "LASSO's selection is unstable because my predictors come in correlated groups," not a strictly-better replacement for either method on its own.

Section 10

Choosing λ, and the tree-based alternative

✓

λ is chosen by cross-validation, never in-sample

In-sample loss is always minimized at λ = 0 by construction — no regularization always fits the training data at least as well. Picking λ to maximize in-sample fit would always pick "don't regularize," which defeats the point.

🌲

Trees answer the same problem differently

A decision tree only ever splits on the single most useful predictor at each node, so predictors that never help a split are implicitly ignored — nonlinearity and interactions come free, but there's no clean per-predictor coefficient and no LASSO-style provable exact-zero guarantee.

🎯

The choice depends on the assumed relationship

Reach for regularized linear models when the relationship is plausibly linear and interpretable coefficients matter; reach for trees or boosting when it's likely nonlinear or involves real interaction effects.

Regularized regression and tree-based methods are two different answers to the same "too many predictors" question — not competitors so much as tools suited to different assumptions about the underlying relationship.
Section 11

Where this gets misused in practice

📏

Forgetting to standardize predictors first

Both penalties act directly on raw coefficient magnitude, which depends entirely on a predictor's scale. Standardizing to mean zero, unit variance before fitting is not optional — skip it and the penalty punishes arbitrarily-scaled predictors unevenly.

📌

Treating "LASSO selected it" as proof

Which predictor survives among a correlated group can be genuinely unstable. A single fit's selected-variable list isn't a validated claim about the "true" drivers on its own — checking selection stability across resamples, or using Elastic Net, is the fix.

📐

Both stay linear models underneath

Regularization changes how coefficients are estimated; it adds no capacity to capture a genuinely nonlinear relationship or an interaction a straight-line combination can't represent.

🔄

Picking λ once and never revisiting it

The right amount of regularization depends on the current noise-to-signal ratio, which can drift — a λ cross-validated on one period isn't guaranteed to still be right once the correlation structure among predictors shifts.

Section 13

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Geometrically, why does LASSO tend to produce exact zeros while Ridge essentially never does?

The regularized solution is where the loss contours first touch the constraint region's boundary. A diamond's sharp corners — which sit on the axes, where one coefficient is exactly zero — catch that tangent point disproportionately often. A circle has no such special points.
Question 2

What does Ridge's penalty term actually sum?

Ridge's L2 penalty is the sum of squared coefficients. Squaring exaggerates large values, which is why Ridge tends to spread weight across correlated predictors rather than piling it onto one.
Question 3

In the tabs example, why did plain OLS give β₂ = −1.3, a negative coefficient, for a predictor with no true negative relationship to y?

Classic multicollinearity: individually unstable, even sign-flipped coefficients that still combine (3.4 − 1.3 = 2.1) to a reasonable joint prediction near the true β₁ = 2.0.
Question 4

True or false: as λ increases in Ridge, a coefficient will eventually be pushed to exactly zero, just as it would under LASSO.

This is the defining structural difference between the two penalties, not a matter of degree. Only LASSO's L1 corner-geometry regularly forces exact zeros.
Question 5

Why must predictors be standardized before fitting Ridge or LASSO, when it isn't strictly required for plain OLS?

A predictor measured in large units naturally needs a small coefficient, and vice versa. Without standardizing, the penalty shrinks predictors unevenly based on arbitrary units rather than actual importance. Plain OLS has no penalty term, so it has no such issue.
Section 14

What to carry forward

01

Plain OLS breaks down under too many, or too correlated, predictors — variance explodes, coefficients can even sign-flip, even while the in-sample fit looks fine. This is the earlier Regression Diagnostics lesson's warning, one step upstream.

02

Both Ridge and LASSO add a penalty on coefficient size to the OLS loss, trading a little bias for a large reduction in variance.

03

Ridge's penalty is the sum of squared coefficients (L2) — it shrinks everything smoothly, but essentially never reaches exactly zero.

04

LASSO's penalty is the sum of absolute coefficients (L1) — it can drive some coefficients to exactly zero, performing automatic feature selection.

05

The difference is geometry: a circle's smooth edge rarely touches at an axis point; a diamond's corners sit exactly on the axes and catch the tangent point disproportionately often.

06

λ is always chosen by cross-validation, never in-sample, and predictors must be standardized first — both penalties are scale-dependent.

What this now lets you do

Build a linear model on many, or highly correlated, predictors that stays stable instead of overfitting their noise. Choose Ridge when the honest prior is "most of these carry a little real signal" and LASSO when it's "most of these are noise, a few really matter" — or reach for Elastic Net to hedge between them when predictors come in correlated groups. And you now know this isn't purely theoretical here: a Ridge regression, as a meta-learner, is genuinely running in this repo's AnalogML walk-forward pipeline today.
