Fama-MacBeth, Pricing the Risk, Not Just the Exposure

A factor-loading regression tells you how exposed an asset is to a risk factor. It never tells you whether the market actually pays anyone for bearing that exposure — or how to get an honest standard error when every asset's return in a given month is pushed around by the same shared shock. Fama and MacBeth's 1973 two-stage procedure answers both, one period at a time.

Section 1

Two different questions, easy to confuse

A beta and a risk premium sound similar. They answer completely different things.

Beta — exposure

"How much does this asset move per unit move in the factor?" Answered by a single time-series regression, one per asset — exactly what a Fama-French/Carhart factor regression gives you.

Lambda — price of risk

"Does the market actually compensate anyone for that exposure, and by how much per unit?" A beta alone cannot answer this — it needs an entirely separate estimation step, run across assets instead of across time.

A factor with a large, reliable payoff per unit of exposure is worth being exposed to. A factor whose exposure earns nothing on average is not — no matter how cleanly its betas were estimated. This deck is about the second question only.
Section 2

Stage 1 — measure exposure once, per asset

One time-series regression per asset, using every period in the sample. Run once; never touched again.

Setup for this deck

→
β̂_A = 0.7
Asset B
R_B,t on F_t
→
β̂_B = 1.0
Asset C
R_C,t on F_t
→
β̂_C = 1.3
Three FX carry-sorted portfolios, three fixed exposures — estimated once from the full history. Every later step in this deck reuses these exact three numbers and never re-estimates them.

This is the identical OLS slope formula the Correlation & Linear Regression lesson derives, β̂ = Cov(X,Y)/Var(X) — here X is the factor's return, Y is one asset's return, and the "sample" being summed over is time. Nothing new is invented in Stage 1; it's just that same regression, run once per asset.

Section 3

Stage 2 — price it fresh, every single period

Same three betas, four different months. Step through each month's cross-sectional regression.

Try it

B: β=1.0, R=2.0%
C: β=1.3, R=3.5%
Regress this month's 3 returns on the 3 fixed betas: λ̂₁ = Cov(β,R)/Var(β) = 0.25/0.06 = 4.1667. Higher exposure paid off handsomely this month.
A: β=0.7, R=−0.5%
B: β=1.0, R=−1.0%
C: β=1.3, R=−2.0%
Same three betas, this month's returns: λ̂₂ = −0.15/0.06 = −2.5000. Higher exposure was punished this month — a bad month for the factor.
A: β=0.7, R=0.8%
B: β=1.0, R=1.0%
C: β=1.3, R=1.1%
Returns barely differ across exposures this month: λ̂₃ = 0.03/0.06 = 0.5000 — a small, nearly flat price of risk.
A: β=0.7, R=−1.0%
B: β=1.0, R=0.0%
C: β=1.3, R=1.3%
A wide spread again: λ̂₄ = 0.23/0.06 = 3.8333.
Var(β̂) = 0.06 is fixed across all four months — it only depends on the three (unchanging) betas, not on that month's returns.

Four months, four completely separate regressions, four different answers: 4.1667, −2.5000, 0.5000, 3.8333. That spread — a good month, a bad month, a flat month, a good month again — is the entire reason a single pooled number can't be trusted without knowing how much it bounces around.

The one idea this whole deck exists to teach

Wrong. The 3 assets within any one month all lean the same direction — they share that month's shock. There are really only about 4 independent "stories" here, not 12. The reported standard error comes out far too small.

Right. Each month is reduced to one number before it ever reaches the averaging step. Whatever correlation existed among the 3 assets within a month is now irrelevant — that month only ever contributes one data point.
The mechanism in one sentence

Fama-MacBeth doesn't model or remove the cross-sectional correlation between assets — it makes that correlation irrelevant, by summarizing each period down to one number before it's ever averaged.

Section 6

More periods, tighter standard error

SE(λ̄) = s_λ/√T. Drag the slider and watch the standard error shrink as more periods are added — holding the spread of the λ̂_t's fixed at this deck's own worked value.

This is the entire practical argument for wanting more history in a Fama-MacBeth study: nothing about the estimation procedure itself changes as T grows, but the honest standard error keeps shrinking — exactly the ordinary standard-error-of-a-mean behavior, applied to a time series of period-level risk-premium estimates instead of to raw returns.

Section 7

A second catch: the betas themselves are estimates

📏

β̂_i isn't the true β_i

Stage 1 only ever produces an estimate, fit from a finite time series — it carries its own sampling error, however small.

↘

A noisy regressor biases the slope toward zero

Using an estimated (not exactly known) β̂_i as Stage 2's regressor is a classical errors-in-variables problem — it attenuates λ̂_t, pulling it toward zero.

🔧

Shanken (1992) is the standard fix

Rather than re-deriving it here: Shanken's correction inflates the reported Fama-MacBeth standard errors, to account for the extra uncertainty that Stage 1's estimation error introduces into Stage 2.

🧮

Two separate corrections, not one

Shanken fixes the errors-in-variables problem. It does not, by itself, fix autocorrelation in the λ̂_t series across time — that needs its own robust standard error, on top.

Where this needs qualifying

Plain, uncorrected Fama-MacBeth standard errors are typically too optimistic — smaller than they should be — for a reason completely separate from the pooling mistake two sections ago. A result that looks significant with plain standard errors can lose that significance once Shanken's correction is applied.

Section 9

A big-looking premium isn't automatically a real one

From the calculator above

With only 4 months, λ̄ = 1.50 with SE ≈ 1.57 gives t ≈ 0.96 — nowhere near the conventional |t| > 2 bar. A 1.50%-per-unit average that looks economically large is, honestly reported, statistically indistinguishable from zero with this little data.

Real Fama-MacBeth studies lean on decades of monthly data, not four months, precisely because a standard deviation computed from a handful of period estimates is itself extremely noisy. Even a properly-computed, statistically significant λ̄ after both the Shanken and autocorrelation corrections only answers one narrow question: was this specific factor's premium reliably nonzero in this specific historical sample. It says nothing about whether the factor was chosen after looking at the same data, or whether the premium survives out-of-sample and after costs.

A correctly-computed standard error is a statement about the estimator, not a certificate of tradable edge.
Section 10

Where practitioners actually use this

📐

The default way to price a factor

Some form of Fama-MacBeth shows up in nearly every asset-pricing paper that estimates a λ, not just a β.

🧭

Shanken-corrected SEs as the reported default

Applied work commonly reports the Shanken-corrected standard error alongside the raw one, precisely to avoid overstating significance.

🔁

Newey-West on top, when λ̂_t trends

A second, separate robustness layer for autocorrelation in the period-level estimates themselves — the same fix the regression lesson's own pitfalls section covers, applied here to λ̂_t instead of raw residuals.

Section 11

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

What does Stage 1 of Fama-MacBeth estimate, and how many times does it run?

Stage 1 regresses each asset's own returns on the factor across all T periods, once per asset — producing a fixed β̂_i that Stage 2 then reuses without ever re-estimating it.
Question 2

What does Stage 2 estimate, and along which axis of the data does it run?

Stage 2 fixes a period and regresses across assets — the exact opposite axis from Stage 1, which fixed an asset and regressed across time.
Question 3

Why does pooling all asset-period observations into one OLS regression understate the true standard error?

OLS's standard-error formula assumes independent errors. Assets in the same month share that month's common shock, so a pooled panel has far fewer genuinely independent pieces of information than its raw row count suggests.
Question 4

How does Fama-MacBeth's design make that cross-sectional correlation irrelevant to the final standard error?

Whatever correlation existed among assets within a period is baked into that period's one λ̂_t, but the final inference step only ever sees T period-level numbers — never the raw correlated observations directly.
Question 5

What does the Shanken (1992) correction actually change about a Fama-MacBeth result?

Shanken's correction addresses errors-in-variables by inflating the reported standard errors — it does not touch λ̄ itself, only how confidently that estimate can be reported as statistically significant.
Section 12

What to carry forward

01

A beta measures exposure; a Fama-MacBeth λ measures the price the market pays for that exposure — two genuinely separate questions.

02

Stage 1 estimates each asset's beta once, across time. Stage 2 estimates one λ̂_t per period, across assets, reusing the fixed betas every time.

03

The final risk premium is the average of the λ̂_t's; its standard error comes from how much they spread out over time — not from a pooled panel's residuals.

04

Pooling every asset-period observation into one regression badly understates the standard error, because assets in the same period share common shocks.

05

Estimated (not true) Stage-1 betas bias λ̂_t toward zero; Shanken's correction inflates the standard errors to account for it.

What this now lets you do

Tell the difference between "this factor has a well-measured exposure" and "this factor is actually priced" — and know exactly why a single pooled panel regression can't honestly answer the second question. Recognize both the pooling mistake and the errors-in-variables correction by name the next time a factor's risk premium is quoted with a standard error attached.
