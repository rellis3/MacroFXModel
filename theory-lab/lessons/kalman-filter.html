The Kalman Filter & Adaptive Relationships

A recursive rule for tracking a hidden quantity — like a hedge ratio that drifts slowly rather than staying fixed — that blends yesterday's estimate with today's noisy observation in exactly the proportion each one deserves.

CONCEPT — not yet built in this repo ~17 min read Prereqs: Bayesian Inference, Ornstein-Uhlenbeck & Mean Reversion

Why this matters

Suppose you believe two assets move together — a currency pair and a rate spread, a stock and its sector ETF, an equity index and a related futures contract — and you want a number that describes how much: "a 1-unit move in A tends to come with a \(\beta\)-unit move in B." The standard way to get that number is ordinary least squares (OLS) regression over some lookback window, say the last 60 days. That gives you one \(\beta\), estimated once, treated as if it were a fixed physical constant until you re-run the regression tomorrow on a new 60-day window.

Two things are quietly wrong with that. First, a fixed-window regression weights every point inside the window equally and every point outside it zero — day 61 counts exactly as much as yesterday, and day 61 disappears completely the moment it rolls out of the window, even though nothing about the world changed at that exact boundary. That produces a real artifact: your estimated \(\beta\) can jump when an old, unremarkable data point falls off the back of the window, a discontinuity that has nothing to do with the market and everything to do with your choice of window length. Second, and more fundamentally, real relationships between assets are rarely constant. A hedge ratio between two related instruments can drift as the underlying economics change — correlations widen and narrow with the macro regime, a company's sensitivity to its sector shifts as its business mix shifts, a spread's lead-lag relationship to a currency pair strengthens or weakens with volatility (a pattern the yield-spread lessons in this repo's macro notes document directly — more in the repo box below). If the true relationship is genuinely moving, you don't want a number that's either "frozen for 60 days" (too slow to react) or "re-fit from scratch on a totally different window" (too jumpy and throws away everything you'd learned).

The Kalman filter solves a different, better-posed version of this problem: instead of assuming \(\beta\) is a constant to be estimated, assume it is a hidden, slowly-drifting quantity you can never observe directly — and use every new noisy observation to update your best running estimate of it, automatically weighing "trust what I already believed" against "trust what I just saw" based on how uncertain each one currently is. It does this with a simple, exact, closed-form recursion — no window to choose, no re-fitting from scratch, and (as this lesson will show explicitly) it is nothing more than the Bayesian updating from the previous lesson, applied one time step at a time to a quantity that moves.

Plain-English intuition

A GPS for a number you can't see directly

Imagine trying to track a boat at night using only a noisy radar ping once a minute. You never see the boat directly — you only get blips with error in them. But you're not starting from zero each minute either: you know roughly where the boat was a minute ago, and boats don't teleport, so you have a good guess of where it probably is now, before the new ping even arrives — it drifted a little, and your uncertainty about its position grew a little, simply because time passed and you haven't checked. That's the predict step. Then the new radar ping arrives. You don't throw away your prediction and trust the ping alone (radar is noisy — a single ping could be a fluke). You don't ignore the ping either (that's the only new information you have). You blend them: pull your estimate part of the way toward the new ping, by an amount that depends on how confident you were in your prediction versus how noisy the radar sensor is known to be. That's the update step, and the blending weight is called the Kalman gain. Do this once per minute, forever, and you get a smooth, continuously-updated track of the boat that's better than either "trust only my last guess" or "trust only the latest ping" alone. A hedge ratio that drifts over time is exactly the same tracking problem with the boat's position replaced by an unobservable number.

The math, step by step

A Kalman filter is built on a state-space model — two equations that separate "the thing you actually care about but can't see" from "the thing you can see but is corrupted by noise." Both equations below are written for the simplest possible case: a single hidden number (not a vector) evolving in one dimension. This is exactly the case you need for tracking a single hedge ratio or beta, and it's the cleanest way to see every piece of the machinery before it gets dressed up in matrix notation for more general problems.

The state equation describes how the hidden, unobservable quantity evolves from one time step to the next:

\[ x_t = x_{t-1} + w_t, \qquad w_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, Q) \]

Read this as: "the true hedge ratio today equals the true hedge ratio yesterday, plus a small random nudge." The nudge \(w_t\) is drawn from a Normal (Gaussian) distribution centered at 0 with variance \(Q\) — meaning the drift has no predictable direction (it's equally likely to nudge the ratio up or down) but it does have a typical size, controlled by \(Q\). A larger \(Q\) says "I believe this relationship can genuinely change quickly"; a smaller \(Q\) says "I believe it's nearly constant, only creeping very slowly." This particular form — today equals yesterday plus noise, with no other term — is called a random walk state equation, and it is a modeling choice, not a law of nature (see the pitfalls box for what that choice does and doesn't assume).

The observation equation describes how the hidden state connects to something you can actually measure:

\[ y_t = H_t\, x_t + v_t, \qquad v_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, R) \]

Read this as: "what I actually observe today, \(y_t\) — say, today's price move in asset B — is approximately \(H_t\) times the hidden true ratio, plus observation noise." In the hedge-ratio use case, \(H_t\) is typically the day's move in asset A (so \(H_t x_t\) is "how much B should have moved if the true ratio were exactly \(x_t\) and there were no noise"), and \(y_t\) is the day's actual move in B. The gap between them, \(v_t\), is everything that isn't explained by the linear relationship — idiosyncratic noise, microstructure, unrelated news — modeled as Gaussian with variance \(R\). A larger \(R\) says "a single day's observed ratio is a noisy, unreliable read on the truth"; a smaller \(R\) says "today's reading is quite trustworthy on its own."

\(x_t\)The hidden true state at time \(t\) — e.g. the true, unobservable hedge ratio. You never see this directly, only estimate it.
\(w_t\)Process noise — the random drift added to the state each step, \(w_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0,Q)\).
\(Q\)Process (state) variance — how much the true state is believed to drift per step. A modeling input you choose, not something observed directly.
\(y_t\)The observation at time \(t\) — the noisy, measurable quantity you actually have data for.
\(H_t\)The observation matrix (here, a scalar) linking the hidden state to the observation. In the hedge-ratio example, this is the day's move in the reference asset.
\(v_t\)Observation noise — the gap between the linear model's prediction and what's actually observed, \(v_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0,R)\).
\(R\)Observation (measurement) variance — how noisy a single observation is believed to be. Also a chosen modeling input.
\(\hat{x}_{t\mid t-1}\)The a priori (predicted) state estimate — the best guess of \(x_t\) using only information through \(t-1\), before seeing \(y_t\).
\(P_{t\mid t-1}\)The a priori error variance — how uncertain that predicted estimate is.
\(e_t\)The innovation (prediction error / residual) — the gap between what was actually observed and what the predicted state said should be observed: \(e_t = y_t - H_t\hat{x}_{t\mid t-1}\). This is literally "the surprise."
\(S_t\)The innovation variance — the total uncertainty in that surprise, combining prediction uncertainty and observation noise.
\(K_t\)The Kalman gain — the weight (between 0 and 1, in this scalar case) given to the new observation's surprise when updating the estimate.
\(\hat{x}_{t\mid t}\)The a posteriori (updated) state estimate — the best guess of \(x_t\) after folding in \(y_t\). This is the answer you actually use, e.g. as today's hedge ratio.
\(P_{t\mid t}\)The a posteriori error variance — the updated uncertainty, after the observation has (typically) sharpened it.

With those defined, the filter is exactly two steps, run once per new observation, forever.

Predict step. Before you look at today's data at all, project yesterday's belief forward using the state equation. Because the state equation here is a pure random walk (no deterministic drift term), the best guess for today is simply yesterday's estimate — but your uncertainty about it must grow, because time has passed and the state may have wandered:

\[ \hat{x}_{t\mid t-1} = \hat{x}_{t-1\mid t-1} \qquad\qquad P_{t\mid t-1} = P_{t-1\mid t-1} + Q \]

The left equation says "predict no change." The right equation is the important one: it inflates the uncertainty by exactly \(Q\), the process variance — the longer you go without checking, or the more that process noise is believed to be, the less confident the predicted estimate becomes. This inflation is what makes the filter willing to move when new data arrives, instead of becoming permanently over-confident.

Update step. Now the new observation \(y_t\) arrives. First compute the innovation — the gap between what was actually seen and what the prediction implied should have been seen — and its variance:

\[ e_t = y_t - H_t\hat{x}_{t\mid t-1} \qquad\qquad S_t = H_t^2 P_{t\mid t-1} + R \]

Then compute the Kalman gain — the heart of the whole method:

\[ K_t = \frac{P_{t\mid t-1}\, H_t}{S_t} = \frac{P_{t\mid t-1}\, H_t}{H_t^2 P_{t\mid t-1} + R} \]

Finally, blend the prediction with the observation, weighted by that gain, and shrink the uncertainty accordingly:

\[ \hat{x}_{t\mid t} = \hat{x}_{t\mid t-1} + K_t\, e_t \qquad\qquad P_{t\mid t} = (1 - K_t H_t)\, P_{t\mid t-1} \]

Look closely at what the gain formula is actually doing. \(K_t\) is a ratio: the uncertainty in the prediction, \(P_{t\mid t-1}H_t\), over the total uncertainty in the innovation, \(S_t\), which combines both prediction uncertainty and observation noise \(R\). If \(R\) is tiny relative to \(P_{t\mid t-1}\) — the new observation is very trustworthy relative to how unsure you currently are — \(K_t\) approaches 1, and the update step moves the estimate almost all the way to what the new data implies. If \(R\) is huge relative to \(P_{t\mid t-1}\) — the observation is mostly noise, and you're already fairly confident — \(K_t\) approaches 0, and the new, noisy data point is nearly ignored. Between those extremes, the filter finds the exact proportional blend, recomputed fresh at every single step, because \(P_{t\mid t-1}\) itself changes every step (it grows during predict, shrinks during update). This is precisely why a Kalman filter is not just "an exponential moving average with a fancy name": an EMA uses one fixed smoothing constant chosen in advance; the Kalman gain is derived, and adapts on its own as the filter's confidence changes.

This update step is a Bayesian update — not "similar to," identical to. Recall from the Bayesian Inference lesson: a prior belief plus new evidence combine, via Bayes' theorem, into a posterior belief. Here, the predicted state distribution \(\mathcal{N}(\hat{x}_{t\mid t-1}, P_{t\mid t-1})\) — your belief about \(x_t\) before seeing today's data — is exactly the prior. The observation model \(y_t \mid x_t \sim \mathcal{N}(H_t x_t, R)\) — how probable the observed \(y_t\) is, for each possible value of the hidden state — is exactly the likelihood. And the updated state distribution \(\mathcal{N}(\hat{x}_{t\mid t}, P_{t\mid t})\) is exactly the posterior: what you should believe about \(x_t\) once you fold in the new evidence, by Bayes' theorem, precisely as derived in that lesson. The reason the Kalman filter gets to skip the messy integral that Bayes' theorem usually requires (recall \(P(E)\), the marginal likelihood, is often the hard term to compute) is a special mathematical fact: when the prior is Gaussian and the likelihood is Gaussian with a linear relationship to the state, the posterior is exactly Gaussian too, and its mean and variance have the simple closed forms above. This property — Gaussian prior in, Gaussian posterior out, same family — is called conjugacy, and it's the entire reason the Kalman filter is a clean formula instead of a numerical simulation. Every predict/update cycle is one full pass of "prior → likelihood → posterior," then that posterior becomes tomorrow's prior — sequential Bayesian updating, exactly as described in that lesson, applied to a state that moves between updates.

Worked example

Tracking a drifting hedge ratio through two updates

Suppose you're estimating the true hedge ratio \(x_t\) between two related instruments — how many units of asset A you'd need to hedge one unit of asset B. You don't observe this ratio directly; each day you get a noisy read on it (e.g. from that day's realized price moves). Set up the filter with these chosen inputs:

Initial estimate \(\hat{x}_{0\mid 0} = 1.20\), initial uncertainty \(P_{0\mid 0} = 0.0400\) (a standard deviation of 0.20 — deliberately wide, since you're not yet confident). Process variance \(Q = 0.0009\) (std dev 0.03 — you believe the true ratio drifts only slightly per day). Observation variance \(R = 0.0100\) (std dev 0.10 — a single day's observed ratio is fairly noisy). For simplicity take \(H_t = 1\) (the observation is a direct, if noisy, read on the ratio itself).

Day 1: observation \(y_1 = 1.35\).

Predict:

\[ \hat{x}_{1\mid 0} = 1.20 \qquad P_{1\mid 0} = 0.0400 + 0.0009 = 0.0409 \]

Update:

\[ e_1 = 1.35 - 1.20 = 0.15 \qquad S_1 = 0.0409 + 0.0100 = 0.0509 \]
\[ K_1 = \frac{0.0409}{0.0509} \approx 0.804 \]
\[ \hat{x}_{1\mid 1} = 1.20 + 0.804 \times 0.15 \approx 1.321 \qquad P_{1\mid 1} = (1-0.804)\times 0.0409 \approx 0.0080 \]

Notice the gain is large — 0.804 — because the prior uncertainty (\(P_{1\mid 0}=0.0409\)) was still larger than the observation noise (\(R=0.0100\)); this early in the tracking process, the filter isn't very confident yet, so it leans heavily on the new data. The updated estimate, 1.321, sits 80% of the way from the old estimate (1.20) toward the new observation (1.35) — arithmetically exactly \(1.20 + 0.804(1.35-1.20)\).

Day 2: observation \(y_2 = 1.30\).

Predict (carry forward Day 1's posterior as today's prior):

\[ \hat{x}_{2\mid 1} = 1.321 \qquad P_{2\mid 1} = 0.0080 + 0.0009 = 0.0089 \]

Update:

\[ e_2 = 1.30 - 1.321 = -0.021 \qquad S_2 = 0.0089 + 0.0100 = 0.0189 \]
\[ K_2 = \frac{0.0089}{0.0189} \approx 0.471 \]
\[ \hat{x}_{2\mid 2} = 1.321 + 0.471\times(-0.021) \approx 1.311 \qquad P_{2\mid 2} = (1-0.471)\times 0.0089 \approx 0.0047 \]

The gain dropped from 0.804 to 0.471 in a single step, with no manual adjustment — it fell purely because \(P\) shrank after Day 1's update, so the prior is now more trustworthy relative to a single noisy observation. Keep running this recursion and the gain keeps settling, approaching a steady-state value governed entirely by the ratio \(Q/R\) (here \(Q/R = 0.09\), a small ratio, so the steady-state gain settles low — the filter trusts its running estimate much more than any single new reading, once it has enough history behind it). That steady-state gain is the filter's implicit, self-chosen "effective window length" — nobody set an \(N\)-day lookback anywhere; it fell out of two variance inputs.

How practitioners actually use this

The Kalman filter was developed by Rudolf Kálmán in 1960 for control and guidance engineering, and its first famous application was tracking a genuinely moving physical object under noisy sensors — it became the on-board navigation algorithm for the Apollo program's guidance computer, fusing noisy inertial-sensor readings into a reliable real-time estimate of the spacecraft's position and velocity. That "fuse a noisy stream of readings into a smooth estimate of a hidden, moving quantity" problem shows up everywhere control theory and estimation touch: GPS receivers, robotics and self-driving vehicle localization (often paired with radar/lidar in a broader sensor-fusion stack), and any tracking problem where you get periodic noisy measurements of something that moves continuously in between them.

In quantitative finance specifically, the dominant use case is exactly the one this lesson builds: time-varying parameter estimation — treating a coefficient that's conventionally estimated by static OLS regression (a hedge ratio, a market beta, a factor loading, a cointegrating relationship's slope) as a hidden state that drifts, and letting the Kalman filter track it online instead of re-running a fixed-window regression. This is the standard alternative to rolling-window OLS in statistical arbitrage and pairs trading — Ernest Chan's practitioner literature (cited below) documents this directly: track the hedge ratio between two cointegrated instruments with a Kalman filter instead of a rolling regression, because the filter reacts smoothly to genuine drift instead of exhibiting the "equal weight inside the window, zero weight outside it" discontinuity described at the top of this lesson. The same idea extends to tracking a time-varying beta of a security to a factor or index, a time-varying volatility level (in more elaborate state-space formulations), or the parameters of a broader dynamic linear model in macro-econometrics, where "time-varying parameter regression" is a standard, named technique built on exactly this recursion.

It's worth being precise about the boundary of the plain (linear-Gaussian) Kalman filter covered here: it is the mathematically optimal estimator only when the state truly follows a linear equation and both noise sources are genuinely Gaussian. When the relationship between state and observation is nonlinear, practitioners reach for extensions — the Extended Kalman Filter (EKF, which linearizes locally at each step) or the Unscented Kalman Filter (UKF, which propagates a small set of representative points through the true nonlinear function instead of linearizing). Those are out of scope here, but worth knowing by name, since "Kalman filter" in a job posting or a paper often silently means one of these variants.

Where this could plug into MacroFXModel

An adaptive coefficient for a lead-lag relationship whose strength already varies with regime

Two places in this repo already describe exactly the kind of relationship a Kalman filter is built for: one whose strength, not just its level, seems to change with market conditions. education/macro-deep-dives-notes.md's Lesson 2 ("The Transatlantic Yield Spread") documents that the lead time between a US–German yield-spread move and the EUR/USD reaction is not constant — its own table (§2.4) states the lead runs "hours to 1–2 days" in high-volatility/news-driven conditions but "2–4 weeks" in range-bound, low-vol conditions, and "1–3 months" around a regime change. Lesson 3's live case study (17–18 Feb 2026) is a single concrete instance of that relationship firing with a ~24-hour lead. That is, in plain language, "the coefficient connecting spread-momentum to the FX/vol response is not fixed — it moves with the regime," which is precisely the premise a state-space model with a drifting \(x_t\) is designed to capture, instead of hand-coding regime buckets with fixed thresholds.

credit-leadlag.html (backed by js/creditCore.js + js/creditHmm.js, spec in docs/CREDIT_SIGNAL_SPEC.md) is the closer, already-built analog: it computes a lead-lag correlation between credit velocity (from ICE BofA US HY OAS) and a risk instrument's forward realized vol, scanning a fixed grid of lags over a fixed sample and reporting an out-of-sample information coefficient. That's a static, single-number characterization of the relationship's strength at each lag, refit over whatever window the job is run on — the same "frozen until you re-run it" property the OLS hedge-ratio example above has. A Kalman-filtered version would instead model the coefficient linking credit velocity to forward vol (at a chosen best lag) as a hidden state \(x_t\) that drifts smoothly, observed each day through the noisy realized relationship — letting the filter's own gain slow down when the relationship has been stable for a while and speed back up right after a genuine regime shift (when innovations \(e_t\) start coming in larger than \(S_t\) expects), without anyone hand-tuning a regime switch.

To be explicit about where this stands: this is not built, not prototyped, and not validated. It is a genuine candidate, not a claim of improvement. Choosing \(Q\) and \(R\) is itself a real modeling decision — as the pitfalls box below makes concrete, grid-searching them against a backtest is just overfitting the window-length problem in a different, more opaque parameterization, which is exactly the anti-pattern this repo's Lego Principle warns against (principle 4: a selector, not more knobs to optimize). Per Lego Principle 5, any Kalman-filtered version of this relationship would need to beat the incumbent fixed-lag correlation approach on true out-of-sample Sharpe or information coefficient, with a non-trivial OOS sample, before it replaced anything live — a more theoretically elegant estimator is not evidence of an improvement by itself.

Common pitfalls

Where "adaptive" quietly stops meaning "principled"

Treating \(Q\) and \(R\) as free parameters to grid-search until the backtest looks good. This is the single most important pitfall to internalize. The whole appeal of the Kalman filter is that it replaces an arbitrary choice (window length \(N\)) with a principled recursion — but \(Q\) and \(R\) are themselves chosen inputs, and if they're tuned by scanning for whichever pair produces the best in-sample equity curve, you have not escaped overfitting, you have relocated it into a less legible parameterization. A window length of 60 days is at least an honest, auditable knob; a \((Q, R)\) pair optimized post hoc is the same overfitting risk wearing a more sophisticated disguise.

Confusing "adaptive" with "reactive to noise." If \(Q\) is set too large relative to \(R\), the steady-state Kalman gain approaches 1 and the filter starts tracking each new noisy observation almost exactly — which defeats the entire purpose of estimating a stable underlying relationship in the first place. An estimate that whipsaws with every noisy print is not "more adaptive," it is simply under-damped.

A Kalman-filtered beta is not the same object as a rolling-window OLS beta, even though casual language calls both "adaptive." A rolling window gives every point inside it identical weight and every point outside it exactly zero weight — a sharp cutoff you chose directly as an \(N\)-day parameter. The Kalman filter instead discounts old information through the recursive predict/update cycle, and its "effective memory" is not a parameter you set directly at all — it emerges from the \(Q/R\) ratio, and (as the worked example shows) it can even change step to step depending on how confident the filter currently is. Don't assume the two methods will produce visually similar output on the same data; they can diverge meaningfully, especially right after a genuine regime break.

The state equation \(x_t = x_{t-1} + w_t\) is a modeling assumption, not an observed fact. A pure random walk assumes the true relationship has no tendency to revert toward any particular structural value and no deterministic trend — it can wander arbitrarily far given enough time. If the quantity you're tracking is plausibly mean-reverting instead (a cointegrating relationship's slope arguably has more structure than an unconstrained random walk), a different state equation — closer to the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck mean-reverting form from this series' prerequisite lesson — may be the more honest specification, and that choice needs to be tested, not assumed by default because it's the textbook example.

The linear-Gaussian assumption can be wrong in ways that matter. The closed-form recursion here is exactly optimal only if the process and observation noise are truly Gaussian. Real financial return series routinely show fat left tails (this repo's metricsCore.js section of CLAUDE.md exists precisely because Sharpe alone hides that) — a standard Kalman filter run on such data is still a reasonable linear estimator, but it is no longer provably optimal, and its uncertainty estimates (\(P_{t\mid t}\)) can understate real tail risk.

Self-test

Q1. What happens to the Kalman gain \(K_t\) if the observation noise \(R\) is huge relative to the process noise \(Q\)?
\(K_t\) approaches 0. A large \(R\) means each new observation is mostly noise and shouldn't be trusted much; a small \(Q\) means the underlying state barely drifts, so the existing estimate stays reliable across steps. With \(K_t \approx 0\), the update step barely moves the estimate toward the new observation — the filter effectively keeps carrying forward its prior belief and treats fresh, noisy readings as close to uninformative.
Q2. What happens to the Kalman gain if \(Q\) is huge relative to \(R\)?
\(K_t\) approaches 1. A large \(Q\) means the true state is believed to move a lot between observations, so any prior estimate goes stale fast; a small \(R\) means each new observation is quite trustworthy. With \(K_t \approx 1\), the updated estimate moves almost all the way to the new observation each step — the filter essentially tracks the raw observations directly, discounting its own accumulated history.
Q3. Why is a Kalman-filtered beta not the same as a rolling-window OLS beta, even though both are called "adaptive"?
A rolling-window OLS beta weights every observation inside a fixed \(N\)-day window identically and every observation outside it exactly zero — a hard cutoff, chosen directly as the parameter \(N\). A Kalman-filtered beta instead discounts older information gradually through the recursive predict step (which inflates uncertainty by \(Q\) every step) and the update step (whose gain depends on current confidence). Its effective "memory" is not a chosen window length at all — it emerges from the ratio \(Q/R\) and can even change over time as the filter's own uncertainty \(P\) evolves, as shown in the worked example where the gain dropped from 0.804 to 0.471 across two steps with no parameter changed.
Q4. What is actually being assumed by the state equation \(x_t = x_{t-1} + w_t\)?
It assumes the hidden state follows a random walk: no deterministic trend (no term pulling it consistently up or down over time) and no mean reversion (nothing pulling it back toward a structural "home" value) — just an unbiased random nudge of typical size \(\sqrt{Q}\) each step. This is a real modeling choice, not a neutral default. If the true quantity being tracked is plausibly mean-reverting instead (as a cointegrating slope might be), a random-walk state equation is mis-specified and a different formulation, closer to an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, may fit better — that needs to be tested, not assumed.
Q5. In what precise sense is the Kalman update step "Bayesian updating"?
The predicted state distribution \(\mathcal{N}(\hat{x}_{t\mid t-1}, P_{t\mid t-1})\), computed in the predict step before seeing today's data, is the prior. The observation model \(y_t \mid x_t \sim \mathcal{N}(H_t x_t, R)\) — how likely the observed \(y_t\) is for each possible hidden state value — is the likelihood. The updated distribution \(\mathcal{N}(\hat{x}_{t\mid t}, P_{t\mid t})\) is the posterior, obtained by combining prior and likelihood exactly as Bayes' theorem prescribes. Because both the prior and the likelihood are Gaussian and linearly related, the posterior comes out exactly Gaussian too (conjugacy), which is why the update has a simple closed-form formula instead of requiring the numerical integration general Bayesian updating often needs.
Q6. Why does choosing \(Q\) and \(R\) by grid-searching against backtest performance reintroduce the exact overfitting risk that "going adaptive" was supposed to avoid?
The appeal of the Kalman filter over a rolling-window regression is that it replaces an arbitrary chosen parameter (the window length \(N\)) with a principled recursion. But \(Q\) and \(R\) are themselves chosen inputs that fully determine the filter's behavior (via the steady-state gain). Tuning them by scanning for whichever pair produces the best in-sample result is functionally identical to tuning \(N\) by scanning for the best-looking window — the overfitting risk hasn't been removed, it has just moved into a parameterization that looks more sophisticated and is therefore easier to mistake for something principled. Per this repo's Lego Principle, any such choice still needs genuine out-of-sample validation before being trusted.
