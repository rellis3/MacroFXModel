The Domino Effect: One Shock Through the Whole Board

A hot inflation number does not hit one market — it knocks over the 2-year, which knocks over the dollar, gold, equities and credit. This lesson walks that chain link by link, shows it biting in real crises, and then shows where this desk tested the popular version and found it does not do what people say.

CONCEPT — explained here; desk verdicts quoted where tested ~20 min read Prereqs: Bond Pricing & YTM, Interest Rate Parity (helpful, not required)
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • Every macro asset is priced off a few shared inputs — the policy-rate path, inflation expectations, the real yield and the dollar. Move one input and every price built on it has to move. That is the whole "domino effect".
  • The textbook chain for a hot inflation print: front-end yields up → dollar up → USD/JPY up; real yields up → gold down and equity multiples down; credit spreads wider; volatility up. Each link is a mechanism someone is forced to act on, not a mood.
  • Which leg moves most tells you which story it is: the 2-year rising more than the 10-year is the signature of a short-run inflation/policy shock rather than a long-run supply or credibility story.
  • Chains can break. In the UK gilt crisis of autumn 2022, yields soared and the currency fell — a credibility shock running the textbook FX link backwards, then amplified by forced selling until the Bank of England stepped in.
  • The desk's verdict: the chain is real as a description of mechanism, and it mostly happens in the same bar. The timing and tradeability claims built on it — "rates turn first, then equities", "a 2-year shock means a wild FX week", "fear buys gold" — were tested here and came back null.
Section 01

Why this matters

Most market commentary is a domino story told after the fact: "oil rose, so inflation expectations rose, so the Fed was priced out, so yields rose, so the dollar strengthened, gold fell and equity multiples compressed." That sentence is a genuinely useful map. Every arrow in it corresponds to a real pricing relationship, and knowing the arrows is what lets you look at a screen full of red and green and say which shock is driving it — and what would have to happen next if your reading is right.

But the same sentence quietly smuggles in two further claims that are much weaker: that the dominoes fall in sequence (so you can see the first one tip and trade the later ones), and that the size of the first push tells you how hard the later ones fall. This lesson separates the two. The first half teaches the mechanism, link by link. The second half shows what happened when this desk tested the timing and tradeability claims, and why "the chain is real" and "the chain is a trading signal" are different statements.

It builds on Bond Pricing & YTM (why yields and prices move inversely), Interest Rate Parity (why rate gaps matter for currencies) and Central Bank Policy (what the front end of the curve is forecasting). The next lesson, Mood, the Quote and Forced Selling, picks up the part of the chain that turns a repricing into a rout: people who have to sell.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Everything is priced with the same ruler

Imagine every asset's price is a sum done with the same few numbers: "what will interest rates be?", "how much will prices rise?", "how much is a dollar worth?". A bond is that sum done one way, a share another way, a bar of gold a third way. When a piece of news changes one of those shared numbers, every sum that uses it has to be redone at once — not because traders are copying each other, but because the arithmetic changed underneath all of them.

The domino picture is how you trace which shared number changed. If the 2-year yield jumped, the dollar firmed and gold slipped all in the same minute, the shared number was the expected policy rate. If gold rose while the dollar fell and yields barely moved, it was something else. The dominoes are a diagnostic first and a forecast — if at all — a distant second.

The dominoes, one mechanism at a time

Take the canonical shock: a consumer-price print that comes in hotter than expected. Here is who is forced to do what, in the order the textbook tells it.

1. The front end moves first and most

A 2-year government bond's yield is, to a good approximation, the market's average forecast of the central bank's policy rate over the next two years plus a small premium. Hot inflation makes cuts less likely and hikes more likely, so that forecast — and the 2-year yield — reprices within seconds. The 10-year also rises, but it averages over a much longer horizon in which today's inflation print matters less, so it usually rises by less. A price move in a bond follows from the yield move through its duration:

\[ \frac{\Delta P}{P} \approx -D\,\Delta y \]
ΔP/Pthe bond's percentage price change
Dmodified duration — roughly how many years of cash flows are exposed
Δythe yield change, as a decimal (15bp = 0.0015)

The diagnostic. Which end of the curve moves more tells you which story the market is telling. The 2-year rising more than the 10-year (a bear flattening) is the signature of a short-run inflation and policy shock. The 10-year rising more (a bear steepening) points at something longer-run: term premium, government borrowing, or doubts about whether the central bank will keep inflation down at all.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

The 2-year bond is a bet on what the central bank does next. The 10-year is a bet on the next ten years. A surprise about this month's prices changes the near bet a lot and the far bet only a little — so if the near one jumps more, you know the surprise was about "soon".

2. Splitting the yield: real plus expected inflation

Any nominal yield is the sum of two legs that both trade in their own right:

\[ y_{\text{nominal}} = r_{\text{real}} + \pi^{e} \]
ythe ordinary bond's yield
rthe real yield — paid directly by an inflation-protected bond
πᵉthe breakeven — the inflation rate the market expects on average

If a hot print lifts nominal yields more than it lifts breakevens, the difference lands in the real yield. That is the money-genuinely-got-more-expensive leg, and it is the one gold and long-duration equities answer to. A yield move you have not split this way is half-read: breakevens rising is gold's friend; real yields rising is its enemy.

3. The dollar: a higher rate attracts money

If US short rates rise relative to everyone else's, holding dollars pays more than holding yen or euros. Money flows toward the higher rate, and the dollar strengthens. USD/JPY tends to be the most rate-sensitive major, because the gap between US and Japanese rates has been among the widest — the Interest Rate Parity lesson covers why a rate gap must show up either in the spot rate or the forward. Because oil, gold and most commodities are quoted in dollars, a firmer dollar also makes them a little cheaper in dollar terms with nothing having happened to them at all.

4. Gold: the cost of holding something that pays nothing

Gold pays no coupon. Holding it costs you the real yield you could have earned in an inflation-protected bond instead. So a higher real yield raises the cost of carrying gold, and gold tends to fall; a firmer dollar adds a second, independent push in the same direction. When gold moves and neither real yields nor the dollar explains it, the buyer is someone who cares about neither — a central bank adding reserves, for instance — and you identify them only by elimination.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Keeping gold in a drawer earns nothing. Keeping money in a safe bond earns interest. When the bond starts paying more, the gold in the drawer looks like a worse deal, so some people swap it — and gold's price slips.

5. Equities: profits far away are worth less today

A share is a claim on profits stretching into the future. The simplest way to see why higher yields hurt is the constant-growth valuation (the Gordon growth model):

\[ P = \frac{E}{k - g} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{P}{E} = \frac{1}{k-g}, \qquad k = r_{\text{real}} + \text{ERP} \]
E
Earnings
Next year's profit per share — the numerator
k
Discount rate
Real yield plus the equity risk premium — what investors demand
g
Growth
The long-run rate profits grow at

Raise \(k\) through the real yield, hold \(E\) and \(g\) fixed, and the multiple \(P/E\) must fall. Because \(k-g\) is a small number, small changes in it move the multiple a lot — and the effect is largest for companies whose profits sit furthest in the future (high \(g\), small \(k-g\)), which is why growth and tech stocks are the most rate-sensitive part of the index.

6. Credit and volatility: the last dominoes

Tighter money raises borrowing costs and the odds that weaker companies struggle to refinance. Lenders demand more extra yield over government bonds for the same risk — credit spreads widen. And when bonds and stocks fall together, the usual cushion (bonds rallying when stocks fall) disappears, portfolios lose on both legs, and the price of protection — implied volatility — rises.

\(\pi^{e}\)Breakeven inflation — the gap between nominal and real yields; the market's average inflation expectation.
\(D\)Modified duration — the percentage price change per unit change in yield.
\(\Delta y\)A change in yield. 1 basis point (bp) = 0.01% = 0.0001.
\(P, E\)Share price and next-year earnings per share.
\(k\)Equity discount rate: real yield plus equity risk premium (ERP).

Section 03

It actually happened: two chains that ran

It actually happened

September–October 2022: the UK gilt crisis

On 23 September 2022 the UK government announced a "mini-budget" of large, unfunded tax cuts, without the usual independent fiscal forecast alongside it. Investors demanded far more to hold long-dated UK government bonds (gilts), and long-dated gilt yields rose at a speed rarely seen in a major bond market.

The textbook says higher yields should support the currency. Instead sterling fell, touching an all-time low against the dollar in early trading on Monday 26 September. That is the first broken domino: when yields rise because investors doubt the government's credibility, the higher yield is a risk premium, not an attraction, and money leaves.

Then the forced sellers arrived. Many UK defined-benefit pension schemes used liability-driven investment (LDI) funds that hedged their long-dated liabilities with leveraged positions in gilts and interest-rate swaps. As yields jumped, those hedges lost value and counterparties demanded more collateral, fast. To raise cash, LDI funds sold what they could sell quickly — gilts — which pushed yields up further, which triggered more collateral calls. The chain had turned into a loop.

On 28 September the Bank of England announced temporary purchases of long-dated gilts on financial-stability grounds — explicitly to stop the spiral, not to ease monetary policy. The purchases ended on 14 October. The Chancellor was replaced that day, and the Prime Minister resigned on 20 October.

It actually happened

August 2024: the yen carry unwind

For years, borrowing in yen at near-zero rates and investing the proceeds in higher-yielding assets — the yen carry trade — had paid. At the end of July 2024 the Bank of Japan raised rates; days later, on 2 August, a weak US jobs report made US rate cuts look closer. The rate gap that supported the trade was now expected to narrow from both ends, and the yen strengthened sharply.

Positions funded in yen were losing money on the currency alone, and holders cut risk across the board. On Monday 5 August the Nikkei 225 fell more than 12% in a single session, its worst day since 1987, and the VIX briefly traded above 60 intraday. Much of the move reversed within days — the Nikkei rebounded sharply the next session. Note the direction: in this chain, US yields falling came with equities falling and the dollar weakening against the yen, the mirror image of the hot-CPI chain above. The same links, run backwards, under leverage.

🧮
Worked example

One hot print, priced through four assets

Illustrative numbers, not a real day. CPI comes in hot. The 2-year yield rises 15bp, the 10-year rises 8bp, and the 10-year breakeven rises 3bp.

1

Read the curve

2-year +15bp versus 10-year +8bp: the front end moved nearly twice as much — a bear flattening, the signature of a short-run policy shock.

2

Split the 10-year

Nominal +8bp = real + breakeven, and breakeven is +3bp, so the real yield rose +5bp. That is the leg gold and equities answer to.

3

Price the bonds

2-year, duration ≈ 1.9: \(-1.9 \times 0.0015 = -0.29\%\). 10-year, duration ≈ 8: \(-8 \times 0.0008 = -0.64\%\). The 2-year moved more in yield, the 10-year more in price — duration does that.

4

Price the equity multiple

Start with \(k = 8\%\), \(g = 3\%\): \(P/E = 1/0.05 = 20.0\). Real yield +5bp makes \(k = 8.05\%\): \(P/E = 1/0.0505 = 19.80\). The multiple falls about 1% with earnings unchanged — numerator down, denominator untouched.

5

Now raise growth expectations instead

Suppose the same print also nudged \(g\) to 3.05% (strong economy). Then \(k-g\) is unchanged at 5% and the multiple is unchanged. Same yield move, different equity outcome. This is why the equity domino depends on what the yield move means, not just its size.

🎯
Real-world trading scenario

A CPI morning, read the honest way

CPI prints hot at 8:30 New York time. In the same five minutes the 2-year jumps, USD/JPY pops, gold dips and Nasdaq futures drop. A trader who knows the chain can say immediately what happened: a front-end repricing, splitting mostly into real yields. That is useful — it tells them the move is about the policy path, so the next things to watch are central-bank speakers and the following data, not, say, oil.

What they should not do is treat the 2-year's jump as a head start on the other markets. On this desk's tests, those other markets moved in the same bar, not later; the following week was not wilder in the dollar pairs (it was, if anything, calmer); and there was no lingering "fear bid" in gold to wait for. The chain explains the five minutes. It does not hand you the next five days.

Section 04

Where the folk chain breaks: what this desk found

The domino story is usually told with a timing claim attached: one market moves first, the others follow, and the follower is the trade. Here is each such claim, tested on this desk's data. The mechanism survives. The timing mostly does not.

Desk test: nothing found "A 2-year shock means a volatile FX week" (front-end-shock, 2026-09-17). Null on EUR/USD, USD/JPY and GBP/USD (~211 shocks each). After a 2Y UP shock EUR/USD and GBP/USD ran calmer (−0.34 and −0.28 ATR, CIs clear of zero); after a 2Y DOWN shock USD/JPY was marginally wider (+0.33). The hawkish domino was followed by a quieter week in the dollar pairs, the opposite of the story.
Desk test: nothing found "Rates turn first, then equities follow" (rates-pivot-lead, 2026-09-25). Null on every cell: 2,430 confirmed 2-year pivots, 2,010 de-clustered events at 1h, signed Nasdaq return −0.005% vs control [−0.021, +0.012]; null at 1h, 2h and 4h. A confirmed rates low was followed by an up-move 54.1% of the time — and the base rate is 54.1%. Rates and equities move in the same bar (+0.23 correlation at 15 minutes), not in sequence.
Desk test: nothing found "Fear buys gold" (fear-gold, 2026-09-21). 58 VIX spikes 2010–2026. Gold five sessions on: +0.42% vs +0.35% ordinary, excess +0.07% [−0.42, +0.30]. Twenty sessions on: +0.01% vs +1.20%, excess −1.19% [−2.30, −0.48]. No fear bid on average, and a month later gold had lagged. If gold is up in a fear week, look for the rates or dollar leg instead.
Desk data: base rate "Stocks and bonds falling together marks an inflation regime" (stock-bond-flip, 2026-09-17). Negative 20-day stock/yield correlation: 71 episodes since 2008, median 5 sessions, p75 17; 17% of days. Only 36 clean flips — too few to test what follows. Say how long such episodes typically last; do not claim what comes next.
Desk test: nothing found "Oil takes time to reach inflation expectations" (oil-to-breakevens, 2026-09-17). No lag: a 20-day oil move of ±10% is followed by no excess breakeven change at 5, 10 or 20 sessions. Oil and breakevens move in the same window or not at all — this sharpens the oil→breakeven domino rather than killing it, but there is no delay to trade.

Put together, these say something specific. The dominoes do not fall one after another over hours or days, giving you time to step in front of the later ones. They fall together, inside the same bar, because every market is repricing off the same shared input at once. The chain is a way of reading a move that has already happened, and of knowing what would contradict your reading — "if the 2-year stops rising while oil keeps rising, the market is looking through the oil move". It is not a queue.

The trap: because every domino is a real mechanism, the story feels like it should be tradeable. On this desk, the timing version failed three different ways (daily coupling, 15-minute lags, 15-minute pivots), and the "big rates move → wild FX" version came back backwards. Explaining a move and predicting the next one are different jobs.
Section 05

How practitioners actually use this

🔎

Attribution, not prediction

After a big move, check which shared input moved: front-end yields, real yields, breakevens, the dollar. The one that explains the most assets at once is the story.

📐

Which leg moved more

2-year versus 10-year (or 30-year): front end leading is a policy story, long end leading is supply, term premium or credibility. Split nominal into real plus breakeven before reading gold or growth stocks.

🧱

Spot the broken link

An asset moving against its domino (yields up, currency down) is the most informative thing on the screen. In 2022 it said "credibility shock"; that reading was available on day one.

🔁

Look for loops

A chain becomes dangerous when a later domino feeds back into an earlier one — forced gilt sales pushing gilt yields up, vol-targeting selling pushing vol up. The next lesson is about exactly that.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where the domino story misleads

Treating the chain as a queue. The links fire together. Waiting for the "later" dominoes usually means arriving after they fell.

Reading a yield move without splitting it. Real yields up and breakevens up mean opposite things for gold. "The 10-year rose" is half a sentence.

Assuming fixed directions. Rates up usually means currency up — unless the rates rise is a credibility premium, as in 2022. Rates down usually means equities up — unless the rates fall is a growth scare, as in August 2024.

Telling it backwards and calling it a forecast. Every big day fits a domino story afterwards. Two cases chosen after the outcome is known are an anecdote, not evidence.

Section 06

Key Takeaways

✓Shared inputs, simultaneous repricing. The dominoes are real because bonds, currencies, gold and equities are all priced off the same rate, inflation and dollar inputs.
✓Split before you read. Nominal = real + breakeven, and 2-year versus 10-year tells you whether it is a short-run policy story or a long-run one.
✓Broken links are the signal. Yields up with the currency down (UK 2022) told you it was a credibility shock on day one.
✗Tested null: "rates turn first, then equities". 2,010 events, Nasdaq −0.005% vs control; 54.1% hit rate against a 54.1% base rate. They move in the same bar.
✗Tested null: "a 2-year shock means a volatile FX week". After hawkish 2-year shocks EUR/USD and GBP/USD ran calmer (−0.34 and −0.28 ATR).
✗Tested null: "fear buys gold". Excess +0.07% at five sessions, −1.19% at twenty. Look for the rates or dollar leg instead.
⚠Leverage turns a chain into a loop. Forced sellers (LDI in 2022, carry traders in 2024) are what made those episodes violent — the subject of the next lesson.
Section 07

Self-test

Q1. CPI prints hot. The 2-year rises 20bp and the 10-year rises 6bp. What kind of shock does the curve say this is?
A short-run inflation and policy shock. The front end moving much more than the long end (a bear flattening) means the market repriced the central bank's next moves, not the long-run inflation or supply outlook.
Q2. The 10-year nominal yield rises 10bp and the 10-year breakeven rises 10bp. What happened to the real yield, and what does that mean for gold?
The real yield is unchanged (10 − 10 = 0). The whole move was inflation expectations, which is gold's friend rather than its enemy — so the "yields up, gold down" domino should not be expected to fire. Splitting the yield is what reveals this.
Q3. With \(k = 7\%\) and \(g = 3\%\), what is \(P/E\)? What is it if the real yield rises 25bp?
\(P/E = 1/(0.07-0.03) = 25\). With \(k = 7.25\%\): \(1/0.0425 \approx 23.5\), about a 6% fall in the multiple with earnings unchanged. The smaller \(k-g\) is, the more sensitive the multiple.
Q4. In September 2022 UK yields rose sharply and sterling fell. Why did the usual "higher yields, stronger currency" link break?
The yield rise was a credibility premium — investors demanding compensation for doubts about the government's finances — not an expected higher policy rate that makes the currency more attractive to hold. When yields rise because investors want out, money leaves the currency too.
Q5. A colleague says: "The 2-year just broke higher — get short Nasdaq before it catches up." What does this desk's evidence say?
Tested null (rates-pivot-lead): across 2,010 de-clustered 2-year pivots, the Nasdaq's signed return after the confirmation was −0.005% versus control, with a hit rate equal to the 54.1% base rate. Rates and equities move in the same bar; by the time the 2-year has visibly broken, the Nasdaq has already repriced.
Q6. What turned the gilt sell-off from a chain into a loop?
Leverage and collateral calls. LDI funds' leveraged hedges lost value as yields rose, forcing them to post collateral, which they raised by selling gilts — pushing yields up further and triggering more calls. A later domino fed back into an earlier one, and only a buyer with no balance-sheet limit (the Bank of England) could break it.
