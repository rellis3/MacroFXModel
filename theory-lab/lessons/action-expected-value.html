Thinking in Expected Value

A hit rate on its own tells you almost nothing. This lesson turns a finished analysis into the one number that decides whether a trade is worth taking: what it pays on average, after costs. It also shows how to get a probability out of an analysis that never gives you one.

CONCEPT — explained here; desk verdicts quoted where tested ~20 min read Prereqs: Probability (helpful); From a View to a Trade Plan (helpful)
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide. It covers the same ground as short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • Expected value per trade, in R (multiples of what you risk), is \(E = p\,b - (1-p) - c\): win rate times payoff, minus loss rate, minus cost. A 40% hit rate paying 2R makes +0.20R a trade; a 70% hit rate paying 0.4R loses 0.02R.
  • Every payoff has a break-even win rate, \(p^{*} = (1+c)/(1+b)\). Your edge is how far your real win rate sits above that line, not how high the win rate is.
  • Moving the target doesn't create an edge. In a market with no edge, the chance of hitting a target \(T\) before a stop \(S\) is \(S/(S+T)\), which is exactly the break-even rate. A wider target only lowers your hit rate to match.
  • Analyses rarely state a probability, so you have to build one. Start from the base rate (the no-edge odds). Then count how often the setup worked, shrink that count toward the base rate when the sample is small, and turn range forecasts into odds.
  • EV is an average, so it takes trades to show up. A real +0.2R edge at a 40% win rate needs a couple of hundred trades before it clears the noise.
  • Desk verdicts: the desk's one audited sleeve (63% win, profit factor 2.2) works out to about +0.44 average-losses per trade, clearing its break-even win rate by about 19 points. The desk's own records also show how far a backtest win rate can fall live (85.7% → 39.6% on one system).
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

I'm right seventy percent of the time. That has to be a good system.

Maybe. What does a win pay, compared with a loss?

I take profits quickly. A win is about four tenths of what a loss costs.

Then do the sum. Seven wins of four tenths is two point eight. Three full losses is three. Over ten trades you're down a little, before any costs.

So being right isn't enough.

Being right is half the sum. The other half is what each win pays. Expected value multiplies them through: how often you win, times what a win pays, minus how often you lose, minus the cost of trading.

And there's a line I have to clear?

Every payoff has a break-even win rate. With wins at four tenths of a loss, you need just over seventy-one percent. You're at seventy, so you're under the line. Now take someone right only forty percent of the time, but whose wins are twice their losses. They need just a third. They're well above it, and they make about a fifth of their risk on every trade.

So I'll just widen my target. Bigger wins.

That's the trap. In a market with no edge, a wider target gets hit less often, by exactly the amount that cancels the bigger payoff. Move from one times your risk to three times, and your hit rate drops from a half to a quarter. Expected value stays at zero, and costs make it negative.

Then where does an edge come from?

From beating that no-edge hit rate. Start from it as your base rate. Count how often your setup actually worked in the past. And if the sample is small, pull the count back toward the base rate, because twelve wins in thirty cases could easily be luck.

How long before my results prove it?

Longer than you'd like. For the forty percent trader with a fifth of a unit of edge, it takes around two hundred trades before the record can tell the edge from luck.

Has a high win rate ever really blown up?

Short volatility in early twenty eighteen. Through the calm of twenty seventeen it made a little money almost every month. On the fifth of February the VIX more than doubled in a day, and the best known of those products lost more than ninety percent. The win rate was real. The size of the rare loss was the number nobody had measured.

The one-line version?

Your edge is how far your win rate sits above the break-even line for your payoff, after costs. Not how often you're right.

Section 01

Why this matters

The previous lesson, From a View to a Trade Plan, turned a view into an entry, a stop and a target. That plan doesn't yet tell you whether the trade is worth taking. For that you need two more numbers: how likely the target is to be hit before the stop, and what the trade costs. Multiply them through and you get expected value, the average you earn per trade if you take this kind of trade many times.

Expected value sits under nearly every other lesson in this category. Combining signals is really about getting a better probability. Edge after costs is the cost term \(c\) taken seriously. Kelly sizing uses the same \(p\) and \(b\). Calibration checks whether the probabilities you feed in are honest. The arithmetic itself is simple. The hard part is getting a probability that means something.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Being right often is not the same as making money

Picture two traders. One is right seven times out of ten, but takes profits quickly and lets losers run to the stop. The other is right only four times in ten, but cuts losers at the stop and lets winners run to twice the risk. Ask people which one is better and most pick the first, because being right feels like skill.

Count the money instead. Over ten trades the first wins seven small amounts and loses three full ones. The second loses six full ones but wins four big ones. Once you weigh each outcome by its size, the second trader is clearly ahead. Expected value is that weighing, done before the trade rather than after.

How it reached trading

From the card table to the trading desk

Expected value · into markets via Edward Thorp (1960s) and the "R-multiple" expectancy of trading education (1990s)

The idea of weighing outcomes by their probabilities goes back to Pascal and Fermat's letters on gambling in 1654. It reached trading through one person in particular. In Beat the Dealer (1962), the mathematician Edward Thorp showed that a blackjack player who tracks the cards can identify moments when the next hand has positive expected value, and should bet more at exactly those moments. He then took the same thinking to markets. Beat the Market (1967, with Sheen Kassouf) priced warrants against their stock and hedged the difference. He later ran Princeton Newport Partners on the same principle: find a positive average, size it, and repeat it many times.

For discretionary traders, the same arithmetic arrived as "expectancy", often written in R-multiples (profit or loss divided by the initial risk). Trading educators such as Van K. Tharp popularised it in the 1990s. Measuring every trade in R makes trades with different stops comparable and puts the payoff ratio on the same footing as the win rate.

On a desk, expected value is the gate between a signal and a position. An analysis can produce a view. EV decides whether that view, at this entry, stop, target and cost, is worth a trade at all. The answer then goes on to sizing.

1. Measure every trade in R

Call the amount you lose if the stop is hit 1R. A trade that risks 40 pips and makes 100 pips is a +2.5R win, and a stop-out is −1R. Working in R lets a 10-pip scalp and a 300-pip swing trade be judged on the same scale. The payoff ratio \(b\) is the average win divided by the average loss.

2. Expected value per trade

If a trade wins with probability \(p\), pays \(b\) R when it wins, loses 1R when it loses, and costs \(c\) R to do (spread, slippage, commission), then the average result per trade is

\[ E = p\,b \;-\; (1-p) \;-\; c \]

Over 100 trades, A expects to lose about 7R and B to make about 15R. A's 70% is below its own break-even rate of 71.4%. B's 40% is well above its break-even rate of 33.3%. The win rate on its own tells you nothing until you know what each win pays.

3. The break-even win rate

Set \(E = 0\) and solve for \(p\):

\[ p^{*} = \frac{1 + c}{1 + b} \qquad\text{and, with no cost,}\qquad p^{*} = \frac{1}{1+b} \]
p*the win rate at which the trade exactly breaks even
1 + ca loss plus the toll: what each losing trade really costs
1 + bthe swing between a win and a loss

Your edge is the gap \(p - p^{*}\). A 2R target needs 33.3% to break even before costs. A 0.5R target needs 66.7%. A strategy with a high hit rate and small wins is not safer for that. It just has a higher bar to clear, and every point of cost raises that bar further.

4. Moving the target doesn't create an edge

Here is the result that matters most when you write a trade plan. Suppose the market is a random walk with no drift, which is what "no edge" means. You place a stop \(S\) below and a target \(T\) above. The chance of reaching the target first is the classic gambler's-ruin result:

\[ P(\text{target before stop}) = \frac{S}{S+T} = \frac{1}{1+b} \qquad (b = T/S) \]
Sdistance to the stop
Tdistance to the target
1/(1+b)the no-edge hit rate, identical to the break-even rate

The no-edge hit rate equals the break-even rate. So in a market with no edge, every combination of stop and target has an expected value of exactly zero before costs and exactly \(-c\) after them. Moving a target from 1R to 3R takes the hit rate from 50% to 25% and leaves EV unchanged. An edge exists only when your analysis lets you beat \(S/(S+T)\) for the stop and target you chose. That makes \(S/(S+T)\) the natural base rate for any stop-and-target trade, and it is where your probability estimate should start.

5. Profit factor, and turning someone else's stats into EV

Track records are often quoted as a win rate and a profit factor, gross profit divided by gross loss: \(\text{PF} = p\,b/(1-p)\). Rearranging gives the payoff and the expectancy, in units of the average loss:

\[ b = \text{PF}\cdot\frac{1-p}{p}, \qquad E = (1-p)\,(\text{PF} - 1) \]
PFprofit factor: gross wins ÷ gross losses
bthe payoff ratio implied by PF and the win rate
Eexpectancy per trade in average losses (gross of any costs not already in the record)

So "60% winners, profit factor 1.5" means \(b = 1.5 \times 0.4/0.6 = 1.0\) and \(E = 0.4 \times 0.5 = 0.20\) average losses per trade. A profit factor near 1 is close to no edge, whatever the win rate.

6. EV is an average, so it takes trades to show up

A two-outcome trade has a standard deviation of \(\sigma = (1+b)\sqrt{p(1-p)}\) R per trade. After \(n\) trades, the average R has a standard error of \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\). To be roughly two standard errors sure the edge is above zero you need

\[ n \approx \left(\frac{2\sigma}{E}\right)^{2} \]
σhow much one trade's result swings, in R
Ethe edge you are trying to detect
ntrades needed before the record can tell the edge from luck

For Trader B, \(\sigma = 3\sqrt{0.24} = 1.47\)R and \(E = 0.20\)R, so \(n \approx (2 \times 1.47/0.20)^2 \approx 216\) trades. A good edge can stay invisible in your results for months. That is why the decision has to rest on the expected value you estimated beforehand, not on how the last ten trades went (see Judge the Decision, Not the Result).

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

A game: you pay 1 sweet to play. Half the time you win 3 sweets, half the time you win nothing. Play ten times: you pay 10 and win about 15. You lost half your games and still came out ahead, because the wins were big. What matters is what you get back on average, not how often you win.

Section 03

Where the probability comes from

A GARCH forecast gives you a volatility. A cointegration test gives you a spread and a half-life. A factor model gives you an exposure. None of them gives you "the target is hit first 38% of the time". To use EV you have to build that number yourself. There are four honest ways to do it, and they work best in this order.

⚖️

1. Start from the base rate

What would the probability be with no analysis at all? For a stop-and-target trade that is \(S/(S+T)\). For "up over the next week" it is the long-run share of up-weeks. Everything you add has to move you away from this. Base Rates First goes into this step in depth.

🧮

2. Count conditional frequencies

Find past cases where the analysis said the same thing, apply the same stop and target, and count how often the target came first: \(k\) of \(n\). Only use information that was available at the time.

🧲

3. Shrink small samples toward the base rate

Twelve wins in twenty cases is not a 60% edge. It could easily be a 45% one. Pull the count toward the base rate by an amount that shrinks as the sample grows (formula below).

📏

4. Turn ranges into odds

A volatility forecast describes a distribution. Combine it with a target distance and you get the probability of reaching the target, under a stated assumption about drift.

Shrinking a small count

The simplest honest estimator treats the base rate as worth \(m\) imaginary past cases, and blends it with the \(n\) real ones:

\[ \hat p = \frac{k + m\,p_0}{n + m} \]
k / nwhat your count says: k wins in n cases
p₀the base rate you start from
mhow many cases the base rate is "worth"; larger m means more scepticism
p̂the probability you actually feed into EV

This is the posterior mean of a Beta prior (see Conjugate Priors). With \(n\) small, \(\hat p\) stays close to \(p_0\). With \(n\) large, it moves toward \(k/n\). A sensible default for a new, untested idea is \(m\) equal to about 20–30 cases. The standard error of a raw count, \(\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}\), tells you how little 20 or 30 cases pin down: about ±9–11 percentage points.

From a range forecast to odds

If the analysis says the next week's move has a standard deviation of \(\sigma\) (from GARCH, HAR or implied vol), and you assume no drift, then for a target a distance \(d\) away:

\[ P(\text{close beyond } d) = 1 - \Phi\!\left(\tfrac{d}{\sigma}\right), \qquad P(\text{touch } d \text{ during the week}) \approx 2\left[1 - \Phi\!\left(\tfrac{d}{\sigma}\right)\right] \]
d/σthe target distance in standard deviations
Φthe normal cumulative probability
2[1 − Φ]touching is about twice as likely as closing beyond (the reflection principle)

The factor of two comes from the reflection principle for a driftless random walk. Fat tails and drift both bend these numbers, so treat them as a base rate to adjust, not as the answer.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Your friend says "I'm good at guessing coin flips, I got 4 out of 5!" Before you believe them, remember that anyone gets 4 out of 5 sometimes. So you start from "half the time, like everyone" and only move a little toward 4 out of 5. If they get 400 out of 500, then you move a lot.

Worked example: one EUR/USD trade, end to end

🧮
Worked example

Long EUR/USD at 1.0850: is it worth taking?

Your analysis says the pair is oversold against its usual relationship with rate differentials. The trade plan says: buy 1.0850, stop 1.0810 where the thesis is wrong, target 1.0950. Round-trip cost is 1.0 pip of spread plus 0.5 pip of expected slippage.

1

Put the plan in R

Stop distance \(S = 40\) pips \(= 1\)R. Target distance \(T = 100\) pips, so \(b = 100/40 = 2.5\). Cost \(c = 1.5/40 = 0.0375\)R.

2

Base rate: what a random market gives you

\(p_0 = S/(S+T) = 40/140 = 28.6\%\). A coin-flip market hits this target first about 29% of the time.

3

Conditional frequency from your analysis

You go back through history and find 30 past cases where the same reading occurred. With the same stop and target in R, the target came first 12 times: \(k/n = 40\%\). On its own that looks like an 11-point edge.

4

Shrink it

With \(m = 30\): \(\hat p = (12 + 30 \times 0.286)/(30 + 30) = 20.57/60 = 34.3\%\). The standard error of the raw 40% is \(\sqrt{0.4 \times 0.6/30} = 8.9\) points, so you can't claim more than that.

5

Expected value after costs

\(E = 0.343 \times 2.5 - 0.657 - 0.0375 = 0.857 - 0.657 - 0.0375 = +0.16\)R per trade, about 6.5 pips. Using the unshrunk 40% would have given +0.36R, more than double.

6

Break-even and margin

\(p^{*} = (1 + 0.0375)/3.5 = 29.6\%\). Your estimate sits 4.6 points above it, which is about half of one standard error. The estimate is positive but thin.

7

How long before you'd know

\(\sigma = 3.5\sqrt{0.343 \times 0.657} = 1.66\)R, so \(n \approx (2 \times 1.66/0.16)^2 \approx 418\) trades. No single trade, and no month of trades, will confirm or refute this.

Section 05

It actually happened: high hit rates, negative skew

It actually happened

February 2018: the short-volatility trade

Through the very calm markets of 2017, one of the most popular trades was to be short volatility, often through exchange-traded notes that rose when the VIX fell. Most days, and most months, the trade made a little money. Its hit rate was high and its equity curve looked smooth, which is exactly what a strategy with many small wins looks like before its rare large loss arrives.

On 5 February 2018 U.S. stocks fell sharply and the VIX more than doubled in a single session. The best-known of these products, Credit Suisse's XIV note, lost more than 90% of its value in a day, and Credit Suisse announced it would terminate it. Several other short-volatility funds took heavy losses in the same days.

Expected value is the right way to read this. A long run of small wins tells you the win rate, but the payoff ratio on the losing side was a large multiple of a typical win, and very few people had a reliable estimate of how often that loss would come. With a high win rate and a huge, rare loss, the sign of EV depends almost entirely on the one number nobody had measured well.

It actually happened

1962: a mathematician beats the dealer

Blackjack has a house edge, so the average hand loses. Edward Thorp's Beat the Dealer showed that the edge isn't fixed. As cards leave the shoe, the remaining deck sometimes favours the player, and a player who tracks it can tell when. The method was to bet the minimum when EV is negative and bet more when it turns positive. Thorp tested it in Nevada casinos, and casinos changed their rules and shuffling practices in response. The same two ideas, measure EV for each opportunity and size by it, became the basis of his later trading career.

Section 06

What this desk found when it tested the claims

Desk test: held up The desk's one fully audited strategy, read as expected value. The FX sleeve built on the US-versus-foreign 2-year yield spread: out of sample 2015–2025 with honest publication lags, ~109 trades, 63% win, profit factor 2.2. Apply this lesson's identities: \(b = 2.2 \times 0.37/0.63 \approx 1.29\), and \(E = 0.37 \times (2.2 - 1) \approx 0.44\) average losses per trade. At that payoff the break-even win rate is \(1/(1+1.29) \approx 44\%\), so the sleeve's 63% sits about 19 points above it. Because the inputs are rounded, the derived figures are approximate. Backtest evidence, paper-traded since 2026-07, not forward-proven.
Desk data: base rate How far a backtest win rate can fall. The desk's own audits are a sobering base rate for the \(p\) you feed into EV. One Fibonacci-level system showed an 85.7% win rate in backtest and 39.6% live, and other "too good" curves traced back to what the engine was allowed to see. EV can't be better than its probability. Shrink a backtest's win rate before you believe it, and audit the backtest before you shrink.
Not tested here "A wider target makes the trade better." Not tested on the desk's data. The maths in section 2 says that with no edge, a wider target lowers the hit rate exactly in step and EV stays at \(-c\). Whether a particular setup's hit rate holds up as the target widens is an empirical question for that setup, and it has to be measured, not assumed.
How to test it: take one setup's logged entries (the yield-spread sleeve's, 2015–2025) and replay each on M1 data with the stop fixed and targets of 1R, 1.5R, 2R and 3R, scoring whichever barrier is touched first. Record \(E\) in R after costs for each target. Fix the gate in advance: a wider target counts as better only if its \(E\) beats the 1R target's by at least 0.05R with \(t > 2\). The baseline is the same grid on random entries on the same days, where \(E\) should sit near \(-c\) at every target.
🛠 On the desk: SL/TP distribution mapper runs mechanical entries through a grid of stops and R-multiple targets on M1 data; the average R sits near zero at every combination, which is section 4 of this lesson measured.
The trap: quoting a win rate without a payoff, or a payoff without a win rate. "Right 70% of the time" and "3-to-1 reward-to-risk" both sound like an edge, and each on its own can be exactly zero. The only statement that means something is the win rate relative to the break-even rate for that payoff, after costs.
Section 07

How practitioners actually use this

📒

Journal in R, not in money

Logging every trade as an R-multiple makes expectancy a running average you can read off directly, and lets you compare across instruments and position sizes.

🧾

An EV row per setup type

Desks keep a table of setups with their \(b\), \(c\), base rate, count and shrunk \(p\). A new trade is checked against its row, not judged fresh each time.

🌪️

Look at the shape, not only the mean

Two strategies with the same EV can have very different worst cases. For high-win-rate, small-win strategies, stress the loss side: what if the rare loss is twice as large, or twice as frequent?

🔁

Re-estimate as trades come in

Every closed trade updates \(k\) and \(n\). The shrunk \(p\) moves slowly and honestly, which is the defence against both over-reacting to a streak and ignoring a decay (When an Edge Has Stopped Working).

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where expected-value thinking goes wrong

Judging by hit rate. A high hit rate with small wins can be below break-even. The question is always "above or below \(p^{*}\)?"

Leaving out costs. Cost is subtracted from every trade. On a 0.4R-target strategy, a 0.05R cost raises the break-even win rate by more than three points.

Using a raw count from a small sample. 12 of 20 is not 60%. Shrink toward the base rate, and quote the standard error.

Forgetting trades that hit neither. If some trades time out between stop and target, they are part of the distribution. Count them at their actual R, not as wins or losses.

Treating EV as what will happen. It is the average over many repetitions. A +0.16R edge will produce losing months, and it needs hundreds of trades to show.

Scenario drill · 31 January 2018

A short-volatility note that almost never loses

Through the very calm markets of 2017 you have held an exchange-traded note that rises when the VIX falls. Most days, and most months, it has made a little money. The equity curve is smooth and the hit rate is high. A friend says you should double the position, since it "basically never loses". What do you do?

  1. Double it. A hit rate that high is proof of an edge.
    A hit rate on its own says nothing. EV also needs the size of the losing side, and with a short-volatility note the rare loss can be a large multiple of a typical win. A long run of small wins is exactly what this shape looks like before the loss arrives.
  2. Write out the EV with the loss side made explicit: how big the rare loss could be and how often it might come. Stress both, then size so that loss is survivable.
    This is the lesson's method. With a high win rate and a huge, rare loss, the sign of EV depends almost entirely on the loss you have not seen yet. If you cannot pin that number down, size as if it is bad.
  3. Keep the position as it is. It has worked, so there is no reason to change.
    Better than doubling, but it still judges the trade by its recent results. The question is whether the position survives the rare loss, and you have not asked it.
  4. Judge it by its smooth equity curve: low volatility means low risk.
    A smooth curve is what many small wins look like. It tells you the win rate, not the tail. The risk sits in the one outcome the curve has not shown you.

What happened. On 5 February 2018 U.S. stocks fell sharply and the VIX more than doubled in a single session. The best-known short-volatility note, Credit Suisse's XIV, lost more than 90% of its value in a day, and Credit Suisse announced it would terminate it. Several other short-volatility funds took heavy losses in the same days. The win rate had been real; the payoff on the losing side was the number nobody had measured well.

Sources: Cboe VIX daily close via FRED (17.31 on 2 February, 37.32 on 5 February 2018); Credit Suisse media release, "Credit Suisse AG Announces Event Acceleration of its XIV ETNs", 6 February 2018.

Section 08

Key Takeaways

✓\(E = p\,b - (1-p) - c\), in R. 40% at 2R makes +0.20R before costs; 70% at 0.4R loses 0.02R.
✓Edge is the gap to break-even, \(p - (1+c)/(1+b)\), not the win rate.
✓Stop and target placement can't create edge. With no edge, the hit rate is \(S/(S+T)\), which is exactly break-even. Use it as the base rate.
✓Build the probability: base rate, then conditional count, then shrink with \((k + m\,p_0)/(n+m)\), and turn range forecasts into odds.
⚠EV takes a long time to show. \(n \approx (2\sigma/E)^2\): a few hundred trades for a typical edge. Don't judge it on a month.
⚠The desk's own records show win rates shrinking live (85.7% backtest → 39.6% live on one system). The validated sleeve's ≈ +0.44 average losses per trade is backtest evidence, not yet forward-proven.
Section 09

Self-test

Q1. A system wins 45% of the time, average win 1.5R, average loss 1R, no costs. What is its EV, and its break-even win rate?
\(E = 0.45 \times 1.5 - 0.55 = 0.675 - 0.55 = +0.125\)R per trade. Break-even \(p^{*} = 1/(1+1.5) = 40\%\). It sits 5 points above the line.
Q2. A colleague's system is "right 70% of the time" and is losing money. How is that possible?
If the average win is small relative to the average loss, the break-even rate is above 70%. With \(b = 0.4\), \(p^{*} = 1/1.4 = 71.4\%\), and costs push it higher still. At 70% the EV is \(0.28 - 0.30 = -0.02\)R before costs.
Q3. In a market with no edge, you move your target from 1R to 3R with the same stop. What happens to the hit rate and to EV?
The hit rate falls from \(1/2 = 50\%\) to \(1/4 = 25\%\), because \(S/(S+T)\) goes from 1/2 to 1/4. EV is \(0.5 \times 1 - 0.5 = 0\) before and \(0.25 \times 3 - 0.75 = 0\) after, so it is \(-c\) after costs in both cases. Moving the target changes the shape of the outcomes, not the average.
Q4. Your back-check says a setup "worked 9 of 12 times". The no-edge base rate for its stop and target is 50%. Using a prior weight of m = 12, what probability should go into EV?
\(\hat p = (9 + 12 \times 0.5)/(12 + 12) = 15/24 = 62.5\%\), not 75%. The raw count's standard error is \(\sqrt{0.75 \times 0.25/12} \approx 12.5\) points, so 75% was never well pinned down.
Q5. A volatility model forecasts a 1-week σ of 1.5% for gold. Your target is 3% away. Roughly how likely is gold to close the week beyond it, and to touch it at some point during the week?
\(d/\sigma = 2\). \(P(\text{close beyond}) = 1 - \Phi(2) \approx 2.3\%\). \(P(\text{touch}) \approx 2 \times 2.3\% \approx 4.6\%\). That is the driftless base rate. The analysis has to justify anything higher.
Q6. A track record shows a 40% win rate and a profit factor of 1.5. What payoff ratio does that imply, and what expectancy?
\(b = \text{PF}(1-p)/p = 1.5 \times 0.6/0.4 = 2.25\). \(E = (1-p)(\text{PF} - 1) = 0.6 \times 0.5 = 0.30\) average losses per trade. Check: \(0.4 \times 2.25 - 0.6 = 0.30\).
