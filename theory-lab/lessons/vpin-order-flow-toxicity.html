Order Flow Toxicity & VPIN

A metric built to answer one question in real time — "is the flow hitting this book right now unusually informed?" — by watching volume instead of the clock, and a famous, still-contested claim that it flashed a warning before the market fell apart in minutes.

CONCEPT — not built or tested in this repo ~16 min read Prereqs: market-microstructure.html (Kyle's Lambda & Glosten-Milgrom)
TL;DR
  • VPIN (Volume-Synchronized Probability of Informed Trading) takes Glosten-Milgrom's informed-vs-noise-trader split and tries to estimate it, live, from the tape — its one real innovation is bucketing trades by volume instead of by time.
  • Equal-volume buckets (each containing exactly \(V\) units of traded volume, however long that takes) track the arrival of new information far better than equal-time bars, because informed traders react to information by trading more, not by trading on a schedule.
  • Each bucket's volume is split into a buy-initiated and sell-initiated share using Bulk Volume Classification (BVC) — a normal-CDF function of the price change over the bucket, not a genuine trade-by-trade buyer/seller tag.
  • VPIN is the rolling average, over the last \(n\) buckets, of each bucket's absolute order imbalance \(|V_\text{buy}-V_\text{sell}|\), normalized by bucket volume — a number between 0 and 1 that rises when volume is one-sided.
  • VPIN is famous for allegedly spiking before the May 6, 2010 "Flash Crash" — but that claim is genuinely disputed in the academic literature, not a settled predictive triumph, and this lesson covers the critique as seriously as the original claim.
  • The worked example below runs 5 hypothetical buckets through the full calculation by hand and lands on VPIN ≈ 0.58 — a high, "one-sided-flow" reading, and shows exactly what number is actually being averaged.
Section 01

Why this matters

The Market Microstructure lesson in this series derives the bid-ask spread from a single idea: a market maker cannot tell whether the order in front of them comes from an informed trader (someone who knows something about fundamental value) or a noise trader (someone trading for reasons unrelated to value), and prices that uncertainty into the spread via a belief parameter, \(\alpha\) — the fraction of order flow believed informed. That lesson treats \(\alpha\) as a fixed, exogenous input: something the market maker is assumed to already know or estimate from context.

VPIN exists to answer the obvious follow-up question: where does that number actually come from, moment to moment, from real trade data? It is an attempt to build a live, computable proxy for "how informed does the current order flow look" directly from the tape — not a new theory of why a spread or price impact exists (Glosten-Milgrom and Kyle already cover that), but an applied estimation technique layered on top of the same informed-vs-noise distinction. Whether that estimation technique actually works as advertised is a genuinely open, actively argued question in the market microstructure literature, and this lesson treats it that way throughout — a metric can be well-motivated, widely cited, and still have a contested track record; both things are true here at once.

🔗

Builds on Glosten-Milgrom

The informed-trader / noise-trader split and the belief parameter \(\alpha\) are exactly the concepts VPIN tries to estimate live from volume data, not a new model of the spread itself.

The core mechanical idea — sample by volume, not by time — reappears anywhere a series needs to track "how much information has actually arrived" rather than "how much time has passed."

A desk that believes flow is currently toxic behaves differently — pulling back size, widening, or slowing an execution schedule — which is exactly the territory the next lesson in this series covers.

Plain-English intuition

Why watch volume instead of the clock?

Imagine trying to detect "something important is happening in this market" by checking in every five minutes, no matter what. On a slow, quiet Tuesday afternoon, a five-minute window might contain almost no trading at all — there's nothing to see. Thirty seconds after a surprise headline hits, that same five-minute window could contain more trading than the rest of the day combined — and a fixed-time sampling scheme is still forced to treat that one window as "one observation," exactly like the quiet one. Time-based bars quietly assume that information arrives at a constant rate throughout the day. It doesn't.

VPIN's founding idea is to flip the sampling rule around: instead of "close out an observation every \(X\) minutes," close one out every time \(V\) units of volume have traded, however long or short that takes. On the quiet Tuesday afternoon, a volume bucket might span twenty minutes. In the thirty seconds after the headline, it might span three. The claim — plausible, and the actual empirical motivation behind the technique — is that volume-based sampling synchronizes much better with the rate at which genuinely new information is entering the market, because informed traders reveal themselves by trading more when they have something to act on, not by trading on a fixed schedule. Once you're sampling in "volume time" instead of calendar time, the next question is simply: within each of those equal-volume buckets, how one-sided was the trading? A bucket where almost everyone was buying (or almost everyone was selling) looks like informed flow pushing in one direction. A bucket split roughly 50/50 looks like ordinary two-sided noise trading. VPIN is just that one-sidedness, averaged over a rolling window of recent buckets.

Time bars vs. volume bars, over the same trading session
Illustrative, not fitted to real data. The time clock (top) forces every 5-minute interval to count as one observation, whether it contained almost no trading or a burst of activity. The volume clock (bottom) instead closes a bucket the instant a fixed amount of volume has traded — buckets narrow sharply during the news burst and widen during quiet stretches, keeping the amount of "information" per observation roughly constant instead of the amount of time.
Section 02

The math, step by step

VPIN (Volume-Synchronized Probability of Informed Trading) was introduced by Easley, López de Prado and O'Hara as a computable, real-time proxy for the informed-trading share \(\alpha\) from Glosten-Milgrom-style models. Building it takes three steps: (1) reorganize the tape into equal-volume buckets, (2) classify each bucket's volume into a buy-initiated and sell-initiated share, and (3) average the resulting imbalance over a rolling window.

1. Equal-volume buckets

Pick a fixed bucket size \(V\) — some fixed number of shares, contracts, or lots (in practice often chosen as a fraction of a typical day's total volume, e.g. \(1/50\) of average daily volume, so that roughly 50 buckets span a normal trading day). Then walk through the raw trade tape in order and close out a new bucket every time cumulative volume since the last bucket boundary reaches exactly \(V\). If a single trade would push cumulative volume past the boundary, that trade's volume is split across the end of one bucket and the start of the next, so that every bucket's total volume is exactly \(V\) — only each bucket's duration in calendar time varies.

\[ \sum_{\text{trades in bucket }\tau} \text{volume} = V \quad \text{for every bucket } \tau \]
Vthe fixed bucket size — the same for every bucket, by construction
τ(tau) the bucket index — 1, 2, 3, … as buckets close out in sequence

This is the entire mechanical innovation relative to an ordinary time bar. Everything downstream — the classification step and the averaging step — could in principle be run on time bars instead, but doing it on volume buckets is the specific choice that gives VPIN its name and its claimed edge over a plain time-sampled toxicity measure.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Instead of checking on a store "every five minutes no matter what," imagine checking in "every time 100 more customers have walked through the door" — however long that takes. On a slow afternoon that might be an hour; during a big sale it might be two minutes. That's the whole trick: measure by how much is happening, not by the clock.

2. Bulk Volume Classification (BVC) — splitting a bucket into buy and sell volume

A genuine trade-by-trade classifier (like the Lee-Ready tick rule used elsewhere in the microstructure literature) needs quote data and trade-by-trade direction tagging. VPIN's original formulation deliberately avoids that requirement and instead classifies an entire bucket's volume at once, using only the bucket's price change and its overall volatility — this is Bulk Volume Classification. The idea: if the price rose over the course of the bucket, more of that bucket's volume was probably buy-initiated (aggressive buyers lifting offers pushes price up); if price fell, more was probably sell-initiated. The size of the price move, standardized by its usual volatility, sets exactly how lopsided that split should be assumed to be:

\[ V_{\text{buy},\tau} = V \cdot Z\!\left(\frac{\Delta P_\tau}{\sigma_{\Delta P}}\right), \qquad V_{\text{sell},\tau} = V - V_{\text{buy},\tau} \]
ΔP_τthe price change (close minus open) over bucket τ
σ_ΔPthe typical (standard deviation of) price change per bucket, estimated over recent history
Z(·)the standard normal cumulative distribution function (CDF) — same object as the "percentile" from the Descriptive Statistics primer
V_buy,τ / V_sell,τthe buy- and sell-classified share of bucket τ's fixed volume V — always summing back to V

Read this the way BVC intends it: standardize the bucket's price change into a z-score (exactly the z-score from the Descriptive Statistics primer in this series — how many "typical bucket moves" this particular move was), then feed that z-score through the normal CDF to get a number between 0 and 1, and use that number directly as the fraction of the bucket's volume assumed buy-initiated. A bucket that closed flat (\(\Delta P_\tau = 0\)) gets \(Z(0)=0.5\) — classified as an even 50/50 split, exactly the "no informed skew" case. A bucket with a large positive price move relative to its usual volatility pushes \(Z(\cdot)\) toward 1 — classified as almost entirely buy volume. This is a genuinely different object from a real per-trade classification: it never actually asks "was this specific trade a buy or a sell," only "given how far the price moved over the whole bucket, what fraction of the bucket's volume was probably on the aggressive-buy side" — a statistical proxy, not a ground-truth tag, and exactly the point the academic critique later in this lesson leans on.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

BVC never actually asks any individual trader "were you buying or selling?" It just looks at whether the price went up or down over the whole bucket and guesses: price went up a lot, so probably mostly buying; price stayed flat, so probably an even split. It's a reasonable guess based on the overall effect, not a real headcount of who did what.

3. Order imbalance and the VPIN average

Once every bucket has a buy/sell split, each bucket's order imbalance is simply the absolute difference between the two:

\[ \text{OI}_\tau = |V_{\text{buy},\tau} - V_{\text{sell},\tau}| \]
OI_τorder imbalance in bucket τ — how one-sided that bucket's classified volume was
| · |absolute value — a bucket dominated by selling counts the same as one dominated by buying

A perfectly balanced bucket (\(V_\text{buy}=V_\text{sell}=V/2\)) has \(\text{OI}_\tau = 0\). A bucket classified as entirely one-sided (\(V_\text{buy}=V\), \(V_\text{sell}=0\), or the mirror image) has the maximum possible \(\text{OI}_\tau = V\). VPIN itself is the average of this imbalance over the last \(n\) buckets, normalized by the total volume those \(n\) buckets represent (\(n\cdot V\), since every bucket has the same fixed volume \(V\)):

\[ \text{VPIN} = \frac{1}{n\cdot V}\sum_{\tau=1}^{n} |V_{\text{buy},\tau} - V_{\text{sell},\tau}| \]
nthe rolling window length — how many recent buckets are averaged together
n·Vthe total volume across the whole window — the normalizer that keeps VPIN between 0 and 1
OI_τ / V
One bucket's toxicity
How one-sided a single volume bucket's classified flow was, between 0 (balanced) and 1 (fully one-sided)
n
The averaging window
Smooths out single-bucket noise, at the cost of lagging behind a sudden change
VPIN
The result
A single number, ~0 to ~1, meant to track "how informed does recent flow look right now"

Because \(0 \le \text{OI}_\tau \le V\) for every bucket, VPIN itself is bounded between 0 (every recent bucket was perfectly balanced — flow looks like ordinary two-sided noise trading) and 1 (every recent bucket was classified as entirely one-sided — flow looks maximally informed by this proxy). In practice VPIN rarely approaches either extreme; it is read as a relative, time-varying signal — is it high or low compared to its own recent history — not as a literal probability in the strict Bayesian sense its name implies (a pitfall covered below).

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

VPIN close to 0 means recent trading was calm and balanced — about as many people buying as selling. VPIN close to 1 means recent trading was heavily one-sided — almost everyone was buying, or almost everyone was selling. It doesn't say who's right or where price is headed, only how lopsided the recent action has looked.

\(V\)The fixed bucket size — every equal-volume bucket contains exactly this much traded volume.
\(\tau\)(tau) The bucket index, in sequence as buckets close out.
\(\Delta P_\tau\)The price change (close minus open) over bucket \(\tau\).
\(\sigma_{\Delta P}\)The standard deviation of bucket-level price changes, estimated over recent history — the "yardstick" for standardizing \(\Delta P_\tau\).
\(Z(\cdot)\)The standard normal cumulative distribution function — converts a standardized price change into a number between 0 and 1.
\(V_{\text{buy},\tau}\), \(V_{\text{sell},\tau}\)The Bulk-Volume-Classified buy- and sell-initiated share of bucket \(\tau\)'s volume; always sum to \(V\).
\(\text{OI}_\tau\)Order imbalance in bucket \(\tau\) — \(|V_{\text{buy},\tau}-V_{\text{sell},\tau}|\), between 0 and \(V\).
\(n\)The rolling window length — how many recent buckets VPIN averages over.
\(\text{VPIN}\)The rolling, normalized average order imbalance — the headline number, between 0 and 1.
\(\alpha\)(from the Glosten-Milgrom lesson) the true, structural informed-trading share — the object VPIN is trying to proxy, not something it observes directly.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

VPIN going up right before a crash sounds like a smoke alarm going off before a fire. But smoke alarms also go off for burnt toast — so before trusting the alarm as proof of danger, you'd want to know how often it goes off for nothing. That's exactly the disagreement among researchers about VPIN: it really did rise before one famous crash, but nobody has proven it doesn't rise just as often on ordinary days too.

Worked example

Computing VPIN by hand over a 5-bucket window

Take a fixed bucket size \(V=1{,}000\) contracts and a rolling window of \(n=5\) buckets. Each bucket's standardized price change, \(\Delta P_\tau/\sigma_{\Delta P}\), has already been computed (that standardization uses exactly the z-score machinery from the Descriptive Statistics primer); this example starts from those five z-scores and their standard-normal CDF values, using the same well-known table values that primer introduces (\(Z(0)=0.5000\), \(Z(1.00)=0.8413\), \(Z(2.00)=0.9772\), \(Z(-0.50)=0.3085\), \(Z(-1.50)=0.0668\)).

1

Classify each bucket's volume with BVC

Multiply each bucket's \(Z(\cdot)\) value by \(V=1{,}000\) to get \(V_\text{buy}\), then subtract from \(V\) to get \(V_\text{sell}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \tau=1:\ Z=0.8413 &\Rightarrow V_\text{buy}=841.3,\ V_\text{sell}=158.7\\ \tau=2:\ Z=0.3085 &\Rightarrow V_\text{buy}=308.5,\ V_\text{sell}=691.5\\ \tau=3:\ Z=0.5000 &\Rightarrow V_\text{buy}=500.0,\ V_\text{sell}=500.0\\ \tau=4:\ Z=0.9772 &\Rightarrow V_\text{buy}=977.2,\ V_\text{sell}=22.8\\ \tau=5:\ Z=0.0668 &\Rightarrow V_\text{buy}=66.8,\ V_\text{sell}=933.2 \end{aligned} \]
2

Order imbalance per bucket

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{OI}_1 &= |841.3-158.7| = 682.6\\ \text{OI}_2 &= |308.5-691.5| = 383.0\\ \text{OI}_3 &= |500.0-500.0| = 0.0\\ \text{OI}_4 &= |977.2-22.8| = 954.4\\ \text{OI}_5 &= |66.8-933.2| = 866.4 \end{aligned} \]

Bucket 3 closed flat — an even split, zero imbalance. Bucket 4 had the largest standardized price move (\(Z=0.9772\), almost entirely buy-classified) and produces the largest imbalance.

3

Sum the imbalances and normalize

\[ \sum_{\tau=1}^{5}\text{OI}_\tau = 682.6+383.0+0.0+954.4+866.4 = 2{,}886.4 \]
\[ \text{VPIN} = \frac{2{,}886.4}{5\times1{,}000} = \frac{2{,}886.4}{5{,}000} \approx 0.577 \]

A VPIN reading of about 0.58 over this window says: averaged across the last five equal-volume buckets, roughly 58% of the classified volume was one-sided rather than balanced. Whether that specific number is "high" depends entirely on this instrument's own recent history of VPIN readings — one flat bucket (bucket 3) sitting next to two very lopsided ones (buckets 4 and 5) is exactly the kind of mixed window real data produces; a single clean number like this toy example is not a claim about what any real market actually looks like.

🎯
Real-world trading scenario

May 6, 2010 — the "Flash Crash," and what VPIN's role in it actually was

On May 6, 2010, U.S. equity index futures and a wide swath of individual stocks fell roughly 5–9% in minutes, then largely recovered within the same session — one of the most-studied episodes in modern market structure. VPIN entered the public conversation almost entirely because of this event: Easley, López de Prado and O'Hara published research arguing that VPIN, computed on E-mini S&P 500 futures order flow, rose to unusually high levels in the hours before the crash, framing it as evidence of building "order flow toxicity" — informed or aggressive one-sided flow accumulating faster than liquidity providers could absorb it — that a real-time monitor could in principle have flagged as an early warning.

That is the famous half of the story. The other half, equally important to state plainly: this claim was directly challenged in the academic literature, most prominently by Andersen & Bondarenko, who re-examined the same data and argued VPIN's apparent predictive power was considerably weaker than portrayed and highly sensitive to modeling choices the original papers did not fully stress-test — particularly the Bulk Volume Classification method itself, the bucket-size parameter \(V\), and the rolling window length \(n\). Different reasonable choices for those parameters produced meaningfully different VPIN paths around the same event, which is a real problem for a metric being proposed as an operational early-warning tool rather than a purely descriptive, after-the-fact statistic.

The claim

VPIN, computed from E-mini order flow, rose to elevated levels ahead of the May 6, 2010 crash — presented as evidence of accumulating toxic, one-sided order flow that overwhelmed available liquidity, and as a proof of concept for VPIN as a real-time systemic-risk monitor.

The critique

Andersen & Bondarenko and related work argue the predictive signal is fragile to the BVC classification choice and to \(V\)/\(n\), that similar VPIN elevations occur at many other times without a subsequent crash, and that at least part of any pre-crash rise plausibly reflects ordinary, already-rising volatility rather than a distinct "toxicity" phenomenon BVC is uniquely detecting.

This is a genuinely unresolved dispute, not a case where one side has been quietly proven right — treat "VPIN predicted the Flash Crash" as a contested claim you should be able to state both sides of, not a settled fact to repeat uncritically. The honest summary is that VPIN measures something real and mechanically well-defined (rolling, volume-bucketed order imbalance), and that a rise in it around a stress event is a fact about the data — but whether that rise constitutes genuine advance warning of a specific type of upcoming instability, as opposed to a coincident or mildly-lagging symptom of volatility that was already building for other reasons, remains an open, actively debated empirical question.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

Whatever one concludes about the Flash Crash specifically, the underlying idea — a live, computable order-flow-imbalance monitor — is genuinely used on trading and risk desks, generally as one input feeding a broader picture rather than a standalone trading signal fired in isolation:

🛡️

Market-making risk controls

A market maker (or automated quoting engine) watching an order-flow-toxicity proxy rise can mechanically widen quoted spreads or shrink quoted size, echoing exactly the Glosten-Milgrom logic — protect against adverse selection when the belief that flow is informed goes up.

🐌

Slowing an execution schedule

A desk executing a large order that sees a toxicity proxy spike may pause or slow its own participation, on the theory that trading into unusually one-sided flow right now is more likely to get run over — directly relevant to the execution-algorithm choices in the next lesson.

📊

One input among many

In practice, VPIN-style measures typically sit alongside realized volatility, quoted-spread widening, and order-book depth as one input into a composite "market is stressed right now" read, rather than being trusted alone.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where VPIN gets over-trusted or misapplied

Treating VPIN as a literal, calibrated probability. The name says "probability of informed trading," but VPIN is a normalized order-imbalance average, not a probability derived from a fitted likelihood model in the way a genuine Bayesian posterior (like the Glosten-Milgrom belief update) is. A VPIN of 0.6 does not mean "60% chance the next trade is informed" in any rigorous statistical sense — it means recent volume buckets, as classified by BVC, were on average 60% one-sided.

Forgetting BVC is a statistical proxy for trade direction, not a real classification. Bulk Volume Classification infers buy/sell volume purely from a bucket's aggregate price change — it never checks an actual trade against the prevailing bid/ask the way a tick-rule classifier does. A large price move driven by, say, a single large resting order being lifted can get classified identically to a genuine wave of many small informed trades — BVC cannot distinguish the two.

Ignoring parameter sensitivity. The bucket size \(V\) and window length \(n\) are free choices, not derived from theory — exactly the concern the Andersen & Bondarenko critique raises. A VPIN series computed with one \(V\) can look meaningfully different from one computed with another on the same underlying tape, which matters a great deal if the metric is being used as an operational threshold trigger rather than a purely descriptive, after-the-fact statistic.

Confusing "VPIN is high" with "a crash is coming." Elevated VPIN readings occur far more often than crashes do — most periods of one-sided flow resolve without any dramatic price dislocation at all. Reading every VPIN spike as an imminent-crash signal overstates what even VPIN's own proponents claim; the disputed evidence concerns one specific historical episode, not a general, reliable early-warning property established across many events.

Using VPIN as a look-ahead-biased backtest input. Like any rolling statistic, VPIN computed with a window that includes data from after the decision point it's supposedly informing is not a live, tradeable signal — a subtle version of the exact lookahead-bias trap this project's own house rules warn about for any rolling series.

Section 04

Key Takeaways

✓VPIN's real innovation is the volume clock, not a new theory. Bucketing by traded volume instead of elapsed time better tracks the rate at which new information enters the market, on the theory that informed traders reveal themselves by trading more, not on a fixed schedule.
✓Bulk Volume Classification turns a price move into a buy/sell split. \(V_\text{buy}=V\cdot Z(\Delta P/\sigma_{\Delta P})\) — a flat bucket splits 50/50, a large standardized price move skews almost entirely to one side.
✓VPIN is a rolling, normalized average of order imbalance. \(\text{VPIN}=\frac{1}{nV}\sum|V_\text{buy,τ}-V_\text{sell,τ}|\), bounded between 0 (balanced flow) and 1 (fully one-sided flow) over the window.
⚠The Flash Crash "early warning" claim is genuinely disputed, not settled. Andersen & Bondarenko's critique — sensitivity to the BVC method and to the \(V\)/\(n\) parameters, and comparable VPIN elevations at other times without a crash following — is a serious, unresolved challenge to the original claim, not a footnote to dismiss.
⚠BVC classifies a bucket, not a trade. It infers direction purely from an aggregate price move, standardized by volatility — a statistical proxy for order flow, not a verified per-trade buyer/seller tag the way a genuine tick-rule classifier is.
✗"VPIN predicted the Flash Crash" is not a fact to repeat as settled. It is one side of an active academic argument — treat it, and this whole metric's forecasting track record, with the same "built ≠ works ≠ has edge" discipline this project applies to every other signal.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. What is the single core mechanical difference between a VPIN volume bucket and an ordinary time bar?
A time bar closes after a fixed amount of elapsed time (e.g. every 5 minutes), regardless of how much volume traded during that window. A VPIN volume bucket closes after a fixed amount of traded volume \(V\), regardless of how much time that takes — a bucket can span twenty minutes on a quiet day and thirty seconds during a burst of activity. The motivation is that this synchronizes sampling with the rate information is actually arriving (proxied by trading activity), rather than with the clock.
Q2. In Bulk Volume Classification, why does a bucket that closes exactly flat (\(\Delta P_\tau=0\)) get classified as a 50/50 buy/sell split?
BVC sets \(V_\text{buy},\tau = V\cdot Z(\Delta P_\tau/\sigma_{\Delta P})\), where \(Z(\cdot)\) is the standard normal CDF. A price change of zero standardizes to a z-score of exactly 0, and the standard normal CDF at 0 is exactly 0.5 by the symmetry of the normal distribution around its mean — so \(V_\text{buy}=0.5V\) and \(V_\text{sell}=0.5V\). Mechanically, a flat bucket is being read as "no net directional evidence in the price move," which BVC translates directly into an even split, whether or not that's actually true of the underlying trades.
Q3. A bucket has \(V=2{,}000\) and a standardized price change with \(Z(\Delta P/\sigma_{\Delta P})=0.90\). What is that bucket's order imbalance \(\text{OI}_\tau\)?
\(V_\text{buy}=2{,}000\times0.90=1{,}800\), so \(V_\text{sell}=2{,}000-1{,}800=200\). Order imbalance is \(|1{,}800-200|=1{,}600\) — 80% of the bucket's volume was classified as one-sided (\(1{,}600/2{,}000=0.8\)), which is exactly what a 90% buy-classification implies: 90% buy versus 10% sell is an 80-percentage-point gap between the two sides.
Q4. Why is a rolling window of \(n\) buckets used, rather than reporting each bucket's own order imbalance directly as "VPIN"?
A single bucket's classification is noisy — it can be dominated by one unusual print, a resting order being lifted, or ordinary short-term randomness, none of which necessarily reflects a sustained shift in how informed the flow really is. Averaging over the last \(n\) buckets smooths that single-bucket noise out, at a real cost: the resulting VPIN reading necessarily lags a sudden true change in flow toxicity, since it's an average that includes older, possibly stale buckets alongside the newest one. Choosing \(n\) is a real bias-variance tradeoff — larger \(n\) is smoother but slower to react; smaller \(n\) reacts faster but is noisier.
Q5. What is the Andersen & Bondarenko critique of VPIN's role in the May 2010 Flash Crash, in plain terms?
They argue that VPIN's apparent pre-crash rise is considerably less robust and less clearly predictive than the original research portrayed — the pattern is sensitive to the Bulk Volume Classification method and to the choice of bucket size \(V\) and window length \(n\), reasonable alternative choices produce meaningfully different VPIN paths around the same event, and comparably elevated VPIN readings occur at many other times that were not followed by a crash. The upshot is that "VPIN spiked before the Flash Crash" is real as a description of one particular computation on one particular dataset, but the stronger claim — that VPIN is a reliable, general early-warning indicator for this type of event — is not something the critique accepts as established.
Q6. Why is BVC described in this lesson as classifying "a bucket, not a trade" — and why does that distinction matter?
A genuine trade-by-trade classifier (like the Lee-Ready tick rule) looks at each individual trade against the prevailing quote to tag it buy- or sell-initiated. BVC never does this — it looks only at the bucket's aggregate price change and volatility, and converts that single number into an assumed buy/sell split applied uniformly to the whole bucket's volume. This matters because two very different underlying realities (many small informed trades all pushing one direction, versus one large resting order getting lifted by a single buyer) can produce the same aggregate price change and therefore get classified identically by BVC — the method cannot tell them apart, which is exactly the kind of approximation error the academic critique targets.
