Lesson 13 · Risk & Sizing

The Kelly Criterion, or Why a Real Edge Can Still Ruin You

Every strategy needs two things: a direction and a size. This lesson is entirely about the size — the formula for the fraction of your bankroll that grows it the fastest over the long run, and the uncomfortable fact that "the fastest-growing bet" is nowhere near "the biggest bet." Overbetting a real edge can still ruin you, and the math below makes that trade-off exact instead of just a warning.

Section 1

Why "bet everything" is wrong, even with a real edge

A coin that lands heads 55% of the time, even-money payoff. A genuine edge — you make money on average, every round.

💥

Bet everything

More at risk means more expected profit per round — until the first tails, which happens 45% of the time. That wipes you out completely. $0 stays $0 forever, no matter how good the coin is.

🐌

Bet almost nothing

Bet 1% per round and you'll basically never go broke — but you'll also barely benefit from a genuinely strong edge. Growth left entirely on the table.

🎯

Somewhere in between

There is a size that grows your bankroll the fastest, compounded over many rounds — not the size that maximizes the expected dollar profit of a single round. That size has a name: the Kelly fraction.

🕳

Why "everything" fails

A catastrophic loss doesn't just cost money — it costs a percentage of everything left to compound with. There is no coming back from losing 100%.

"Maximize expected profit per bet" and "maximize how fast my bankroll compounds over many bets" sound like the same goal. They are not — and the gap between them is the entire subject of this lesson.
Section 2

Wealth multiplies — so the right objective is log-growth, not wealth

Play the coin n times in a row. Each round multiplies your bankroll, so total wealth is a product, not a sum.

Follow it through
Wₙ = W₀ × Π(1 + fXᵢ)
Maximizing the plain expected value of Wₙ pushes f toward 1 — bet everything, every round — because the astronomically lucky all-wins path pulls the average up, even though it contributes nothing to what almost every real path looks like.
ln Wₙ = ln W₀ + Σ ln(1 + fXᵢ)
Logarithms turn a product into a sum — exactly the tool for a multiplicative process. Dividing by n and taking n large, the average per-round log-growth converges to E[ln(1 + fX)].
maximize E[ln(1 + fX)]
Kelly (1956) proved this is the quantity whose expectation, maximized round by round, also maximizes the bankroll's actual long-run compounded growth rate — with probability 1. Not a preference for "log," a mathematical fact about compounding.

This is why the trap in Section 1 is so easy to fall into: expected value doesn't "see" that a single wipeout is catastrophic and irreversible. It just averages over all paths. Log-growth does see it, because ln(0) is negative infinity — total ruin is not just a bad outcome, it's an infinitely bad one to this objective, which is exactly why it never recommends f = 1.

Section 3

The formula: bet a fraction equal to your edge

Even-money coin, win probability p, lose probability q = 1 − p. Solve for the f that maximizes g(f) = p·ln(1+f) + q·ln(1−f).

The result
p − q
=
f* = 2p − 1
Setting g′(f) = p/(1+f) − q/(1−f) = 0 and simplifying (using p + q = 1) collapses straight to f* = p − q. At p = 0.5 (no edge), f* = 0 — bet nothing, correctly. As p → 1, f* → 1 — bet everything, also correctly, because a certain win has nothing left to protect.

Real bets rarely pay even money. If a win pays b units per unit staked (b = 1 recovers the coin-flip case), the same derivation on g(f) = p·ln(1+bf) + q·ln(1−f) gives the general-odds form:

f* = (bp − q) / b = p − q/b

Read the numerator as the bet's true edge per unit staked. If bp − q is negative, there's no edge — f* comes out negative, meaning don't take the bet at all. Dividing by b rescales the edge by how big the payoff swings are: a bet with a bigger payoff-to-risk ratio needs a smaller fraction of bankroll to extract the same growth.

Section 4

Markets aren't coin flips — the continuous form

A return stream with drift μ and variance σ² per period. Applying the same log-growth logic gives edge over variance again, just in continuous form.

μ

The edge

Expected excess return (drift) — the numerator. Bigger edge, bigger optimal size, linearly.

σ²

The risk

Variance of the return stream — the denominator, penalizing size for risk. This is variance, not volatility — it enters squared.

f*

The result

f* = μ / σ² — optimal fraction of capital to allocate. Edge divided by variance, exactly the same shape as p − q divided by odds.

Substitute the Sharpe ratio S = μ/σ (μ = Sσ) and the formula rearranges to f* = S/σ: Kelly-optimal leverage is Sharpe ratio divided by volatility. A higher Sharpe justifies more leverage; a higher-volatility instrument, at the same Sharpe, justifies less — because doubling volatility while holding the edge fixed cuts the optimal size by a factor of four, not two, since variance sits in the denominator squared relative to μ's linear term.

Where this needs qualifying

This continuous form comes from a second-order (Taylor) approximation of the log around the mean — reasonable for a diversified, roughly-Gaussian return stream, but it quietly assumes away fat tails. Real FX and macro returns have fatter tails than a Gaussian, which is exactly why sizing meaningfully below whatever this formula outputs, not just trusting the point estimate, matters in practice.

Section 5

Overbetting doesn't just add risk — it turns growth negative

Exactly full Kelly — the single largest bet size for which the growth rate is still maximal.

Position size scales drift μ linearly (bet twice as big, drift roughly doubles) but scales variance σ² quadratically (bet twice as big, variance roughly quadruples). The growth-rate curve g(f) ≈ fμ − f²σ²/2 is an inverted parabola: it rises, peaks exactly at f* = μ/σ², then falls back down, crossing zero again at 2f* and going negative beyond that — even though the underlying bet's edge never changed, only the size did.

Section 6

Why practitioners bet less than full Kelly, on purpose

g(f) is a smooth, single-peaked curve — which means it's flat near its own peak.

Full Kelly
  • Growth-optimal, by construction — the theoretical ceiling
  • Assumes p, b, μ and σ² are known exactly
  • The size most exposed to estimation error — an overestimated edge or underestimated variance sizes up a bet that isn't really there
  • Still produces large, painful drawdowns purely from ordinary variance — growth-optimal is not the same as gentle
Half-Kelly (c = 0.5)
  • Captures roughly 75% of full Kelly's growth rate — the curve is nearly flat near the peak, so this costs little
  • Cuts bet-to-bet variance, and typical drawdown depth, by roughly half or more
  • Trades a modest, controlled haircut on best-case growth for real protection against a wrong edge estimate
  • The standard practitioner default in the Kelly literature (Thorp's writing is the canonical source) — not a compromise nobody actually uses
Growth given up near the peak is small and second-order. Risk given up is large and first-order, everywhere along the curve. That asymmetry is the entire argument for fractional Kelly.

Section 8

Kelly and this repo's own position sizing — a reality check

This repo's own working notes (CLAUDE.md) say plainly: "volatility-based position sizing, cutting losers and letting winners run are powerful drivers of results — worth building and validating alongside an entry signal." The Kelly formula, f* = μ/σ², is the textbook answer to the sizing half of that sentence.

Where this needs qualifying

Checked directly against the code: a live, working vol-based position sizer already exists here — position-sizer.html, backed by js/positionSizerEngine.js and the /api/position-size endpoint. It turns a forecast volatility range into a position size so every trade risks the same dollar amount regardless of the instrument's volatility, and it caps total portfolio "heat." So volatility-based sizing is not purely a "worth building" idea any more — some of it is built and live.

What is not built is Kelly itself: that sizer risks a fixed, hand-picked percentage of equity per trade, scaled by volatility — it never takes an edge estimate (p or μ) as an input, so it isn't optimizing anything the way f* = μ/σ² does. There is no kellySizing.js, no Kelly-fraction column on any backtest, nothing that turns a strategy's own out-of-sample Sharpe into a bet size. That gap is real: Kelly sizing needs an honestly out-of-sample edge as an input, and per this repo's own IS/OOS discipline, that comes only after a signal has already proven itself — sizing is the last step, not the first.

Section 9

Where Kelly sizing quietly goes wrong

📈

Plugging in a backtested edge

Kelly assumes p or μ is known exactly. A backtested win rate is an estimate, and in-sample results are systematically optimistic — feeding an inflated edge into Kelly produces an inflated, overconfident bet size.

🧮

Sizing each position alone

Real books hold multiple, often-correlated positions. Sizing each one to its own standalone Kelly fraction ignores the shared variance between them and overstates the combined book's safe aggregate size.

📉

Confusing "optimal" with "survivable"

Full Kelly is growth-optimal by construction, but growth-optimal paths still routinely include 50%+ drawdowns purely from ordinary variance. A fund with redemption risk is optimizing for something Kelly's objective doesn't include at all.

〰

Ignoring fat tails

f* = μ/σ² and the volatility-drag approximation both rest on a roughly-Gaussian assumption. Real FX and macro returns have fatter tails than a plain historical σ captures.

None of these pitfalls are arguments against Kelly's math — they're arguments for feeding it honest numbers, sizing down from whatever it outputs, and treating it as a ceiling, not a target.
Section 10

Quick check

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

A coin wins 55% of the time at even money. What is the full-Kelly bet size?

f* = 2p − 1 = 2(0.55) − 1 = 0.10. Bet a fraction equal to your edge over 50/50.
Question 2

You bet exactly 2× the full-Kelly fraction on a genuinely positive-edge bet. What happens to the long-run growth rate?

Drift scales linearly with bet size, variance drag scales with the square — at 2f* the two terms exactly cancel. Bet any larger and growth goes negative, even though every bet still has positive expected value.
Question 3

Why do practitioners commonly use half-Kelly instead of full Kelly?

Half-Kelly captures roughly 75% of full Kelly's growth rate while cutting variance and typical drawdown by half or more — a favorable trade, especially given that real edges are estimated, not known exactly.
Question 4

Is it safe to plug a strategy's in-sample backtested win rate directly into the Kelly formula as p?

Kelly amplifies whatever number you feed it. An inflated in-sample edge produces an inflated, overconfident bet size — precisely the overbetting regime where growth can turn negative.
Question 5

In f* = μ/σ², if volatility doubles while the edge μ stays fixed, the optimal position size shrinks by roughly a factor of:

Variance σ² sits in the denominator and scales with the square of volatility, while μ enters linearly — doubling σ quadruples σ², cutting f* by four.
Section 11

What to carry forward

01

Maximizing plain expected value pushes toward betting everything, which eventually meets a loss and wipes you out — wealth compounds multiplicatively, so a single ruinous bet is irreversible.

02

The right objective is expected log-growth, E[ln(1+fX)] — maximizing it is provably equivalent to maximizing long-run compounded wealth, not an arbitrary preference for logs.

03

f* = 2p − 1 (even money) and f* = μ/σ² (continuous) are both "edge divided by variance" — bet proportional to your true edge, scaled down by how volatile it has to survive.

04

Growth rises to a peak at f*, hits zero at 2f*, and goes negative beyond it — overbetting a real edge doesn't just add risk, it makes the growth rate itself go negative.

05

Fractional Kelly (half-Kelly, c = 0.5) captures ~75% of full Kelly's growth while cutting typical drawdown by half or more — the practitioner default, precisely because real edges are estimated, not known.

06

This repo already has a live volatility-based position sizer, but not a Kelly-edge sizer — Kelly needs an honestly out-of-sample edge as input, which only comes after a signal has already cleared the IS/OOS bar.

What this now lets you do

Read any bet's or strategy's growth-rate curve for what it actually says: a peak at f*, a zero-crossing at 2f*, and a reason "more leverage always means more expected profit" is only true for arithmetic expectation, never for what a compounding account actually experiences. Recognize why fractional Kelly is standard, disciplined practice — not timidity — and why plugging a backtested number into f* without qualification is a fast way to compute a confidently wrong bet size.
