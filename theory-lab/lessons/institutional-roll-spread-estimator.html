Roll's Implicit Spread Estimator

No quotes, no order book, no depth data — just a raw series of trade prices. Roll's 1984 insight is that the bid-ask spread leaves a fingerprint even there: it forces consecutive price changes to bounce against each other, and that bounce alone is enough to back the spread out.

CONCEPT — a classical price-only estimator, verified nowhere in this repo: no file computes a serial covariance of price changes for spread estimation, and this repo's own Market Microstructure lesson never mentions Roll's estimator (it derives Glosten-Milgrom and Kyle's Lambda, both of which assume quotes are visible) ~14 min read Prereqs: Trade Classification: Lee-Ready & Bulk Volume, Market Microstructure: Price Impact & the Bid-Ask Spread

TL;DR
  • Roll (1984) assumes the true efficient price \(m_t\) is a random walk with no serial correlation in its own changes, but every observed trade price bounces between the bid and the ask: \(P_t = m_t + \tfrac{s}{2}q_t\), \(q_t=\pm1\).
  • That mechanical bounce alone — with no real price movement required — forces the observed price-change series to have negative lag-1 serial covariance: \(\mathrm{Cov}(\Delta P_t,\Delta P_{t-1}) = -\tfrac{s^2}{4}\). Solving backwards gives the estimator, \(s = 2\sqrt{-\mathrm{Cov}(\Delta P_t,\Delta P_{t-1})}\).
  • The critical catch: that square root only exists when the empirical covariance actually comes out negative, as the theory predicts. In short, noisy, or strongly trending samples it very often comes out positive instead — and the estimator is then simply undefined, not just imprecise.
  • This lesson's own 14-print worked example, built from a spread of exactly \(s=0.10\), recovers a Roll estimate of \(0.0986\) — within about 1.4% — while a second, purely trending series with no bounce at all produces a positive covariance and no estimate at all.
  • Corwin & Schultz (2012) is the modern successor most worth knowing by name: it estimates the effective spread from daily high/low ranges instead of transaction-price serial covariance, and is considerably more robust in exactly the short-sample, trending conditions that break Roll's estimator.
  • How it reached trading: Roll (1984) backed the spread out of trade prices alone, and with Hasbrouck's 2009 Bayesian refinement and Corwin & Schultz's 2012 alternative it is how researchers and quants put a cost on long histories and quote-less markets.
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

I've got years of trade prices for a thin market, but no quotes at all. My backtest needs a spread. Am I stuck?

Not quite. Richard Roll showed in 1984 that the spread leaves a fingerprint in the prices themselves.

How? The prices don't say where the bid and ask were.

Think about what a trade is. A buyer who crosses pays the ask. A seller who crosses gets the bid. So even if the true value never moves, the printed price jumps up and down between the two.

The bid-ask bounce.

Right. And here's the key. A bounce up to the ask tends to be followed by a bounce back down. So each printed move tends to undo the move before it. Statisticians call that negative covariance between one move and the next.

And the size of that tells you the spread?

Exactly. Under Roll's assumptions, the covariance equals minus a quarter of the spread squared. Each bounce is half a spread, and the covariance multiplies two of them. Turn it around and the spread is twice the square root of minus the covariance.

Does it actually work?

The lesson builds fourteen prints from a true spread of exactly nought point one, with the true price drifting up nought point nought two a trade. The covariance of the moves comes out at about minus nought point nought nought two four. Twice the square root of that is nought point nought nine eight six. Within about one and a half percent of the truth, from prices alone.

What about the drift? Doesn't that spoil it?

No. A smooth drift with no memory adds nothing to the covariance between one move and the next. The bounce does all the work.

So what's the catch?

The covariance has to come out negative. In short or trending samples it often comes out positive. Then you'd need the square root of a negative number, and the estimate simply doesn't exist.

So I'd get gaps.

You will. Record those windows as missing, never as zero, because a positive covariance means the assumptions failed, not that the spread was tiny. And cross-check with a high-low estimator like Corwin and Schultz from 2012, which fails less often.

And if I do have quotes?

Then use them. Roll is for when the quotes aren't there.

One line?

The bounce makes prices undo themselves, and how hard they undo tells you the spread, when it works at all.

Section 01

Why this matters

The Market Microstructure: Price Impact & the Bid-Ask Spread lesson in this Theory Lab derives, from first principles, why a bid-ask spread exists at all: Glosten-Milgrom's \(\text{Spread}=\alpha(V_H-V_L)\), a market maker's calculated defense against adverse selection. That derivation — and Kyle's Lambda alongside it — takes for granted that you can actually see the bid and the ask being quoted. Checked against that lesson directly: it never mentions Roll's estimator, and neither does anywhere else in this repository — a repo-wide search for a serial-covariance computation on price changes, specifically for the purpose of backing out a spread, turns up nothing. This lesson is new ground, not a restatement of anything already here.

In practice, a researcher is frequently handed something much thinner than a full quote history: a plain series of trade prints, timestamp and price, nothing else. That was the entire state of publicly available U.S. equity data for decades before consolidated quote feeds became standard, and it remains the state of many datasets today — some FX venues, many retail-facing data feeds, older or lower-tier historical archives, and any dataset that simply never captured the book. Roll's (1984) question is exactly this practical one: given nothing but a series of transaction prices, with no bid, no ask, and no depth, can the effective spread still be recovered? The answer turns out to be yes — not by observing the spread directly, but by noticing a very specific statistical fingerprint it leaves behind in how consecutive trade prices move.

💡
Plain-English intuition

The bid-ask bounce: apparent price moves that aren't real price moves

Picture a security with a genuinely stable "true" value — nothing about it is actually changing. Every trade, though, executes at one of two prices: the bid (a seller crosses in) or the ask (a buyer crosses in). Purely by chance, suppose the sequence of trades goes ask, bid, ask, bid. Look only at the raw sequence of printed prices, with no idea which side each trade hit: the price appears to fall (ask → bid), then rise back (bid → ask), then fall again, then rise again — a jagged, oscillating series that looks exactly like genuine volatility, even though the underlying value of the security never moved by a single cent. This is the bid-ask bounce: mechanical noise injected into the observed price series purely by which side of the spread each trade happened to hit, with zero contribution from any real change in value.

Roll's insight is that this bounce isn't just noise to be filtered out — it's structured noise, and its structure depends on exactly one unknown quantity: the spread \(s\) itself. A trade that hits the ask sits \(s/2\) above the true price; a trade that hits the bid sits \(s/2\) below it. Every time a trade alternates from one side to the other, the observed price swings by roughly a full spread's worth, with no true movement behind it at all. That swinging has a very specific statistical signature — and reverse-engineering the size of the spread from that signature, using nothing but the printed prices, is the whole of what follows.

How it reached trading

A spread from a tape with no quotes

Roll measure · Roll, 1984 → cost estimates for long histories and quote-less markets

For most of market history there are trade prices but no quotes: decades of daily closing prices exist, while intraday quote data for US stocks (TAQ) begins only in 1993, and many OTC markets never published quotes at all. Anyone testing a strategy over a long sample, or in a market without a quote feed, had no direct way to know what crossing the spread cost.

Richard Roll's 1984 Journal of Finance paper, A Simple Implicit Measure of the Effective Bid-Ask Spread in an Efficient Market, backed the spread out of the negative autocovariance of price changes. Hasbrouck (2009) made it far more usable with a Bayesian Gibbs-sampler version on daily data; Bao, Pan & Wang (2011) used the same autocovariance as a corporate-bond illiquidity measure; Corwin & Schultz (2012) offered a high-low alternative.

Today it is a first-pass transaction-cost and liquidity estimate for long backtests, for instruments without clean quote history, and a standard liquidity control in empirical research.

The math, step by step

1. Setting up the model: a true price plus a bounce

Roll's model has exactly two moving parts. First, an unobservable "efficient" or "true" price \(m_t\) — the fair value the security would trade at if every trade happened right at the midpoint — which follows a random walk with no serial correlation in its own changes: \(\Delta m_t = m_t - m_{t-1}\) is mean-zero and uncorrelated across time, exactly the informationally-efficient-price assumption already used throughout this Theory Lab (the same "prices, not price changes, carry memory" idea behind the Stationarity, ACF & PACF lesson's treatment of a random walk as the canonical non-stationary series with zero own-autocovariance in its differences). Second, every observed transaction price is not \(m_t\) itself, but \(m_t\) plus or minus half the spread, depending on which side of the market the trade hit:

\[ P_t = m_t + \frac{s}{2}\,q_t, \qquad q_t = \begin{cases}+1 & \text{trade at the ask}\\-1 & \text{trade at the bid}\end{cases} \]
P_tthe observed, printed transaction price — the only thing actually recorded
m_tthe unobservable true efficient price — never printed anywhere
sthe (here assumed constant) effective bid-ask spread — the unknown this lesson solves for
q_tthe trade-side indicator: +1 at the ask, −1 at the bid, assumed i.i.d. and equally likely each trade, independent of \(\Delta m_t\)

\(q_t\) being independent and identically distributed, coin-flip-like across trades is the model's key simplifying assumption: it says nothing about which trades land on which side beyond "roughly half and half, with no memory from one trade to the next." Real order flow can violate this (a run of buyer-initiated trades during a genuine rally, for instance) — a point this lesson returns to directly when discussing why the estimator can fail.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine a see-saw sitting perfectly balanced and never actually tipping over — that's the true price, calm and steady. Now imagine someone keeps tapping one end and then the other, completely at random, just to make it wobble a little each time — that's a trade landing at the ask, then the bid, then the ask again. Watching only the wobble, without knowing which end got tapped, it looks like the see-saw is doing something on its own. It isn't. The wobble is entirely explained by which end got tapped, and how hard — which here means the size of the spread.

2. From the bounce to a price change

The quantity that actually gets analyzed is the observed price change from one trade to the next, \(\Delta P_t = P_t - P_{t-1}\). Substituting the model in:

\[ \Delta P_t = \Delta m_t + \frac{s}{2}\big(q_t - q_{t-1}\big) \]
ΔP_tthe observed, printed price change — what any transaction-price dataset actually contains
Δm_tthe genuine, unobservable change in fair value — mean zero, no serial correlation
(s/2)(q_t − q_{t-1})the bounce term — the entire spurious part of the observed move, driven purely by which side the last two trades hit

Every observed price change is a sum of two pieces that have nothing to do with each other: a genuine (if usually tiny) move in fair value, and a mechanical bounce term that depends only on whether consecutive trades landed on the same side of the market or opposite sides. If two consecutive trades hit the same side (\(q_t = q_{t-1}\)), the bounce term is exactly zero — no bounce, no spurious move. If they land on opposite sides, the bounce term is \(\pm s\), a full spread's worth of spurious price change, in whichever direction the flip went.

3. Deriving the covariance result

Roll's central result is about how \(\Delta P_t\) relates to \(\Delta P_{t-1}\), the previous price change — exactly the lag-1 serial covariance this curriculum calls \(\gamma(1)\) in the Stationarity, ACF & PACF lesson, applied here to the price-change series rather than a generic series \(X_t\). Because \(\Delta m_t\) is mean-zero, serially uncorrelated, and independent of every \(q\), every cross-term involving a \(\Delta m\) vanishes in expectation, leaving only the bounce terms to interact with each other:

\[ \mathrm{Cov}(\Delta P_t,\Delta P_{t-1}) = \left(\frac{s}{2}\right)^{2}\mathrm{Cov}\big(q_t - q_{t-1},\; q_{t-1} - q_{t-2}\big) \]

Expand the covariance of the two bracketed terms into four pieces. Since the \(q\)'s are i.i.d. across trades, \(\mathrm{Cov}(q_t,q_{t-1})=0\) and \(\mathrm{Cov}(q_t,q_{t-2})=0\) and \(\mathrm{Cov}(q_{t-1},q_{t-2})=0\) — the only surviving term is a \(q_{t-1}\) covarying with itself:

\[ \mathrm{Cov}(q_t-q_{t-1},\,q_{t-1}-q_{t-2}) = \underbrace{\mathrm{Cov}(q_t,q_{t-1})}_{0} - \underbrace{\mathrm{Cov}(q_t,q_{t-2})}_{0} - \underbrace{\mathrm{Var}(q_{t-1})}_{=\,1} + \underbrace{\mathrm{Cov}(q_{t-1},q_{t-2})}_{0} = -1 \]
Var(q_{t-1}) = 1q is ±1 with equal probability, so E[q]=0 and E[q²]=1 — its variance is exactly 1
all other terms = 0i.i.d. trade sides mean nothing about one trade's side predicts a different trade's side

The only surviving piece is the shared \(q_{t-1}\) term, appearing with a negative sign because it's being subtracted in one factor and added in the other. Substituting back:

\[ \boxed{\ \mathrm{Cov}(\Delta P_t,\Delta P_{t-1}) = -\frac{s^{2}}{4}\ } \]
Cov(ΔP_t, ΔP_t−1)
Lag-1 serial covariance
Of the observed price-change series — the only quantity computed directly from data
−s²/4
Roll's theoretical result
A pure artifact of the bid-ask bounce — no real price movement is required to produce it

Two things are worth sitting with here, because both are exactly what the intuition section promised. Why negative: a bounce that pushes \(\Delta P_t\) one direction is, on average, undone by the next bounce — a trade that just crossed to the ask (pushing the observed price up) is exactly as likely as ever to be followed by a trade back at the bid (pushing it back down), and that systematic "what went up mechanically comes back down mechanically" pattern is negative serial covariance, definitionally. Real information-driven price moves have no reason to reverse themselves this way; the bid-ask bounce mechanically does, every time consecutive trades land on different sides. Why a factor of 4, not 1: each individual bounce displaces the observed price by a half-spread, \(s/2\), not a full spread — and covariance is built from a product of two such displacements, so the half-spread gets squared: \((s/2)^2 = s^2/4\). The \(\tfrac14\) isn't an arbitrary constant tucked into the formula; it falls straight out of measuring the bounce in half-spread units and then squaring it.

One further structural implication worth stating plainly, since the self-test below returns to it: because the \(q\)'s are i.i.d. with no memory beyond one lag, the model predicts \(\mathrm{Cov}(\Delta P_t,\Delta P_{t-k})=0\) for every \(k\ge 2\) — the bid-ask bounce is a strictly one-period phenomenon in this model. Only the very next price change is systematically related to the current one; anything two or more trades apart carries no mechanical bounce relationship at all.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

If a ball keeps bouncing between a low wall and a high wall, and you only ever measure how far it moved on each bounce, you'll notice something reliable: a big move toward the high wall is almost always followed by a move back toward the low wall, and vice versa. The moves aren't random noise pointing every which way — they're specifically anti-correlated, because the ball can only be bouncing between the same two walls. The spread is the distance between the walls; the bounce pattern in the moves is the clue that lets you measure that distance without ever seeing the walls themselves.

4. Solving for the spread — and the crucial caveat

Rearranging Roll's result to solve for the one unknown, \(s\), is a single algebraic step: multiply both sides by \(-4\) and take a square root.

\[ s = 2\sqrt{-\,\mathrm{Cov}(\Delta P_t,\Delta P_{t-1})} \]
sRoll's estimated effective spread — the entire output of the method
Cov(ΔP_t, ΔP_t−1)the empirical lag-1 serial covariance, computed directly from a real transaction-price series

That negative sign under the square root is not a cosmetic detail — it is the single most important practical fact about this estimator, and it deserves to be stated as plainly as possible.

The estimator only exists when the empirical covariance actually comes out negative. Roll's derivation is a statement about what the covariance is on average, under a specific idealized model — it is not a guarantee about what any one finite, real, noisy sample of trade prices will produce. In short windows, in strongly trending markets, or whenever the "true price is a pure, memoryless random walk" assumption is violated even mildly (momentum, mean-reversion, a real autocorrelated drift in the fundamental value itself), the empirical serial covariance very commonly comes out positive instead of negative. When that happens, there is no real number whose square is that (positive) quantity times \(-4\) — the formula asks for the square root of a negative number, and Roll's estimator is simply undefined, full stop, not just noisy or imprecise. This is a routine, expected outcome of applying the method to real short or trending samples, not a rare edge case — anyone using Roll's estimator in practice needs a plan for what to do with a window that returns no answer at all, because a meaningful share of windows will.

5. Beyond Roll: high-low range estimators

Roll's estimator is the foundational member of a much broader literature of price-only spread and impact estimators, not the last word in it. The modern estimator most worth knowing by name is Corwin & Schultz (2012), which takes a completely different route to the same goal: instead of the serial covariance of transaction prices, it uses the observed high and low price over a trading day (or other fixed interval), comparing the range over single days against the range over overlapping two-day windows. The core idea, stated without deriving it here, is that the daily high-low range grows with both genuine volatility and the spread (a high print is inflated toward the ask, a low print is deflated toward the bid), while volatility itself scales differently across a one-day versus a two-day window than the spread's fixed per-trade contribution does — comparing the two ranges lets the spread component be isolated algebraically. Because it never needs a negative serial covariance to exist, it does not suffer from Roll's "sometimes returns nothing at all" failure mode nearly as often, and it has become the more commonly cited choice in recent empirical microstructure work specifically for that robustness. This lesson's depth budget goes to Roll, as the historically foundational and conceptually clearest result; Corwin-Schultz is flagged here by name so it is recognized on sight, not derived.

Recovering a known 0.10 spread from 14 toy transaction prints

Pair each \(\Delta P_t\) (for \(t=2,\dots,13\), 12 values) with the previous change \(\Delta P_{t-1}\), and compute their sample covariance the same way this curriculum's own Correlation & Linear Regression primer does — averaging the product of deviations from each series' own mean, dividing by the number of pairs:

\[ \overline{\Delta P_t} = 0.0283,\qquad \overline{\Delta P_{t-1}} = 0.0117,\qquad \widehat{\mathrm{Cov}}(\Delta P_t,\Delta P_{t-1}) = \frac{-0.02917}{12} = -0.002431 \]
−0.02917the sum of all 12 cross-products of deviations, computed pair by pair

The empirical covariance is negative, so the square root is real, and Roll's estimator applies cleanly:

\[ s_{\text{Roll}} = 2\sqrt{0.002431} \approx 0.0986 \]

Compare that to the true spread used to construct the data, \(s=0.10\): the estimator recovers \(0.0986\), about 1.4% below the true value — a genuinely close recovery of a known quantity, using nothing but the 14 printed prices and Roll's single formula. Nothing about the trend in \(m_t\) contaminated the result, exactly as the derivation above predicts: a smooth drift with no serial correlation of its own contributes nothing to \(\mathrm{Cov}(\Delta P_t,\Delta P_{t-1})\), leaving the bounce term to do all the work.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

Roll's estimator is rarely a desk's final word on spread cost — it is a cheap, quote-free sanity check, and its main practical value is exactly that cheapness. A few habits follow directly from everything derived above:

📉

1. Compute it over rolling windows, expect gaps

Because the estimator is undefined whenever the empirical covariance is non-negative, a rolling-window application of Roll's estimator to real data routinely produces a series with real holes in it — windows with no estimate at all — rather than a smooth, always-populated time series. Any pipeline built on this estimator needs to treat "no estimate this window" as a normal, expected outcome, not an error to suppress.

✅

2. Cross-check against Corwin-Schultz where possible

Since the high-low estimator doesn't share Roll's specific failure mode, running both side by side on the same data — even without deriving Corwin-Schultz in full — gives a useful robustness check: broad agreement between the two is reassuring, and Roll simply failing to return a number in a window where Corwin-Schultz does is itself informative about how much bounce structure that window actually contains.

🕰️

3. Reach for it specifically when quotes genuinely aren't available

When quote or order-book data exists, a direct, observed quoted or effective spread is almost always preferable to any implicit estimator — Roll's method exists for the specific situation where that data simply isn't there, historically or in a given venue, not as a general substitute for looking at the actual quotes when they're on hand.

🎯
Real-world trading scenario

Backfilling a spread estimate for a thin, quote-poor historical window

Suppose a research project needs a rough spread history for a thinly-traded FX cross going back further than any usable quote feed is available, but a clean series of transaction prints exists for the whole period. Rather than leaving the spread column blank for those early years, a rolling application of Roll's estimator (say, over monthly windows of daily closes, or intraday windows of individual prints, depending on what the data actually supports) fills in a genuinely informative — if noisy, and sometimes missing — implied spread series, built from data that was already there. The honest caveat travels with the result: some windows, especially the more volatile or thinly-traded ones, will come back with no estimate at all, and that absence needs to be represented explicitly (a missing value, not a zero, and certainly not silently interpolated over) rather than quietly dropped or backfilled with a neighboring window's number.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where Roll's estimator is misread or misapplied

Treating an undefined result as "the spread is roughly zero." A non-negative empirical covariance means the model's assumptions broke down for that window — usually a real trend, a short sample, or correlated order flow — not that the true spread was small. Silently substituting zero, or dropping the observation without flagging why, throws away exactly the information that the estimator's own assumptions didn't hold there.

Confusing this lesson's "bid-ask bounce" with the same phrase used elsewhere in this curriculum for a different job. The Hurst & the Variance Ratio Test and Bipower Variation lessons both mention bid-ask bounce too — but there it shows up as contamination to be filtered out of a realized-variance or mean-reversion estimate, a nuisance the estimator has to survive. Here, the exact same underlying phenomenon is the signal being measured — Roll's method exists specifically to extract the bounce's size, not to remove its influence from something else. Same mechanism, opposite role in the two applications.

Assuming a constant spread over the estimation window. Roll's derivation assumes \(s\) is fixed for the whole sample. A real spread that widens around news or thins during a session transition — exactly the kind of time-varying behavior the Market Microstructure lesson's \(\lambda\) discussion warns about for price impact — is being averaged over by a single Roll estimate, not tracked through the window.

You are backfilling a monthly spread history for a thin FX cross from trade prints, because no usable quote feed goes back that far. Most months give a sensible Roll estimate. One volatile, trending month comes back with a positive covariance, so Roll returns nothing. The backtest needs a number for every month. What goes in that month's cell?

  1. Zero. No estimate means no measurable spread.
    A positive covariance means the model's assumptions failed that month, usually a trend or correlated order flow. It says nothing about the spread being small, and a volatile month is when spreads are usually wider, not narrower.
  2. Copy the previous month's estimate forward quietly.
    It hides the failure. Anyone reading the series later sees a smooth, populated history and has no idea that month's number was never measured.
  3. Record it as missing with a flag, cross-check with a high-low estimator such as Corwin and Schultz, and charge the backtest a cost you state openly for that month.
    This keeps the gap visible and uses an estimator that does not need a negative covariance. Whatever cost the backtest uses there is then a labelled assumption, not a fake measurement.
  4. Widen the window to two months until the covariance turns negative.
    You may get a number, but it averages the troubled month with a calmer one and assumes the spread stayed constant across both. Defensible as a rough fill if you say so, but it blurs exactly the month you care about.

What the lesson says. This is an illustrative situation, not a historical episode. The lesson's guidance is direct: an undefined Roll window is a normal outcome in short or trending samples, and it must be represented explicitly as missing, never as zero and never silently interpolated. A high-low estimator such as Corwin and Schultz (2012) is the natural cross-check because it does not share Roll's failure mode.

Section 04

Key Takeaways

✓Roll's estimator recovers an effective spread from transaction prices alone — no quotes, no order book, exploiting the fact that the bid-ask bounce mechanically creates negative serial covariance in observed price changes.
✓The core result, derived directly: \(\mathrm{Cov}(\Delta P_t,\Delta P_{t-1}) = -s^2/4\) — negative because a bounce in one direction is mechanically undone by the next, and a factor of 4 because the bounce is measured in half-spread units, then squared.
✗The estimator, \(s=2\sqrt{-\mathrm{Cov}(\Delta P_t,\Delta P_{t-1})}\), is undefined whenever the empirical covariance is non-negative — a routine, expected outcome in short or trending samples, not a rare failure. Any real application needs a plan for windows that return no answer at all.
✓The worked 14-print example recovers 0.0986 against a true spread of 0.10 — about 1.4% off, and only because a genuine drift in the true price contributes nothing to the lag-1 covariance, leaving the bounce term to do all the work.
⚠Corwin & Schultz (2012) is the more robust modern successor, estimating the effective spread from daily high-low ranges instead — worth knowing by name even without deriving it, specifically because it doesn't share Roll's undefined-result failure mode.
ℹIts desk use is costing a backtest when there are no quotes. For long daily samples, corporate bonds or other OTC instruments, a Roll-type estimate (or Hasbrouck's 2009 Bayesian version) supplies the spread a backtest must charge.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. What data does Roll's estimator require, and why was that a significant advantage historically?
Only a series of transaction (trade) prices — no bid, no ask, no order-book depth. This mattered because, for decades, consolidated quote data was either unavailable, expensive, or simply never recorded for large stretches of market history, while transaction prices were far more commonly captured. Roll's method let researchers estimate the effective bid-ask spread from exactly the kind of thin data — price and timestamp only — that was often all that existed, and it remains relevant today anywhere only transaction prices are reported.
Q2. Explain, in your own words, why the bid-ask bounce produces negative serial covariance in the observed price-change series, even when the true efficient price never moves.
Every observed price is the true price plus or minus a half-spread, depending on whether the trade hit the ask or the bid. When two consecutive trades land on different sides, the observed price swings by roughly a full spread, and that swing tends to reverse itself on the following trade if it, too, switches sides — a rise driven by hitting the ask is systematically prone to being followed by a fall back toward (or through) the bid, and vice versa. That "what bounced one way mechanically tends to bounce back" pattern is negative serial covariance by definition, and the formal derivation confirms it: with \(q_t\) i.i.d., the only surviving term in \(\mathrm{Cov}(q_t-q_{t-1},\,q_{t-1}-q_{t-2})\) is \(-\mathrm{Var}(q_{t-1})=-1\), producing \(\mathrm{Cov}(\Delta P_t,\Delta P_{t-1})=-s^2/4\), strictly negative for any spread \(s>0\).
Q3. Why does Roll's covariance result come out as \(-s^2/4\) rather than \(-s^2\)?
Because each individual trade's bounce displaces the observed price by a half-spread, \(s/2\), away from the true price — not by a full spread. Covariance is a product of two such displacements, so the half-spread gets squared: \((s/2)^2 = s^2/4\). The factor of 4 in the denominator isn't an arbitrary constant; it falls directly out of measuring the bounce in half-spread units and then squaring that quantity when forming the covariance.
Q4. A real transaction-price sample produces a lag-1 serial covariance of \(\Delta P_t\) equal to \(+0.0004\). What does Roll's estimator say, and what does this most plausibly indicate about the sample?
Roll's estimator is undefined here: \(s=2\sqrt{-\mathrm{Cov}}\) requires a negative covariance, and \(+0.0004\) is positive, so there is no real square root to take. This is not a rare or exotic outcome — it happens routinely in short windows, in strongly trending samples, or whenever the underlying assumption that the true price is a memoryless random walk with no serial correlation is violated (a real, persistent momentum or mean-reversion in the fundamental value itself, or correlated rather than i.i.d. trade-side flow). The correct response is to report "no estimate for this window," not to force a number out of the formula or to interpret the failure as "the spread must be near zero."
Q5. Given a computed \(\mathrm{Cov}(\Delta P_t,\Delta P_{t-1}) = -0.0025\), what spread does Roll's estimator imply?
\(s = 2\sqrt{-(-0.0025)} = 2\sqrt{0.0025} = 2(0.05) = 0.10\). The covariance is negative, so the estimator applies cleanly, and the implied effective spread is exactly 0.10 in whatever price units the series was measured in.
Q6. How does Corwin & Schultz's (2012) high-low estimator differ from Roll's in what data it uses, and why does that make it more robust in practice?
Corwin-Schultz uses each period's observed high and low price — not the serial covariance of consecutive transaction prices — comparing the range measured over single-day windows against the range measured over overlapping two-day windows to isolate the spread's contribution to that range algebraically. Because it never depends on a serial covariance coming out negative, it doesn't inherit Roll's specific failure mode of returning no estimate at all in short, trending, or otherwise assumption-violating samples — the exact weakness this lesson spends the most time on for Roll's own estimator.
