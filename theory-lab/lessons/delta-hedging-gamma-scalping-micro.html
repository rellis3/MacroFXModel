Lesson 6 · Options in Practice

Delta Hedging & Gamma Scalping, or Paid for Movement, Charged Rent

Hedge away the direction of an option and what is left is a bet on how much the stock moves, set against a rent you pay every day. One equation explains it, and the same equation shows what it costs to run.

Risk: educational, not advice. A bought option can lose its whole premium, and a sold option can lose far more. A delta hedge swaps one risk for others. All numbers use Black-Scholes with zero rates and are simulated, not a track record.
The whole idea
🎧 Listen · the five-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

Everyone says dealers hedge their options. What does that actually mean?

Say you buy a call that costs two dollars thirty. If the stock rises you make money, about fifty-one cents for every dollar it moves, to start with. That fifty-one cents is the delta.

So I'm betting on direction.

Unless you cancel it. Short fifty-one shares for every hundred the option covers, and a small move in the stock barely changes your total. You have hedged the direction away.

Then what am I left with? Nothing?

Two things that pull against each other. First, the delta changes as the stock moves. Stock up, delta up, your hedge is now too small, so you sell more shares, higher. Stock down, delta down, you buy shares back, lower. Sell high, buy low, mechanically. That is gamma, and it pays you for movement.

And the catch?

Time. The option loses a little value every day. For this one it is about five and a half cents a day if the stock sits still. That is theta, and it is the rent you pay for owning the gamma.

So who wins?

Whoever is right about how far the stock really moves. The option price assumes twenty percent volatility a year. Divide by the square root of the number of trading days in a year, about sixteen, and you get one point two six percent a day. Move more than that on a typical day and the trading gains beat the rent. Move less and you lose.

Everything hangs on that one comparison?

Over a month it works out to roughly the option's vega, about eleven and a half cents per point of volatility, times the gap between realised and implied. Realised at twenty-five against twenty implied is worth about fifty-eight cents on a two-thirty option. At fifteen, you lose about the same.

That sounds attractive.

Here is the honest part. On a single month the spread of outcomes is about forty cents even if realised exactly matches implied. And implied volatility itself moves. If it drops three points while you hold, that costs about thirty-five cents, more than the whole edge you were hoping for.

What about hedging more often, to cut the noise?

The noise falls with the square root of the number of hedges. But every hedge crosses a spread, and the total traded grows with the same square root. There is no free setting.

Has this ever gone badly in public?

The nineteenth of October, 1987. Portfolio insurers copied a put option by selling index futures as the market fell. Those are the same trades as a dealer hedging a put he has sold. Sell when it falls, so they became part of the fall. The Dow dropped twenty-two point six percent, and the official inquiry put the insurers at roughly forty percent of non-market-maker futures sales that day.

So the one-line version?

Hedging removes direction, not risk. A gamma scalper is long volatility: paid for movement, charged rent every day, and hurt if implied volatility sinks. Know what your rent is before you buy it.

Section 1

A hedge chooses which risk you keep

Own a 21-day at-the-money call on a $100 stock at 20% implied volatility: $2.30. Short about 51 shares per 100 (delta 0.51). What is left?

Δ

Direction: removed

Small moves in the stock no longer change your total. The short shares cancel the call's first-order exposure.

Γ

Convexity: kept, and it pays

Delta changes as the stock moves. You sell shares into rises and buy them back into falls. Every move, up or down, earns something.

Θ

Time: the rent

The option loses value daily, about $0.055 a share here if the stock sits still. It is paid whatever happens.

ν

Volatility: still exposed

Hedging delta does nothing about implied vol. If it falls, you lose, whatever the stock did.

The question every hedged long option reduces to: will the stock move more than the option's price assumed?
Section 2

The one equation behind it

Hold one call and short Δ shares. Let a short time dt pass and the stock move by dS.

Follow it through
Π = C − Δ·S
Long one call worth C, short Δ shares at price S. Delta is the call's price change per $1 in the stock, so the short shares offset it.
dΠ = Θ·dt + ½·Γ·dS²
The call's change is Δ·dS + ½Γ·dS² + Θ·dt. Subtract the hedge's Δ·dS and the delta terms cancel. Left: time decay and curvature. The move enters squared, so up and down pay alike.
dΠ = ½·Γ·(dS² − σ²·S²·dt)
Black-Scholes with zero rates gives Θ = −½σ²S²Γ. So you earn half of gamma times the gap between the stock's squared move and the squared move the option priced in. Breakeven: a day's move of σ·√dt.

With σ = 20% and dt = 1/252, the breakeven move is 0.20 ÷ √252 = 1.26% a day. That is the one-day move the option's price implies.

Section 6

How often to hedge: noise falls, cost rises

Same option, realised = implied = 20%. Simulated hedging errors and shares traded, per option share.

3 hedges in the month
  • Std dev of P&L: $1.09, 47% of the premium
  • Shares traded: 0.91
  • Cost at 2¢ a share: $0.018
Daily (21 hedges)
  • Std dev of P&L: $0.42, 18% of the premium
  • Shares traded: 1.93
  • Cost at 2¢ a share: $0.039
8 times a day (168 hedges)
  • Std dev of P&L: $0.155, 6.7% of the premium
  • Shares traded: 4.63
  • Cost at 2¢ a share: $0.093
Noise falls like 1/√n and shares traded rise like √n. Std dev × √n stays near 1.9. There is no free setting, so practitioners hedge to a delta band, not "as often as possible".
Section 7

What the identity leaves out

📉

Implied vol moves

The identity holds it fixed. A 3-point fall costs about $0.35 a share on this option, more than a week of gamma gains. Vega is untouched by a delta hedge.

💸

The option's own spread

One volatility point of bid-ask on this contract is $0.115, about 5% of the premium. That usually outweighs the cost of the share hedge.

🧮

The delta is a model output

With a smile, the hedge ratio depends on how you assume implied vol moves with the stock. Two models, two hedges, two P&Ls.

🕳

Gaps and pins

Long gamma benefits from a jump. A short-gamma book takes the whole thing. Near expiry at the strike, delta flips fast and the hedge needs constant care.

Where this needs qualifying

The theory assumes constant volatility, continuous trading, no costs and no jumps. The numbers here are the clean case, which real outcomes are measured against, not a forecast of them.

Section 8 · It actually happened

19 October 1987: hedging an option you do not own

It actually happened

Portfolio insurance copied a put option by trading: hold less stock as prices fall (Rubinstein and Leland, 1981). Many insurers did it in index futures. Those are the same trades as a dealer hedging a put he has sold: sell into weakness. On 19 October 1987 the Dow fell 508 points, 22.6%, and the S&P 500 about 20%. The Brady Commission, as cited in a Federal Reserve staff paper, put portfolio insurers at roughly 40% of non-market-maker futures sales that day. The Chicago Mercantile Exchange's inquiry said insurance "does not by itself explain the magnitude of the crash".

Section 9

The sign of gamma decides who steadies the market

Long gamma
  • Price falls: delta falls, you buy shares back
  • Price rises: you sell shares
  • Trades against the move, supplies liquidity
  • Pays theta, wins when the stock moves more than priced
Short gamma, or copying it
  • Price falls: you sell more
  • Price rises: you buy more
  • Trades with the move, can add to it
  • Collects theta, loses when the stock moves more than priced
Where the textbook version breaks

The identity assumes you can trade at the price on your screen. A hedge is a rule that trades when price moves, and a rule followed by enough people becomes part of the price.

Section 10

Quick check

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Implied volatility is 20%. About how big a one-day move does a delta-hedged long call need to break even that day?

The breakeven is σ·√dt = 0.20 ÷ √252 ≈ 1.26%. Up and down moves pay alike because gamma acts on the square of the move.
Question 2

What does a delta hedge leave you exposed to?

Delta hedging cancels the first-order effect of the stock's direction. Convexity, time decay and implied volatility all remain.
Question 3

Realised volatility comes in 5 points above implied on this option. Roughly what is the average hedged P&L over the month?

Vega × the gap: $0.115 × 5 ≈ $0.58. That is an average. In the simulation the standard deviation of one month at this setting is about $0.57.
Question 4

Why not hedge as often as possible?

Each re-hedge crosses a spread. Total shares traded grow with the square root of the number of hedges, while hedging error shrinks with it, so practitioners use a delta band.
Question 5

Why did portfolio insurance trade with the market in 1987?

Replicating a put means holding less stock as prices fall. That is the same direction of trade as a dealer hedging a put he sold, so many insurers following the rule added to the selling.
Your call
Scenario drill · an illustrative week (not a historical episode)

Five quiet days, and the option is bleeding

This is a made-up situation with computed numbers. You bought the lesson's 21-day at-the-money call at 20% implied ($2.30, stock $100) because you expect the stock to move more than 20% a year implies. You hedge once a day. Over the last five days the stock has moved only 0.4% a day, up, down, up, down, up, and is now $100.40. Rent has exceeded gamma gains, you are down about $0.26 a share on the hedged position, and implied volatility has also slipped from 20% to 18%, which costs a further $0.20 or so. Sixteen trading days remain. What do you do?

  1. Double the position so a single big move would make the money back.
    Doubling doubles the rent as well as the gamma. The $0.26 already lost is gone whatever you do next, and chasing it turns a small bleed into a bigger bet on the same unproven view.
  2. Stop hedging until the stock moves, to save trading costs.
    With delta still near 0.5 you are no longer hedged. You are running a directional position you did not mean to hold, and you give up exactly the buy-low, sell-high trades that make the strategy work. The cost saved is a few cents.
  3. Keep hedging by your rule, and judge the trade on what you now expect over the remaining 16 days, against 18% implied.
    The five quiet days are sunk. What matters is whether you still expect realised volatility to exceed what the option now prices. If yes, hold: the rent per day is the same and the price of the view is lower. If your forecast has changed, close it. Either way the decision comes from the forecast, not from the bleed.
  4. Close it now to stop the bleed.
    Defensible if your forecast of volatility has come down. It is not defensible as a reaction to five quiet days alone: on a one-month trade, five quiet days are well within the normal spread of outcomes, as the simulation showed.

What this shows. This is an illustrative scenario, not a recorded trade. The numbers come from repricing the option each day with the stated moves: five days of ±0.4% cost about $0.26 per share, and the lost two vol points are worth roughly $0.10 a point at 16 days. The point is the discipline. A hedged long-gamma position has a known daily cost and an uncertain payoff, so the useful questions are about the forecast and the size, not about the days already gone.

Section 11

What to carry forward

01

A delta hedge removes direction, not risk. Gamma, theta and vega remain, and which one hurts depends on whether you bought or sold the option.

02

One day's hedged P&L is ½Γ(dS² − σ²S²dt): realised movement against implied movement, scaled by gamma. Breakeven is σ√dt, 1.26% a day at 20%.

03

Over a month the average is about vega × (realised − implied vol), $0.115 per point here, but a single month's spread is as large as the edge.

04

Hedging often trades noise (1/√n) for cost (√n). There is no free setting.

05

The identity ignores implied vol moves (3 points ≈ $0.35 here), the option's spread, model delta and gaps. Those are where hedged books really lose.

06

It actually happened, 19 October 1987: portfolio insurers hedged a put they did not own by selling into the fall, roughly 40% of non-market-maker futures sales, in a 22.6% Dow decline. The sign of gamma decides who steadies the market.

What this now lets you do

Read any hedged option position as a bet on realised against implied movement, with a known daily rent. You can compute the breakeven move, estimate the month's average result from vega and the volatility gap, and name the risks the hedge leaves behind before you take the position.
