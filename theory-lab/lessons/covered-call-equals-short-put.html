The Covered Call Is a Short Put

Owning a stock and selling a call against it is sold as the "conservative" income strategy; selling a naked put is sold as the risky one. At expiry they are the same position, to the cent — and once you see that, call open interest stops looking like a crowd of bulls.

CONCEPT — explained here; desk verdicts quoted where tested ~16 min read Prereqs: The Four Option Positions, Same Greeks, Different Risk, Black-Scholes-Merton
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • Long stock + short call ≡ short put + cash (same strike, same expiry). Put-call parity, \(S - C = Ke^{-rT} - P\), says the two cost the same today; at expiry both pay exactly \(\min(S_T, K)\).
  • What you give up: everything above the strike. What you keep: the whole downside, cushioned only by the premium. "Conservative" means "slightly less bad than owning the stock outright", by the size of the premium.
  • The interactive chart overlays the two P&L lines: drag strike and premium and they never separate.
  • The open-interest angle: covered-call overwriting is one of the largest systematic option flows, and it lands in call open interest from a seller with capped upside. Open interest never records who opened a contract, so call-heavy OI is not evidence of bulls, and put-heavy OI is not evidence of bears (a sold put is bullish).
  • The textbook chain says the fund's sold calls leave dealers long gamma near the strike, and that their hedging damps moves. The desk tested the gamma part: the range half is real on the Nasdaq only; the direction half is falsified. Max-pain pinning came back null, and the desk keeps no entry on covered calls themselves.
Section 01

Why this matters

The first two lessons in this sequence built the four basic positions (The Four Option Positions) and then showed that two positions can share every Greek sign and still carry completely different risk (Payoff Shape vs. Greek Signs). This lesson is the mirror image of that one: two strategies that are usually taught separately, with different names, different reputations and different marketing, which are in fact the same payoff.

The covered call (own the stock, sell a call above the price) is pitched to retirement savers as a conservative way to "get paid while you wait." The short put (sell someone the right to put the stock to you at a lower price) is often pitched as aggressive or even reckless. Put-call parity, a no-arbitrage identity that predates Black-Scholes, says they cannot differ by more than financing and dividends. Seeing that one fact clearly does two jobs. It tells you what you actually own when you write a covered call, and it explains why open interest cannot be read as a vote: the most popular institutional options strategy produces exactly the call-heavy open interest that a naive reader would call bullish, from a seller who has deliberately given up the upside.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Two doors into the same room

Picture the covered-call writer. They own a share at $100 and sell someone the right to buy it from them at $105. If the stock finishes above $105, the share gets called away and they end up with $105 in cash. If it finishes below, they keep the share, worth whatever it's worth. Either way they ended with the smaller of the stock price and $105, plus the premium they pocketed.

Now picture the put writer. They hold enough cash to buy the stock at $105 and sell someone the right to sell it to them at $105. If the stock finishes above $105, nobody puts anything to them and they keep $105 in cash. If it finishes below, they're forced to pay $105 for a share worth less, so they end up holding the share. Again: the smaller of the stock price and $105, plus a premium. Same ending in every possible world. And when two things pay the same in every world, they must cost the same today, or someone collects a free lunch.

The math, step by step

1. Put-call parity

For a European call and put on the same non-dividend stock, with the same strike \(K\) and expiry \(T\), no-arbitrage forces:

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Two shopping baskets. Basket one: a share, minus a "you can buy my share at $105" ticket that you sold. Basket two: a jar of cash that grows to $105, minus a "you can make me buy a share at $105" ticket that you sold. The baskets end up with exactly the same thing inside, whatever the stock does, so they have to cost the same at the till.

2. The payoffs at expiry are identical

Covered call at expiry: the share, minus what the call holder takes off you.

\[ S_T - \max(S_T - K,\,0) = \min(S_T,\,K) \]
S_Tthe share you own
− max(S_T − K, 0)the call you sold: you hand over everything above K

Cash-secured short put at expiry: the cash (grown to \(K\)), minus what the put holder takes off you.

\[ K - \max(K - S_T,\,0) = \min(S_T,\,K) \]
Kthe cash, grown at the risk-free rate
− max(K − S_T, 0)the put you sold: you absorb everything below K

Both equal \(\min(S_T, K)\). Both took in a premium today. Parity says the net outlays (\(S - C\) and \(Ke^{-rT} - P\)) are equal, so their profit-and-loss lines are the same line. Not "similar". The same.

\(S\)The stock price today.
\(S_T\)The stock price at expiry.
\(K\)The strike, shared by the call and the put.
\(C, P\)Today's premium for the call and the put at that strike and expiry.
\(r\)The continuously compounded risk-free rate.
\(T\)Time to expiry, in years.
\(Ke^{-rT}\)The strike discounted to today: the cash that grows into exactly \(K\) by expiry.
\(\max(x,0)\)"x if positive, else zero": the payoff of a long option.
\(\min(S_T,K)\)The smaller of the expiry price and the strike: what both strategies hold at expiry.
Where "identical" stops: the equivalence is exact at expiry for European options on the same strike with no dividends. In practice dividends (the stock holder collects them; the put writer doesn't), financing, taxes, and early assignment on American options separate the two a little. They are second-order differences, not a different risk profile.

🧮
Worked example

Three months, a $105 strike, priced with Black-Scholes

\(S = 100\), \(K = 105\), \(r = 4\%\), \(\sigma = 20\%\), \(T = 0.25\)y. The Black-Scholes formula gives a call worth about $2.39 and a put worth about $6.35.

1

Covered call outlay

Buy the share for $100, sell the call for $2.39: \(S - C = 100 - 2.39 = 97.61\).

2

Cash-secured short put outlay

Put \(Ke^{-rT} = 105\,e^{-0.01} \approx 103.96\) in bills, sell the put for $6.35: \(103.96 - 6.35 \approx 97.61\). Same number, as parity promised.

3

Three possible endings

Stock at $130: both hold $105, profit \(105 - 97.61 = +\$7.39\). The stock alone made +$30, so $22.61 of rally was sold.
Stock at $100: both hold $100, profit \(+\$2.39\), the call premium.
Stock at $80: both hold $80, loss \(80 - 97.61 = -\$17.61\). The stock alone lost $20; the cushion was $2.39.

The best case is +7.4% in three months and is reached at any price above $105. The worst case is the whole outlay, $97.61, if the stock goes to zero. That asymmetry is the trade.

What you give up

Every dollar above the strike. Profit is capped at \(K - S + C\) no matter how far the stock runs. In a sharp rally the covered-call writer watches the stock leave without them, and if they roll the call up to stay in, they buy back the old call at a loss each time.

What you keep

The full downside, all the way to zero, less the premium. The premium is a cushion the size of the premium and nothing more. "Conservative" is only true relative to owning the stock outright, and only by that one premium.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Selling a covered call is like renting out your bike's top speed. Someone pays you a little now, and in return, if the bike would have gone really fast, they get the fast part. If the bike crashes, though, it's still your bike and your crash. The rent you collected only pays for a plaster.

🎯
Real-world trading scenario

Same strategy, two sales pitches

An investor holds a broad equity fund and is offered two products: an "equity income" fund that writes monthly calls on the index, marketed as lower-risk; and a "put-writing" fund that sells monthly index puts against Treasury bills, which sounds racier. If both write at the same strike and tenor, put-call parity says they hold the same exposure, up to dividends and fees. The useful question is not which one sounds safer but which strike each sells and how the premium compares with the upside it gives away. A fund that sells at-the-money calls every month gives away every up-month above the strike and keeps every down-month.

Section 03

The OI angle: open interest never records who opened the contract

An open-interest number is a count of contracts outstanding. Every contract has a buyer and a seller, and the count is the same whichever of them started the trade. So the instrument does not tell you the opinion:

One put, two meanings

A bought put is a bearish bet or a hedge. A sold put is a bullish trade: the seller wins if the stock stays up. Both add one contract to put open interest.

One call, two meanings

A bought call is a bullish bet. A call sold against stock is a covered call: the seller is neutral to mildly bullish with the upside capped. Both add one contract to call open interest.

Covered-call overwriting is one of the largest and most mechanical option flows there is. Income funds and institutional overwriting programmes sell calls on a schedule, month after month, regardless of view. That flow piles into call open interest from a seller whose upside is capped by construction. It pushes the put/call open-interest ratio down, which is exactly the signature a naive reading calls "bullish, the market is positioned up." Put-heavy open interest has the same problem in the other direction, because a sold put is the covered call by another name, and it is bullish.

The trap: reading call-heavy open interest as bullish conviction. The most popular institutional options strategy produces exactly that signature from sellers who gave up the upside. Without the side of each trade (who initiated, and whether it opened or closed a position), open interest is a count, not a vote.

The chain the textbook tells

Here is the mechanism usually attached to heavy overwriting, with what each actor is forced to do:

FundWrites calls on a schedule against stock it owns: income mandate, not a view
→
DealerBuys those calls to make the market, and is now long calls: long gamma
→
HedgeMust stay delta-neutral: sells stock as price rises toward the strike, buys as it falls
→
OutcomeHedging leans against moves near the strike; the story says moves are damped

The first three links are mechanics: if a dealer is long the calls and hedges, it trades against the move. Whether that hedging is big enough to change how far the index travels is an empirical question, and this desk has tested the gamma part of it.

Range only Tested here (gex-range): the range half is real on the Nasdaq only. 793 days of NQ: next-day realised range against a same-trailing-vol Brownian baseline is +0.176 higher on short-gamma days (p 0.0008), +0.315 matched on trailing vol; positive in 4 of 4 specifications and 6 of 6 usable years. The reliable state is "long gamma is quiet" (range ratio ~0.85 versus ~1.02 on short-gamma days). It does not generalise to FX.
Falsified Direction: the gamma sign carries no directional content, and the adjacent "walls pull price toward them" direction was falsified at 48.8% out-of-sample against a 49.9% placebo. Long gamma near a big call strike can mean a quieter tape; it does not tell you which way.
It actually happened

2023: the income funds that sold the rally

By 2022–23, covered-call ETFs had become some of the most popular income products in the US, collectively holding tens of billions of dollars. Some of them are simple to describe: a widely held Nasdaq-100 covered-call ETF owns the index and sells at-the-money index calls every month.

In 2022 the Nasdaq-100 fell by roughly a third. Covered-call funds on it fell too, by less, because the monthly premium cushioned each down-month. Cushioned, not protected: the full downside, less premium.

In 2023 the Nasdaq-100 rose more than 50%. Covered-call funds on the same index captured only part of that, and trailed it by a wide margin. An at-the-money call written each month hands over everything above that month's starting level, so in a market that goes up most months, the fund keeps the premium and gives away the trend. Both years are the payoff diagram above, played out month by month.

The same year produced a much-aired argument on trading desks and in the financial press: that the sheer volume of systematic call selling was leaving dealers long gamma and helping keep index volatility subdued during the rally. That is the chain above. It was argued, not demonstrated, and it is a statement about range, not direction.

What the desk's own record says

Untested Covered calls themselves: the desk's evidence ledger holds no entry on overwriting flow, and nothing in the live code models it. Whether call overwriting measurably damps the index is not tested here.
Context Put/call tilt: the desk reports whether open interest is put-heavy or call-heavy as a positioning tilt only, and explicitly declines to infer a direction from it, because open interest does not record who opened the contract (a sold put is bullish, a sold call is not). That inference has never been scored.
Null Pinning to max pain (oi-max-pain): "price pins to options max pain into expiry" was tested on 2026-09-10 and came back null; the publish-lag timing risk, DTE, gamma, sign convention and spot basis were all checked. The honest weakness: the entry records no sample size and no interval, which makes it the weakest null in the ledger. Read it as "not shown", not "proven false". Max pain is a level to know about, not a magnet.
Section 04

How practitioners actually use this

📈

Buy-write benchmarks

Index providers publish buy-write indices (the best known writes one-month at-the-money calls on the S&P 500) so overwriting funds can be judged against the strategy rather than against the plain index it will lag in strong rallies.

🔁

Choosing the side by cost

Because the two are equivalent, desks pick whichever leg is cheaper to run: the put version needs no stock and no borrow, while the covered call keeps the dividends and voting rights.

🎚️

Strike selection

A lower strike pays more premium and caps sooner; a higher strike keeps more upside for less income. That is the moneyness trade-off, the same for the put writer at the same strike.

🔎

Reading the chain honestly

Before reading a big call strike as bullish conviction, ask who sells calls there on a schedule. A large overwriting programme can explain a call wall with no bulls involved.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where the covered call gets misread

"It's conservative." Relative to the stock alone, by the size of one premium. The maximum loss is still nearly the whole stock position.

"The premium is free income." It is the price of the upside you sold. Over a strong year the upside given away can dwarf the premiums collected.

"Short puts are dangerous, covered calls are safe." At the same strike and expiry they are the same position. If one frightens you, so should the other.

"Call-heavy open interest means the market is bullish." Open interest carries no side. Systematic call sellers create call-heavy open interest with capped upside, and put sellers create put-heavy open interest while being bullish.

Key Takeaways

✓Long stock + short call ≡ cash + short put. Put-call parity, \(S - C = Ke^{-rT} - P\): same cost today, same \(\min(S_T,K)\) at expiry, one P&L line.
✓You sell the upside and keep the downside. Profit caps at \(K - S + C\); losses run to zero less the premium.
✓Open interest never records who opened the contract. A sold put is bullish; a sold call against stock is capped. Call-heavy open interest is not a crowd of bulls.
⚠The equivalence is exact only at expiry, same strike, European, no dividends. Dividends, financing, taxes and early assignment separate them a little in practice.

Section 06

Self-test

Q1. Show that long stock plus a short call at strike \(K\) has the same payoff at expiry as cash of \(K\) plus a short put at \(K\).
Covered call: \(S_T - \max(S_T-K,0)\). If \(S_T \ge K\) this is \(K\); if \(S_T < K\) it is \(S_T\). So it equals \(\min(S_T,K)\). Short put with cash: \(K - \max(K-S_T,0)\). If \(S_T \ge K\) this is \(K\); if \(S_T < K\) it is \(S_T\). Also \(\min(S_T,K)\). Same payoff in every state, so by no-arbitrage the same cost today: \(S - C = Ke^{-rT} - P\).
Q2. Using the worked example (\(S=100\), \(K=105\), call $2.39), what is the covered call's maximum profit, its breakeven, and its loss if the stock halves?
Outlay \(100 - 2.39 = 97.61\). Maximum profit \(105 - 97.61 = \$7.39\), reached at any price at or above $105. Breakeven at \(S_T = \$97.61\). If the stock halves to $50, the position is worth $50, a loss of \(\$47.61\) against $50 for the stock alone: the premium saved $2.39.
Q3. A large income fund sells calls on an index every month. What does that do to the index's put/call open-interest ratio, and why is reading the result as "bullish" wrong?
It adds to call open interest, lowering the put/call ratio. Read naively, a low ratio looks call-heavy and bullish. But the calls were opened by a seller who owns the stock and has capped their upside; their stance is neutral to mildly bullish with no participation above the strike. Open interest counts contracts, not who initiated them, so it cannot separate this from speculative call buying.
Q4. Why is a sold put bullish, and what does that imply for reading put-heavy open interest?
The put seller keeps the premium if the stock stays above the strike and loses if it falls: they want the stock to hold up. And by parity a cash-secured short put is a covered call. Put-heavy open interest is therefore consistent with bearish hedgers buying puts or bullish sellers writing them; without the trade side, the data cannot tell which.
Q5. Walk the chain from "a fund writes calls" to "moves are damped". Which part did the desk find real, and which part failed?
The fund sells calls; a dealer buys them and is long gamma; to stay delta-neutral the dealer sells into rallies and buys dips near the strike, leaning against moves. Tested on gamma generally (not on overwriting itself): the range half holds on the Nasdaq only, with long gamma the quiet state (range ratio ~0.85), and does not carry to FX. The directional half is falsified: wall-magnet direction scored 48.8% out-of-sample against a 49.9% placebo. So "quieter" is supported on one index; "pinned toward the strike" or any direction is not.
