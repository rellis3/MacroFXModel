The Merton Jump-Diffusion Model

Black-Scholes assumes price paths never tear — Merton's 1976 fix bolts a random-jump process onto ordinary diffusion so a model can finally admit that FX gaps on NFP day.

CONCEPT — not yet built in this repo ~16 min read Prereqs: Black-Scholes-Merton Option Pricing, Extreme Value Theory helpful
TL;DR
  • Merton (1976) bolts a Poisson-driven jump term \(dJ_t\) onto ordinary geometric Brownian motion, so a price process can admit genuine discontinuities (like an NFP gap) instead of only continuous wiggling.
  • Three parameters drive the jump: \(\lambda\) (jump intensity — how often jumps arrive), and \(\mu_J,\sigma_J\) (the mean and standard deviation of the lognormal jump size once one occurs).
  • Mixing rare, wider jump outcomes into ordinary diffusion produces a taller, narrower center plus fatter tails than a single normal distribution — that's excess kurtosis (leptokurtosis) — and is one of the two classical explanations for the volatility smile in option markets.
  • This repo doesn't fit a jump-diffusion model anywhere, but js/metricsCore.js's Tier-1 primitives (skewness, excessKurtosis, histVaR, histCVaR) already catch the same fat-tail symptom empirically in a trade-return series, without ever estimating \(\lambda, \mu_J, \sigma_J\).
Section 01

Why this matters

The Black-Scholes-Merton model (the previous lesson) prices options by assuming the underlying follows geometric Brownian motion — a continuous-path random process. "Continuous" is doing a lot of work in that sentence: it means that over any short enough interval of time, price can only move by a small amount. Zoom in far enough on a GBM path and it never has a literal break in it — no teleporting, no instantaneous double-digit-pip vertical line. Mathematically, this is exactly why Black-Scholes assigns vanishingly small probability to a large move happening in an infinitesimally short window of time.

Real FX markets do not respect that assumption, and they violate it in a very specific, very predictable way: scheduled macro releases. Non-Farm Payrolls (NFP), CPI prints, and central bank rate decisions are calendar events, known in advance down to the second, after which price can — and routinely does — move 50, 100, even 200+ pips in a handful of seconds, with no meaningful trading in between the pre-release price and the post-release price. That is not "high volatility diffusion happening very fast." It is a genuine discontinuity: the market re-prices to new information essentially instantaneously, skipping over the intermediate price levels a continuous process would have to pass through. A pure Black-Scholes world has no mechanism to represent this at all — every path in that world is continuous by construction, full stop.

Robert C. Merton's 1976 extension is the textbook fix: keep the continuous Brownian motion for "normal" trading, and add a separate, independent random process whose entire job is to occasionally inject a sudden, discrete jump. The result is a model that can tell two different stories about the same underlying at once — "most of the time, this diffuses quietly" and "once in a while, without warning, it jumps" — and that combination turns out to explain two things a flat-sigma Black-Scholes model cannot: fat-tailed (leptokurtic) return distributions, and the volatility smile actually observed in option markets.

💡
Plain-English intuition

A calm river with the occasional flash flood

Picture a river's water level. Most days it just drifts up and down a little with rainfall and evaporation — small, smooth, continuous changes, the kind you could plot as an unbroken wiggly line. That's the diffusion part: Brownian motion, the same \(\sigma\,dW_t\) noise term from the Black-Scholes and Ornstein-Uhlenbeck lessons.

Now suppose that river also has a dam upstream, and every so often — rarely, unpredictably, but not never — the dam operators release a burst of water without warning. The level doesn't drift up to the new height over an hour; it jumps there in the space of the release. If you only ever modeled the river with the "smooth drift" description, you would be shocked every time a release happened and, worse, you would have priced any bet that depended on the water level as if those releases could never occur.

FX price around a scheduled data release is exactly this river. Between releases, EUR/USD mostly diffuses — the ordinary tick-by-tick noise of a liquid, continuously-traded market. At 8:30am ET on NFP Friday, the "dam" opens: a number hits the wire, algos and humans reprice in an instant, and the pair can gap through several pips of quoted spread and dozens of pips of "normal" range before the next real two-sided market forms. Merton's model is the formal acknowledgment that both of these regimes are real and coexist in the same instrument — it doesn't replace the smooth-diffusion story, it adds the flash-flood story on top of it.

Section 02

The math, step by step

Recall the Black-Scholes world describes the underlying's price as geometric Brownian motion — in words, "a constant drift plus continuous random noise, both scaled by the current price level." Written as a stochastic differential equation (an equation for how a random quantity changes over an infinitesimally small instant of time \(dt\)):

\[ \frac{dS_t}{S_t} = \mu\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t \]
μdtthe ordinary continuous drift — a deterministic average direction
σdW_tcontinuous Brownian noise — smooth, never a break in the path
dS_t/S_tthe instantaneous percentage return over \(dt\)

Merton's move is to add one more term to the right-hand side — a jump term \(dJ_t\) — that is zero almost all the time and occasionally, at random moments, adds a sudden percentage jump to the return:

\[ \frac{dS_t}{S_{t^-}} = \mu\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t + dJ_t \]
dJ_tthe jump term — zero almost always, a sudden jolt rarely
λjump intensity — how often jumps arrive
μ_J, σ_Jmean and spread of the lognormal jump size
S_t⁻the price just before a jump — returns measure off the pre-jump level
μdt + σdW_t

dJ_t

How often a jump arrives (λ), and its average size and variability once it does (μ_J, σ_J)

The only new object here is \(dJ_t\), and everything about the model's added power lives in how that term is constructed. It is built from two separate random ingredients stacked together: a process that decides when jumps happen, and a distribution that decides how big each jump is once it happens. Every symbol in the full model, spelled out:

\(S_t\)The underlying's price at time \(t\).
\(S_{t^-}\)The price the instant just before \(t\) — written this way (rather than plain \(S_t\)) specifically because at a jump instant, the price right before and right after are different by construction; the return is measured relative to the pre-jump level.
\(\mu\)(mu) The ordinary continuous drift rate — the deterministic "average direction" of the diffusion part alone, same role as in plain Black-Scholes.
\(\sigma\)(sigma) The diffusion volatility — how large the continuous, everyday random wiggles are, same role as in plain Black-Scholes and the same conceptual quantity computed throughout this repo's vol math.
\(dW_t\)The infinitesimal increment of a Wiener process (standard Brownian motion) — pure continuous random noise, mean zero, variance \(dt\). Covered in the OU lesson.
\(N_t\)A Poisson process: a counting process that ticks up by exactly 1 at each random jump arrival. \(N_t\) counts "how many jumps have happened by time \(t\)."
\(\lambda\)(lambda) The jump intensity — the average rate of jump arrivals per unit time (e.g. "0.05 jumps per trading day" on an average day). This is the single parameter of the Poisson process: over a short interval of length \(dt\), the probability of exactly one jump occurring is \(\lambda\,dt\), and the probability of more than one is negligible for small \(dt\). A bigger \(\lambda\) means jumps are more frequent.
\(Y_i\)The (random) percentage size of the \(i\)-th jump, drawn independently each time a jump occurs. Merton's original choice: \(\ln(1+Y_i) \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_J, \sigma_J^2)\) — i.e. the jump's log-size is normally distributed, so the jump size itself is lognormally distributed, the same distributional family Black-Scholes already uses for the diffusion part. This choice is analytically convenient, not physically forced — later models (Kou's, mentioned below) swap in other jump-size distributions.
\(\mu_J\)(mu-sub-J) The mean of the log-jump-size — controls whether jumps are, on average, upward or downward, and roughly how large. For a symmetric "could gap either way" model, \(\mu_J = 0\); for an instrument where surprise releases skew one direction (e.g. a currency more prone to selling off hard on bad news than rallying on good news), \(\mu_J\) could be set negative.
\(\sigma_J\)(sigma-sub-J) The standard deviation of the log-jump-size — controls how variable jump sizes are from one jump to the next. A small \(\sigma_J\) means jumps are roughly the same size every time; a large \(\sigma_J\) means some releases barely move price while others are violent, which matches how NFP surprises actually behave (a print near consensus barely moves the pair; a huge beat or miss can move it many times as far).
\(dJ_t\)The full jump term: \(dJ_t = \left(\prod_{i=N_{t^-}+1}^{N_t} (1+Y_i)\right) - 1\) in exact compound form, or more intuitively, "zero on almost every instant \(dt\), and equal to \(Y_i\) on the rare instant a jump numbered \(i\) actually occurs." This is what makes \(dJ_t\) a compound Poisson process: "compound" because each event carries its own random size (\(Y_i\)), not just a fixed unit increment like plain \(N_t\).

Put in one sentence: the return over the next instant is the usual Black-Scholes drift-plus-diffusion, plus — on the rare instants a Poisson clock ticks — an extra multiplicative jolt of random size drawn from a separate distribution. Three independent sources of randomness are now stacked together: whether a jump happens this instant (Poisson, governed by \(\lambda\)), how big it is given that it happens (lognormal, governed by \(\mu_J,\sigma_J\)), and the ordinary continuous noise that's running the whole time regardless (\(\sigma\,dW_t\)).

Why this produces fat tails

Under plain Black-Scholes, log-returns over any fixed interval are exactly normally distributed — that is a direct consequence of GBM having only the diffusion term. A normal distribution has thin tails: the probability of a return many standard deviations from the mean is astronomically small and shrinks off a cliff the further out you go.

Add the jump term, and the return over a fixed interval is no longer a single normal distribution. It is, informally, a mixture: with probability roughly \((1-\lambda\,\Delta t)\) no jump occurred over an interval of length \(\Delta t\) and the return is (approximately) the usual normal diffusion outcome; with the remaining small probability \(\lambda\,\Delta t\) one or more jumps occurred, and the return is the usual normal diffusion outcome plus a jump drawn from a distribution with its own, typically wider, spread. A mixture of "mostly a narrow normal" with "occasionally a wider, shifted normal" is no longer normal at all — it has extra mass out in the tails relative to a single normal with the same overall variance. That extra tail mass is precisely what excess kurtosis (a fatter-than-normal tail, also called leptokurtosis) measures: a kurtosis number above the normal distribution's baseline of 3 (or, in "excess" form, above 0). This is the direct mathematical reason jump-diffusion models produce leptokurtic returns while pure GBM cannot, by construction, produce anything but a normal one.

Why this produces the volatility smile

In a pure Black-Scholes world, there is exactly one \(\sigma\) for a given underlying and expiry, and it is supposed to price every strike consistently — plug the same \(\sigma\) into the formula for a deep out-of-the-money put, an at-the-money call, and a deep out-of-the-money call, and each gets a theoretically consistent price. If you instead observe real option market prices and back out the \(\sigma\) that would have produced each one (the "implied volatility"), you do not get a flat line across strikes — you get a curve, usually higher at the wings than at the money, called the volatility smile (or, when asymmetric, the volatility skew).

Jump risk is one of the two classical explanations for why (the other being stochastic volatility, a different repair to Black-Scholes not covered in this lesson). Deep out-of-the-money options are exactly the payoffs that only pay off if a large, sudden move happens before expiry — the kind of move a pure diffusion process considers almost impossible over typical horizons, but that jump risk explicitly assigns real probability to. If the true underlying process has jump risk and you price it with plain Black-Scholes at one flat \(\sigma\), you will systematically underprice those far-out-of-the-money strikes relative to a market that correctly prices in the chance of a jump — because the flat-sigma model has no jump channel at all, only ordinary diffusion stretched thin. The market corrects for this by demanding a higher implied volatility at the wings to make the flat-sigma formula spit out the "correct," jump-aware price. The smile is, in this reading, the options market's fingerprint of a jump-diffusion (or similar fat-tailed) reality being forced through a Black-Scholes-shaped pricing formula.

The pricing formula, conceptually

Merton's key result (not derived in full here — this is a conceptual summary, and the original derivation relies on the diversifiability assumption discussed in the pitfalls below) is that, when jump sizes are lognormal and jump risk is idiosyncratic, the fair option price is a Poisson-weighted mixture of ordinary Black-Scholes prices, one for each possible number of jumps \(n = 0, 1, 2, \dots\) between now and expiry, each computed at its own adjusted volatility:

\[ C_{\text{Merton}} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{e^{-\lambda' T}(\lambda' T)^n}{n!} \; C_{\text{BS}}\!\left(S, K, T, r, \sigma_n\right) \]
e^{-λ'T}(λ'T)^n/n!Poisson probability of exactly n jumps before expiry
C_BS(·,σ_n)the ordinary Black-Scholes price, at a volatility bumped up for n jumps
λ'λ adjusted for the jump-risk premium in Merton's derivation
Σ over na probability-weighted average across every possible jump count

Reading this without grinding through the derivation: the term \(\frac{e^{-\lambda' T}(\lambda' T)^n}{n!}\) is just the ordinary Poisson probability of exactly \(n\) jumps occurring over the option's remaining life \(T\) (\(\lambda'\) is \(\lambda\) adjusted for the jump-risk premium — a detail of Merton's original derivation, not something to re-derive here). For each possible jump count \(n\), \(C_{\text{BS}}(\cdot,\sigma_n)\) is just the plain, familiar Black-Scholes call price, but computed at a volatility \(\sigma_n\) that has been bumped up to also account for the extra variance those \(n\) jumps would have contributed. The Merton price is the probability-weighted average of "what would Black-Scholes say if exactly \(n\) jumps happen" over every possible \(n\), weighted by how likely that many jumps actually is. The practical takeaway, without the algebra: a jump-diffusion price is always at least as expensive as the zero-jump Black-Scholes price for the same \(\sigma\), because it is explicitly paying for extra scenarios (jump paths) that the flat model ignores entirely — and that extra payment is concentrated exactly where those scenarios matter most: far out-of-the-money strikes, which is the smile again from a different angle.

🧮
Worked example

NFP Friday versus an average Tuesday

Consider two ways of thinking about the same pair, say GBP/USD, and how \(\lambda\) — the jump intensity — should differ between them if you tried to fit a jump-diffusion model honestly rather than assume one constant \(\lambda\) all month.

An average Tuesday with no major scheduled release. Price moves are dominated by ordinary order flow, position adjustment, and cross-market spillover — the diffusion term. Genuinely discontinuous moves (a real gap through the spread with no intervening trades) are rare enough that a jump-diffusion fit on this kind of day alone would want a \(\lambda\) close to zero: jumps essentially don't happen, and the diffusion-only Black-Scholes assumption is a reasonably honest description of the day.

The two-minute window around 8:30am ET on NFP Friday. The calendar makes the timing of the news itself certain — everyone knows when the number lands, down to the second — but the number's value, and therefore the market's reaction, is genuinely unknown until it prints. In the handful of seconds after release, a very large share of the day's total realized variance can be concentrated into that one window, often accompanied by a real, tradeable-liquidity-gap in price rather than a smooth series of small ticks. A jump-diffusion model that is honest about this reality would not treat \(\lambda\) as a single constant for the whole day; it would want \(\lambda\) to spike sharply for the few minutes around the release and sit near zero the rest of the time — an intensity that is itself a (deterministic, calendar-driven) function of time rather than a flat constant, sometimes called a time-inhomogeneous or "self-exciting near known events" extension of the base model.

This is exactly the intuition behind why option desks price "event vol" differently from ordinary daily vol, and why a one-week option that happens to straddle an NFP print trades at a noticeably higher implied volatility than a one-week option over a quiet week with no scheduled releases — the market is pricing in the elevated \(\lambda\) concentrated in that specific two-minute window, not smearing the same expected jump risk evenly across all seven days. "Vol crush" after the print — implied volatility dropping sharply right after the release — is the mirror image: once the jump has either happened or the window has passed without one, the near-term event risk is resolved, and \(\lambda\) for the option's remaining life drops back toward its quiet, no-event baseline, so the option is repriced accordingly cheaper.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

Full closed-form Merton jump-diffusion pricing shows up less often in modern practice than the underlying idea it stands for. In production option desks, the two much more common tools that carry the same insight forward are: (1) local/stochastic volatility models and Lévy-process models (of which Merton's is the earliest and simplest example — a jump-diffusion process is a specific member of the broader family of Lévy processes, which allow both continuous and jump components) that are calibrated directly to the observed volatility smile/skew rather than assumed from theory, and (2) simply quoting and trading implied volatility itself as the primary object — desks routinely mark separate implied vols for the days surrounding known releases ("event vol") distinct from the base term structure, which is a purely empirical, model-light way of respecting the same jump-risk reality without ever fitting \(\lambda, \mu_J, \sigma_J\) explicitly.

Kou's double-exponential model

A widely cited 2002 refinement swapping Merton's symmetric lognormal jump size for an asymmetric double-exponential one — separate, independently tunable up-jump and down-jump parameters.

Where the explicit jump-diffusion machinery is used directly: single-name equity and index options around known binary events (earnings, drug-trial results, court rulings) where a jump-diffusion or a related pure-jump model gives a principled way to separate "ordinary daily variance" from "the variance attributable to the specific event," which matters for pricing an option that spans the event correctly rather than blending the two regimes into one flat number. Kou's (2002) double-exponential jump model is a widely cited refinement that replaced Merton's lognormal jump-size distribution with an asymmetric double-exponential one, giving separate, independently tunable parameters for the size of up-jumps versus down-jumps — useful because many real markets (equities crashing harder than they melt up; certain FX pairs selling off harder on bad news than they rally on good news) are not symmetric the way Merton's original lognormal jump assumption implies.

More broadly, jump-diffusion and Lévy-process thinking underpins the academic and practitioner literature on tail risk and fat-tailed return modeling generally — value-at-risk and expected-shortfall estimation, stress testing, and risk-margin/haircut setting for leveraged positions all draw on the same "returns are not normal, and the excess is concentrated in occasional large jumps" framing, whether or not a full option-pricing formula is ever fit.

Where this could plug into MacroFXModel

The theory behind fat tails this repo already measures — and a very concrete practical echo

This repo does not fit a jump-diffusion model anywhere, does not price options, and has no \(\lambda, \mu_J, \sigma_J\) estimator. Say that plainly: this is unbuilt here, a theory lesson, not a description of existing code.

What the repo does already have, built and tested, is the honest empirical way to respect exactly the risk this lesson describes without needing to fit a full parametric jump model. Per CLAUDE.md, js/metricsCore.js ships Tier-1 fat-tail primitives — skewness, excessKurtosis, histVaR, histCVaR — built 2026-07-21 and hand-checked in legoBricks.test.mjs. summarizeTrades (the shared trade-log summarizer used across the platform's backtest report cards) emits skew, excessKurt, var95, and cvar95 on every trade-return series it summarizes. Jump-diffusion is the theoretical story for exactly why a real FX return series should have nonzero excess kurtosis and a heavier left tail than a Gaussian model would predict — a sudden macro-release gap is a literal, observable instance of the \(dJ_t\) term this lesson defines. excessKurtosis and histCVaR are the empirical, model-free way this codebase already catches that reality in a trade-return series, without ever having to estimate a jump intensity or a jump-size distribution. This is a real, already-built connection — not a proposal.

There is a second, very concrete practical echo, unrelated to option pricing: CLAUDE.md's own anti-pattern list warns against "assuming the intrabar take-profit was hit on a daily bar (path is unknown) — prefer M1 fills or mark-to-window-close," and fill-realism.html documents a real instance of this exact failure mode in the Per-Line Volatility tearsheet (~44% zero-duration trades where TP is unknowable on the fill bar, so the walker marks to close and hides real outer-line stop losses on continuation days). That bug and Merton's jump term are the same underlying risk wearing two different hats: a fill walker that assumes a limit or stop was touched at its exact quoted price implicitly assumes the path between bars was continuous — that price had to pass through every intermediate level to get from the open to the close. A genuine gap (a jump) can blow straight through a resting limit or stop without ever trading at the assumed price, the same way Merton's \(dJ_t\) term skips over the intermediate price levels a pure-diffusion path would have been forced to cross. A backtest's fill assumption and an option-pricing model's path assumption are answering the same underlying question — "can price teleport?" — for two different purposes.

Building an actual jump-diffusion estimator or option-pricing engine here would be a new, unvalidated undertaking — it would need synthetic-data unit tests that recover known \(\lambda,\mu_J,\sigma_J\) parameters before touching real prices, and, per the Lego Principle, a clear reason two or more callers would use it rather than building it because the math is elegant. Nothing here proposes that. The honest summary is: the fat-tail metrics already in metricsCore.js are the built, tested, empirical answer to "does this repo respect jump/gap risk" — a full parametric jump-diffusion model is not built, and is not needed to get that empirical honesty.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where jump-diffusion thinking is misused or overextended

Treating \(\lambda\) as a single flat constant. Real jump risk is not uniformly distributed across the calendar. A model that fits one average \(\lambda\) across a whole month averages away exactly the structure that matters — the NFP-Friday spike and the quiet-Tuesday baseline blur into one number that describes neither day well. The worked example above is deliberately built around this: naive constant-\(\lambda\) fitting is the single most common misuse of the base model in practice.

Parameter identifiability is genuinely hard. Separating "a lot of small jumps" from "high diffusion volatility" from "a few large jumps" using only a historical return series is a notoriously ill-conditioned estimation problem — multiple very different \((\sigma, \lambda, \mu_J, \sigma_J)\) combinations can produce statistically similar-looking return distributions over realistic sample sizes. Don't trust a single point-estimate fit of jump parameters without checking stability across sub-periods and estimation methods.

The original derivation leans on a diversifiability assumption that doesn't obviously hold for FX event risk. Merton's original 1976 pricing formula assumes jump risk is idiosyncratic — uncorrelated with the broader market, and therefore diversifiable away in a large portfolio, which is why it doesn't need its own risk premium in the simplest version of the formula. A scheduled macro release moving one currency pair is not obviously idiosyncratic in this sense — the same NFP print that jolts EUR/USD can simultaneously move equity indices, rates, and other pairs, i.e. it's closer to systematic than idiosyncratic. This matters for whether the simplest version of the pricing formula is even theoretically appropriate for FX event risk versus a single stock's earnings surprise.

A discrete jump model is still an approximation of something more continuous. Real post-release price action is often not one clean instantaneous jump but a burst of extremely fast, wide-ranging trading over a few seconds — a blur that a single discrete jump approximates reasonably well at longer horizons but not perfectly at sub-second resolution. Don't over-interpret a fitted jump size as "the exact price the market teleported to."

Fat tails in a trade-return series are not proof a jump-diffusion model would improve pricing or sizing. Seeing high excessKurtosis or a large histCVaR in this repo's metrics confirms the empirical symptom jump risk would predict; it is not, by itself, evidence that building a parametric jump model and layering it into a strategy would improve anything OOS. Per the Lego Principle and this repo's validation discipline, that would need to be tested, not assumed from the theory being a good story.

Section 05

Self-test

Q1. Why does a pure Black-Scholes model systematically underprice deep out-of-the-money options relative to a market that has genuine jump risk?
Deep out-of-the-money options only pay off on a large, sudden move before expiry. A pure Black-Scholes world has only continuous diffusion — no mechanism for a large move to happen in a short window, since paths must pass through every intermediate price level. It therefore assigns those large-move scenarios a near-vanishing probability under any single flat \(\sigma\). A market that correctly prices in real jump risk assigns those scenarios meaningfully more probability (and hence more value) than the flat-diffusion model does, so the flat-sigma Black-Scholes price for that strike comes out too low relative to the market. This gap is exactly what forces implied volatility higher at the wings when you back it out of market prices — the volatility smile.
Q2. What does each of \(\lambda\), \(\mu_J\), and \(\sigma_J\) control in the jump term, and what would it mean for one of them to be near zero?
\(\lambda\) is the jump intensity — how frequently jumps arrive on average (jumps per unit time). \(\lambda \to 0\) means jumps essentially never happen, and the model collapses back to plain Black-Scholes (no jump term contributes anything). \(\mu_J\) is the mean of the log-jump-size — the average direction and rough magnitude of a jump when one occurs; \(\mu_J = 0\) means jumps are unbiased in direction on average (as likely to be a gap up as a gap down). \(\sigma_J\) is the standard deviation of the log-jump-size — how variable jump sizes are from one jump to the next; \(\sigma_J \to 0\) means every jump, when it happens, is approximately the same fixed size, while a large \(\sigma_J\) means jump sizes range from negligible to violent.
Q3. Why does mixing a normal diffusion outcome with an occasional jump produce a fat-tailed (leptokurtic) distribution instead of just a wider normal distribution?
The return over a fixed interval is a probability-weighted mixture: mostly "no jump happened, return is approximately normal with the base variance," and rarely "a jump happened, return is that normal outcome plus an extra jump-sized shift." A mixture of a narrow normal with a wider, shifted one is not itself a single normal distribution — it has more probability mass out in the tails than any single normal distribution with the same overall variance would, because the rare-but-large jump scenarios pile extra mass far from the center while the vast majority of ordinary (no-jump) outcomes stay clustered near the mean. That combination — a tall, narrow center from the frequent no-jump case plus extra far-out mass from the rare jump case — is precisely what excess kurtosis measures.
Q4. Why should a jump-diffusion model in principle let \(\lambda\) vary through a trading day around a known scheduled release, rather than treating it as one flat constant for the whole day?
\(\lambda\) is meant to represent the true rate at which discontinuous moves occur, and that rate is not remotely uniform across a day that contains a scheduled macro release. In the minutes around NFP, CPI, or a central bank decision, the chance of a genuine gap is far higher than during an ordinary quiet stretch of trading, because the news itself is concentrated at a known, calendar-fixed instant even though its content is unknown until it prints. A single flat \(\lambda\) fit across the whole day would either overstate jump risk on quiet stretches or understate it around the release, misrepresenting both regimes. This is exactly why option desks quote separate "event vol" for the specific window around a known release rather than smearing the same implied jump risk evenly across the full week.
Q5. Why does a fill-walker that assumes an intrabar take-profit was hit, without checking the actual bar's real path, risk exactly the same kind of error as ignoring jump risk in option pricing?
Both mistakes rest on the same false assumption: that price moved continuously through every intermediate level between two known points (the bar's open and close, or the pre- and post-jump price). Assuming a resting limit or stop was filled at its exact quoted level on a coarse bar implicitly assumes price had to pass through that level on its way from open to close — exactly the continuity assumption a pure-diffusion Black-Scholes path makes and a genuine jump violates. A real gap (a jump) can skip straight over a resting order's price without ever trading there, the same way Merton's \(dJ_t\) term lets the underlying skip over intermediate price levels a continuous path would have been forced to cross. This repo's own anti-pattern warning against assuming intrabar TP fills on a daily bar, and the fill-realism findings on the Per-Line Volatility tearsheet's zero-duration trades, are a concrete, practical instance of ignoring exactly this risk.
Q6. Does this repo currently fit a jump-diffusion model or price options with one? If not, what does it have instead, and why does that still count as respecting jump/gap risk?
No — there is no \(\lambda, \mu_J, \sigma_J\) estimator and no option pricing engine anywhere in this codebase; this lesson is a theory background page, not a description of a built feature. What the repo does have is the empirical, model-free alternative: js/metricsCore.js's Tier-1 fat-tail primitives (skewness, excessKurtosis, histVaR, histCVaR), which summarizeTrades already computes on every trade-return series it summarizes. These directly measure the empirical symptom jump risk predicts — a heavier left tail and higher-than-Gaussian kurtosis — without requiring anyone to first estimate a parametric jump process. That's a legitimate, already-built way to respect the risk this lesson describes, even without a full Merton model anywhere in the code.
