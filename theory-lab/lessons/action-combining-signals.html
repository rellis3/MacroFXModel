Combining Signals That Disagree

Three indicators say long, two say short. The vote says long. The honest answer may be "no view". This lesson turns five readings into one number you can act on, without counting the same opinion three times.

CONCEPT — explained here; desk verdicts quoted where tested ~22 min read Prereqs: Correlation & Regression (helpful); Thinking in Expected Value
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • Correlated signals are not separate votes. Three trend indicators that move together with correlation 0.8 carry the information of about 1.15 independent signals, not 3: \(N_{\text{eff}} = N/(1+(N-1)\rho)\).
  • Weight by track record, then shrink it. A measured hit rate or correlation with returns (IC) from a short record is mostly noise. Pull it toward zero by \(\tau^2/(\tau^2 + 1/n)\): with 50 observations you keep about a ninth of it, with 400 about half.
  • The honest combination is a regression, not a vote: weights \(w = R^{-1}\,\text{IC}\) undo the double-counting. On this lesson's EUR/USD example the vote says long 3–2, the score sum says long, and the correlation-aware, shrunk combination says a 50.6% lean short — no view.
  • "No view" is an output, not a failure. Write the rule in advance: no trade if the combined score is under one standard deviation, if the expected move does not clear twice the cost, or if none of the signals has a record.
  • The desk's own test: agreement across three macro domains did not predict forward direction (77 episodes, 2009–2026); two genuinely different yield-spread signals with a 0.30 correlation did combine into a better book than either alone.
Section 01

Why this matters

By the time you sit down to decide, the analysis is done. You have a momentum reading, a moving-average cross, a breakout, a yield-spread z-score and a positioning number. Some point one way, some the other. The previous lesson, Thinking in Expected Value, showed how to turn one view into a probability and a payoff. This one is the step before that: getting from five readings to one view without fooling yourself.

The two classic mistakes are mirror images. The first is counting votes: three of five say long, so go long — even though the three are the same idea measured three ways. The second is believing the loudest record: one indicator had a great last year, so it gets all the weight — even though a year is a few dozen observations. The tools that fix both already live elsewhere in this curriculum: correlation and regression, shrinkage, partial pooling and the fundamental law of active management. Here they are put to work on one decision.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Five friends who all read the same newspaper

You ask five friends whether it will rain. Three say yes. Before you take an umbrella, you notice those three all read the same morning paper, and the paper said "rain likely". That is one opinion repeated three times, not three opinions. The other two friends — one who looked at the sky, one who checked a barometer — are each giving you something new.

Now suppose the barometer friend has been right far more often than the paper over years, and the sky-watcher only started last week. You would lean on the barometer, give the paper about one friend's worth of weight however many people repeat it, and mostly ignore the sky-watcher until he has a record. If, after all that, the evidence points nowhere in particular, the right answer is "I don't know" — and you take the umbrella or not on other grounds.

How it reached trading

From "weigh the indicators in your head" to a formula

Signal combination · into active management via Grinold's fundamental law (1989) and Grinold & Kahn's alpha recipe (1995)

For most of trading's history, combining indicators was a judgement call: a discretionary trader looked at the screens and "weighed it all up". The problem was invisible until someone tried to measure it. In 1989 Richard Grinold published the fundamental law of active management: the quality of a strategy grows with the skill of each forecast (its information coefficient, IC) times the square root of breadth — the number of independent bets. The word "independent" carries the whole lesson: ten correlated signals are not ten bets.

Grinold and Ronald Kahn's book Active Portfolio Management (1995) turned it into a desk recipe: express every signal as a standardised score, convert it to a forecast as volatility × IC × score, and combine several signals with weights that account for how they correlate. Systematic equity and macro managers built their multi-signal models this way. The underlying idea of blending forecasts rather than picking one goes back to Bates and Granger's 1969 work on combining economic forecasts.

Section 02

The math, step by step

1. Put every signal on the same scale

A momentum reading in pips, a yield spread in basis points and a positioning number in contracts cannot be added. Convert each to a score \(s_i\): how many of its own standard deviations it sits from its usual level, signed so that positive means "long". A score of +1.5 means "unusually bullish for this indicator"; 0 means "nothing to say".

Each signal also has a track record: its information coefficient \(\text{IC}_i\), the correlation between its past scores and the returns that followed. Real trading signals have small ICs. Practitioners treat something around 0.05 as a good signal and above 0.10 as rare. That smallness is the reason this lesson exists: with ICs this low, how you combine matters as much as what you combine.

2. Correlated signals: the effective number

If \(N\) standardised signals all have the same pairwise correlation \(\rho\), the variance of their average is \((1 + (N-1)\rho)/N\). An average of truly independent signals would have variance \(1/N_{\text{eff}}\). Setting the two equal gives the effective number of signals:

\[ N_{\text{eff}} = \frac{N}{1 + (N-1)\,\rho} \]
N

Three trend indicators with \(\rho = 0.8\): \(N_{\text{eff}} = 3/(1 + 2 \times 0.8) = 3/2.6 = 1.15\). Add a fourth and a fifth trend indicator and you reach only \(5/4.2 = 1.19\). As \(N\) grows, \(N_{\text{eff}}\) creeps toward \(1/\rho = 1.25\) and stops. Adding more of the same kind of indicator makes a screen look more convincing without making it more informative.

3. Combining: let a regression do the counting

Suppose the scores are standardised, \(R\) is the matrix of their correlations with each other, and \(\text{IC}\) is the column of their correlations with the next return. The best linear forecast of the standardised return — what a regression of returns on all the signals at once would give you if it knew the true numbers — is

\[ F = \sum_i w_i\,s_i, \qquad w = R^{-1}\,\text{IC}, \qquad \text{IC}_{\text{comb}} = \sqrt{\text{IC}^{\top} R^{-1}\,\text{IC}} \]
sᵢtoday's score on signal i, in its own standard deviations
R⁻¹the inverse correlation matrix: the part that stops correlated signals being counted twice
wthe weight each signal gets in the combined forecast
IC_combthe skill of the combined forecast: what all the signals together are worth

Two signals make the point. Each has IC 0.05. If they are independent (\(\rho = 0\)), together they are worth \(0.05\sqrt{2} = 0.071\). If they are correlated at 0.8, together they are worth only \(0.05\sqrt{2/1.8} = 0.053\) — barely more than one. The general two-signal formula is \(\text{IC}_{\text{comb}} = \text{IC}\sqrt{2/(1+\rho)}\).

For a family of \(m\) signals with equal ICs and internal correlation \(\rho\), the inverse has a simple effect: each member's weight is its IC divided by \(1 + (m-1)\rho\). That is the same factor as in \(N_{\text{eff}}\). The regression automatically gives a family of three near-copies roughly one signal's worth of influence between them.

4. Weight by track record — then shrink it

The ICs you plug in are estimates. A correlation measured on \(n\) independent observations has a standard error of roughly \(1/\sqrt{n}\): with 100 observations, about 0.10 — twice the size of a good signal's true IC. A measured IC of 0.08 from 100 trades is entirely consistent with a true IC of zero. Treat it as a measurement of the truth plus noise, and pull it toward a prior of zero by how noisy it is:

\[ \widehat{\text{IC}}^{\,\text{shrunk}}_i = B_i \,\widehat{\text{IC}}_i, \qquad B_i = \frac{\tau^2}{\tau^2 + 1/n_i} \]
ÎCᵢthe IC you measured on signal i's own history
nᵢhow many independent observations that measurement rests on
τhow widely true ICs vary across signals in general (0.05 here)
Bᵢthe share of the measured IC you keep: near 0 for a short record, near 1 for a long one

With \(\tau = 0.05\) (\(\tau^2 = 0.0025\)): 50 observations keep \(0.0025/0.0225 = 11\%\) of the measured IC, 100 keep 20%, 400 keep 50%, 1,600 keep 80%. This is the same logic as Bayesian updating and the James–Stein result discussed in partial pooling: estimates from little data are pulled hardest toward the prior. An untested indicator (\(n = 0\)) gets weight zero, however convincing its chart looks.

Forecasting research has a well-known version of this result, the "forecast combination puzzle": simple equal-weight averages of forecasts often beat combinations whose weights were estimated from data, because the estimated weights carry so much noise. Shrinkage sits between the two — trust the record exactly as much as its length deserves.

5. From a combined score to a view — or to no view

The combined forecast \(F\) is in standardised-return units, and its own standard deviation is \(\text{IC}_{\text{comb}}\). Three numbers turn it into something you can act on:

\[ z = \frac{F}{\text{IC}_{\text{comb}}}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[r] = \sigma\,F, \qquad P(\text{direction right}) = \Phi\!\left(\frac{|F|}{\sqrt{1-\text{IC}_{\text{comb}}^2}}\right) \]
zhow unusual today's combined reading is, in its own standard deviations
σthe instrument's volatility over the holding horizon
E[r]the expected move, in return units — what goes into the cost check
Φthe normal CDF: turns the forecast into a probability of being right about direction

The probability line is sobering. With a combined IC of 0.10 — an excellent set of signals — a two-standard-deviation reading (\(F = 0.20\)) gives \(\Phi(0.20/0.995) = 58\%\). Small edges are the normal case, which is why the next step is a written rule for when the answer is no view:

The thresholds are a choice, not a law. What matters is choosing them before you look at today's screen, so that "the signals disagree" leads to a pre-agreed answer instead of a story.

Worked example

Three trend signals say long, the rates signal says short

A one-month view on EUR/USD. Monthly volatility \(\sigma = 2.0\%\). Your screen:

D. 2-year yield spread zRates−1.00.08400
E. Speculative positioningPositioning−0.50.0650

The three trend signals are correlated 0.8 with each other; the families are treated as uncorrelated with each other.

1

Shrink each record

\(\tau^2 = 0.0025\). For \(n = 400\): \(B = 0.0025/(0.0025 + 0.0025) = 0.5\). For \(n = 50\): \(B = 0.0025/(0.0025+0.02) = 0.111\). Shrunk ICs: trend signals \(0.04 \times 0.5 = 0.020\) each; rates \(0.08 \times 0.5 = 0.040\); positioning \(0.06 \times 0.111 = 0.0067\).

2

Count the trend family once

Three signals at \(\rho = 0.8\) are worth \(N_{\text{eff}} = 3/2.6 = 1.15\). With equal ICs, \(R^{-1}\) gives each \(w = 0.020/2.6 = 0.00769\). Rates and positioning sit in their own families, so their weights are their shrunk ICs: 0.040 and 0.0067.

3

Combine

\(F = 0.00769 \times (1.2 + 1.0 + 1.5) + 0.040 \times (-1.0) + 0.0067 \times (-0.5) = 0.0285 - 0.0400 - 0.0033 = -0.0149\).

Skill of the combination: \(\text{IC}_{\text{comb}}^2 = 3 \times 0.00769 \times 0.020 + 0.040^2 + 0.0067^2 = 0.000462 + 0.0016 + 0.000044 = 0.00211\), so \(\text{IC}_{\text{comb}} = 0.046\).

4

Turn it into a view

\(z = -0.0149/0.046 = -0.32\). Expected move \(\sigma F = 2.0\% \times -0.0149 = -0.030\%\), about 3 pips. Probability the direction is right: \(\Phi(0.0149/\sqrt{1-0.0021}) = \Phi(0.0149) = 50.6\%\).

5

The output

NO VIEW \(|z| = 0.32 < 1\). The screen looked bullish; the evidence, counted honestly, is a coin flip with a faint lean short. Write down "no view, signals cancel", and do not trade EUR/USD on this set this month.

Ignoring correlation flips the answer to long: the trend family gets counted three times. Skipping the shrinkage keeps the verdict but more than doubles the claimed skill (0.109 against 0.046) and hands 50 observations of positioning data real weight. What would make this a trade? Independent agreement. If the yield spread read +1.0 instead of −1.0, the same arithmetic gives \(F = +0.065\), \(z = +1.42\), an expected move of +0.13% and a 52.6% chance of being right — a modest, legitimate long, ready for the cost check in the next lesson.

Section 04

It actually happened: "independent" bets that were one bet

It actually happened

August 2007: the quant quake

In the week beginning 6 August 2007, a number of large quantitative equity funds — market-neutral strategies built from many signals such as value, momentum and earnings quality — suffered unusually large losses within a few days, while the stock market as a whole moved comparatively little. Each fund had its own models and believed its blend of signals was its own.

Amir Khandani and Andrew Lo studied the episode that autumn ("What Happened to the Quants in August 2007?"). Their explanation was that the funds' positions overlapped far more than anyone had appreciated: many had arrived at similar portfolios from similar signals. When one or more large portfolios were unwound quickly, the selling pushed prices against everyone holding the same positions, which forced further selling. A sharp partial rebound followed within days.

This is the effective-number problem at the scale of an industry. Signals that looked like many independent sources of return were, in a crisis, close to one crowded position. The diversification was counted, not measured.

It actually happened

1998: Long-Term Capital Management

LTCM, founded in 1994 by John Meriwether with partners including Myron Scholes and Robert Merton, ran a large book of relative-value trades across many countries and markets — bond spreads, swap spreads, equity pairs, volatility. On paper these were many separate trades with modest correlations to each other, so the fund could use high leverage.

After Russia's default and devaluation in August 1998, investors everywhere rushed toward the most liquid, safest assets at once. Spreads widened across almost all of LTCM's markets together: the trades turned out to share one underlying exposure, to liquidity and credit risk premia. In September 1998 the Federal Reserve Bank of New York organised a recapitalisation of about $3.6 billion by a consortium of banks. The lesson for anyone combining signals or trades: correlations measured in calm periods can understate how much a set of "different" positions is really the same position.

Section 05

What this desk found when it tested the claims

Desk test: nothing found "When several independent macro domains agree, direction follows." The desk pre-registered exactly this: Fed net-liquidity momentum, the 10-year-minus-2-year curve regime and investment-grade credit stress all +1 or all −1 on the same day, forward direction on EUR/USD, the S&P 500 and gold. With 39 positive- and 38 negative-confluence episodes from 2009 to 2026, it found nothing. One cell looked real — EUR/USD over 20 days after positive confluence, −0.616% [−1.117, −0.080] — but negative confluence pointed the same way (−0.023%), so the "signal" was EUR/USD's long drift lower, not agreement predicting anything. Agreement is not evidence unless the agreeing signals each carry some.
Desk test: held up Two genuinely different signals, combined. The desk ran its yield-spread mechanism on the 10-year rate differential as well as the 2-year, and checked first that it was a different signal rather than a relabelled one: 15.7% trade overlap and a 0.300 daily-return correlation. The equal-risk combination's Sharpe ratio of 1.15 beat either leg alone (2-year 1.02, 10-year 0.81). This lesson's arithmetic predicts it: for two equal-risk legs, combined Sharpe \(= (S_1+S_2)/\sqrt{2(1+\rho)} = 1.83/\sqrt{2.6} \approx 1.13\). Backtest evidence; not forward-proven.
The trap: agreement feels like confirmation, and the feeling grows with every extra indicator that agrees. But three readings of the same thing agreeing is what you would see whether or not any of them works. Agreement is only evidence when the signals are different and each one has a record.
Section 06

How practitioners actually use this

🧺

Average within a family first

Collapse each family of near-copies into one composite before weighting across families. It avoids the strange weights a full matrix inverse can produce when two members are almost identical.

⚖️

Shrink toward equal weights

Systematic managers rarely use raw estimated weights. They blend them with equal weights or a prior, for the same reason as the forecast-combination puzzle: estimated weights are noisy (see Ridge & LASSO).

🧪

Test the combination out of sample

A combination is itself a model, and choosing it from many alternatives is a multiple-testing problem. Validate the combined score walk-forward, not just each part.

📉

Check correlations in stress

Measure signal and position correlations in bad periods as well as on average. 1998 and 2007 both show correlations jumping toward one when they matter most (see PCA & factor regimes).

🧩
Where this connects to the desk

The desk combines rarely, and only tested legs

The one combination the desk runs is the two yield-spread legs above, and it was accepted only after a pre-registered check that the second leg was different. No desk indicator set is currently combined into a single score with the shrinkage and correlation steps shown here; the worked example's numbers are illustrative, not desk measurements.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where combining goes wrong

Counting indicators. Three ways of measuring trend are one opinion about trend. Count families, not lines on a chart.

Trusting a short record. A 0.08 IC from 50 trades is noise-sized. Shrink it, or give it no weight until it has more.

Weights from the same data as the test. Choosing weights on a sample and reporting the combined score's performance on that sample overstates it, often badly.

Calm-period correlations. Signals and positions that look unrelated on average can move together in a crisis. Check stress periods.

Treating "no view" as weakness. A desk that always has a view is either very good or not measuring. Written no-view rules are what make the views it does take mean something.

Section 07

Key Takeaways

✓Correlated signals are one vote. \(N_{\text{eff}} = N/(1+(N-1)\rho)\): three at 0.8 are worth 1.15, and no number of them is worth more than \(1/\rho\).
✓Combine by regression, not by vote: \(w = R^{-1}\,\text{IC}\). In the example it turned "long 3–2" into "no view".
✓Shrink every record by its length: keep \(\tau^2/(\tau^2+1/n)\) of the measured IC — about half at 400 observations, a ninth at 50, nothing at zero.
✓"No view" is a written output. Fix the thresholds — combined z, expected move against cost, combined IC — before looking at today's screen.
✗Desk test, nothing found: agreement across three macro domains did not predict forward direction (77 episodes, 2009–2026). The desk's test found no edge in agreement itself; that says nothing against combining signals that each have a record.
⚠Held up, in backtest: two yield-spread legs correlated 0.30 combined to a Sharpe of 1.15, above either alone — diversification works when the signals really are different. Not forward-proven.
Section 08

Self-test

Q1. You have four momentum indicators with average pairwise correlation 0.7. How many independent signals are they worth?
\(N_{\text{eff}} = 4/(1 + 3 \times 0.7) = 4/3.1 = 1.29\). Adding a fifth would give \(5/3.8 = 1.32\); the ceiling is \(1/0.7 = 1.43\).
Q2. Two signals each have IC 0.06. What are they worth combined if their correlation is 0, and if it is 0.5?
\(\text{IC}_{\text{comb}} = 0.06\sqrt{2/(1+\rho)}\). At \(\rho = 0\): \(0.06 \times 1.414 = 0.085\). At \(\rho = 0.5\): \(0.06 \times \sqrt{1.333} = 0.069\).
Q3. A new indicator shows IC 0.12 over 100 trades. With τ = 0.05, what IC should you use?
\(B = 0.0025/(0.0025 + 0.01) = 0.2\), so \(0.12 \times 0.2 = 0.024\). The standard error of the measurement (about \(1/\sqrt{100} = 0.10\)) is nearly as large as the measurement itself.
Q4. In the worked example, why does ignoring correlation flip the answer from "no view" to "long"?
Without the correlation step each trend signal keeps its full shrunk IC of 0.020, so the trend family contributes \(0.020 \times 3.7 = 0.074\) against the short side's 0.043: net long. With it, the three share one signal's worth of weight (\(0.020/2.6\) each), contribute 0.028, and the rates signal's −0.040 wins. The difference is the trend idea being counted three times.
Q5. Your combined forecast has ICcomb = 0.08 and today z = 2.5. What is the probability your direction is right, and the expected move if σ = 1.5%?
\(F = z \times \text{IC}_{\text{comb}} = 0.20\). \(P = \Phi(0.20/\sqrt{1-0.0064}) = \Phi(0.2006) \approx 58\%\). Expected move \(= 1.5\% \times 0.20 = 0.30\%\). A strong reading on a good set of signals is still only a 58/42 bet — which is why sizing and costs matter so much.
Q6. The desk found that agreement across three macro domains did not predict direction. Does that mean combining signals is useless?
No. It means that this desk's test of agreement among those three domains found no forward edge in direction over 5 and 20 days. Agreement only adds information if the agreeing signals each carry some; the desk's two yield-spread legs, each with a record and correlated only 0.30, did combine into a better book. The lesson is to combine signals with records, weighted for correlation — not to treat agreement itself as a signal.
