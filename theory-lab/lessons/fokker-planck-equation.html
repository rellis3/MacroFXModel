The Fokker-Planck Equation

Itô's Lemma answers "how does a function of a random process evolve?" This is the mirror question: how does the process's own probability density evolve? The answer is a completely deterministic PDE — and confusing its forward direction with the Black-Scholes-style backward equation is one of the most common mix-ups in the whole subject.

CONCEPT — the density-evolution equation every SDE in this curriculum implicitly relies on ~19 min read Prereqs: Itô's Lemma, Existence & Uniqueness of SDE Solutions
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • For an SDE \(dX_t=\mu(X_t,t)dt+\sigma(X_t,t)dW_t\), the transition density \(p(x,t)\) — the probability of finding \(X_t\) near \(x\) — satisfies the Fokker-Planck equation (also called the forward Kolmogorov equation): \(\partial p/\partial t = -\partial_x[\mu p] + \tfrac12\partial_x^2[\sigma^2 p]\).
  • The first term (drift/advection) shifts the density's location without changing its shape; the second term (diffusion) spreads it out. Both terms are exactly the ones Itô's Lemma produces, moved from the test function onto the density via integration by parts — the Fokker-Planck operator is the formal adjoint of the generator that appears there.
  • This is the forward equation: it evolves a fixed initial density forward in time. It is the mirror image of the backward Kolmogorov equation, which evolves an expected payoff backward from a fixed terminal condition — the Black-Scholes PDE is precisely a backward-Kolmogorov-type equation with an added discounting term.
  • Verified here by direct substitution: geometric Brownian motion's known lognormal transition density actually solves its own Fokker-Planck equation — not merely asserted, computed term by term.
  • The Fokker-Planck equation is a fully deterministic PDE describing how a density evolves. That is exactly the conceptual stepping-stone to stochastic PDEs, where the object evolving is no longer a deterministic density but a genuinely random field.
  • How it reached trading: Bachelier's 1900 bond-price thesis already had it; Breeden & Litzenberger (1978) read the market's density from option prices and Dupire (1994) wrote the forward equation in strikes, which is how local-vol models are calibrated to the smile.
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

I know Itô's Lemma tells me how a function of a random price moves. What does Fokker-Planck add?

It asks the mirror question. Forget any one path. If you ran the price from the same start a million times, how does the whole cloud of outcomes change shape over time?

A cloud of possible prices.

Exactly. Picture a puff of smoke in a breeze. The breeze carries the whole puff sideways without changing its shape. That is the drift. And the smoke spreads out on its own, breeze or no breeze. That is the diffusion. The equation is just the rule for both happening at once.

But every particle is random. How can the rule not be random?

That is the surprising part. Each particle bounces unpredictably, but the shape of the cloud evolves in a completely predictable way. All the randomness has been soaked up into the shape. The equation for the shape has no noise in it at all.

Give me a number.

Take a price that drifts up five percent a year with twenty percent volatility. Work in the log of the price, and the cloud's centre moves up only three percent a year. Five, minus half of twenty percent squared, which is two. That is the same volatility drag Itô's Lemma gives you, and the lesson checks that the textbook lognormal shape really does solve the equation.

This looks a lot like the Black-Scholes equation.

It is its mirror image, and mixing them up is the classic mistake. Fokker-Planck runs forward. You know where you start, and you get the whole distribution of where you might end up.

And Black-Scholes runs backward.

Right. You know the payoff at expiry, and you march back to one number today: the price. Same drift, same noise, opposite ends of the time line. Black-Scholes adds one discounting term to turn an expected payoff into today's money.

Does anyone on a desk actually run it forward?

Yes, for calibration. Breeden and Litzenberger showed in 1978 that option prices contain the market's own distribution. Dupire in 1994 wrote the forward equation in strikes and maturities. One forward solve prices every strike and maturity at once, which is how local volatility models get fitted to the smile.

And the danger?

Reusing a backward pricing solver for a forward risk question. It will not crash. It will quietly compute the wrong thing.

One line to remember?

If you know where you start and want the spread of outcomes, run forward; if you know the payoff and want today's value, run backward.

Section 01

Why this matters

ito-lemma.html answers a specific question: given an Itô process \(X_t\) and a function \(f\), how does \(f(X_t)\) evolve? That lesson derives the extra \(\tfrac12 f''\sigma^2\,dt\) term ordinary calculus misses, and shows it is exactly the mechanical origin of Black- Scholes's \(\sigma^2/2\). This lesson asks the mirror-image question, about the process itself rather than a function of it: forget any single path — if you ran the SDE many, many times from the same starting point, how does the whole distribution of possible outcomes evolve? The Fokker-Planck equation answers that, and every SDE this curriculum has used so far — Ornstein-Uhlenbeck, geometric Brownian motion, Vasicek/CIR (short-rate-models-vasicek-cir-hull-white.html) — has an implicit Fokker-Planck equation governing how its transition density evolves, whether or not that equation was ever written down explicitly.

It also settles a distinction that is genuinely easy to blur: black-scholes.html derives a PDE for an option's value \(V(S,t)\) that is solved backward in time, from a known payoff at expiration. That PDE looks superficially similar to the one built here, and the two are close relatives — but they run in opposite directions, answer different questions, and start from opposite ends of the time axis. Getting this straight is the single most useful thing this lesson delivers, and it earns its own section below rather than a footnote. sde-existence-uniqueness.html's well-posedness conditions matter here too: the Fokker-Planck equation presupposes that \(X_t\) is a well-defined process with a density in the first place — without a unique strong solution, there is no \(p(x,t)\) to write an equation for.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Itô's Lemma asked about a function of the process. This asks about the process's own shape.

Picture releasing a huge cloud of particles, all starting at the exact same point, each one independently buffeted by the same drift and the same random noise — each particle is one possible realization of \(X_t\). A moment later, that cloud has spread into some shape: shifted a bit in the direction of the drift, blurred out a bit by the noise. A moment after that, it has spread further. The Fokker-Planck equation is simply the equation that shape obeys, instant by instant. It says nothing about any individual particle's jagged, unpredictable path — that randomness is exactly what Itô's Lemma and the SDE itself already describe. It describes something else entirely: the population-level density of where the particles are, which turns out to evolve by a completely ordinary, deterministic partial differential equation — no randomness left in it at all, even though every individual particle inside the cloud is still bouncing around randomly.

That's the whole intuition. The math below just makes it precise: which deterministic PDE, exactly, and why.

How it reached trading

Reading the market's whole distribution, not one price

Forward equation · Bachelier, 1900; on desks via Breeden & Litzenberger (1978) and Dupire (1994)

A pricing model is usually run backwards: one payoff, one PDE, one price. A desk calibrating to a whole smile needs the other direction: given today's spot, what distribution does the model imply at every future date, and does it match what option prices say? The idea began in finance: Bachelier's 1900 thesis on Paris bond prices already had probability spreading like heat.

Breeden and Litzenberger (1978) showed that the market's risk-neutral density can be read off option prices, as the second derivative of the call price with respect to strike. Dupire (1994) wrote the forward equation in strikes and maturities, which gives the one local-volatility function that reproduces every quoted vanilla, and with it smile-consistent pricing of exotics.

On desks today the forward equation calibrates local-volatility and local-stochastic-volatility models for exotics. One forward solve gives prices for every strike and maturity at once, which is why calibration runs forward while pricing a single exotic runs backward.

Section 02

The math, step by step

Watch the formula build
From Itô's generator to the forward equation, one term at a time
\[ \mathcal{L}f = \color{#fbbf24}{\mu f' + \tfrac12\sigma^2 f''} \]

Start with the generator: the non-random part of Itô's Lemma. It says how the expected value of a function \(f\) of the process changes, given where the process is now.

\[ \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + \mathcal{L}u = 0, \qquad \color{#fbbf24}{u(x,T) = g(x)} \]

Use it directly with a payoff fixed at the end, and you get the backward equation: solve from \(T\) back to today. Black-Scholes is this with \(\mu = rS\), plus a discounting term \(-rV\).

\[ \mathcal{L}^*p = \color{#fbbf24}{-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\big[\mu\,p\big] + \frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}\big[\sigma^2 p\big]} \]

Integrate by parts to move the derivatives off \(f\) and onto the density \(p\). Each move flips a sign. The result is the adjoint: a drift term that shifts \(p\) and a diffusion term that spreads it.

\[ \color{#10b981}{\frac{\partial p}{\partial t} = -\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\big[\mu\,p\big] + \frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}\big[\sigma^2 p\big], \qquad p(x,t_0) = \delta(x-x_0)} \]

Set the density's rate of change equal to the adjoint and start it from a known point. That is the Fokker-Planck equation, solved forward in time from the start.

Start from the same general SDE sde-existence-uniqueness.html and ito-lemma.html both use:

\[ dX_t = \mu(X_t,t)\,dt + \sigma(X_t,t)\,dW_t \]
μ(X_t,t)the drift — how the process moves on average
σ(X_t,t)the diffusion coefficient — the size of the random shock

1. Stating the equation

Let \(p(x,t)\) denote the transition density of \(X_t\): the probability density that \(X_t\) is near \(x\), given a fixed starting point at an earlier time. \(p(x,t)\) satisfies the Fokker-Planck equation (equivalently, the forward Kolmogorov equation — both names are standard; see Hannes Risken, The Fokker-Planck Equation: Methods of Solution and Applications, Springer, 2nd ed. 1996, the standard reference for this entire equation and everything built on it):

\[ \frac{\partial p}{\partial t} = -\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\big[\mu(x,t)\,p(x,t)\big] + \frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}\big[\sigma^2(x,t)\,p(x,t)\big] \]
−∂/∂x[μ(x,t)p(x,t)]
Drift / advection term
Shifts the density's location — carries probability mass in the direction μ points, without changing the shape
½ ∂²/∂x²[σ²(x,t)p(x,t)]
Diffusion term
Spreads the density out — the same structural form as the heat equation, driven by the squared diffusion coefficient

Read the equation exactly as those two cards describe it: the density \(p(x,\cdot)\) at a fixed time is a curve over \(x\), and the equation says how that curve's height changes at each point \(x\), one instant later. The advection term is a translation — it moves probability mass along \(x\) at the local rate \(\mu(x,t)\), the same way smoke drifts downwind without dispersing on its own. The diffusion term is a spreading — mathematically it is the ordinary heat equation's Laplacian term, with the diffusion coefficient \(\sigma^2\) taking the place of thermal conductivity, blurring the density outward regardless of which way it is drifting. A process with \(\sigma=0\) (no noise) has a Fokker-Planck equation that reduces to pure advection — the density just translates rigidly, exactly matching the deterministic ODE case.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Picture releasing a puff of smoke into a gently moving breeze. The breeze carries the whole cloud sideways without changing its shape — that's the drift term. Meanwhile the smoke also spreads out and blurs on its own, breeze or no breeze — that's the diffusion term. The Fokker-Planck equation is just the rule describing both of those things happening to a probability cloud at once, instant by instant.

2. Where it comes from: the formal adjoint of Itô's Lemma

This lesson states the equation as a result rather than proving it with full rigor — the same choice black-scholes.html makes for Delta and Gamma, stated as the PDE's named partial derivatives without re-deriving the whole replication argument each time a Greek is used. What follows is the informal motivation, using exactly the machinery ito-lemma.html already built, so the result doesn't have to be taken purely on faith.

Take any smooth test function \(f(x)\) (not depending on \(t\)) and apply Itô's Lemma to \(f(X_t)\), exactly as in ito-lemma.html:

\[ df(X_t) = \left[\mu(X_t,t)f'(X_t) + \tfrac12\sigma^2(X_t,t)f''(X_t)\right]dt + \sigma(X_t,t)f'(X_t)\,dW_t \]

Take expectations on both sides. The \(dW_t\) term has zero mean (an Itô integral is a martingale), so it drops out entirely, leaving:

\[ \frac{d}{dt}\,\mathbb{E}[f(X_t)] = \mathbb{E}\Big[\mu(X_t,t)f'(X_t) + \tfrac12\sigma^2(X_t,t)f''(X_t)\Big] \]

The right-hand side's bracketed expression, \(\mathcal{L}f = \mu f' + \tfrac12\sigma^2 f''\), is called the generator of the process \(X_t\) — it is exactly the deterministic part of Itô's Lemma, with the random \(dW_t\) piece stripped away. Now write both sides as integrals against the density \(p(x,t)\), since \(\mathbb{E}[f(X_t)]=\int f(x)\,p(x,t)\,dx\):

\[ \int f(x)\,\frac{\partial p}{\partial t}\,dx = \int \big[\mu(x,t)f'(x) + \tfrac12\sigma^2(x,t)f''(x)\big]\,p(x,t)\,dx \]

The left side already has \(\partial p/\partial t\) sitting where it needs to be. The right side still has derivatives landing on \(f\), not on \(p\) — integrating each term by parts (twice, for the second-order term) moves those derivatives off \(f\) and onto \(\mu p\) and \(\sigma^2 p\) instead, at the cost of a minus sign each time a derivative crosses the integral sign, and assuming \(p\to0\) fast enough at \(x=\pm\infty\) that every boundary term vanishes:

\[ \int f(x)\mu(x,t)f'(x)\text{-style terms} \;\longrightarrow\; \int f(x)\Big(-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}[\mu p] + \frac12\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}[\sigma^2 p]\Big)dx \]

Since the resulting identity \(\int f(x)\,\partial_t p\,dx = \int f(x) \big(-\partial_x[\mu p]+\tfrac12\partial_x^2[\sigma^2 p]\big)dx\) has to hold for every test function \(f\), the two integrands themselves must be equal — which is exactly the Fokker-Planck equation from step 1. Stated the way functional analysis states it: the operator on the right-hand side, \(\mathcal{L}^*p := -\partial_x[\mu p] + \tfrac12\partial_x^2[\sigma^2 p]\), is the formal adjoint of the generator \(\mathcal{L}\) — "adjoint" in exactly the integration- by-parts sense used to move it there. This is a genuinely deep structural fact (the forward equation and the generator are two faces of the same object), and Risken (1996), Ch. 4-5, works through the full rigorous version, including the technical conditions on \(p\) and its derivatives that make the boundary terms actually vanish. The derivation above carries the idea, not the full rigor.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Think of the generator as a rule for asking "given where a particle is right now, how does some quantity I care about change?" The Fokker-Planck operator is the same rule turned inside-out through some algebra (integration by parts) so it instead answers "given the whole cloud of particles, how does the cloud's shape change?" Same underlying physics, just asked from the particle's point of view versus the cloud's point of view.

3. Forward vs. backward — the single most important distinction in this lesson

Everything above evolves \(p(x,t)\) forward in time, starting from a fixed initial condition — typically \(p(x,t_0)=\delta(x-x_0)\), certainty about where the process starts, at the earliest time. There is a close relative that runs the other way. Define \(u(x,t) = \mathbb{E}\big[g(X_T)\,\big|\,X_t=x\big]\), the expected value of some terminal payoff function \(g\) applied to the process at a later, fixed time \(T\), given that the process is at \(x\) right now, at time \(t\). It satisfies the backward Kolmogorov equation:

\[ \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + \mu(x,t)\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + \frac12\sigma^2(x,t)\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} = 0, \qquad u(x,T) = g(x) \]
u(x,t)an expected future payoff, not a density — no probabilistic normalization requirement
u(x,T)=g(x)a TERMINAL condition fixed at the end of time, not an initial one

Notice this is literally \(\mathcal{L}u=0\) with a time derivative added — the generator \(\mathcal{L}\) itself, acting on \(u\) directly, with no adjoint and no integration by parts. That is the algebraic fingerprint of "backward": the un-adjointed generator, solved from a condition at the end of the time interval back to the start.

Forward (Fokker-Planck)

Unknown: a density \(p(x,t)\). Fixed data: an initial condition at the start, \(t_0\). Direction of solution: forward, increasing \(t\). Operator: the adjoint \(\mathcal{L}^*\). Question answered: "given where I start, what is the whole distribution of where I might end up?"

Backward (Kolmogorov backward)

Unknown: an expected value / payoff function \(u(x,t)\). Fixed data: a terminal condition at the end, \(T\). Direction of solution: backward, decreasing \(t\) from \(T\). Operator: the plain generator \(\mathcal{L}\), un-adjointed. Question answered: "given what I want to know at the end, what is it worth right now?"

black-scholes.html's no-arbitrage PDE, \(\partial_t V + rS\,\partial_S V + \tfrac12\sigma^2S^2\partial_S^2V - rV = 0\), solved backward from the terminal payoff \(V(S,T)=\max(S_T-K,0)\), is precisely a backward-Kolmogorov-type equation for the risk-neutral drift \(\mu(S,t)=rS\), with exactly one addition: the \(-rV\) term, which is a discounting (or "killing") term that has no counterpart in the plain backward equation above — it is what turns "the expected terminal payoff" into "the expected terminal payoff, discounted back to today's dollars," which is what an option's fair price actually needs to be. Strip that one term out and the Black-Scholes PDE is exactly \(\mathcal{L}u=0\) with \(u=V\), \(g(S)=\max (S-K,0)\), \(T\) fixed. This is not a loose analogy — it is the identical equation, plus one discounting term that a pricing (rather than a plain conditional-expectation) problem specifically needs.

The one-sentence rule that prevents the mix-up: if the question is "given where I start, where might I end up?" — a distribution over a future outcome — that's forward, Fokker-Planck. If the question is "given what I want to know at some fixed future time, what is it worth (or what do I expect) right now?" — a value computed backward from a known future — that's backward, Kolmogorov. Same underlying generator, opposite direction, different unknown function, different fixed condition.
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Forward is a weather forecast: you know today's conditions and want to know the range of things that might happen next. Backward is an insurance payout calculation: you know exactly what you'll owe on a fixed future date and want to know what that's worth today. Same kind of math machinery under the hood, but pointed in opposite directions and answering opposite questions — mixing them up gives you a confident, wrong answer instead of an obvious error.

Worked example

Verifying that GBM's own lognormal density solves its own Fokker-Planck equation

Predict first. After the change of variable \(y = \ln x\) below, the GBM equation turns into one with a constant drift. If the price drifts at \(\mu = 5\%\) a year with \(\sigma = 20\%\) volatility, what is that constant drift? Pick one, then check it against step 4.
  • 5% a year, the same as the price
  • 3% a year
  • 7% a year
  • 0%, because the noise cancels the drift

3% a year. Step 4 shows the drift becomes \(a = \mu - \sigma^2/2 = 0.05 - \tfrac12(0.20)^2 = 0.05 - 0.02 = 0.03\). It is the same volatility drag Itô's Lemma gives for \(\ln X_t\). Now try \(\sigma = 40\%\): the drag is 8%, and the log drift turns negative, at −3%.

Geometric Brownian motion, \(dX_t=\mu X_t\,dt+\sigma X_t\,dW_t\) (\(X_0=x_0>0\)) — the process behind black-scholes.html — has a well-known closed-form transition density, the lognormal:

\[ p(x,t) = \frac{1}{x\sigma\sqrt{2\pi t}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{\big(\ln x - m(t)\big)^2}{2\sigma^2 t}\right), \qquad m(t)=\ln x_0+\Big(\mu-\frac{\sigma^2}{2}\Big)t,\ \ x>0 \]

This is stated everywhere as a fact. Actually checking that it solves its own Fokker-Planck equation, \(\partial_tp = -\partial_x[\mu x\,p]+\tfrac12\partial_x^2[\sigma^2x^2p]\), is a genuine computation — done here rather than skipped.

1

Change variables to \(y=\ln x\)

Write \(p(x,t) = q(y,t)/x\) with \(y=\ln x\), where \(q(y,t) = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi v}}\exp\big(-(y-m)^2/2v\big)\) is an ordinary Gaussian density in \(y\), \(v=v(t)=\sigma^2t\). This is exactly the standard density-transformation (Jacobian) formula for \(y=\ln x\), \(dy/dx=1/x\) — not an approximation, an identity.

2

Differentiate p(x,t) in x, in terms of q's y-derivatives

Using the chain rule through \(y=\ln x\) (writing \(q_y,q_{yy}\) for \(\partial q/\partial y,\ \partial^2q/\partial y^2\)):

\[ \frac{\partial p}{\partial x} = \frac{q_y - q}{x^2}, \qquad \frac{\partial^2 p}{\partial x^2} = \frac{q_{yy}-3q_y+2q}{x^3} \]
3

Substitute into the GBM Fokker-Planck equation's right-hand side

Since \(xp=q\), the drift term simplifies directly: \(\mu xp = \mu q\), so \(-\partial_x[\mu xp]=-\mu q_y/x\). Since \(x^2p=xq\), the diffusion term simplifies to \(\sigma^2x^2p=\sigma^2xq\), and differentiating twice gives \(\tfrac12\partial_x^2[\sigma^2x^2p]=\sigma^2(q_y+q_{yy})/(2x)\). Adding them:

\[ -\partial_x[\mu xp]+\tfrac12\partial_x^2[\sigma^2x^2p] = \frac{1}{x}\left[-\Big(\mu-\frac{\sigma^2}{2}\Big)q_y + \frac{\sigma^2}{2}q_{yy}\right] \]
4

Compare against ∂p/∂t

Since \(y=\ln x\) doesn't depend on \(t\), \(\partial p/\partial t = q_t/x\) directly. So the GBM Fokker-Planck equation, divided through by the common factor \(1/x\), reduces exactly to:

\[ q_t = -\Big(\mu-\frac{\sigma^2}{2}\Big)q_y + \frac{\sigma^2}{2}q_{yy} \]

This is the Fokker-Planck equation for a process with constant drift \(a=\mu-\sigma^2/2\) and diffusion coefficient \(b=\sigma^2\) — and \(a=\mu-\sigma^2/2\) is exactly \(d(\ln X_t)\)'s drift, derived independently in ito-lemma.html by applying Itô's Lemma to \(f(x)=\ln x\). The substitution has turned "does the lognormal solve the GBM equation" into "does a Gaussian solve the constant- coefficient equation" — a cleaner, fully checkable question.

5

Verify the Gaussian solves the reduced equation, directly

With \(m(t)=m_0+at\), \(v(t)=v_0+bt\) (so \(m'=a\), \(v'=b\)), differentiate \(q(y,t)=(2\pi v)^{-1/2}\exp(-(y-m)^2/2v)\) via \(\ln q\):

\[ q_t = q\left[-\frac{b}{2v} + \frac{(y-m)a}{v} + \frac{(y-m)^2b}{2v^2}\right] \]

and separately, from \(q_y=-\tfrac{y-m}{v}q\):

\[ -aq_y+\frac{b}{2}q_{yy} = q\left[\frac{a(y-m)}{v} + \frac{b(y-m)^2}{2v^2} - \frac{b}{2v}\right] \]

The two bracketed expressions are identical, term for term — the \(a(y-m)/v\) pieces match, the \(b(y-m)^2/2v^2\) pieces match, and the \(-b/2v\) pieces match. \(q_t=-aq_y+\tfrac{b}{2}q_{yy}\) holds exactly, which — winding back through steps 1-4 — is precisely the statement that GBM's lognormal \(p(x,t)\) solves its own Fokker-Planck equation. Not asserted: computed.

Real-world trading scenario

Two PDE solvers on the same desk, running in opposite directions

A quant sits down with a colleague to explain why two numerical PDE solvers on the desk's shared library, both operating on what looks like "the same kind of equation" for the same underlying diffusion, produce completely different kinds of output. The first solver is inside the options-pricing engine: it takes a known payoff at expiration, \(\max(S_T-K,0)\), and marches the Black-Scholes PDE backward from \(T\) to today, producing a single number — today's fair value. The second solver is inside a risk system, projecting where a risk factor (say, a commodity price or an FX rate) might plausibly sit in six months: it takes today's known starting point as a delta-function initial condition and marches the Fokker-Planck equation forward from today to six months out, producing an entire projected distribution, not a single number.

The colleague's confusion is understandable — both solvers discretize a second-order parabolic PDE with the same \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\), on the same grid, using the same finite-difference machinery. But swapping one solver's direction convention into the other's code is not a harmless refactor: feeding the pricing engine's terminal-condition, march- backward logic a risk-projection problem (which has an initial condition and needs an adjoint operator, not the plain generator) would silently produce nonsense — a numerically stable computation of the wrong quantity, not a crash that would flag the bug. The one- sentence rule from the solid callout above — "given where I start, where might I end up" is forward; "given what I want at the end, what's it worth now" is backward — is exactly the check that catches this before it ships.

The forward-looking payoff: everything in this lesson is a fully deterministic PDE — no randomness survives once you're asking about the density \(p(x,t)\) rather than any one path \(X_t\). That's exactly the conceptual stepping-stone to stochastic PDEs: instead of a deterministic equation describing how a density evolves over one spatial dimension and time, a stochastic PDE describes a genuinely random field — itself buffeted by noise — evolving over space and time. The Fokker-Planck equation is the "easy" half of that story: a PDE, but with no noise term of its own. The harder half puts noise back into the PDE itself.
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Even though each individual particle in the cloud is bouncing around unpredictably, the cloud's overall shape evolves in a perfectly predictable, deterministic way — like knowing exactly how a wave of dye will spread through water, even though you can't predict any single dye molecule's path. That's the whole point being set up here: a stochastic PDE is what you get when you stop being so lucky, and the shape itself starts wiggling randomly too.

Section 03

How practitioners actually use this

The Fokker-Planck equation itself is rarely solved by hand outside of the handful of coefficient families (GBM, Ornstein-Uhlenbeck, CIR) that have known closed-form densities — for anything more exotic, it is solved numerically, or its role is played implicitly by Monte Carlo simulation instead (simulating many paths and building a histogram is, in effect, a Monte Carlo estimate of \(p(x,t)\), without ever writing the PDE down). Where the underlying idea genuinely surfaces:

📊

Projecting a risk factor's distribution

A risk system projecting "where might this rate/price plausibly be in N days" is, conceptually, evolving a density forward — whether it does so via a closed-form Fokker-Planck solution, a numerical PDE solve, or a Monte Carlo histogram standing in for the same thing.

🧭

Checking which direction a PDE solver actually runs

Before trusting or reusing a numerical PDE solver, the honest first question is whether it is set up as a forward (adjoint operator, initial condition) or backward (plain generator, terminal condition) problem — conflating the two produces a stable computation of the wrong quantity, not an obvious crash.

🔬

Diagnosing a physical/statistical-mechanics-flavored model

Fokker-Planck equations originate in statistical physics (Brownian particle diffusion), and Risken (1996) is written primarily for that audience — any model borrowing physics-style diffusion language (a "potential," a "stationary distribution") is very likely leaning on this exact equation under the hood.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where this gets misapplied

Mixing up forward and backward. This is by far the most common mistake with this equation, and it's easy to make because both equations share the same \(\mu\), the same \(\sigma\), and a visually similar PDE structure. Tie the question to the concrete direction, every time: "given where I start, where might I end up" is forward (Fokker-Planck); "given what payoff I want at the end, what's it worth now" is backward (Kolmogorov / Black-Scholes-style). If either question isn't the one actually being asked, the wrong equation is about to get solved.

Treating the Fokker-Planck equation as itself stochastic. It is not — \(p(x,t)\) is a completely deterministic PDE, even though the process \(X_t\) it describes is random. The randomness has already been fully absorbed into the shape of \(p\); there is no remaining random term left in the equation for \(p\) itself. Confusing this with a stochastic PDE (where the equation for the evolving object has an explicit noise term of its own) mixes up two genuinely different objects — see the forward-looking callout above.

Assuming a closed-form density always exists. GBM, Ornstein-Uhlenbeck, and CIR are special precisely because their Fokker-Planck equations happen to admit known closed-form solutions. Most SDEs with more general \(\mu(x,t)\), \(\sigma(x,t)\) do not — the equation is still perfectly well-posed and still governs the true density, but solving it requires numerical PDE methods or simulation, not a formula to look up.

Forgetting the well-posedness prerequisite. sde-existence-uniqueness.html's Lipschitz and growth conditions are not a separate, skippable concern here — if the SDE itself doesn't have a unique solution, there is no well-defined \(p(x,t)\) for the Fokker-Planck equation to even be about.

Scenario drill · an illustrative risk meeting

Risk wants a six-month distribution, and the pricer is right there

Your risk team asks for the full distribution of where a currency pair could sit in six months, under the same drift and volatility your option pricer uses. A colleague suggests reusing the pricer's PDE solver, which marches the Black-Scholes equation backward from a payoff at expiry. The deadline is tonight. What do you do?

  1. Reuse the pricer as it is, with the payoff set to the spot level at six months.
    The pricer solves a backward problem: a terminal condition and the plain generator. It returns today's value of one payoff, not a distribution. It will run cleanly and give a stable answer to the wrong question.
  2. Run the forward equation: start from a spike at today's spot and march the Fokker-Planck equation six months ahead.
    This is exactly the forward question: a known start, an unknown spread of outcomes. Same drift, same noise, but the adjoint operator and an initial condition, which is what a distribution needs.
  3. Simulate many paths from today's spot and build a histogram of where they end.
    A valid estimate of the same density, and often what risk systems actually do. It is noisier and slower to converge than a direct forward solve, so check that you ran enough paths.
  4. Read the distribution off the pricer's grid at today's date.
    The pricer's grid holds option values for each possible spot, not probabilities. Those numbers do not sum to one and are not a density of anything.

The lesson. This is an illustrative situation, not a dated event. The check that catches the mistake is the lesson's one-sentence rule: "given where I start, where might I end up" is forward; "given what I want at the end, what is it worth now" is backward. A backward solver used for a forward question does not crash. It quietly computes the wrong quantity.

Section 04

Key Takeaways

✓The Fokker-Planck equation governs how a transition density evolves forward in time. \(\partial_tp=-\partial_x[\mu p]+\tfrac12\partial_x^2[\sigma^2p]\) — drift shifts the density's location, diffusion spreads it out.
✓It is the formal adjoint of the generator that appears in Itô's Lemma. Taking expectations of Itô's Lemma and integrating by parts moves the derivatives off the test function and onto the density.
⚠Forward (Fokker-Planck) and backward (Kolmogorov / Black-Scholes) equations are mirror images, not the same equation. Forward: density, initial condition, adjoint operator. Backward: expected value, terminal condition, plain generator. Mixing them up produces a stable computation of the wrong quantity.
✓GBM's lognormal density was verified, by direct substitution, to solve its own Fokker-Planck equation. Reduced via \(y=\ln x\) to a constant-coefficient equation whose Gaussian solution was checked term by term.
✗Closed-form transition densities are the exception, not the rule. GBM, Ornstein-Uhlenbeck, and CIR admit known solutions; most SDEs with more general coefficients require numerical PDE solution or simulation instead — don't expect a formula to just be sitting there.
⚠The equation is deterministic — a stepping-stone to stochastic PDEs, not one itself. Stochastic PDEs put a genuine noise term into the evolution equation for the field itself, which the Fokker-Planck equation never has.
ℹThe forward equation is the calibration direction. Breeden & Litzenberger (1978) read the density from option prices; Dupire's 1994 forward equation turns a whole smile into a local-vol model, and one forward solve prices every strike and maturity.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. Write the Fokker-Planck equation for \(dX_t=\mu(X_t,t)dt+\sigma(X_t,t)dW_t\), and explain in one sentence each what the two terms on the right-hand side do.
\(\partial p/\partial t = -\partial_x[\mu(x,t)p(x,t)] + \tfrac12\partial_x^2[\sigma^2(x,t)p(x,t)]\). The first term (drift/advection) shifts the density's location, carrying probability mass along at the local drift rate, without changing its shape. The second term (diffusion) spreads the density out, the same structural role the Laplacian plays in the ordinary heat equation.
Q2. What operation turns the generator \(\mathcal{L}f=\mu f'+\tfrac12\sigma^2f''\) (which appears directly in Itô's Lemma) into the Fokker-Planck operator that acts on the density?
Taking the formal adjoint — in practice, integration by parts (twice, for the second-order term), which moves the derivatives off the test function f and onto μp and σ²p instead, picking up a sign flip each time a derivative crosses the integral sign. The Fokker-Planck operator \(\mathcal{L}^*p=-\partial_x[\mu p]+\tfrac12\partial_x^2[\sigma^2p]\) is exactly \(\mathcal{L}\)'s formal adjoint under that transfer.
Q3. A colleague asks: "given today's spot price, what's the fair value of an option paying off in six months?" versus "given today's spot price, what's the distribution of where it might be in six months?" — which of these is a forward problem and which is backward, and why?
The option-value question is backward: it starts from a known condition at a fixed future time (the terminal payoff) and needs a value computed backward to today — exactly the structure of the Kolmogorov backward equation (and, with an added discounting term, the Black-Scholes PDE). The distribution question is forward: it starts from a known condition today (certainty about the current spot price) and asks how that density evolves forward to the future time — exactly the structure of the Fokker-Planck equation.
Q4. The Black-Scholes PDE is \(\partial_tV+rS\partial_SV+\tfrac12\sigma^2S^2\partial_S^2V-rV=0\). Which piece of this equation is NOT part of the plain backward Kolmogorov equation, and what does it represent?
The \(-rV\) term. The plain backward Kolmogorov equation for \(u(x,t)=\mathbb{E}[g(X_T)|X_t=x]\) is just \(\partial_tu+\mu\partial_xu+\tfrac12\sigma^2\partial_x^2u=0\) — no extra term. The \(-rV\) in Black-Scholes is a discounting (or "killing") term, needed because an option's price is the expected terminal payoff discounted back to today's dollars, not simply the raw conditional expectation the plain backward equation computes.
Q5. In the GBM worked example, why does substituting \(y=\ln x\) simplify the verification, and what specific constant-coefficient equation does it reduce the problem to?
Because GBM's coefficients \(\mu x\) and \(\sigma x\) are level-proportional, the substitution \(p(x,t)=q(\ln x,t)/x\) causes the level-dependence to cancel algebraically (xp=q, x²p=xq), turning the variable-coefficient GBM Fokker-Planck equation into the constant-coefficient equation \(q_t=-(\mu-\sigma^2/2)q_y+\tfrac{\sigma^2}{2}q_{yy}\) for a process with constant drift \(a=\mu-\sigma^2/2\) — exactly the drift Itô's Lemma derives for \(d(\ln X_t)\) — and constant diffusion \(b=\sigma^2\). Verifying a Gaussian solves that constant-coefficient equation is then a direct, mechanical differentiation.
