When the Kalman Filter Runs Out of Road, Send in a Crowd

The Kalman filter lesson tracked a hidden hedge ratio with two running numbers — a best estimate and a variance — and that trick worked only because of a convenient coincidence: linear dynamics plus Gaussian noise happen to keep the posterior belief exactly Gaussian, forever. Break either assumption — a genuinely nonlinear system, or noise with a fat tail — and that coincidence disappears. This lesson is the direct sequel: it replaces the single confident estimate with a weighted swarm of thousands of simulated guesses, called a particle filter, that can track a belief no clean formula could ever capture.

Section 1

Why the closed form quietly disappears

The Kalman recursion isn't a general-purpose trick — it's a special case that happens to have an exact answer.

∑

Conjugacy is the whole reason it works

A Gaussian prior, combined with a Gaussian likelihood that's linearly related to the state, produces an exactly Gaussian posterior. That's the only reason "mean plus variance" is ever enough to summarize a belief.

∿

Nonlinearity breaks it

A squared term, a saturating function, a regime-dependent coefficient — feed a Gaussian through any of these and what comes out the other side is no longer Gaussian. There's generally no formula left to solve.

⚡

Fat-tailed or mixed noise breaks it too

"Mostly small nudges, occasionally a large jump" is a two-component mixture, not one bell curve. A linear Kalman filter has exactly one Gaussian process-noise term, Q, and no way to represent that shape honestly.

🔇

It fails quietly, not loudly

Forced onto a nonlinear or non-Gaussian problem, a Kalman filter doesn't crash — it silently returns a Gaussian approximation that can misjudge both the state and how confident to be about it.

The true posterior, once either assumption breaks, can be skewed, fat-tailed, or genuinely multimodal — two or more separate clusters of plausible states, not one bump. A single mean and variance cannot represent "probably here, but there's a real chance it's actually over there instead." Something else has to.
Section 2

Trade one confident guess for a crowd of guessers

The intuition

Recall the Kalman filter's boat-at-night analogy — one radar ping a minute, one running estimate. Now suppose the boat isn't drifting in a straight line at all: it's caught in a current that curves unpredictably, and once in a while it doesn't drift, it gets shoved hard by a wave. A single confident estimate has no way to represent "probably here, but there's a real chance it just got shoved somewhere else entirely."

So put a thousand guessers on the problem instead of one. Each holds their own candidate position. Every minute, each guesser moves their own candidate forward using their own understanding of how the boat drifts — including, for a few of them, "maybe a wave just hit." When the radar ping arrives, score every guesser by how consistent their candidate is with it. Guessers who turn out to be way off get quietly retired; guessers sitting where the data says the boat probably is get cloned. That crowd, re-scored every step, is a particle filter.

🎲

A particle is one concrete guess

Not a formula, not a distribution — one specific candidate value for the hidden state, exactly like one guesser's current position.

⚖

A weight is how much the data believes it

High weight means the observation is consistent with that guess; near-zero weight means the data has effectively ruled it out.

◈◈

Many guesses can be lopsided, or split

A pile of weighted guesses can smear toward one side, or form two separate clumps — shapes a single tidy mean-and-variance summary simply cannot draw.

Section 3

Same state-space idea, generalized

Nothing about "a hidden state that evolves and produces noisy observations" changed — only what f, h, and the noise are allowed to be.

The Kalman filter's special case

xᵩ = xᵩ₋₁ + wᵩ and yᵩ = Hᵩxᵩ + vᵩ — the transition and observation are both linear, and wᵩ, vᵩ are Gaussian. That combination is exactly what keeps the posterior Gaussian, forever.

The particle filter's general case

xᵩ = f(xᵩ₋₁, wᵩ) and yᵩ = h(xᵩ, vᵩ) — f and h can be any function, nonlinear included, and wᵩ, vᵩ can be drawn from any distribution at all, Gaussian or not.

Instead of tracking a mean and a variance, a particle filter represents the belief about the hidden state as a weighted pile of concrete candidate values — formally, a sum of weighted spikes (Dirac deltas) at each particle's location, standing in for a full continuous probability curve. More particles, with higher weight, concentrated in one region means that region is believed more strongly. Given enough particles, that pile can approximate any shape — skewed, multimodal, fat-tailed — not just the one symmetric bump a Gaussian is stuck with.

The price of that generality

A particle filter never solves an integral in closed form — it approximates it with samples instead. That means the answer is now a Monte Carlo approximation with its own sampling variance, not an exact number the way a closed-form Kalman estimate is.

Section 4

One cycle, four steps

Initialize once; then propagate, weight, and (sometimes) resample, every single time a new observation arrives.

1

Initialize

Draw N particles from the prior belief about the starting state, and give every one an equal weight, 1/N. Nobody has any reason to favor one guess over another yet.

2

Propagate

Push every particle forward through the — possibly nonlinear — state transition, drawing a fresh, independent noise sample for each one. This is "predict," generalized: no data used yet.

3

Weight

Once the new observation arrives, score each particle by the likelihood of seeing that observation given its own candidate state, then normalize so all weights sum to 1.

4

Resample

Check how lopsided the weights have become. If it's gotten bad, draw a fresh set of N particles proportional to weight and reset every weight back to 1/N — discrediting the weak guesses, cloning the strong ones.

Steps 2 and 3 repeat every time step. Step 4 is conditional — it only fires when the swarm actually needs it, which is exactly what the next few slides quantify.

Section 6

Degeneracy: the swarm collapsing to one opinion

Left unchecked, the weighting step has a well-known failure mode.

The diagnostic
N_eff
=
1 ÷ Σ(w_i²)
Ranges from 1 (total degeneracy — one particle carries essentially all the weight, the other N−1 are dead weight) up to N (perfectly healthy — every particle equally informative).
Equal weights

Four particles, each weighted 0.25: Σw² = 4×0.25² = 0.25, so N_eff = 1/0.25 = 4 — the maximum possible. Every particle is pulling its weight.

Lopsided weights

Same four particles, weighted 0.70, 0.10, 0.10, 0.10: Σw² = 0.49 + 0.03 = 0.52, so N_eff = 1/0.52 ≈ 1.92. There are still four particles — but the swarm is only behaving like about two.

Degeneracy is what motivates step 4 of the cycle in the first place: without resampling, one particle's weight tends to dominate almost completely after enough steps, and the population of N particles is effectively wasted on candidates that no longer matter. A common rule of thumb triggers resampling whenever N_eff drops below N/2.

Section 9

Resampling isn't a free fix

Cloning a credible particle isn't the same as generating new information about where the state actually is.

Resample too rarely
  • Weight keeps concentrating onto fewer and fewer particles
  • N_eff drifts toward 1 — particle degeneracy
  • Most of the particle budget is spent on candidates the data has already ruled out
Resample every single step
  • Cloning happens even when the swarm was already healthy
  • Repeated cloning collapses the swarm to near-duplicate copies of a shrinking handful of originals
  • This is particle impoverishment — diversity lost even though N_eff looked fine right before the resample
The fix for both failure modes is the same one already on the diagnostic slide: resample only when N_eff actually drops below a threshold, commonly N/2 — not unconditionally, and not never. Resampling is a real trade-off between fighting degeneracy and preserving diversity, not a correction with no cost.
Section 10

What a swarm can track that one bell curve can't

Picture a hedge ratio that usually drifts slowly — but around a scheduled rate decision, it sometimes re-prices in a single discontinuous jump instead.

A linear Kalman filter has exactly one Gaussian process-noise variance, Q — it can only ever produce one symmetric bump, which either overreacts to ordinary noise (Q set large to accommodate rare jumps) or underreacts to a genuine jump (Q set small for ordinary days). A particle filter can hold a cluster of particles tracking "ordinary drift continues" alongside a separate cluster tracking "the jump was real," simultaneously, and let the next observation's weighting decide which cluster the data actually supports — without ever having to commit to one branch in advance.

This is presented as an illustration of what the method is for, not a claim that this exact hedge-ratio scenario has been built, backtested, or shown to add anything over the existing linear approach.
Section 11

Where this shows up — and what's actually running here

🤖

Robot localization

A robot with a rough map and noisy sensors, genuinely uncertain which of several matching corridors it's in — "Monte Carlo Localization" is a direct particle-filter application.

📡

Radar/sonar tracking

A maneuvering target whose motion isn't well described by a straight line — the same nonlinear-dynamics problem this lesson generalizes the Kalman filter to handle.

🎥

Video object tracking

A target temporarily occluded and later reappearing is a genuinely multimodal situation — exactly the shape a single Gaussian estimate handles poorly.

📈

Stochastic volatility, jump-diffusion

Estimating a latent, continuously evolving volatility path, or fitting a model where price paths jump discontinuously — both genuinely nonlinear or non-Gaussian state-space problems.

Factual grounding — the full lesson's status line is now out of date

The full particle-filter.html lesson labels this a "CONCEPT — not built or tested anywhere in this repo," and its takeaways state plainly: "Not built, not prototyped, not validated in this repo." That was true when written, but it no longer is: js/particleFilter.js now implements a real bootstrap particle filter — 200 particles, systematic resampling, resampling triggered when N_eff falls below N/2 — used for live 5-minute regime classification (BULL/BEAR/RANGE/CHOP) across instruments, gated by FEATURE_FLAGS.PARTICLE_FILTER in js/config.js, which is currently set to true, and wired into js/main.js's live dashboard output. That is a genuinely different problem from the continuous hedge-ratio-tracking scenario this lesson illustrates — it tracks a discrete 4-state Markov regime, not a continuous nonlinear state — so the specific hedge-ratio scenario above remains hypothetical. But the core algorithm this lesson teaches — predict, weight, systematically resample, gated on N_eff — is not merely a concept in this repo; it is live, enabled-by-default code.

Section 12

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Why does the Kalman filter's closed form stop working once the state transition is nonlinear?

The exact-Gaussian-posterior result relies on linear transformations of Gaussians staying Gaussian. Break linearity and there's generally no closed form left for the posterior.
Question 2

What does resampling actually do to the particle weights?

Resampling reallocates the fixed particle budget toward the candidates the data currently supports — cloning high-weight particles, dropping low-weight ones — then starts the next cycle from equal weights again.
Question 3

Four particles with weights 0.70, 0.10, 0.10, 0.10 — what is N_eff, and what does it mean?

Σw² = 0.70² + 3×0.10² = 0.52, so N_eff = 1/0.52 ≈ 1.92 — a clear sign of degeneracy this diagnostic exists to catch.
Question 4

Why isn't resampling on every single step, regardless of N_eff, the automatically safest choice?

Cloning is not new information — it just duplicates already-credible guesses. Doing that when the swarm didn't need it (N_eff already close to N) pays the diversity cost for nothing.
Question 5

What kind of belief can a particle filter represent that a Kalman filter fundamentally cannot?

A Kalman filter's posterior is always one symmetric Gaussian bump. A particle filter's weighted swarm can hold two distinct clusters simultaneously — e.g. "ordinary drift" versus "the jump was real" — until new data favors one.
Section 13

What to carry forward

01

The Kalman filter's closed form only exists because linear dynamics plus Gaussian noise happen to keep the posterior exactly Gaussian — break either assumption and there's generally no formula left.

02

A particle filter replaces "mean and variance" with a weighted swarm of concrete candidate guesses — it needs no closed form, so it works for nonlinear dynamics and any noise shape.

03

The cycle is four steps: initialize from the prior, propagate through the dynamics, weight by the observation's likelihood, resample when the swarm gets lopsided.

04

N_eff = 1/Σ(w_i²) measures swarm health — from N (every particle informative) down to 1 (total degeneracy, one particle carries everything).

05

Resampling fixes degeneracy but costs diversity (particle impoverishment) — trigger it on N_eff crossing a threshold, not unconditionally and not never.

06

Unlike a Kalman filter, a particle swarm can hold two or more live, separate clusters of belief at once — a genuinely multimodal state a single Gaussian can never represent.

What this now lets you do

Recognize the exact moment a linear-Gaussian assumption is quietly failing — nonlinearity, fat tails, or a plausibly two-branch belief — and know the standard remedy by name. Read N_eff on sight and know whether a swarm needs resampling or is still healthy. Explain why resampling is a genuine trade-off, not a free fix, and why a particle filter is strictly more general than a Kalman filter but not automatically the better choice for a problem that's already linear-Gaussian.
