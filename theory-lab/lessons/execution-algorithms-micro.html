Execution Algorithms, Or How You Actually Place The Order

"Buy 40,000 shares" is a decision, not an order ticket. Dump it all in at once and you push the price against yourself, paying a large, avoidable impact cost. Spread it out, and you're exposed to a new risk — the price simply drifting away while you're still working the order. TWAP, VWAP, and Implementation Shortfall are three named, concrete answers to exactly that pacing problem, plus the honest scorecard for judging, after the fact, whichever one you picked.

The whole idea
🎧 Listen · the four-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

My portfolio manager wants forty thousand shares bought today. Why not just send one big order?

Because you'd push the price up against yourself all at once. So you spread it out. The question is how.

Evenly seems fair. Ten thousand shares in each quarter of the day.

That's TWAP, slicing by the clock. Simple, and anyone can check it. The flaw is that the market doesn't trade evenly. Volume is heavy at the open and the close and thin at lunchtime, so your midday slices are a big share of a thin market.

So follow the volume instead.

That's VWAP. If thirty-five percent of a normal day trades in the first bucket, you do thirty-five percent of your order there: fourteen thousand shares. Six thousand in each midday bucket, fourteen thousand at the close. You're never an outsized part of a quiet stretch.

And if today isn't a normal day?

Then you use POV. Forget the schedule and trade a fixed share of whatever volume actually happens, say ten percent. Busy market, you speed up. Quiet market, you slow down. The catch is you don't know when you'll finish.

How do I know afterwards if I did well?

Implementation Shortfall. Say the decision was made at fifty dollars. By the time you started, the price was fifty dollars and five cents. You filled nine thousand shares at about fifty dollars thirteen, and a thousand never filled before the close at fifty dollars thirty.

So what did that cost?

Four hundred and fifty dollars of delay, the drift before you started. Seven hundred and fifty of impact, your own footprint. Three hundred of opportunity cost on the shares you never got. Fifteen hundred dollars in all, thirty basis points, and each piece points at a different fix.

Has a schedule ever gone badly wrong?

The Flash Crash, sixth of May, twenty ten. A fund hedging with seventy-five thousand E-mini futures used a POV rule: nine percent of the last minute's volume, with no limit on price or time.

That sounds patient.

It wasn't. Fast traders passed the contracts back and forth, so volume soared, so the algorithm sold faster. The whole order went in about twenty minutes. A similar sale before had taken more than five hours.

So the volume it was following was partly its own selling.

Exactly. Volume isn't liquidity. Pick the schedule for your urgency and your footprint, give it a price limit, and score it honestly with the shortfall afterwards.

Section 1

A decision isn't an order ticket

The previous lesson, Market Impact Models & Almgren-Chriss, proves a large order should be sliced over time. This lesson is what a desk actually types into the order-management system to make that happen.

📉

Builds directly on Almgren-Chriss

Every schedule below is a practical answer to the same impact-vs-time tradeoff that lesson solves for theoretically: Δp = λQ says size against liquidity moves price, so pacing matters.

🕰️

A volume clock, not a wall clock

Real trading volume is heaviest at the open and close, thinnest at midday. A schedule that ignores this shape mis-times its own liquidity.

🎯

The arrival price is the real benchmark

The price at the exact moment the decision was made pins the "true" cost — everything after that is drift, footprint, or the cost of never finishing.

🧭

The right choice depends on the goal

Minimizing benchmark-tracking error, minimizing total cost, and minimizing timing risk are three different goals that pull toward three different schedules.

Three names get typed into an order-management system constantly: TWAP, VWAP, and Implementation Shortfall (often just called "arrival price"). The first two are schedules — rules for pacing the same order over time. The third is not a schedule at all — it's the scorecard used to judge any of them, including itself, after the fact.
Section 2

TWAP — split it evenly, by the clock

Time-Weighted Average Price: divide the order into K equal time intervals and trade an identical fraction in each.

The rule

Simple, fully predictable, and easy for a compliance reviewer to verify with nothing more than a calculator. Its flaw is exactly what it ignores: trading volume itself is never spread evenly through the day, so TWAP over-trades the thin midday lull and under-trades the heaviest, most liquid windows.

Section 3

VWAP — matching the market's own shape

Volume-Weighted Average Price fixes TWAP's blind spot: x_k = X · w_k, where w_k is the historical share of a typical day's volume that trades in interval k, and all the w_k sum to exactly 1 — the whole order.

A typical U-shaped intraday volume curve
Blue bars are the historical volume curve — and, since VWAP trades in direct proportion to it, they're also the VWAP order schedule. The dashed amber line is where TWAP sits regardless: a flat 25% in every bucket, ignoring the shape entirely.

Trading in proportion to w_k means the order participates at roughly the same rate as the rest of the market all day — heavier when the market is naturally heavier, lighter when it's naturally lighter. By the same impact-scales-with-size logic as before, that tends to produce less impact per share than TWAP's flat schedule, because the order is never forced to represent an outsized fraction of a thin period's volume.

Section 6

Implementation Shortfall — the arrival-price scorecard

Perold's framework (1988) is the gap between a "paper" world — the whole order fills instantly, free, at the decision-moment price — and the real world, where execution takes time, moves the price, and sometimes doesn't finish at all.

How it adds up
Delay
+
Impact
+
Opportunity
=
Total Implementation Shortfall
Nothing is double-counted: delay and impact are measured only on the shares that actually filled; opportunity cost is measured only on the shares that didn't, valued at the original decision price.
⏳

Delay — the cost of hesitation

(price when trading started − decision price) × shares filled. Drift before your own trading even began, for reasons that have nothing to do with you.

🌊

Impact — the cost of your own footprint

(average execution price − price when trading started) × shares filled. This literally is the Almgren-Chriss cost, now measured after the fact.

🕳️

Opportunity — the cost of giving up

(cancel/close price − decision price) × shares unfilled. What the unfinished part of the decision "should have" cost.

Trading more aggressively up front — exactly what the earlier Almgren-Chriss lesson's optimal trajectory does under higher risk-aversion — exists specifically to shrink the gap between the arrival price and the realized execution price: less exposure to drift while the order is still open and to opportunity cost, at the cost of more market impact (delay cost, incurred before trading starts, is untouched).

Section 8

Which one you pick depends on what you're optimizing

📏

Minimize benchmark-tracking error

If you'll be judged against VWAP, trade VWAP. Matching a specific benchmark's own schedule is the only reliable way to land close to it — an aggressive front-loaded schedule can badly miss a VWAP benchmark even with a great average price.

💰

Minimize total expected cost

A large order in a thin name favors patient, VWAP-style pacing to avoid a self-inflicted impact cost. A small order in a deep, liquid name may cost almost nothing to finish immediately.

⏱️

Minimize timing risk

High conviction, or information that could go stale, favors finishing fast — the high-risk-aversion end of the Almgren-Chriss trajectory, in practice a front-loaded, Implementation-Shortfall-style schedule.

Patient — VWAP style
  • Pre-committed to the historical volume shape
  • Lower footprint per unit of time
  • More exposure to the price simply drifting away
Aggressive — Implementation Shortfall style
  • Front-loaded, trades most of the order early
  • Higher footprint per unit of time (more impact)
  • Less exposure to drift before the arrival price can move
Section 9

Where this reasoning goes wrong — and what actually runs here

⚠️

"VWAP" isn't a synonym for "good execution"

Matching the VWAP benchmark only means you traded in proportion to how the whole market traded. If the price trended against you all session, a perfectly-paced VWAP order still pays that trend in full.

📜

History isn't a guarantee

VWAP's weights come from historical volume, not today's. An unscheduled news event can concentrate real volume somewhere the historical profile never expected.

🔒

The decision price can't move after the fact

Implementation Shortfall's honesty depends on fixing the decision price before anything about the execution's outcome is known. Redefining it afterward turns an honest scorecard into flattery.

Section 10 · It actually happened

6 May 2010: nine percent of the volume, at any price

It actually happened

A mutual fund complex hedged by selling 75,000 E-mini contracts (about $4.1 billion) through an algorithm set to sell 9% of the previous minute's volume, "without regard to price or time": POV with p = 0.09 and no limits. A similar order before had taken more than five hours. This time intermediaries bought, then passed contracts among themselves (27,000+ in 14 seconds for a net change of about 200), volume soared, and the algorithm sold faster. It finished in about 20 minutes, as E-mini buy depth fell below 1% of the morning's.

Section 11

Where the textbook breaks, and the desk's verdict

Where the textbook version breaks

POV treats observed volume as liquidity you can hide inside. That assumes the volume is independent of your order. In May 2010 much of it was the algorithm's own selling, recycled, so the rule meant to slow down in thin markets sped up. Volume soared while depth collapsed. This is why practitioners attach a price limit, and often a participation cap, to a POV order.

Desk test: held up execution-gate: no schedule removes the spread. Slicing manages impact; this desk sends single small orders where the spread is most of the cost. Above a spread/ATR of 0.15, zero of 30 strategy × timeframe cells were profitable. Cost outruns edge as the timeframe speeds up.
Section 12

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

TWAP splits an order into equal-sized clips across equal time intervals. What is its main blind spot?

Real intraday volume is U-shaped — heavy at the open and close, thin at midday. TWAP's flat schedule over-trades the thin periods and under-trades the heavy ones relative to natural liquidity.
Question 2

In x_k = X · w_k, what must the weights w_k sum to across all K intervals?

Each w_k is the historical fraction of a day's volume in interval k. Summed across all intervals they must total 1, so x_k = X·w_k allocates the entire order with nothing left over and nothing double-counted.
Question 3

A 20,000-share order has decision price $100.00, starts trading at $100.02, and fills completely at an average $100.10 (nothing unfilled). What is the total Implementation Shortfall?

Delay = (100.02 − 100.00) × 20,000 = $400. Impact = (100.10 − 100.02) × 20,000 = $1,600. Opportunity = $0 (fully filled). Total = $2,000 — matching the direct check (100.10 − 100.00) × 20,000 = $2,000.
Question 4

Front-loading a schedule (trading more early) is the practical analogue of which end of the Almgren-Chriss risk-aversion spectrum?

Higher risk-aversion in Almgren-Chriss means finishing faster to reduce exposure to price uncertainty over time, accepting a higher expected impact cost in exchange — exactly what a front-loaded, Implementation-Shortfall-style schedule does in practice.
Your call
Scenario drill · 6 May 2010

A $4.1 billion hedge into a falling market

It is the afternoon of 6 May 2010. US markets are already falling on European debt worries. You execute for a large fund complex that has decided to hedge an equity position by selling 75,000 E-mini S&P 500 futures, about \$4.1 billion. How do you work the order?

  1. Send it as one market order and get it done.
    This is maximum footprint. A \$4.1 billion sale in one go walks straight through the book in a nervous market, and you pay the whole impact cost at once. It is the problem every schedule in this lesson exists to avoid.
  2. POV at 9% of the previous minute's volume, with no price or time limit.
    It sounds patient, but it is only as patient as the market's volume. If intermediaries start passing your contracts back and forth, the volume you are following is partly your own selling, and the rule speeds up exactly when depth is vanishing.
  3. Work it patiently over hours, taking price, time and volume into account: a participation cap and a price limit, with a person watching.
    On the urgency-versus-impact chart this trade sits in high impact concern, where a patient, footprint-minimizing schedule belongs. The price limit and cap stop the order from feeding on its own volume. The same trader had sold a similar order this way before.
  4. A VWAP schedule over the rest of the day, following the usual volume curve.
    Patient and footprint-aware, which is the right instinct. But a pre-committed schedule with no price limit still sells into any air pocket, and today's volume is unlikely to look like a normal day's.

What happened. According to the joint SEC/CFTC staff report, the order went to an algorithm set to sell 9% of the previous minute's volume "without regard to price or time." High-frequency traders and other intermediaries bought the first contracts and then traded them among themselves: more than 27,000 contracts in 14 seconds for a net change of about 200. The rising volume made the algorithm sell faster, and the 75,000 contracts went through in about 20 minutes. A similar sale before, worked with a mix of manual and automated execution, had taken more than five hours. E-mini buy-side depth fell to less than 1% of its morning level, the contract fell more than 5% in about four and a half minutes, and an automatic five-second pause let buyers come back.

Section 13

What to carry forward

01

TWAP splits an order evenly across time — simple, predictable, and blind to where the market's own liquidity actually is.

02

VWAP schedules proportional to the historical U-shaped intraday volume curve, so the order participates in proportion to how much everyone else is trading.

03

Implementation Shortfall benchmarks against the arrival price — the moment the decision was made — and splits the gap into delay, impact, and opportunity cost.

04

Front-loading a schedule is the practical, named version of Almgren-Chriss's high-risk-aversion optimal trajectory: trade more upfront to shrink exposure to price drift, at the cost of more impact.

05

Algorithm choice depends on the goal — tracking a benchmark, minimizing total cost, and minimizing timing risk each point toward a different schedule.

06

Matching a benchmark isn't the same as a good price — a perfectly-paced VWAP order still pays in full for a trend that ran through the whole session.

07

Volume isn't liquidity. In the 2010 Flash Crash a POV order with no price limit sped up as recycled volume rose: 75,000 contracts in about 20 minutes. And no schedule removes the spread: above spread/ATR 0.15, 0 of 30 cells were profitable here (execution-gate, Validated).

What this now lets you do

Read an order-management ticket's algorithm choice — TWAP, VWAP, or an arrival-price/Implementation-Shortfall style — as a real, named tradeoff instead of an arbitrary setting. Connect a front-loaded schedule straight back to the Almgren-Chriss risk-aversion parameter from the previous lesson. Decompose any execution's real cost into delay, impact, and opportunity instead of trusting one blended average-price number.
