Lesson 26 · Advanced Stochastic Calculus & Derivatives

Itô's Lemma, the Calculus Brownian Motion Actually Obeys

The Black-Scholes formula contains a term, σ²/2, hiding inside d₁ and d₂, that the Black-Scholes lesson simply asserts without deriving. It isn't a risk premium or a calibration fudge — it's the mechanical output of applying the chain rule correctly to a random process, and ordinary calculus's chain rule is quietly built on an assumption that a random process breaks. This deck derives exactly where that σ²/2 comes from, in fewer steps than the full lesson but with more to click on.

The whole idea
🎧 Listen · the five-minute version

A trader and a quant talk the lesson through. No symbols, just the idea and one worked number, so it works on a walk or a commute.

The Black-Scholes formula has a "sigma squared over two" inside it. Where does that come from? It looks like a fudge.

It isn't. It's what the chain rule gives you when you apply it properly to a random price. That proper version is Itô's lemma.

What's wrong with the ordinary chain rule?

It quietly drops a term. When something moves smoothly, its change over a tiny step is about the size of the step. Square it and it becomes tiny. If the step is one hundredth, the square is one ten-thousandth. So ordinary calculus throws it away.

And a price isn't smooth.

Not even close. Zoom in on a random path and it never straightens out. Its move over a tiny step is about the square root of the step. Square that and you get the step itself: one hundredth, not one ten-thousandth. That's the same size as the drift, so you can't throw it away.

That's the whole trick?

That's the whole trick. The squared random wiggles add up to exactly the time that has passed. Keep that one term and you have Itô's lemma: the ordinary chain rule, plus half the second derivative times the variance.

Now apply it to a price.

Take the log of a price that grows with some drift and volatility. The second derivative of a log is negative, so the correction is negative. The log price grows at the drift minus half the variance. That's your sigma squared over two.

Give me a number.

Use the Black-Scholes example: spot and strike both a hundred, rates two percent, volatility twenty percent, one year. Half the variance is half of point oh four, which is two percent. It exactly cancels the two percent rate, so the log price has zero drift and d two is zero. Add volatility times the square root of time, point two, and d one is point two.

Is that cancelling a coincidence?

Of those round numbers, yes. The correction itself is always there.

Where else does it show up?

Wherever you compound. Up ten percent and then down ten percent leaves you at ninety-nine, not a hundred. That's volatility drag, the same term, and it's why overbetting hurts in Kelly sizing.

How did it reach trading?

Robert Merton brought Itô's calculus into finance from 1969. By 1973 it was the step that turned a continuously rebalanced hedge into an option price, and Black and Scholes published their formula that year, the year the Chicago Board Options Exchange opened.

And on a desk today?

Every Greek comes from it. Delta, gamma and theta are the terms of the lemma. Earning theta while paying for gamma, or the reverse, is that half sigma squared, read as money.

One line?

Randomness has a texture that never smooths out, so the chain rule gains one extra term, and that term is the sigma squared over two in every option price.

Section 2

A coastline that never gets straighter

Zoom in far enough on any smooth curve and it looks like a straight line — that's the entire justification for ordinary calculus. Zoom in on a Brownian-motion path and it never straightens. Compare the two zoom levels below.

Try it

Same pair, replotted 256× deeper. The blue curve is now a straight line, exactly as calculus assumes. The red path is unchanged — it never resolves, at any zoom, because its quadratic variation over any interval is nonzero however small the interval.
The assumption hiding inside the chain rule

A smooth function's own change Δx over a short time Δt is itself order Δt — so (Δx)², the term the chain rule quietly drops, is order Δt². As Δt → 0, Δt² shrinks far faster than Δt (Δt=0.01 makes Δt²=0.0001 — a hundred times smaller), so that term vanishes and only the first-order term survives. That's the entire chain rule. It holds for every smooth function calculus normally meets. Brownian motion is about to break it.

Section 3

The one fact: (dW)² = dt

A Brownian increment isn't the same size as a smooth function's — and that one difference is the entire reason ordinary calculus fails here.

Smooth function

Its increment Δx over a step Δt is itself order Δt. Square it: (Δx)² is order Δt² — negligible next to Δt as Δt → 0. Quadratic variation over any interval is exactly zero.

Brownian motion

Its increment ΔW has variance Δt, so it's typically size √Δt — not Δt. Square it: (ΔW)² is order Δt, the same order as the first term. Not negligible.

Why the aggregate, not any one draw
Σ (Wₜᵢ₊₁ − Wₜᵢ)²
→
T, exactly
Partition [0,T] into n tiny steps. Each squared increment has expectation Δt, and there are n = T/Δt of them, so the sum's expectation is T for every partition — and as the partition is refined, the sum converges to the fixed number T, with no randomness left. This is Brownian motion's quadratic variation, written as shorthand: (dWₜ)² = dt.
What this identity is NOT saying

It is not a claim that any single random draw of dWₜ, squared, literally equals the number dt — dWₜ stays random, mean zero, scattered on both sides of zero. It's a statement about the sum over many increments: the randomness of individual terms cancels out in the limit, leaving a fixed accumulation rate. Reading it as a single-draw fact is the single most common way this identity gets misunderstood.

Section 4

Redo the Taylor expansion, honestly

Same second-order Taylor expansion as before, df = f′dX + ½f″(dX)² — but now (dX)² doesn't vanish. Expand it for dXₜ = μdt + σdWₜ: the (dt)² and dt·dWₜ cross terms are still negligible, but σ²(dWₜ)² becomes σ²dt — order dt, not negligible. Substituting that back in produces Itô's Lemma.

Exactly what the plain chain rule would have produced. Nothing new here.

This is the whole lesson. It's the second-order Taylor term, evaluated with (Δx)² → σ²dt instead of → 0. No new derivative, no new function — just one substitution ordinary calculus never needs.

Also exactly what ordinary calculus would have produced. The randomness itself doesn't change; only the drift picks up the extra term.

There is no additional machinery beyond one substitution. Itô's Lemma is the Taylor expansion everyone already knows, applied to a random process, plus exactly one term ordinary calculus is entitled to throw away and stochastic calculus is not.
Section 5

Ordinary chain rule vs. Itô's Lemma

Side by side, the difference is exactly one extra term.

Ordinary chain rule

d/dt f(x(t)) = f′(x(t)) · dx/dt

  • One term — first-order only
  • Valid because a smooth x(t)'s own increment is order Δt
  • The second-order Taylor term is order Δt² and vanishes
Itô's Lemma

df = (f′μ + ½f″σ²)dt + f′σ dWₜ

  • Two dt-order terms — drift and the ½f″σ² correction
  • Needed because dXₜ's own increment is order √dt
  • The second-order Taylor term is order dt and survives

Every other piece of the derivation — the function f, its derivatives f′ and f″, the drift μ, the volatility σ — is identical between the two rules. The entire apparent strangeness of "stochastic calculus" reduces to this one extra surviving term.

Section 6

The correction's sign depends on f″

Itô's Lemma applies to any twice-differentiable f — not just the log. The sign of the correction always matches the sign of f″. Pick a function.

Section 7

Applying it to ln S — the lognormal SDE

Let Xₜ = Sₜ, following GBM dSₜ = μSₜdt + σSₜdWₜ, and apply Itô's Lemma with f(x) = ln x, f′(x) = 1/x, f″(x) = −1/x².

Every S cancels
(1/S)·μS + ½·(−1/S²)·(σS)²
The 1/S cancels against μS and against S² from squaring the diffusion. What's left is pure numbers — no S at all.
d(ln Sₜ)
=
(μ − σ²/2) dt

σ dWₜ
The drift of ln Sₜ is not μ — the price's own drift — it's μ − σ²/2. This is not a risk premium or a fudge; it's the direct, mechanical output of f″(x) = −1/x² being negative combined with (dSₜ)² = σ²dt being positive.

Section 9

Where this gets misread

Ø

σ²/2 shrinks the log's drift, not the price's mean

E[Sₜ] under GBM is completely unaffected by σ — what shrinks is the drift of ln S (the typical/median path). The gap exists because E[eˣ] ≠ eᴱ⁽ˣ⁾ (Jensen's inequality, since eˣ is convex), not because volatility destroys expected value.

±

The correction isn't always negative

It's specific to f″ < 0, which ln happens to be. A convex f (like S²) gets a positive correction instead — check the sign of f″ rather than assuming a downward σ²/2 adjustment by default.

⧉

Two correlated processes need a cross term

This single-variable formula covers a function of one Itô process. A basket, spread, or ratio of two correlated underlyings needs the multivariate (Itô-Doeblin) version, adding a covariance term ρσ₁σ₂ — using the plain formula silently drops correlation risk.

∑

(dW)² = dt is about the sum, never one draw

Any single dWₜ stays random. The identity describes what happens after summing many squared increments across a partition — the randomness cancels only in that aggregate limit.

Section 10

One correction term, three appearances

📐

Every exotic option's pricing PDE

Barriers, Asians, lookbacks — each is derived the same way as Black-Scholes: apply Itô's Lemma to the payoff's value function, build the hedge, set its return to r.

🔗

Multivariate extensions

Basket and spread options need the multivariate form, adding a covariance cross-term ρσ₁σ₂ alongside each asset's own σ²/2.

📊

Convexity adjustments

Futures-vs-forward convexity, CMS convexity, XVA — anywhere a desk needs the expected value of a nonlinear function of a random rate, Itô's Lemma (via Feynman-Kac) makes it rigorous.

The identical σ²/2 term already appears twice elsewhere in this curriculum, informally: as "volatility drag" in the Kelly Criterion lesson, and in the Returns & Compounding primer's log-return approximation. Both use a discrete-time version of the same second-order Taylor expansion this lesson makes rigorous in continuous time. Three parts of this curriculum independently hit the same term because all three are really asking one question: what happens to a growth rate when you take the log of something random?
Section 11

Check yourself

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Why does the ordinary chain rule drop the second-order Taylor term, but Itô's Lemma can't?

A smooth function's own change over Δt is order Δt, so squaring it gives order Δt² — negligible in the limit. Brownian motion's increment has variance Δt, so it's typically size √Δt, making its square order Δt — the same order as the surviving first term.
Question 2

Under GBM dSₜ = μSₜdt + σSₜdWₜ, what is d(ln Sₜ)?

Applying Itô's Lemma with f(x)=ln x, f′(x)=1/x, f″(x)=−1/x² makes every S cancel, leaving a drift of μ − σ²/2 — strictly below the price's own drift μ, because f″ is negative.
Question 3

Does the σ²/2 correction mean E[Sₜ] is smaller than it would be with zero volatility?

This is the volatility-drag confusion: σ²/2 shrinks the drift of the log (the typical/median path), not the arithmetic mean of the price itself. The gap between the two is Jensen's inequality, since eˣ is convex.
Question 4

For f(S) = S² instead of ln S, is the Itô correction term positive or negative?

The sign of the Itô correction always matches the sign of f″. For f(S)=S², f″(S)=2 is positive, so the correction adds to the drift instead of subtracting — the opposite of the ln S case, because S² is convex where ln S is concave.
Your call
Scenario drill · an illustrative forecast

An 8% expected return at 40% volatility

A colleague models a stock as geometric Brownian motion with an expected return \(\mu = 8\%\) a year and volatility \(\sigma = 40\%\). Their slide says "the typical investor compounds at 8% a year". You have to sign off on the slide. What do you tell them?

  1. Sign it off. The expected return is 8%, so that is the typical compound growth.
    8% is the drift of the price, an average across paths. The typical path compounds at the drift of \(\ln S\), which is lower.
  2. Typical compound growth is \(\mu - \sigma^2/2\), which is 8% − 8% = 0%: the median investor ends roughly where they started, and about half of paths end lower.
    Itô's Lemma applied to \(\ln S\) gives drift \(\mu - \sigma^2/2\). With \(\sigma = 40\%\), the correction is 0.08 and wipes out the whole 8%. Under this model the median future price equals today's price.
  3. Knock a little off for volatility drag, say 1%, and call it 7%.
    Right direction, wrong size. The drag is not a small haircut to guess at: it is exactly \(\sigma^2/2\), and at 40% volatility that is 8 points.
  4. Leave it. The \(\sigma^2/2\) term only matters for pricing options.
    It is the same term as volatility drag in compounding and in Kelly sizing. It matters any time you take the log of something random.

The arithmetic. This is an illustrative case, not a historical one. Under geometric Brownian motion with \(\mu = 8\%\) and \(\sigma = 40\%\), the log price drifts at 0.08 minus half of 0.40 squared, which is 0. The mean outcome still grows at 8%, carried by a few very large winners, while the median path goes nowhere. Same model, two different "typical" numbers, and Itô's correction is the gap between them.

Section 12

What to carry forward

01

The ordinary chain rule drops a term that isn't negligible for Brownian motion — a smooth increment is order Δt, a Brownian increment is order √Δt, so its square doesn't vanish.

02

(dWₜ)² = dt is a quadratic-variation statement about the sum of many squared increments, not a claim about any single random draw.

03

Itô's Lemma is the same Taylor expansion as ordinary calculus, plus one extra term: df = (f′μ + ½f″σ²)dt + f′σ dWₜ.

04

Applying it to ln Sₜ under GBM derives d(ln Sₜ) = (μ − σ²/2)dt + σ dWₜ — the mechanical, non-arbitrary source of Black-Scholes's σ²/2.

05

Swapping μ for r produces exactly Black-Scholes's d₂, and shifting by σ√T produces d₁ — the same code path is live today in this repo's own options-Greeks calculator.

06

The correction's sign follows f″, not the log specifically — and it needs a covariance cross-term the moment two correlated processes are involved.

07

How it reached trading: Merton (1969–73) and Black & Scholes (1973) — the step that turns a delta hedge into an option price, and the source of every Greek.

What this now lets you do

Read the σ²/2 inside any option-pricing formula as a derived fact, not an asserted one — and know exactly which second-order term to add whenever you apply the chain rule to a random process, whether that's deriving a new exotic option's PDE, checking a convexity adjustment, or explaining why compounded growth sits below the arithmetic average return.
