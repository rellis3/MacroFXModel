Lesson 25 · Applied Statistics & Validation

One Sample, Many Plausible Stories

A backtest hands you exactly one Sharpe ratio, computed from exactly one run of history. Bootstrapping and Monte Carlo simulation are the two standard tools for asking how much that one number would have wobbled if history had unfolded even slightly differently — without needing a second, independent dataset. Both are genuinely useful. Neither one, on its own, tells you whether the signal that produced that number will work on data it has never seen.

Section 2

One number, no formula for its own uncertainty

A Sharpe ratio or a max drawdown has no clean, off-the-shelf formula for how much it would vary across different samples.

📉

One backtest, one number

The reported Sharpe ratio, mean return, or max drawdown is a single point estimate — computed from the one historical sample that actually happened.

❓

The honest question

If a few trades had landed differently, or the same market moves had arrived in a different order, how much would that number have moved?

🧮

The classical answer breaks down

A CLT-based standard error usually assumes normality — and there's rarely a clean closed-form formula for the sampling distribution of a Sharpe ratio or a max drawdown on a short, lumpy, non-normal trade sequence.

🎲

No second dataset exists

You can't collect a second, independent history of the same strategy to see how the number varies. Whatever answers this has to be built from the one sample you already have.

This is the exact gap bootstrapping and Monte Carlo simulation each fill, in two different ways — one resamples the real data you have, the other simulates from a model fitted to it. Both manufacture "many possible outcomes" out of a single dataset.
Section 3

The bootstrap: reach into your own bag of trades, over and over

Efron's 1979 idea — treat your one sample as a stand-in for the true, unknown distribution it came from.

Try it
F̂ₙ
=
put 1/n on each of your n observed values
You don't know the true distribution F your data came from. The bootstrap's move is to stop trying — treat the empirical distribution of the sample you already have as its stand-in.
x₁*, x₂*, …, xₙ*
=
n draws WITH replacement from your n originals
Each new resample is the same size as the original, but drawn with replacement — so some original values show up twice or more, and others get left out entirely. That's what makes it a genuinely different sample, not a reshuffled copy.
T*₁, T*₂, …, T*_B
=
the statistic recomputed on each resample
Compute your statistic T (mean, Sharpe, max drawdown) on each resample, and repeat the whole draw-and-compute step B times — typically 1,000 to 10,000+. Those B numbers are the bootstrap's answer: an approximate sampling distribution for T, built entirely from data you already had.
From that collection of replicates you get two standard outputs: the bootstrap standard error (the plain standard deviation of the replicates), and a 95% percentile confidence interval (the 2.5th and 97.5th percentile of the sorted replicates) — read straight off, no formula needed.

Notice what this does and doesn't require: only the original n observations and a rule for computing T. It works identically whether T is a simple mean with a known formula, or a Sharpe ratio and a max drawdown with no clean closed-form sampling distribution at all — which is the entire reason the bootstrap became one of the most widely used tools in applied statistics after 1979.

Section 6

Time series break the "independent draws" assumption

The plain bootstrap above assumes each observation is independent. Financial returns routinely aren't.

Why the plain bootstrap fails here

Picking x₇* has no effect on the chance of picking x₁₂* next — that's the plain bootstrap's built-in assumption. Volatility clustering, trending runs, and mean-reverting bounces mean adjacent real observations carry genuine serial correlation. Shuffling a return series into independent single-day draws erases that structure, understating how much a real, contiguous run of bad days can compound — the resulting confidence interval looks tighter and more confident than it should.

Plain (i.i.d.) bootstrap
  • Resamples single observations, independently
  • Shuffles away volatility clustering and serial correlation
  • Confidence interval ends up falsely narrow — quietly overconfident
  • Fine for genuinely independent data; wrong for most raw return series
Block bootstrap (Künsch, 1989)
  • Resamples contiguous blocks of l consecutive observations
  • Whatever short-term dependence exists inside a block survives intact
  • A related refinement (Politis & Romano, 1994) draws block lengths at random instead of fixing one l
  • Block length has to be chosen: too short leaks correlation at boundaries, too long leaves too few distinct blocks

Concretely: fix a block length l (say, 10 trading days), form every overlapping block of that length from the series, draw enough of those blocks with replacement to cover n observations, and concatenate them into one resampled series. Because each block is a real, intact, consecutive run lifted straight out of history, the day-to-day correlation the plain bootstrap would have shuffled away survives inside the resample.

Section 7

Monte Carlo: don't resample the data, simulate from a model of it

A different move entirely — fit a process, then generate brand-new synthetic paths from it.

rₜ⁽ˢⁱᵐ⁾
=
μ̂ + σ̂ₜ · zₜ,  zₜ ~ N(0,1)

Instead of reaching into a bag of real observations, fit a parametric model to the data — a GARCH(1,1) process for volatility, geometric Brownian motion for a price path — with parameters μ̂ and σ̂ estimated from your sample. Then run that model forward, drawing a fresh random innovation zₜ at every step, to generate an entirely synthetic path. Do this independently many times and you get M synthetic paths, S⁽¹⁾, S⁽²⁾, …, S⁽ᴹ⁾ — each one plausible under the assumed model.

🎴

Bootstrap: resamples real cards

Every value in a bootstrap resample is a genuine observation that actually happened, just reshuffled and re-drawn with replacement.

🎲

Monte Carlo: rolls a designed die

Every value in a Monte Carlo path is synthetic — generated by a model built to resemble the data's mean and typical swings, not lifted from real history at all.

⚙️

Only as good as the model

More simulated paths reduce simulation noise around the model's own implied distribution — they do not correct a model that's wrong to begin with (understated tail risk, ignored jumps, a calm-period volatility estimate).

Section 9

Read this one twice: neither tool can leave the sample it started from

Both techniques answer some version of the same question: "given what I already have — one historical sample, or one fitted model built from that sample — what range of outcomes is consistent with it?" That's a real, useful, well-posed statistical question, and both tools answer it validly. It is not the same question as "will this signal work on a market, or a time period, it has never seen?"

Every bootstrap resample is built from the same n original observations, generated by the same signal, over the same historical period — reshuffling and re-drawing from that fixed pool cannot create information about a regime that pool never contained. Every Monte Carlo path is generated from a model whose parameters were themselves estimated from that same historical sample — if the fitted parameters are wrong for tomorrow's regime, every synthetic path inherits that same mis-specification, no matter how many thousands of paths you generate.
The trap

A narrow bootstrap or Monte Carlo interval proves the sample (or the fitted model) is internally consistent — it proves nothing about whether the signal that generated it will work on data it has never seen. A tight interval and a genuinely tradeable edge are two different questions with two different answers; conflating "the resample is stable" with "the strategy is robust" is a documented, easy way backtests mislead their own authors.

Section 11

Quick check

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

What does a bootstrap resample actually consist of?

The bootstrap treats the empirical distribution of your sample as a stand-in for the true one, then draws n new values with replacement from it — some originals repeat, some get left out.
Question 2

Why does a plain (i.i.d.) bootstrap understate uncertainty on a volatility-clustered return series?

Picking one observation independently of the next assumes no serial dependence. Financial returns often have volatility clustering — plain resampling erases that structure, making the resulting confidence interval falsely narrow.
Question 3

What does the block bootstrap change about the resampling step?

Because each block is a real, intact, consecutive run, whatever short-term dependence existed inside it survives in the resample — fixing the plain bootstrap's understated uncertainty on dependent data.
Question 4

How does Monte Carlo simulation differ from bootstrapping in what it actually generates?

The bootstrap reshuffles real observations. Monte Carlo fits a model (GARCH, GBM) to the data and then rolls that model forward with fresh random innovations, producing brand-new synthetic paths.
Question 5

A backtest reports a tight, reassuring 95% bootstrap Sharpe interval. What has that actually shown?

Every resample is drawn from the exact same historical trades, generated by the same signal over the same period. A tight interval means those trades don't include a wild outlier — a fact about sample quality, not about generalization.
Section 12

What to carry forward

01

The bootstrap resamples your one dataset with replacement, thousands of times, to approximate a statistic's sampling distribution — a mean, a Sharpe ratio, a max drawdown — with no assumption about its shape.

02

More resamples (larger B) stabilize the estimate of the spread — they never shrink the spread below what the original observations can actually support.

03

Use the block bootstrap for time series — a plain bootstrap shuffles away volatility clustering and serial correlation, understating true uncertainty.

04

Monte Carlo simulates from a fitted model (GARCH, GBM), not real observations — every synthetic path inherits any mis-specification baked into that model.

05

Neither tool can leave the sample it started from — both describe what's plausible given what you already have, never what would happen under conditions that sample never contained.

06

Walk-forward validation, not resampling, is the tool that actually tests generalization — it evaluates a signal on real, later data it never touched during fitting.

07

How it reached trading: Monte Carlo option pricing (Boyle, 1977) and bootstrap tests of trading rules (Brock et al., 1992; White, 2000).

What this now lets you do

Read a bootstrap confidence interval or a Monte Carlo fan chart for exactly what it is — a measure of variability within one sample, not evidence of generalization. Recognize when a plain bootstrap needs to be a block bootstrap instead, the moment the data has real serial correlation. Tell apart "the sample is internally consistent" from "the signal has out-of-sample edge," and never let a tight resampled interval quietly stand in for a real out-of-sample test.
