Advanced Portfolio & Market Structure

Alpha vs. Beta Separation
🧩
Where this could plug into MacroFXModel

An honest, currently-missing decomposition

No strategy in this repo currently has its returns regressed against a market/risk factor to separate beta from alpha — every backtest card reports a strategy's raw Sharpe/return, not how much of it is exposure to a common factor. The regression machinery already exists: js/statsCore.js's linregSlope is exactly the \(\beta\) estimator this lesson uses, and pca-factors.html / random-matrix-theory.html's eigenvalue work is exactly how you'd identify what the dominant "market factor" even is for a multi-pair FX book (PC1 of the correlation matrix, filtered against the Marchenko-Pastur noise band). Wiring these together — regress each strategy's trade-level or daily returns against that dominant factor, report \(\hat\alpha\)/\(\hat\beta\)/its standard error alongside the existing Sharpe card — would be a genuine, currently-unbuilt candidate that this repo's own tooling already has every ingredient for. Like everything else in this Theory Lab, it would need the same OOS discipline before the resulting \(\hat\alpha\) is trusted as real.

Fractional Brownian Motion & Long Memory
🧩
Where this connects to MacroFXModel

A descriptive statistic already in production, a generative model not yet built

js/rangeBiasCore.js's computeHurst, covered in the previous lesson, is exactly the R/S-regression-based estimator this lesson gives a formal home to — it produces an empirical \(H\) but does not, and was never designed to, fit an underlying generative model like fBm or ARFIMA to the data. That is a reasonable scope for a live entry-bias feature: it needs a fast, robust regime read, not a full model fit. It does mean the live feature's \(H\) is a description of the recent past's scaling behavior, not a forecast derived from a fitted long-memory process — a distinction worth keeping straight given how easily "the Hurst exponent is 0.85" can be misread as "and therefore the model predicts continued persistence for N more days," which computeHurst's descriptive R/S procedure does not, by itself, license.

Separately, js/volBacktestEngine.js's GARCH-family sigma estimator is a genuinely short-memory model by construction — its volatility persistence decays geometrically, exactly the AR(1)-style curve in the chart above. Long memory in realized volatility is a widely documented empirical regularity in the academic literature (not a claim specific to this repo's data), distinct from and additional to the short-memory clustering GARCH already captures. If this repo ever needs to forecast volatility meaningfully further out than a short-memory GARCH model can honestly support — which it does not currently attempt to do — fitting a genuine long-memory volatility model (ARFIMA on log-realized-vol, or a long-memory GARCH variant such as FIGARCH) would be the natural, currently-unbuilt next step, and it would need the same OOS validation discipline (Lego Principle 5) as any other model before being trusted over the existing GARCH sigma series. Nothing described in this lesson is wired into any live forecaster today; it is background theory for a future extension, not a description of current behavior.

Market Microstructure: Price Impact & the Bid-Ask Spread
🧩
Where this connects to MacroFXModel

The theory underneath a rule this repo already enforces without citing it

This repo's own project memory states, as a hard rule for every new strategy: "Costs on by default. Round-trip spread + commission, plus slippage on stop/breakout entries. Free fills are not honest," and separately flags, as an explicit anti-pattern to avoid: "Assuming the intrabar take-profit was hit on a daily bar (path is unknown) — prefer M1 fills or mark-to-window-close." Until now, both rules have stood purely as practical discipline, learned the hard way, with no formal citation behind the "why." Glosten-Milgrom and Kyle's Lambda are that "why": the spread every backtest here pays on entry and exit is, in theory, exactly the adverse-selection premium derived in section 3 above — a cost that exists because whoever is quoting the other side of the trade cannot tell an informed order from a noise one, and prices accordingly. And the additional slippage this repo specifically flags for stop and breakout entries — orders that, by design, fire during fast, directional moves, exactly the moments when momentary liquidity thins out and suspected informed flow spikes — is precisely a high-\(\lambda\) event: the same order size that would be cheap in calm, deep conditions costs more exactly when a stop is triggering, because \(\lambda\) is elevated in exactly that instant.

The engineering already reflects this even without naming it. This repo's realistic fill walker — walkBars in js/forecastCore.js, the shared Tier-1 primitive every forecast-family strategy imports per the Lego Principle's core-modules table (never copy it; the vol-sigma series and band constants it works alongside live in js/volBacktestEngine.js) — exists specifically to avoid assuming a fill happened at a clean, frictionless price on an unknown intrabar path. fill-realism.html is the empirical version of the same question this lesson answers theoretically: it re-prices the per-line volatility tearsheet at a ladder of bar resolutions and checks whether the reported edge survives being repriced through progressively more honest — that is, more microstructure-realistic — fills, or whether it was a fill artifact all along (its own framing: "if OOS Sharpe collapses as bars get finer toward 1-min truth, the edge is a fill artifact"). Kyle's Lambda is the formal parameter that predicts exactly this kind of collapse should be expected wherever a strategy's simulated fills understate how thin liquidity actually was on the path between entry and exit. Nothing about the code changes as a result of reading this lesson — the fill walker and the cost discipline are already correct. What changes is having a rigorous, citable reason for a rule that was previously enforced purely as hard-earned practical caution.

Markowitz & Black-Litterman
🧩
Where this could plug into MacroFXModel

A real, currently-missing formal portfolio-construction layer

To be direct about the current state of this repo: nothing here runs a Markowitz or Black-Litterman-style optimizer to set position weights. Every strategy — the reversion/continuation engine, the range and confluence engines, the volatility-band forecasters — operates on one instrument at a time and outputs a signal or a plan for that instrument in isolation. There is no layer that takes several such signals across a basket and asks "given everything I currently believe about these instruments, and how they co-move, what's the actual capital allocation that makes sense across all of them at once."

The pieces this lesson depends on are, encouragingly, already partly built: js/diversificationCore.js's correlationMatrix and symmetricEigenvalues compute exactly the \(\Sigma\) (or its correlation-normalized cousin) this lesson's variance formula and Black-Litterman's \(\Pi = \delta \Sigma w_{mkt}\) both need, and the existing "effective number of bets" metrics (effectiveBetsPCA, effectiveBetsEntropy, effectiveBetsWeighted, surfaced on diversification.html) are already answering a closely related — but distinctly narrower — question: "how concentrated is this basket's risk," not "what weights should I hold." Wiring an actual optimizer on top would mean: sourcing or estimating an expected- return vector (the hard, fragile part, which is exactly why Black- Litterman's equilibrium-prior-plus-views approach is the more honest starting point than raw Markowitz); deciding what "market-equilibrium weights" even means in an FX context, where there's no obvious market-cap analogue the way there is for equities (position/flow data, or simply equal-weight, would need to stand in, each with real trade-offs); and building the actual constrained-optimization solver (a small, well-understood numerical problem, but not one this repo currently has any code for).

This would be a meaningfully large, genuinely new piece of infrastructure — not a quick extension of the existing diversification tooling, even though it builds directly on that tooling's covariance-matrix foundation. It is the natural formal portfolio-construction layer if and when this repo moves from single-instrument signals toward true multi-instrument portfolio construction, but that "if" is doing real work: until there's an actual need to allocate capital jointly across several live signals at once, building this optimizer would be infrastructure in search of a use case. Per this repo's standard discipline, were it built, it would still need its own honest validation — a portfolio-construction layer doesn't get to skip the IS/OOS split just because its inputs (the individual signals) were separately validated; the way those signals get combined is itself a claim that needs testing on its own terms (Lego Principle 5).

Random Matrix Theory & the Marchenko-Pastur Law
🧩
Where this could plug into MacroFXModel

A concrete, unbuilt guardrail for the existing effective-bets metric

As the PCA lesson's repo box lays out, js/diversificationCore.js already computes symmetricEigenvalues — the full eigenvalue spectrum of a correlation matrix, via a from-scratch Jacobi rotation — and feeds it into effectiveBetsPCA(corr), the \((\sum_i \lambda_i)^2 / \sum_i \lambda_i^2\) inverse-participation-ratio metric that diversification.html displays live as "effective number of bets." That metric already uses the entire eigenvalue spectrum this lesson is about — every \(\lambda_i\) it computes goes into the formula, with no distinction made between an eigenvalue that is an overwhelming, obvious common factor and one that is statistically indistinguishable from noise at the basket's actual \(N\) and \(T\).

To be explicit about what is and isn't already there: the Marchenko-Pastur noise-filtering step described in this lesson is not currently applied anywhere in this repo. It is a specific, well-defined, low-risk enhancement candidate, not a claim about existing behavior. Concretely, it would mean: given the eigenvalues symmetricEigenvalues already returns, and the \(N\) and \(T\) already known from the basket and the lookback window used to build the correlation matrix, compute \(q = N/T\) and the \((\lambda_{\min}, \lambda_{\max})\) band from the two-line formula in this lesson, and flag which of the returned eigenvalues fall inside it. That flag doesn't have to change the effective-bets number itself — it could ship first as a simple annotation on diversification.html ("2 of 5 eigenvalues distinguishable from noise at this window length") — a direct, honest caveat on a metric that currently has none, and a guardrail against a very specific overstatement: a basket whose effective-bets score looks reassuringly close to \(N\) only because several of its "distinct" eigenvalues are actually noise-band clutter that happens to sum correctly, not because the basket is genuinely that diversified.

This is a buildable next step, not a validated one. Any change to how effectiveBetsPCA itself treats noise-band eigenvalues (excluding them, shrinking them, reweighting the formula around only the "real" ones) would be a new piece of decision logic on top of an existing metric, and per Lego Principle 5 it would need its own honest before/after comparison — does flagging or filtering noise-band eigenvalues actually produce a more decision-useful diversification read in practice, on real baskets, not just a theoretically cleaner one — before being trusted, and it should be registered as a candidate in LEGO_MODULES.md §2 before it's built, not assumed to help because the math is elegant.

Bootstrapping & Monte Carlo Simulation
🧩
Where this connects to MacroFXModel

The rule this lesson formalizes, and the tool that actually tests generalization

This repo's CLAUDE.md states, in the "Backtest build discipline" section:

"Don't let Monte Carlo stand in for OOS. Resampling from a strategy's own realized return distribution shows the range of outcomes consistent with that mean/std — it says nothing about whether the signal generalizes to data it hasn't seen. It's a legitimate expectation-setting tool; label it as that, not as robustness evidence."

Everything above this box is the from-scratch statistical justification for why that sentence is true rather than merely asserted: a bootstrap resample and a Monte Carlo path are both, mechanically, incapable of containing information the original sample or its fitted model didn't already have. Neither can substitute for what this repo's Lego Principle #5 actually requires — judging a strategy against a true in-sample / out-of-sample split, with a non-trivial OOS trade count, on data the signal was never fit to. This tier's Walk-Forward Validation & Purged Cross-Validation lesson is the tool built to test exactly that different question — repeatedly re-fitting on one stretch of real history and testing, un-touched, on a later stretch it never saw, with a purge gap to prevent leakage across the split. Bootstrapping and Monte Carlo cannot stand in for that, no matter how many replicates are run, because they were never built to touch data outside the original sample in the first place. Use them for exactly what they're good at — an honest confidence interval, an expectation-setting distribution of outcomes given what you already believe — and reach for walk-forward validation, not a bigger \(B\), whenever the actual question is generalization.

Zooming out: this is the seventh and final lesson in this Applied Statistics & Validation tier, and read in order the seven lessons trace one continuous validation toolkit rather than seven unrelated topics — checking a fitted model's residuals for autocorrelation and heteroskedasticity, choosing between competing models honestly rather than by in-sample fit alone, modeling a time series' own dynamics with ARIMA/VAR, capturing how markets move together beyond a single correlation number with copulas, judging a strategy's risk-adjusted return with a Sharpe ratio corrected for multiple testing (the Deflated Sharpe Ratio), testing whether a signal survives on data it never saw with walk-forward and purged cross-validation, and — here — being precise about what resampling your own results can and cannot tell you once that OOS test is done. None of the seven replaces the others; each one closes a different way a backtest can quietly lie to its author.

Copulas — Modeling Dependence Beyond Correlation
🧩
Where this could plug into MacroFXModel

A genuine gap next to the existing correlation tools — not yet built

This repo has no copula implementation anywhere, and this lesson is not pretending otherwise — it is flagged as a concept lesson precisely because there is nothing to point at in the codebase yet. What does exist, and what makes the gap concrete rather than abstract, is correlations.html (the Correlation Lab), which computes a rolling Pearson correlation on log returns over a 60-bar window and blends it with a beta spread into a "hedge score," and diversification.html, which builds its "effective number of bets" metrics (effectiveBetsPCA, effectiveBetsEntropy, effectiveBetsWeighted in js/diversificationCore.js) directly off the eigenvalues of that same kind of correlation matrix. Both tools are honest and useful at the question they answer — "how linked are these instruments on an ordinary day, and how concentrated is my book around that linkage" — and neither one, by construction, can see tail dependence, because a Pearson correlation matrix is exactly the object this lesson describes as blind to it.

A copula-based dependence model would be a meaningful complement to those tools specifically for stress-testing a multi-pair book's tail behavior — not a replacement for the rolling-correlation view, which remains the right, simpler tool for ordinary-day diversification checks. A candidate shape: fit realistic fat-tailed marginals per instrument (reusing the EVT lesson's machinery), fit a small set of candidate copulas (Gaussian as the baseline, Clayton or Student-t as the tail-dependent alternative) to historical pair data, and compare what each implies for joint drawdown probability versus what a naive correlation-only Monte Carlo would show — surfaced as a "tail dependence" companion metric next to the existing hedge score and effective-bets numbers, not a new standalone page.

To be explicit about what this is and isn't: this is an unbuilt candidate, not a claim that it would necessarily improve anything. Fitting and validating a copula's tail dependence parameter honestly needs real historical crisis-period data — 2008, the 2015 CHF unpeg, the March 2020 COVID shock, and similar episodes — because tail events are by definition rare, and a tail dependence estimate fit only on calm years is exactly the failure mode the Gaussian-copula/CDO history above warns about. Any such module would need to be built as a proper Tier-1 or Tier-2 brick (per this repo's Lego Principle 6), registered as a candidate in LEGO_MODULES.md §2, and validated honestly on out-of-sample crisis windows before it informed any real sizing decision — not assumed to help because the underlying theory is well-established.

Model Selection: AIC & BIC
🧩
Where this connects to MacroFXModel

The formal version of a rule this repo already states in prose

To be direct about what is and isn't built here: there is currently no coded AIC/BIC step anywhere in this codebase — no engine here formally computes and compares information criteria across candidate model orders. That is a genuine, currently-missing formal step, not a solved problem being merely under-documented. This lesson exists to make the theory available before that gap gets filled, not to claim it already has been.

What already exists is the house instinct AIC/BIC would formalize. CLAUDE.md's own backtest-build-discipline section says it plainly: "only add tunable parameters after, knowing each one you add is overfit risk you're buying" — that is, in prose, exactly what the AIC/BIC penalty terms (\(2k\) and \(k\ln n\)) price numerically. And the Multiple Testing lesson's discipline of "count the cells" before declaring a slice a real survivor is the same instinct applied to a different axis of the same problem: multiple testing counts how many looks at the data you took before calling one significant, while AIC/BIC count how many parameters a single model was allowed before calling its fit meaningful. Both are answers to the same underlying question — "how much of what I'm seeing is the price of giving myself more chances to find something?" — aimed at two different places a model builder can quietly buy overfitting without noticing: too many slices, or too many knobs on one model. Any future engine here that searches over lag length, HMM state count, or GARCH order — anywhere a "how much complexity" choice currently relies on eyeballing in-sample fit — is a legitimate candidate for a real AIC/BIC (or AICc) step, logged the same honest way the rest of this repo's validation is: reported alongside, not instead of, a true out-of-sample check.

Regression Diagnostics: Durbin-Watson, Breusch-Pagan & Jarque-Bera
🧩
Where this connects to MacroFXModel

The from-scratch math behind an existing house rule

CLAUDE.md's backtest-build-discipline section states the rule this lesson exists to formalize, word for word: "Add residual diagnostics to any regression-based signal: Durbin-Watson (autocorrelation), Jarque-Bera (residual normality), Breusch-Pagan (heteroskedasticity) before trusting its p-value/coefficient. A statistically 'significant' coefficient on a heteroskedastic fit has optimistic standard errors — check before leaning on it." That sentence has been sitting in this repo's prose discipline as a rule to follow; this lesson is the derivation underneath it — where each statistic comes from, why the specific formula catches the specific failure it's named for, and why a failure specifically inflates apparent significance rather than just generically "being a problem."

The Jarque-Bera test in particular reuses building blocks that already exist in this codebase rather than introducing new ones: js/metricsCore.js's skewness and excessKurtosis functions — built 2026-07-21 as Tier-1 primitives, unit-tested in legoBricks.test.mjs, and already wired into summarizeTrades's skew/ excessKurt output fields — are the exact two ingredients the \(JB = \tfrac{n}{6}(S^2 + K^2/4)\) formula combines. The only difference between how this repo currently uses them and what Jarque-Bera does is the input series: today they describe the shape of a strategy's realized trade P&L; Jarque-Bera applies the identical math to a regression's residual series instead. Nothing new needs building to compute \(S\) and \(K\) for a regression's residuals — the brick already exists.

education/regression-analysis-course-notes.md covers the applied, practical side of regression work in this repo — how to set up and interpret a regression for a trading signal day to day. This lesson is deliberately the theory layer underneath that practice: the three checks a regression should pass before its p-value is quoted anywhere, derived from first principles rather than invoked as a checklist item.

This is Lesson 1 of a new Applied Statistics & Validation category — the from-scratch math behind the broader honest-harness discipline (Lego Principle 5, the backtest-build-discipline section) that this repo already enforces in prose across every regression-based signal it builds.

Sharpe, Sortino, Calmar & the Deflated Sharpe Ratio
🧩
Where this could plug into MacroFXModel

metricsCore.js already has the Sharpe honesty pair — DSR is the next layer, and it isn't built yet

This repo is unusually well set up to add DSR properly, because the pieces it depends on already exist as bricks, not as something that would need to be invented. js/metricsCore.js already ships sharpeRatio, sortinoRatio and calmar exactly as derived above, each taking its annualization explicitly rather than assuming one — the module's own header comment explains this was built specifically because the same three ratios had drifted into 8+ different, silently disagreeing implementations across the codebase's various backtest engines before this brick existed. It also already ships the Sharpe-honesty pair: sharpeStdError (Lo 2002's standard error of an annualized Sharpe) and minTrackRecordLength (Bailey & López de Prado, 2012), the latter already using the exact skew/kurtosis-adjusted variance-inflation term this lesson's DSR formula uses too. Per CLAUDE.md, summarizeTrades already feeds every strategy's real per-trade skew and excess kurtosis into that adjustment rather than assuming Gaussian returns, so every result card already reports \(SR \pm SE\) and a fat-tail-adjusted minimum track record length alongside the raw Sharpe.

What's genuinely missing is the piece this lesson is centrally about: nothing in the current metrics stack tracks, or corrects for, how many variations were tried before a "best" Sharpe was reported. minTrackRecordLength answers "how much live data would it take to trust this specific Sharpe," which is a valuable but different question from "given the parameter sweep that produced this Sharpe, how much of it is the sweep talking." There is no N (trial count) tracked anywhere in a backtest card today, no deflatedSharpe.js, and no UI element that asks "how many variations did you actually try to get here" before rendering a headline Sharpe. This is a genuine, valuable, currently-missing validation layer — the natural next step for this metrics stack, not a claim that it already exists in some other form. Building it would mean instrumenting the places that already run parameter sweeps (any backtest with a grid of thresholds/lookbacks/windows) to log every trial's Sharpe, not just the winner's, so \(N\) and \(\sigma_{SR}\) are real numbers pulled from the actual search rather than guessed after the fact — the single most common way DSR gets misapplied in practice is plugging in a guessed or undercounted \(N\) instead of the sweep's true trial count, which silently understates \(SR_0\) and overstates the resulting DSR.

Walk-Forward & Purged Cross-Validation
🧩
Where this connects to MacroFXModel

This is the formal name for a rule CLAUDE.md already enforces — and a refinement worth checking against

This repo's build discipline states the walk-forward principle directly, twice: the adding-a- strategy checklist's "No lookahead. σ/regime/score for window \(i\) use data \(< i\) only", and the Lego Principle's "true in-sample / out-of-sample split" requirement, paired with the ≥30 OOS trade count gate that keeps a fold's out-of-sample result from being too thin to mean anything. Those two rules, together, are exactly what this lesson formalizes: train strictly on the past, evaluate strictly on the unseen future, and don't trust a thin sliver of OOS trades as if it were a real fold.

Where this lesson goes further than the current codebase does: summarizeSplit in js/honestForecastEngine.js performs exactly one chronological split — everything before a computed split date is IS, everything from that date on is OOS — and it does not currently purge or embargo rows straddling that boundary. That's an honest gap, not a hidden bug: a single clean chronological split (no shuffling) already eliminates the crude lookahead problem this lesson opens with. What it does not eliminate is the subtler boundary leak described above, and this repo has no shortage of features built on exactly the kind of overlapping rolling windows where that leak lives — HV20 and the other vol-sigma series in js/volBacktestEngine.js, the rolling z-scores and percentiles in js/statsCore.js (rollingZScore, rollingPercentile), and the day-type estimators in js/dayTypeCore.js all compute a value at index \(i\) from a window of nearby bars. Any of them used near a train/OOS split boundary is a candidate for exactly the label/feature overlap this lesson walks through. This isn't a claim that any specific reported OOS Sharpe in this repo is wrong — that's an empirical question a purge/embargo check would have to answer per-strategy — but it is the concrete, checkable refinement worth applying before trusting an OOS number on a strategy whose features lean on multi-day rolling windows near the split date.

Foundations & Epistemics

Bayesian Inference & Updating
🧩
Where this could plug into MacroFXModel

Confluence scoring is currently additive, not Bayesian — and that's a genuine, unbuilt design question

js/levelSources.js emits a Level[] from several independent sources (daily open, prior hi/lo, pivots, volume profile, swing S&R, round numbers, VWAP), and collectLevels / clusterLevels group nearby levels from different sources into a confluence cluster. Separately, js/entryGradeCore.js's computeSignalScore combines several component scores (hmmScore, momScore, rbScore, structScore, optionally macroScore) into a single 0–100 signal score via a fixed weighted sum — 25/25/20/20/10 with macro data on, 38/25/25/12 without.

A weighted sum treats each confirming signal as contributing a fixed, independent slice of "confidence," regardless of how correlated that signal is with the others already counted, and regardless of how common or rare the situation it's flagging actually is. A Bayesian alternative is a genuinely different design: treat "fade" vs. "follow" as two competing hypotheses with a prior (e.g. the historical base rate of fade vs. follow outcomes at this kind of level, the way the worked example above used the 20% base rate of trend days), and treat each additional confirming signal (structural confluence, momentum alignment, range-bias conviction, macro alignment) as evidence that updates the posterior via its own likelihood ratio — sequential updating exactly as derived above, each new signal multiplying in a Bayes factor rather than adding a fixed point weight. The structural appeal, in principle, is that this can naturally down-weight correlated signals (two features that are mostly saying the same thing shouldn't each buy a full, independent slice of confidence the way a linear sum lets them) and it would produce an actual calibrated probability ("62% likely to fade") instead of an unitless 0–100 score.

To be explicit about where this stands: this is not built, not prototyped, and not validated — it is a candidate reframing of an existing brick, described honestly as a hypothesis about design, not a claim of improvement. Building it would require estimating real likelihood ratios per signal from historical data (not guessed weights), handling the correlation-between-signals problem explicitly rather than just asserting Bayes handles it gracefully, and — per this repo's Lego Principle #5 — proving it beats the incumbent additive computeSignalScore on true out-of-sample Sharpe with a non-trivial OOS trade count before it would replace anything live. A more "principled-sounding" formula is not evidence by itself; only the OOS comparison is.

The Efficient Market Hypothesis
🧩
Where this connects to MacroFXModel

The house discipline in CLAUDE.md is EMH turned into engineering practice

This repo's build discipline — spelled out in CLAUDE.md — is not an arbitrary process checklist. It is what taking EMH seriously as a prior looks like in code. A few direct lines:

  • The in-sample / out-of-sample split, and the "≥30 OOS trades" gate (js/honestForecastEngine.js's summarizeSplit, and the OOS Sharpe gate referenced throughout the repo's strategy-validation rules) exist because an in-sample result is exactly the kind of thing the joint-hypothesis problem warns about: you fit a rule to a known history, and the history you fit to no longer has any power to distinguish "found a genuine, persistent edge" from "found the specific noise pattern that happened to occur in this sample." Only performance on data the rule never saw is evidence against the null that "this is just noise the fitting process found."
  • "Name the benchmark before claiming improvement" is the buy-and-hold / no-skill floor from the bullet above, made a hard rule rather than a suggestion. An equity curve that looks good in isolation is not evidence of anything until it's shown next to what a passive, EMH-consistent baseline would have produced over the same period with the same costs.
  • "Costs on by default" and the requirement for realistic fills (the fill walker in js/forecastCore.js, never a zero-cost backtest) matter because EMH's prediction is specifically about costless, riskless arbitrage. A pattern that only "works" before spread and slippage isn't a counterexample to EMH at all — it's consistent with it. EMH doesn't claim markets are efficient net of frictions it never promised to overcome; it claims the free lunch isn't there. Charging real costs is what makes the test actually about the free lunch.

None of this says any particular strategy in this repo does or doesn't beat efficient pricing — that's an empirical question answered per-strategy by its own OOS card, not by this lesson. What this lesson explains is why the discipline exists at all: because the default, working assumption going in is that markets are efficient enough that a real edge is the exception, not the expectation, and the entire validation harness is built to make that a hard bar to clear rather than an easy one.

This lesson is the pricing theory that produces that fact rather than just asserting it: delta (\(N(d_1)\) above) is the hedge ratio the course notes describe a dealer maintaining, and gamma (\(\partial^2C/\partial S^2\)) is precisely the rate at which that hedge ratio must change as price moves — which is why the course notes' claim that gamma effects intensify as expiry approaches is not an empirical curiosity but a direct, derivable consequence of the Black-Scholes formula: gamma for an at-the-money option provably rises as \(T \to 0\). Reading this lesson does not replace that course's applied trading framework — the OI notes' treatment of walls, max pain, magnetism, and daily trading process is the practical, tested-against-a-real-heatmap layer this repo actually uses; this page is the theoretical foundation underneath it, for understanding why the mechanism works rather than only that it does. The Merton Jump-Diffusion Model
🧩
Where this could plug into MacroFXModel

The theory behind fat tails this repo already measures — and a very concrete practical echo

This repo does not fit a jump-diffusion model anywhere, does not price options, and has no \(\lambda, \mu_J, \sigma_J\) estimator. Say that plainly: this is unbuilt here, a theory lesson, not a description of existing code.

What the repo does already have, built and tested, is the honest empirical way to respect exactly the risk this lesson describes without needing to fit a full parametric jump model. Per CLAUDE.md, js/metricsCore.js ships Tier-1 fat-tail primitives — skewness, excessKurtosis, histVaR, histCVaR — built 2026-07-21 and hand-checked in legoBricks.test.mjs. summarizeTrades (the shared trade-log summarizer used across the platform's backtest report cards) emits skew, excessKurt, var95, and cvar95 on every trade-return series it summarizes. Jump-diffusion is the theoretical story for exactly why a real FX return series should have nonzero excess kurtosis and a heavier left tail than a Gaussian model would predict — a sudden macro-release gap is a literal, observable instance of the \(dJ_t\) term this lesson defines. excessKurtosis and histCVaR are the empirical, model-free way this codebase already catches that reality in a trade-return series, without ever having to estimate a jump intensity or a jump-size distribution. This is a real, already-built connection — not a proposal.

There is a second, very concrete practical echo, unrelated to option pricing: CLAUDE.md's own anti-pattern list warns against "assuming the intrabar take-profit was hit on a daily bar (path is unknown) — prefer M1 fills or mark-to-window-close," and fill-realism.html documents a real instance of this exact failure mode in the Per-Line Volatility tearsheet (~44% zero-duration trades where TP is unknowable on the fill bar, so the walker marks to close and hides real outer-line stop losses on continuation days). That bug and Merton's jump term are the same underlying risk wearing two different hats: a fill walker that assumes a limit or stop was touched at its exact quoted price implicitly assumes the path between bars was continuous — that price had to pass through every intermediate level to get from the open to the close. A genuine gap (a jump) can blow straight through a resting limit or stop without ever trading at the assumed price, the same way Merton's \(dJ_t\) term skips over the intermediate price levels a pure-diffusion path would have been forced to cross. A backtest's fill assumption and an option-pricing model's path assumption are answering the same underlying question — "can price teleport?" — for two different purposes.

Building an actual jump-diffusion estimator or option-pricing engine here would be a new, unvalidated undertaking — it would need synthetic-data unit tests that recover known \(\lambda,\mu_J,\sigma_J\) parameters before touching real prices, and, per the Lego Principle, a clear reason two or more callers would use it rather than building it because the math is elegant. Nothing here proposes that. The honest summary is: the fat-tail metrics already in metricsCore.js are the built, tested, empirical answer to "does this repo respect jump/gap risk" — a full parametric jump-diffusion model is not built, and is not needed to get that empirical honesty.

🧩
Where this could plug into MacroFXModel

This isn't a hypothetical extension — education/macro-deep-dives-notes.md names the gap directly. Its stat-arb entry-model section states the requirement plainly: "Key requirements: cointegration (not just correlation), an economic linkage explaining the relationship, sufficient liquidity in both legs"; its quick-revision sheet repeats it as a bare fact to remember — "Stat arb needs cointegration, not correlation. This lesson is, in effect, the deep dive that item was asking for.

Three existing tools in this repo currently answer adjacent but different questions, and it's worth being precise about which question each one answers so a future extension doesn't conflate them:

correlations.html (the Correlation Lab) computes a rolling Pearson correlation on log returns over a 60-bar window, and its "hedge score" blends that correlation with a beta spread to rank pairs as hedge candidates. That is a well-posed answer to "do these two pairs' short-term moves tend to offset each other" — it is not a test of whether either pair's level relationship to the other is stationary. A pair the tool ranks as a strong hedge on return correlation could still have a spread, in levels, that is a pure random walk with no equilibrium to revert to.

cross-pair-research.html already states the principle in its own UI copy — "Correlation ≠ edge" — and runs an independent-bets gate off the participation ratio of the return-correlation matrix plus a fade/follow sign-test between pairs. That's a sound check for a different failure mode (treating correlated bets as independent bets), but it is, again, working from a return-correlation matrix, not a cointegration test on levels.

credit-leadlag.html is the closest of the three in spirit — it deliberately does not run a naive level correlation, and instead tests whether credit-spread velocity (a differenced, largely-stationary transform) leads risk-instrument forward volatility out-of-sample, reporting a genuine IS/OOS information coefficient. That is squarely a lead-lag / Granger-causality-style question, which macro-deep-dives-notes.md correctly separates from the cointegration question — different tool, for a different family of relationship (does A's change predict B's future change, versus does A and B's combined level have a stable equilibrium).

The suggested validation check, to be explicit: before any of this repo's cross-asset pages (or the Transatlantic Yield Spread lesson in macro-deep-dives-notes.md, which currently reasons about the US-Germany yield spread leading EUR/USD narratively and via cross-correlation) treats a relationship as tradeable mean-reversion rather than a shared-trend coincidence, running the actual Engle-Granger (or, for a basket, Johansen) test on the levels would be the correct next check — the honest reading of "is there an equilibrium here" that a rolling correlation coefficient cannot provide by itself. This is a statement about what a rigorous next step would look like, not a claim that any of these three pages is currently wrong or incomplete for what it's built to do — each already does its own stated job. And per this repo's Lego Principle 5, none of that would be worth anything without the usual discipline: build it as a shared brick (not a bespoke test bolted onto one page), and prove any cointegration-filtered pair selection beats the incumbent approach on true out-of-sample Sharpe with a non-trivial OOS trade count before treating it as an improvement, not just a more theoretically satisfying story.

🧩
Where this could plug into MacroFXModel

Extending eigenvalues (already built) to eigenvectors/loadings (not yet built)

This repo is not starting from zero on PCA. js/diversificationCore.js already implements symmetricEigenvalues — a cyclic Jacobi rotation that computes the eigenvalues of a correlation matrix from scratch, no external linear-algebra library — and feeds those eigenvalues into effectiveBetsPCA(corr), which computes \((\sum_i \lambda_i)^2 \big/ \sum_i \lambda_i^2\), the "inverse participation ratio." That formula is a compact summary statistic built directly from the eigenvalue spectrum this lesson derives: it equals \(N\) (the number of instruments) when every eigenvalue is equal — no dominant factor, every instrument is its own independent bet — and shrinks toward 1 as one eigenvalue comes to dominate the rest — one factor, many costumes. This is wired live into diversification.html alongside effectiveBetsEntropy (a Meucci-style entropy version of the same idea) and effectiveBetsWeighted (allocation-aware). correlations.html is the pairwise view one level below this — the raw correlation heatmap PCA is built on top of, useful for eyeballing individual pair relationships but, as the basket grows past a handful of instruments, hard to read as a whole in a way a single \(\text{EVR}_1\) number isn't.

What's genuinely missing is the eigenvector side. symmetricEigenvalues currently returns only the eigenvalues (a deliberate, documented simplification — see the "eigenvalues are rotation-invariant" comment in that file — because the effective-bets metrics only ever needed the spectrum, not the directions). Extracting the eigenvectors from the same Jacobi sweep (they fall out as the accumulated rotation matrix, a small, well-understood addition to the existing algorithm rather than a new one) would unlock exactly the piece this lesson emphasizes: which instruments load on PC1, and by how much — turning "your basket's effective number of bets is 1.6 out of 4" into "your basket's dominant factor is basically EURUSD and GBPUSD moving together; AUDJPY and USDCAD are your genuinely separate bets." That is a materially more actionable diagnostic for someone deciding what to trim from an over-concentrated book, and it would sit as a natural addition to the existing diversification.html page rather than a new one — same data, same eigen-decomposition, one more output surfaced.

To be explicit about what this is and isn't: everything above is a description of an unbuilt extension to existing, working code — not a claim that loadings are already displayed anywhere, and absolutely not a claim that "PC1 dominance" by itself is a trading signal. This is a data-summarization and risk-visibility tool: it tells you how concentrated your current basket's variance is and around what, today, on historical data. Any trading rule built on top of it — for example, sizing down when \(\text{EVR}_1\) is unusually high, on the theory that a market dominated by one factor is a market where diversification benefits have temporarily evaporated — is a genuinely separate claim that would need its own honest OOS validation per this repo's standard discipline (Lego Principle 5), built as a proper Tier-1 or Tier-2 brick and registered in LEGO_MODULES.md §2 as a candidate before it's assumed to help.

Extreme Value Theory & Tail Risk
🧩
Where this connects to MacroFXModel

The empirical half is already a Tier-1 brick; the EVT half is a described-not-built next layer

js/metricsCore.js already has exactly the honest, sample-bound version of this lesson's core question, built 2026-07-21 per CLAUDE.md's output-analysis discipline ("report distributional shape, not just Sharpe"). skewness(xs) and excessKurtosis(xs) compute the sample skew and excess kurtosis of a pnl series — cheap diagnostics that catch a fat or asymmetric tail a lone Sharpe ratio hides. histVaR(xs, p) and histCVaR(xs, p) are, precisely, the "plain historical method" section of this lesson: histVaR takes the \((1-p)\) empirical quantile of the series (via linear- interpolated sample quantiles — the standard type-7/numpy-default method, no distributional assumption at all), and histCVaR averages every observation at or beyond that quantile — exactly the VaR and CVaR definitions given above, computed the fully honest, assumption- free way. summarizeTrades wires these into every performance card as skew, excessKurt, var95, cvar95, and — worth repeating from CLAUDE.md because it's an easy thing to misread — these are per-trade tail statistics computed on a per-trade pnl series, not portfolio VaR over a fixed time horizon; the two are related but not interchangeable, and nothing in this repo currently claims otherwise.

The GPD-fitting, peaks-over-threshold layer this lesson covers is genuinely the next rung up the same ladder — a natural extension if this repo ever needs to answer "what does a worse-than-anything-I've-seen-yet drawdown plausibly look like," which histVaR/ histCVaR structurally cannot answer once you ask for a quantile beyond what the sample itself supports (exactly the "2.5th-worst observation" problem in the worked example above). But it is important to say plainly: this is not built here, and building it right now would not be a good idea. Most strategies in this repo have well under 1,000 closed trades — several have well under 100. Fitting a GPD needs a reasonable number of tail exceedances to be remotely stable (the worked example used 50, itself on the low side of common practice), and a strategy with, say, 150 trades might offer 10–15 exceedances above a sensible threshold — nowhere near enough to trust a fitted \(\xi\) at all, let alone extrapolate it out to a rarer quantile than the sample can even weakly support. Fitting a GPD to a sample that small would trade a data-point problem for a tinier, harder-to-detect version of the same data-point problem, dressed up in more sophisticated machinery — precisely the kind of overreach the pitfalls section below and this project's honest- teammate contract both warn against. The correct read of this lesson for this repo, today, is: the empirical version already built is the right tool for the trade-count this repo actually has: know the theory exists, know exactly what it would extend, and don't reach for it until the sample sizes justify it.

The Kelly Criterion & Position Sizing
🧩
Where this could plug into MacroFXModel

Formalizes a principle CLAUDE.md already states — but it needs an honestly-estimated edge as input, and there isn't one built yet

This repo's own CLAUDE.md already names the target: "volatility-based position sizing, cutting losers and letting winners run are powerful drivers of results — worth building and validating alongside an entry signal." Kelly and fractional Kelly are the textbook formalization of exactly the first half of that sentence — a principled way to turn "how volatile is this instrument, and how good is my edge" into "how much capital should this specific trade risk," instead of a flat, hand-picked percentage-of-equity risk-per-trade rule. The continuous form, \(f^*=\mu/\sigma^2\), maps directly onto quantities this repo already computes per-instrument and per-horizon — the vol-sigma series in js/volBacktestEngine.js and the OOS Sharpe/return statistics that js/metricsCore.js's summarizeTrades already produces for every strategy that goes through the standard IS/OOS harness.

book-stress.html ("does diversification survive a liquidity contraction?") and diversification.html (the combined-book explorer) are the closest existing pieces of infrastructure, and the connection is direct: both already look at portfolio-level risk across multiple lines/instruments held together, which is precisely the level at which Kelly sizing should actually be applied in a multi-strategy book — not line-by-line in isolation, but jointly, because Kelly's variance term is supposed to be the variance of the combined position, and two correlated lines sized independently by their own standalone Kelly fractions will systematically overstate the combined book's safe aggregate size (the same correlated-signal double-counting problem this series' Bayesian Inference lesson raises for confluence scoring, here showing up as correlated-return double-counting instead of correlated-evidence double-counting). A book-level Kelly or fractional-Kelly sizing layer sitting on top of book-stress.html's existing cross-instrument view would be the natural extraction point.

To be explicit about where this stands: none of this is built as a sizing engine in the repo today — there is no kellySizing.js, no Kelly-fraction column on any backtest card, nothing wired into a live bot's position-size calculation. This is a genuine, unbuilt design candidate, not a claim that the repo already does this. And per CLAUDE.md's own method-vs-edge distinction — "a method is not a strategy... state the dependency up front, not after the primary signal tests empty" — Kelly sizing has a hard dependency this page has stressed throughout: it needs an honestly-estimated win probability/edge (\(p\), or \(\mu\)) and variance (\(\sigma^2\)) as inputs, sourced from true out-of-sample performance, not from an in-sample backtest's optimistic numbers. Plugging an in-sample Sharpe or win rate directly into the Kelly formula is a textbook way to compute a confidently wrong, oversized bet — the formula does exactly what it's told, and what it's told matters enormously. The correct sequencing, consistent with the Lego Principle's OOS-Sharpe bar (Lego Principle #5), is: a signal clears the repo's real IS/OOS split with a non-trivial OOS trade count first, on its own merits — then Kelly or fractional Kelly is layered on as a sizing decision for that already-validated edge. Applying it earlier, to make a weak or null signal's equity curve look more aggressive, would be using the sizing math to paper over a signal problem rather than solve a sizing problem.

GARCH & Volatility Clustering
🧩

Per CLAUDE.md's core-modules table, js/volBacktestEngine.js owns the "vol-sigma series (HV20 / GARCH / Yang-Zhang), ASSET_PARAMS, classifyRegime, band constants... fetchD1" — this lesson's math is exactly what powers the middle one of those three estimators. Concretely, the engine's garchSigmas(bars, omega) function implements the same recursion derived above:

\[ v \leftarrow \omega + \alpha\, r_{i-1}^2 + \beta\, v,\qquad \sigma_i = \sqrt{v} \]

with the series initialized at the unconditional variance \(\omega/(1-\alpha-\beta)\) for the first two bars (rather than at zero) — precisely the long-run-variance formula derived above, used as a sane starting point before any history has accumulated. The repo's actual constants, G_ALPHA = 0.06 and G_BETA = 0.91, give a persistence of \(\alpha+\beta = 0.97\) — a half-life of \(\ln(0.5)/\ln(0.97) \approx 23\) trading days, meaning a shock's imprint on this engine's variance forecast takes roughly a month to decay by half. Per ASSET_PARAMS.index (garch_omega: 4.76e-6), GARCH(1,1) is applied as the index-asset-class estimator (currently NQ), alongside HV20 for the commodity class and Yang-Zhang(30) for the fx class — three different sigma estimators computed and compared under one walk-forward harness, not three redundant copies of the same idea. This lesson does not take a position on which of the three performs best out-of-sample for any given instrument — that's an empirical question the repo's own walk-forward comparison is built to answer, not something to assume from the math alone (Lego Principle 5: OOS evidence, not a prior about the method).

Whichever \(\sigma\) estimator is selected feeds forward into js/forecastCore.js's computeBands, which builds the actual tradable price band from it using the Feller driftless-Brownian-range construction that CLAUDE.md flags as load-bearing: \(HL = BM\_const \times corr \times \sigma\). In other words, GARCH's job in this system is narrowly scoped but consequential — it is one candidate answer to "what is \(\sigma\) today," and everything downstream (band width, regime classification thresholds, entry-zone confidence) inherits whatever that answer says. Note also that classifyRegime (BULL/BEAR/RANGE via EMA-20 slope) is a separate, directional classifier — it answers "which way," not "how big" — so it should not be confused with, or substituted for, the volatility-clustering question GARCH answers here.

A statistically fitted alternative to the existing threshold rules — genuinely unbuilt, not a plan

This repo already has two regime/state classifiers, and it's worth being precise about how an HMM would differ from each, rather than treating "regime detection" as one undifferentiated idea:

  • js/volBacktestEngine.js's classifyRegime is a hand-set threshold rule: it looks at a rolling slope of closes over a fixed window and compares it against a fixed numeric cutoff (thresh) to call a period bullish, bearish, or neutral. The threshold, window, and slope parameters are chosen by the person writing the code, not estimated from the data's own transition/persistence structure.
  • js/dayTypeCore.js's classifyDayType / dayTypeScore is a step up in sophistication — a weighted, score-based selector (per this repo's Lego Principle 4, "the brain is a selector, not more knobs") that combines several estimators from an ESTIMATORS registry into a single trend-day-ness score \(T\) = drift ÷ diffusion, which then drives fade-vs-follow strategy choice. It's principled in the sense that it's a composed score rather than one raw threshold, but the weights in DAYTYPE_PRESETS are still chosen, not fitted, and there's no explicit model of regime persistence or state-transition dynamics — each bar's score is computed fresh, not conditioned on "how long have we already been in this apparent regime."

An HMM would be a genuinely different kind of object from both: instead of a fixed cutoff or a fixed weighting, you'd fit \(A\) and the emission parameters directly from historical return data via Baum-Welch, letting the data itself tell you how persistent each regime actually is (the \(1/(1-A_{ii})\) expected-duration number from the worked example above, but estimated rather than assumed) and how differently returns actually behave in each state, rather than encoding that as a hand-picked threshold or weight. That is a real, substantive advantage in principle: it replaces a guessed constant with an estimated one, and it gives you a probabilistic confidence (the filtered state probability) rather than a hard binary flag.

It would also add real complexity and real estimation risk, and none of that should be understated. Fitting an HMM well requires enough data and enough genuine regime-switching behavior for Baum-Welch to converge to a sensible, non-degenerate solution — a real risk given how few FX regimes are cleanly, stably Markov-stationary over any window long enough to fit on (regimes in practice drift, macro backdrops change, and a 2-state or 3-state model is a strong simplification of a much messier reality — this is expanded on in the pitfalls below). And per this repo's Lego Principle 5 — no change counts as an improvement without beating the incumbent on out-of-sample Sharpe with a non-trivial OOS trade count — an HMM-based regime classifier would need to actually outperform classifyRegime or dayTypeScore OOS before it would be worth the added complexity, not merely be more statistically sophisticated in the abstract. Sophistication is not the bar; OOS performance is. This lesson describes the concept and the honest tradeoff — it is not a claim that an HMM would improve this repo's regime detection, and nothing here has been built or tested.

The Kalman Filter & Adaptive Relationships
🧩
Where this could plug into MacroFXModel

An adaptive coefficient for a lead-lag relationship whose strength already varies with regime

Two places in this repo already describe exactly the kind of relationship a Kalman filter is built for: one whose strength, not just its level, seems to change with market conditions. education/macro-deep-dives-notes.md's Lesson 2 ("The Transatlantic Yield Spread") documents that the lead time between a US–German yield-spread move and the EUR/USD reaction is not constant — its own table (§2.4) states the lead runs "hours to 1–2 days" in high-volatility/news-driven conditions but "2–4 weeks" in range-bound, low-vol conditions, and "1–3 months" around a regime change. Lesson 3's live case study (17–18 Feb 2026) is a single concrete instance of that relationship firing with a ~24-hour lead. That is, in plain language, "the coefficient connecting spread-momentum to the FX/vol response is not fixed — it moves with the regime," which is precisely the premise a state-space model with a drifting \(x_t\) is designed to capture, instead of hand-coding regime buckets with fixed thresholds.

credit-leadlag.html (backed by js/creditCore.js + js/creditHmm.js, spec in docs/CREDIT_SIGNAL_SPEC.md) is the closer, already-built analog: it computes a lead-lag correlation between credit velocity (from ICE BofA US HY OAS) and a risk instrument's forward realized vol, scanning a fixed grid of lags over a fixed sample and reporting an out-of-sample information coefficient. That's a static, single-number characterization of the relationship's strength at each lag, refit over whatever window the job is run on — the same "frozen until you re-run it" property the OLS hedge-ratio example above has. A Kalman-filtered version would instead model the coefficient linking credit velocity to forward vol (at a chosen best lag) as a hidden state \(x_t\) that drifts smoothly, observed each day through the noisy realized relationship — letting the filter's own gain slow down when the relationship has been stable for a while and speed back up right after a genuine regime shift (when innovations \(e_t\) start coming in larger than \(S_t\) expects), without anyone hand-tuning a regime switch.

To be explicit about where this stands: this is not built, not prototyped, and not validated. It is a genuine candidate, not a claim of improvement. Choosing \(Q\) and \(R\) is itself a real modeling decision — as the pitfalls box below makes concrete, grid-searching them against a backtest is just overfitting the window-length problem in a different, more opaque parameterization, which is exactly the anti-pattern this repo's Lego Principle warns against (principle 4: a selector, not more knobs to optimize). Per Lego Principle 5, any Kalman-filtered version of this relationship would need to beat the incumbent fixed-lag correlation approach on true out-of-sample Sharpe or information coefficient, with a non-trivial OOS sample, before it replaced anything live — a more theoretically elegant estimator is not evidence of an improvement by itself.

The Ornstein-Uhlenbeck Process & Mean Reversion
🧩
Where this could plug into MacroFXModel

An unbuilt candidate next to dayTypeCore.js's drift/diffusion score

js/dayTypeCore.js already estimates something conceptually adjacent to \(\theta\): its dayTypeScore blends several rolling-window estimators (efficiency ratio, variance ratio, Hurst exponent, etc.) into a single \(T \in [0,1]\) trend-day-ness score — high \(T\) meaning drift-dominated (follow), low \(T\) meaning diffusion-dominated / mean-reverting (fade), all computed fresh within each day's window with no explicit reversion-speed or half-life concept. If an instrument's fitted half-life is very long (say 30+ days) even when dayTypeScore currently flags it as low-\(T\)/fade-favoring, that would be worth knowing — the "fade" bet might be directionally right but too slow for the exit logic the fade legs actually use.

To be explicit about what this is and isn't: this is a candidate connection, not a claim that dayTypeCore.js already does OU fitting, and not a claim that layering an OU half-life on top of it would improve anything. Per the Lego Principle, that would need to be built as a proper Tier-1 estimator (a pure function, unit-tested on synthetic OU-generated data so you can verify the fit recovers a known \(\theta\), before ever touching real prices), then validated OOS with a non-trivial trade count against the existing selector — not assumed to help because the math is elegant. It is currently unbuilt and untested here; treat it as a research candidate for LEGO_MODULES.md §2, not as a described feature.

The Heston Model
🧩
Where this could plug into MacroFXModel

Continuous-time cousin of the GARCH sigma this repo already runs — and no options module to connect it to

Say this plainly up front: this repo has no options-pricing module of any kind — no Black-Scholes implementation, no Heston implementation, no implied-volatility surface, nothing that prices a derivative contract. This lesson is pure theory background, exactly like the Black-Scholes and Merton jump-diffusion lessons before it. Nothing here describes existing code, and nothing here proposes building an options desk inside this repo.

What the repo does already have, built and running in production, is the discrete-time cousin of exactly the insight Heston's variance SDE formalizes. Per CLAUDE.md's core-modules table, js/volBacktestEngine.js implements GARCH(1,1) as one of three sigma estimators (alongside HV20 and Yang-Zhang) feeding the live forecaster's bands, with the recursion \(v \leftarrow \omega + \alpha\,r_{i-1}^2 + \beta\,v,\ \sigma_i=\sqrt v\) and fitted constants \(\alpha=0.06,\ \beta=0.91\) (a persistence of 0.97, roughly a 23-trading-day half-life). That recursion is discrete-time variance with its own memory — today's variance forecast depends on yesterday's shock and yesterday's own forecast. Heston's \(dv_t=\kappa(\theta-v_t)dt+\xi\sqrt{v_t}\,dW_t^2\) is, informally, the same idea — variance reverting toward a long-run level with its own source of randomness — expressed as a continuous-time SDE instead of a discrete daily recursion. Neither engine copies the other's code, and they are not claimed to be mathematically equivalent (GARCH is discrete and this repo's GARCH has no explicit correlation-with-price term the way Heston's \(\rho\) does); the connection is conceptual — "let variance itself be a process with memory and dynamics" is the shared insight, one lesson's discrete-time home and the other's continuous-time home.

The Merton jump-diffusion lesson is the other half of this repo's theory coverage of the smile: that lesson covers discontinuous jumps as one way Black-Scholes gets extended to explain a real smile; this lesson covers correlated stochastic volatility as a different mechanism producing the same observable symptom. Real practitioner models often combine both (the "stochastic-vol-plus-jumps" family mentioned above) rather than picking one mechanism as the single true explanation — this lesson does not take a position on which mechanism, or which combination, would best describe any specific FX pair's real smile, because that would require fitting real option data this repo does not currently source or store.

Building an actual Heston calibration engine here would be new, unvalidated work requiring a characteristic-function pricer, a calibration routine against a real implied-vol surface this repo does not currently have a data source for, and — per the Lego Principle — a concrete reason at least two callers would use it. Nothing here proposes that; this is theory background connecting an existing, tested brick (GARCH in volBacktestEngine.js) to the wider stochastic- volatility literature it belongs to.

Returns, Log-Returns & Compounding
🧩
Where this connects to MacroFXModel

The literal design rule behind the forecast engine's horizons

CLAUDE.md states the Lego Principle's third rule plainly: strategies here must be "horizon-agnostic. Daily / weekly / 20-day differ only by σ scale (×1 / ×√5 / ×√20) and window length." That is the square-root-of-time rule derived above, applied directly: 5 trading days scales daily σ by \(\sqrt{5}\), 20 trading days scales it by \(\sqrt{20}\), because log returns across those days are (approximately) independent and additive, so their variances add and their standard deviations scale by the square root of the day count. This is not a coincidental resemblance — it is the exact mechanism, and it's why js/forecastCore.js's HORIZONS table and js/volBacktestEngine.js's vol-sigma series are built to share one volatility calculation scaled by horizon, rather than re-estimating volatility separately at each horizon from scratch.

The gap this primer derives between arithmetic and geometric means, \(g \approx \mu - \sigma^2/2\), reappears by name and by formula in the Kelly Criterion lesson, where it is the mechanism behind volatility drag and the reason overbetting past the Kelly-optimal size can turn a genuinely positive-expectancy bet's compounded growth rate negative. And the lognormal price assumption this primer's log-additivity argument justifies is the starting assumption of the upcoming Black-Scholes lesson — geometric Brownian motion, the stochastic process Black-Scholes prices options under, is defined directly in terms of log returns being the natural additive unit of price movement, for exactly the reasons worked out above.
