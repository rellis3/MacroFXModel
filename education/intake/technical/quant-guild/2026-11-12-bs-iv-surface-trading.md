## Lesson
Four of the five Black-Scholes inputs (spot, strike, rate, maturity) are fixed by the market or the contract, and so is the
option's price. Volatility is the only unknown, so you invert the formula to get implied vol. Doing this per strike
and maturity gives the IV surface, which is not flat: OTM puts carry higher IV in equities ("fear is on the downside"),
and commodities can skew the other way. A desk that quotes every strike off one constant vol is internally inconsistent
and can be picked off. That's why exotic quotes come from a model calibrated to the whole surface (local vol: Derman,
Dupire; stochastic vol: Heston, SABR; jumps). He chooses that model on three criteria: consistent (arbitrage-free)
prices, efficient to compute ("10 million paths over the course of an hour" is useless), and "say as little as
possible about the true market distribution".

## Approach
- **Framing**: pricing, not forecasting. The surface is a consistency constraint for a market maker. Repeats the
  playbook's "name the job first".
- **Model choice**: the trade-off is richness against calibration efficiency. Use the fewest assumptions that still
  give consistent quotes.
- **Validation**: convergence plot of the IV root-finder (error to ~0). The Heston demo reproduces skew (put IV 22.71%
  vs call IV 17.54%) without being fitted, as a qualitative check.
- **Costs**: the desk's quote = risk-neutral price + adjustments + market-maker margin (deferred to a later video).

## What the code actually does
- IV solver: Newton's method on the *squared* pricing error (`newton(objective, sigma_init)`, where objective = (BS − C)²).
  That's fragile (the root of a square is a double root, so convergence is slow and the derivative vanishes there).
  Plain root-finding on BS − C, or Brent with vega, is standard. It works here for a well-behaved example.
- **The "arbitrage" simulation does not show the claim.** Paths are simulated with σ = 22.5%, which is the desk's own
  flat vol (the average of the 25% and 20% "market" IVs). Under that simulation the desk's quotes are fair by
  construction. The desk P&L uses undiscounted payoffs against discounted premia (r = 2%, T = 1), so the mean
  "desk EV" of about −$0.2 is the carry term (I recomputed: −0.196 at these inputs) plus Monte Carlo noise. It is not
  skew arbitrage. The narrative ("hit our bid on call A") has the desk *buying* A, while the code has the desk short A. No delta hedge is
  coded although step 3 says "delta hedge". The markdown prices ($12.50 / $4.20) don't match the code's BS prices
  ($15.59 / $5.91). The wealth-path plot then hard-codes `desk_ev = -0.25`, so it shows an assumed edge, not a found one.

## What we could learn
- **A simulation that is meant to show an edge must simulate under a world where the edge exists, and must be checked for
  a known zero.** Run it under the desk's own vol and the EV should be 0 to within MC error. His wasn't checked, so a
  carry term was read as edge. REPEAT of #1 (positive/negative control before the real run) and #2 (MC error band).
  That's the third QG item, and the first where the missing control produced a wrong conclusion. Strengthens the PROPOSED edits.
- **Don't hard-code the effect into a downstream illustration** (`desk_ev = -0.25`): DISAGREE as practice. Ours derive
  every number from the run (results files regenerated by script).
- Surface calibration, local/stochastic vol and exotics are outside the desk's job (rough-volatility agenda already
  records this). No card.
