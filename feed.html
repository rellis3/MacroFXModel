The US Treasury curve, week by week (5-week mean). Click any cell to pull out that week's curve and that tenor's history; arrow keys move the pick.

How to read the curve
  1. Colour and height are the yield. Short tenors at the front, 30-year at the back, five years left to right.
  2. Compare two weeks. Click this week, then the same week a year ago: the amber and dotted lines on the right show how the shape changed.
  3. Name the move. Short end falling faster than the long end is a bull steepener (markets pricing cuts). Long end rising faster is a bear steepener (inflation or supply worries). The label in the read line does this for you.
  4. 2s10s below zero (an inverted curve) has historically come before slowdowns, but with long and variable lags.

Ask: is the short end or the long end doing the moving, and does that match what the Fed is saying?

Liquidity

The Fed balance sheet, weekly.

Treasury General Account, reverse repo, bank reserves and net liquidity (balance sheet − TGA − RRP), plus the SOFR-less-IORB funding spread. "z of the week" scores this week's change against three years of weekly changes.

global liquidity ↗daily TGA/RRP vs NQ ↗net-liquidity regression ↗
blue: up on the week · amber: down · a rising TGA or RRP drains money from the system · the z column says how unusual the week wasFRED WALCL · WDTGAL · RRPONTSYD · WRESBAL · SOFR · IORB · weekly (H.4.1)
How to read liquidity
  1. Net liquidity = Fed balance sheet − TGA − reverse repo. It is roughly the cash the banking system has to work with.
  2. The TGA is the Treasury's bank account. When it rises (tax receipts, debt issuance) money leaves the system; when it is spent down, money comes back. The reverse repo works the same way.
  3. Read the z column first. Most weeks are noise. Beyond ±2 is a genuinely unusual week, worth opening the daily page for.
  4. SOFR − IORB above zero means banks are paying up for overnight cash: a sign of funding strain (as in September 2019).

Ask: is this a one-off (tax day, quarter-end) or the start of a trend? Remember it tested null for direction: background, not a trigger.

Cross-asset

Rolling correlations between markets.

63-day correlation of daily changes (log returns; the 10-year yield uses its change in level), three years, weekly. Blue above zero, amber below. Sorted by how much each pair moved over the last quarter: the change is the news.

FX correlation lab ↗one-trade absorption ↗yield coupling ↗
63-day window on daily changes · the biggest moves in the last quarter come firstOANDA SPX500 · XAU · WTICO · FRED DGS10 · DTWEXBGS
How to read correlations
  1. +1 the two move together, 0 unrelated, −1 opposite. Blue above zero, amber below.
  2. The change is the news. The pairs that moved most over the last quarter come first.
  3. S&P × 10-year yield is the big one. When it is negative, bonds hedge stocks (growth scares drive both). When it turns positive, stocks and bonds fall together (inflation scares), and a 60/40 portfolio loses its cushion.
  4. Dollar × gold is usually negative; when it is not, something else (central-bank buying, a crisis bid) is driving gold.

Ask: which relationship broke, and what story would explain it?

Context only. No correlation level has been tested as a signal here. The tested cousins are on the linked pages: Surface Lab's absorption (how much of everything is one trade) and the yield-coupling studies.

How to read the commodity map
  1. Each row is one market, each column a week. Height and colour show where it sat in its own two-year range: amber at the low, blue at the high, white at a new two-year high.
  2. Rows are ranked by how stretched they are now (z-score), most stretched first.
  3. Broad or narrow? If the basket row is high too, it is a broad commodity move (dollar, growth, inflation). If only one market is white, it is that market's own story (weather, supply, a squeeze).
  4. Click a row for its standing now, how long it has lived above its median, and its price.

Ask: is the move broad, and does it agree with what rates and the dollar are saying? A stretched market is not a signal to fade: that was not tested here.

Options

The implied vol curve, through time.

S&P implied vol at five expiries (VIX1D, VIX9D, VIX, VIX3M, VIX6M), daily. A curve that inverts (front above back) is the market paying up for protection now.

3D vol term surface ↗OI dashboard v2: dealer hedging map ↗VIX calculus ↗
height is the level of implied vol · colour is how stressed it is for that expiry · drag to turn itCBOE VIX1D · VIX9D · VIX · VIX3M · VIX6M · daily
How to read the vol curve
  1. Implied vol is the price of insurance. Each line is the expected size of S&P moves over 1 day, 9 days, 30 days, 3 and 6 months.
  2. Normally the curve slopes up (longer insurance costs more). When the front rises above the back (inverted), the market is paying up for protection now.
  3. The front ratio (9-day ÷ 30-day) is the useful number. Calm, normal or stressed sorts how often NQ and the S&P break their p75 range: about 21% / 29% / 35% of days.
  4. Use it for size, not direction. Stressed means expect bigger ranges: smaller positions, wider stops.

Ask: does the expected range this week change how big I would trade?
