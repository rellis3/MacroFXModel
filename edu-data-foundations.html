Data Foundations · Lesson DF-01

The Institutional Data Hierarchy

Where financial data actually comes from — and why some of the most valuable macro data in the world is completely free. By the end of this lesson you'll know the four tiers of the data world, which ones you already have access to, and how to avoid the silent data mistakes that make strategies look brilliant in a backtest and bleed in real life.

📌 Note

These are study notes, not conclusions — nothing here has been tested or judged yet. The frameworks are durable; specific prices and vendor costs drift over time.

1

Data sourcing is a risk function

First principles

At professional quant desks, choosing and checking data is not an IT chore — it's treated like risk management, with documented processes, validation steps and clear ownership. The reason is simple: your output can never be more reliable than your input.

💡 The Data Quality Principle — memorise this

"A sophisticated model applied to corrupted, misaligned, or biased data does not produce sophisticated analysis. It produces sophisticated-looking noise with a false sense of precision."

A signal that looks exceptional may be built on data that was unavailable at signal time (look-ahead bias), that excludes dead instruments (survivorship bias), or that was heavily revised after publication (revision blindspot). The model can't detect any of this on its own — you catch it, with process.

⚠ Why this matters for trading

Look-ahead bias has been documented in many published backtests. It is a plausible reason so many strategies look strong in testing and disappoint live. Data governance is the defence — and it's a skill, which means you can learn it. That's this whole block.

2

Provenance — where data actually comes from

Follow the chain

Most people consume data through Bloomberg or Refinitiv without ever asking: where did Bloomberg get it? For US government macro data, the chain looks like this:

Primary source
BLS · BEA · Fed
free, authoritative — the actual publishers
Aggregator / API
FRED
free, 800k+ series, one API
Tier-1 vendor
Bloomberg / Refinitiv
~$24k per seat per year
End user
You / your model
same numbers for these series
✓ What this means

For CPI, GDP, payrolls, unemployment, Treasury yields and the Fed Funds Rate, Bloomberg aggregates data that originates from the same public sources you can reach through FRED. The underlying numbers are identical. The premium buys normalisation, delivery and unified access — not exclusive data.

✕ What it does NOT mean

Bloomberg is not just a relay. For tick-level prices, OTC fixed income, global corporate fundamentals, analyst estimates and real-time exchange feeds, it provides genuine access with no meaningful free equivalent. The free-data argument applies to government macro statistics specifically.

3

The four tiers of the data world

The complete institutional hierarchy

Group every source by reliability, latency, publication governance, revision practice and cost. The tier a source sits in tells you how to use it and how much to trust it.

T1

Institutional premium vendors

$15,000 – $30,000+ / year per seat

Bloomberg, Refinitiv/LSEG, FactSet, S&P Global, Morningstar Direct, ICE, MSCI, IHS Markit. The infrastructure layer used by funds and banks.

T2

Primary government & central-bank sources

Free — the actual publishers

BLS, BEA, the Fed, Treasury, ECB, World Bank, OECD. Authoritative by definition — everyone else quotes them.

T3

Exchange, derivatives & positioning data

$0 – $5,000 / year

Exchange feeds and positioning reports. Star of this lesson: the CFTC COT report — institutional-grade positioning data, completely free (§5).

T4

Accessible APIs & retail aggregators

Free / freemium

The retail-accessible layer. Perfectly usable when you know each feed's provenance and limits.

Tier 1 under the microscope

These platforms do not create macro data — they ingest it from primary sources, clean it, normalise it, and deliver it fast with service guarantees. The premium pays for infrastructure, not data exclusivity.

What Tier 1 gives you
  • Point-in-time vintage data with full revision history
  • Tick-level resolution for equities, FX and rates
  • Cross-asset coverage from a single endpoint
  • Fundamentals with standardised accounting
  • Real-time delivery, <100ms latency guarantees
What Tier 1 cannot give you
  • A better model
  • Better analytical judgment
  • Protection against look-ahead bias in your pipeline
  • Statistical validity of your backtest
  • "Any of the things that actually determine whether a systematic strategy works"
🎯 Checkpoint — the big idea so far

The Tier 2 → Tier 4 path is entirely viable for macro systematic research. Pulling CPI from FRED reaches the same government numbers the $24k terminal shows. What you give up is tooling and latency — not truth. For a systematic macro stack like this one, that's a trade you can make deliberately, and it's why this platform already runs on FRED + free feeds.

Vintages — the feature that makes honest backtesting possible

FRED stores the full revision history of most series — called vintages — so you can pull exactly what was known on any historical date. GDP gets revised. Payrolls get revised. If your backtest uses today's revised numbers for a 2019 signal, it "knows" things nobody knew in 2019.

The one API call to remember
fred.get_series_vintage_dates("GDPC1")
fredthe authenticated FRED client (our FRED_KEY is already configured in Railway)
get_series_vintage_datesreturns every date this series was revised — the doorway into the revision archive
"GDPC1"the series ID — here real GDP, one of the most heavily revised series there is
Why you care: pick any historical signal date, pull the vintage that existed that day, and your backtest sees only what the market saw. That's the honesty upgrade Tier-1 vendors charge for — free.

The COT report — free institutional positioning

Tier 3's hidden gem

Every week the CFTC publishes who holds what across the major futures markets — FX, rates, indices, energy, metals, ags. Macro funds read it. CTAs read it. Most retail traders have never opened it. It's free, and it goes back to 1986.

Who's in the report — three kinds of player

Commercial hedgers

The "smart money"

Businesses with real exposure: corn producers hedging harvest, airlines hedging fuel, corporates hedging FX. They trade from need, not opinion — but at extremes they signal important fundamentals.

Large speculators

The most-watched

Hedge funds, CTAs, managed money. Extreme net positioning here has historically been contrarian at major turning points — when everyone is already long, who's left to buy?

Non-reportable

The noise category

Small specs below reporting thresholds. Treated as noise — computed as what's left over (see the formula below).

Where "non-reportable" comes from
non-reportable = total open interest − commercial − non-commercial
open interestevery futures contract currently outstanding in that market — the whole pie
commercialthe hedgers' slice (reported to the CFTC)
non-commercialthe large specs' slice (also reported)
non-reportablewhoever's left — too small to report individually: retail and small institutions

The six-step systematic method

This is the lesson's full recipe for turning raw positioning into a usable input — each step exists to fix a specific problem with the step before it:

1
Compute net speculator position

Longs minus shorts for the non-commercial category. Raw direction of the crowd.

2
Normalise by open interest

50,000 contracts is huge in one market, trivial in another. Dividing by OI makes markets comparable.

3
Apply a rolling z-score

"Is this position unusual for this market?" — the formula below.

4
Percentile rank vs 3-year history

The threshold: 95th percentile means specs have rarely been this stretched in three years.

5
Combine with price momentum

Extreme positioning alone is not a trigger — pairing it with momentum avoids catching falling knives.

6
Watch the commercials

Hedger extremes are the leading indicator — the people with real exposure repositioning first.

Step 3 unpacked — the positioning z-score
z = ( net% − mean(net%, 3y) ) ÷ stdev(net%, 3y)
net%this week's net spec position as a share of open interest (that's steps 1 and 2 combined)
mean(net%, 3y)what "normal" positioning has looked like in this market over the last 3 years
stdev(net%, 3y)how much positioning usually wobbles — the yardstick for "unusual"
zhow many yardsticks from normal we are right now. z = +2 → more crowded-long than ~97% of normal weeks
Why a z-score and not the raw number? Because "specs are net long 120k contracts" means nothing without context. The z-score answers the only question that matters: is this extreme for this market? In this repo, that's statsCore.rollingZScore — never re-write it inline.
⚠ Honest caveat

Extreme positioning has sometimes preceded reversals — the lesson is explicit that this is not reliable enough to trade in isolation. It's one input into a bigger picture, and any use here goes through the house harness: real costs, true out-of-sample split, ≥30 OOS trades.

6

Key takeaways & vocabulary

What to keep
  • Data sourcing is a risk function. Output quality is bounded above by input quality — always.
  • Government macro data is the same numbers everywhere. Bloomberg's edge for CPI/GDP/payrolls is plumbing, not access.
  • FRED is the sensible start: 800k+ series, vintages included, before paying for anything.
  • The Tier 2 → 4 path is viable for macro systematic research — this platform is proof-in-progress.
  • COT is free institutional positioning since 1986 — one input, not a standalone edge.
  • Provenance before trust. Can't name the primary source? Then you can't assess revision risk, reliability, or fit.
Look-ahead bias

Using data in a backtest that wasn't available at signal time.

Survivorship bias

Data that quietly drops dead/delisted instruments, flattering history.

Revision blindspot

Building on a series that was materially revised after first publication.

Provenance

The chain from primary publisher to you — who actually originated the series.

Vintage

The version of a series exactly as it existed on a given historical date.

Point-in-time data

Data reconstructed to show only what was known at each moment.

Open interest

Total outstanding futures contracts — the normaliser for COT comparisons.

Primary source

The original publisher (BLS, BEA, Fed…) — not an aggregator or vendor.
