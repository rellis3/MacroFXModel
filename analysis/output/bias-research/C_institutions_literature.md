The common structure across these, which I infer and which is **[UNVERIFIED]** as a description of any single bank:

1. **Slow anchor (months):** fair value (PPP/BEER/DEER), terms of trade, rate differentials. This sets the *structural* side only.
2. **Medium driver (weeks):** relative monetary policy expectations, usually read as front-end (2y or OIS) rate spreads. Carry and trend/momentum also sit here.
3. **Fast overlay (days):** positioning (CFTC/IMM, bank flow data), options skew (risk reversals), event calendar, month-end/fix flows.
4. **Risk filter:** volatility regime and event risk set the size of the position, including zero.

The rate-spread link is widely used, but the evidence I found is mostly commentary. Two-year spreads track EUR/USD closely. USD/JPY is said to follow 10-year or US-only yields more. The same note warns that both may be driven by risk appetite. https://www.fxstreet.com/analysis/will-the-rbnz-edge-out-norges-bank-to-be-the-first-high-income-country-to-hike-rates-next-week-202108151435 [SEC]. On the academic side, the BIS finds that the way hedge funds and leveraged investors trade can shape how exchange rates respond to monetary policy. The BIS treats CME non-commercial net positioning as "a reliable indicator of carry trades". https://www.bis.org/publ/bisbull124.htm [ABS]

### 1.2 Systematic macro / CTA frameworks

- **Trend speed is a choice between return and protection.** Man AHL ("The Need for Speed in Trend-Following Strategies", 2023) tested double-EWMA crossover models at several speeds. All have significantly positive Sharpe ratios before costs. After costs, risk-adjusted returns are "materially lower for faster speeds". Convexity in the worst S&P 500 quintile rises with speed. The fastest and slowest speeds correlate only 0.17. "The slowest trend speed cannot shift to a short position over a one- or three-month horizon." https://www.man.com/maninstitute/need-for-speed-trend-following [ABS: text read, the tables did not load]
- **TSMOM and moving-average crossovers are the same signal family.** In their general forms they are equivalent, and both are linear filters, as are HP and Kalman filters. In the empirical test on 58 futures, 12-month windows beat 1- and 3-month windows. Levine & Pedersen, FAJ 2016. https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/ufajxx/v72y2016i3p51-66.html [ABS]
- **Multi-lookback blend is the industry default.** Hurst, Ooi & Pedersen use 1-, 3- and 12-month TSMOM signals, each scaled to equal volatility and then combined, with the portfolio targeted at 10% volatility. All three lookbacks are positive across 137 years and in every decade. https://fairmodel.econ.yale.edu/ec439/hurst.pdf [SEC/ABS]
- **Time series vs cross section.** Momentum works better as a time-series (directional) strategy. Carry works about equally well either way. Value works much better cross-sectionally. In FX the difference is smaller because there is less of a global factor. Baz, Granger, Harvey, Le Roux & Rattray (Man AHL, 2015). https://cmegroup.com/education/alternative-investment-resource-center/research/dissecting-investment-strategies.html ; https://www.cxoadvisory.com/?p=27885 [SEC]

### 1.3 When they stand aside or cut size (documented mechanisms)

- **Volatility scaling.** Scaling exposure by 1/(last month's realised variance) raises Sharpe ratios for several factors, the currency carry trade among them, because expected returns do not rise with volatility. Moreira & Muir, JF 2017. https://www.nber.org/papers/w22208 [ABS]. CTA practice (vol targeting in Hurst-Ooi-Pedersen above) is the same idea.
- **Trend "turning points".** When the fast (2-month) and slow (12-month) signals disagree, the market is at a turning point. Assets with more turning points have worse 12-month trend Sharpe ratios. A dynamic blend of slow and fast signals helps. Goulding, Harvey & Mazzoleni, "Breaking Bad Trends" / "Momentum Turning Points" (JFE 2023). https://people.duke.edu/~charvey/Research/Published_Papers/P167_Breaking_bad_trends.pdf ; https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jfinec:v:149:y:2023:i:3:p:378-406 [ABS]
- **Crowded carry / crash risk.** Carry crashes come from sudden unwinds when funding liquidity dries up. Funding-liquidity measures predict FX moves. Carry losses *lower* future crash risk but *raise* the price of crash risk. Brunnermeier, Nagel & Pedersen (NBER Macro Annual 2008). https://www.nber.org/papers/w14473 [ABS]. The BIS describes the August 2024 yen-carry unwind in the same terms: https://bis.org/publ/qtrpdf/r_qt2409x.htm [ABS]
- **Scheduled events.** Real-time FX shows conditional mean *jumps* on announcement surprises. Bad news has a larger effect than good news. Volatility adjusts gradually. Andersen, Bollerslev, Diebold & Vega, AER 2003. https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w8959/w8959.pdf [ABS]. The direction of an event-day move depends on a surprise that cannot be known in advance. This is the empirical basis for standing aside or cutting size before tier-1 releases. That desks actually do this is **[UNVERIFIED]** as a documented policy.

## 2. Evidence on daily / short-horizon direction predictability

**Baseline: the random walk is very hard to beat.** Rossi's JEL survey (2013) says out-of-sample exchange rate predictability depends on the predictor, horizon, sample, model and evaluation method. The driftless random walk is the toughest benchmark. Taylor-rule and net-foreign-asset predictors, simple linear models and few parameters do best. https://econ-papers.upf.edu/papers/1369.pdf ; https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:aea:jeclit:v:51:y:2013:i:4:p:1063-1119 [ABS]

## 3. HMM / regime-switching and multi-timeframe evidence

### 3.1 What works: volatility and allocation regimes

- **Hamilton (1989)** introduced Markov switching (Econometrica 57(2):357-384). https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:aea:aecrev:v:80:y:1990:i:4:p:689-713 (cited within Engel-Hamilton) [ABS]
- **Engel & Hamilton (1990), "long swings" in the dollar.** In sample, a 2-state switching-drift model is preferred to the random walk. https://www.nber.org/papers/w3165 [ABS]. A later Monte Carlo likelihood-ratio re-test finds the evidence for switching in their quarterly data **inconclusive**. https://ideas.repec.org/a/bes/jnlbes/v23y2005p87-95.html [ABS]
- **Engel (1994), JIE: the most directly relevant FX result.** A Markov-switching model was fit to 18 exchange rates at quarterly frequency. It "fits well in-sample for many exchange rates". **By MSE it does not beat a random walk or the forward rate. There is some evidence it is better at predicting the direction of change.** At the 1-quarter horizon no Diebold-Mariano MSE difference was significant at 10%. https://users.ssc.wisc.edu/~cengel/PublishedPapers/JIEMarkov.pdf **[FT]**
- **Dueker & Neely (2007, JBF): Markov-switching FX trading rules.** Out-of-sample excess returns "modestly exceed" standard technical rules and stay profitable in the recent subsample. A portfolio of Markov and technical rules beats either alone on a risk-adjusted basis. **Statistical forecast-accuracy tests were mixed.** Risk-adjusted returns beat the best conventional rule for 3 of 4 currencies, but none of the differences is individually significant. https://s3.amazonaws.com/real.stlouisfed.org/wp/2001/2001-021.pdf [ABS]
- **Ang & Bekaert (RFS 2002; FAJ 2004).** They find a high-volatility regime with higher correlations and *lower means*. For all-equity portfolios the cost of ignoring regimes is small. The value is large when the investor can switch to cash or bonds. Out of sample, the regime strategy beats static allocation. https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:rfinst:v:15:y:2002:i:4:p:1137-1187 ; https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/research/financial-analysts-journal/2004/how-regimes-affect-asset-allocation [ABS]
- **Guidolin & Timmermann (2007).** Four regimes (crash, slow growth, bull, recovery) are needed for the joint stock-bond distribution. Means, volatilities and correlations all shift. Regimes matter out of sample. https://rady.ucsd.edu/_files/faculty-research/timmermann/regime-switching.pdf [ABS]
- **Kritzman, Page & Turkington (FAJ 2012).** Markov-switching models of turbulence, inflation and growth each give a persistent "normal" and "event" regime. Using them to scale risk-premium exposure beats static allocation out of sample, "especially for investors who seek to avoid large losses". https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/ufajxx/v68y2012i3p22-39.html [ABS]
- **Nystrup, Madsen & Lindström.** A 2-state model of stock returns is enough to make regime-based allocation profitable. Their MPC + time-varying HMM work (Quant Finance 2018) adds a trading penalty for robustness. https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:taf:quantf:v:18:y:2018:i:1:p:83-95 ; https://www2.imm.dtu.dk/pubdb/pubs/6808-full.html [ABS]
- **Shu, Yu & Mulvey (2024).** Statistical jump models add a penalty for every state change. They beat HMM-guided 0/1 strategies on US, German and Japanese equities (1990-2023, with costs and delays) in volatility, drawdown and Sharpe. The paper notes that HMM signals can be "short-lived and frequently inaccurate", which adds trading cost. https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.05272v3 [ABS]
- **Quantpedia (independent study).** A 4-state HMM on volatility, term structure, drawdown, credit and sector features, using filtered states only, with a sealed holdout. The model "**de-risks; it does not time**". It reduces drawdown, but its edge over a static portfolio with the same exposure is slightly negative. Its Sharpe is no better than inverse-volatility. A permutation test fails to reject the null. https://quantpedia.com/does-more-history-help-a-market-regime-hmm-and-the-trouble-with-out-of-sample-evaluation/ [ABS: article read, the tables are images]

**Synthesis.** Regime models reliably identify *volatility/turbulence* states, and they add value by **sizing exposure** (cutting risk in the bad state). The evidence that they predict *direction* is thin: in-sample fit is good, out-of-sample MSE is no better than a random walk, direction gains are modest and individually insignificant (Engel 1994; Dueker-Neely 2007; Quantpedia). Christoffersen-Diebold give the mechanism: volatility predictability turns into sign predictability only when the expected return is non-zero.

### 3.2 Common pitfalls (documented)

1. **Smoothed vs filtered probabilities.** Smoothed (forward-backward) state probabilities use future data. Only filtered probabilities can be traded. https://quantpedia.com/does-more-history-help-a-market-regime-hmm-and-the-trouble-with-out-of-sample-evaluation/ ; https://www.luxalgo.com/library/concept/hidden-markov-markov-switching-regimes.md [SEC]. An R/depmix example: strong SPY results in sample, "atrocious" once the look-ahead is removed. https://www.r-bloggers.com/2016/10/the-problem-with-depmix-for-online-regime-prediction/amp/ [SEC]. The QuantConnect HMM tutorial labels regimes with `smoothed_marginal_probabilities`, which is the look-ahead pattern. https://www.quantconnect.com/docs/v2/research-environment/applying-research/hidden-markov-models.md [SEC]
2. **Refit relabelling.** Refitting on new data moves past labels even with filtered decoding. Refit only on data before each decision date, and version the fitted model. https://www.luxalgo.com/library/concept/hidden-markov-markov-switching-regimes.md [SEC]
3. **Label switching and state collapse.** Short training windows leave some states empty out of sample, or collapse 4 states into 3. Train-test likelihood gaps widen as the window shortens. https://quantpedia.com/does-more-history-help-a-market-regime-hmm-and-the-trouble-with-out-of-sample-evaluation/ [ABS]
4. **Flicker / short-lived states lead to turnover.** Use persistence penalties (jump models) or trading penalties. Shu-Yu-Mulvey and Nystrup et al. above. [ABS]
5. **In-sample fit is not forecastability.** Engel (1994) [FT]. Also: the long-swing evidence is not robust to proper LR tests [ABS].
6. **Statistical vs economic evaluation can disagree.** Dueker-Neely found trading returns despite mixed accuracy tests [ABS]. Pre-register which metric counts.

### 3.3 Multi-timeframe structure: what is published

- **Horizon structure of trend.** TSMOM returns persist for **1 to 12 months, then partially reverse**. The paper studies the full lookback-by-holding grid and highlights 12-month lookback with 1-month holding. A diversified TSMOM portfolio has an annual **Sharpe > 1, about 2.5x the equity market**. Speculators (CFTC) trade *with* the trend for ~12 months and cut positions as it reverses. Only spot (not roll) contributes to the long-term reversal. Moskowitz, Ooi & Pedersen, JFE 2012. https://fairmodel.econ.yale.edu/ec439/jpde.pdf **[FT]**
- **FX momentum decays with holding period.** Cross-sectional FX momentum earns **~6-10% p.a. at 1-month holding periods**, and profits "slowly fade out" as the holding period lengthens, declining monotonically. With full bid-ask costs the best strategy (1-month formation, 1-month holding) **falls from ~10% to ~4% p.a.** Time-series (own-currency) momentum is profitable on average, but an equal-weight aggregate earns about half the cross-sectional return and Sharpe. Menkhoff, Sarno, Schmeling & Schrimpf, JFE 2012 (48 currencies, 1976-2010). https://faculty.washington.edu/ss1110/IF/Sarno%20Currency%20Momentum%20JFE%20%281%29.pdf **[FT]**
- **Fast/slow disagreement = turning points (an "alignment" result).** Goulding-Harvey-Mazzoleni define market cycles by the signs of a slow (12-month) and a fast (2-month) TSMOM signal: Bull = both +, Correction = slow + / fast −, Bear = both −, Rebound = slow − / fast +. Blends of intermediate speed turn this cycle information into positive unconditional alpha. More turning points mean a worse 12-month trend Sharpe. https://people.duke.edu/~charvey/Research/Published_Papers/P167_Breaking_bad_trends.pdf ; https://benny.aeaweb.org/conference/2021/preliminary/paper/492Ds6fk [ABS]. **This is the closest published analogue to "multi-timeframe alignment".** Note that their best result *blends* the speeds; it does not require them to agree.
- **Blending horizons.** An equal-weight short + long trend blend beats either alone in CTA replication (arXiv 2507.15876). A dynamic-weight study finds the medium horizon adds little (arXiv 2510.23150). With 21- and 252-day signals, a combination improves Sharpe and lowers turnover (arXiv 2202.10817). https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.15876 ; https://arxiv.org/html/2510.23150v2 ; https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.10817 [ABS; preprints, results conflict]
- **Hierarchical / multi-scale HMMs.** Oelschläger & Adam (Statistical Modelling 2021) put a coarse-scale HMM (bull/bear) over fine-scale HMMs on daily returns, separating short-term moves from long trends. Implemented in R package fHMM. https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2007.14874 ; https://cran.r-universe.dev/fHMM/doc/fHMM.html [ABS]. Chaudhary (2026) runs daily, 4h and 1h MS-GARCH on EUR/USD with a 27-state cross-scale tensor. It claims better *volatility* forecasts than GARCH, gives no numbers in the abstract and does not forecast direction. https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.06190 [ABS]
- **Not found:** a peer-reviewed test of the retail "trade only when daily, 4h and 1h trends agree" rule with out-of-sample hit rates. **[UNVERIFIED / gap]**

## 5. Implications for a desk that wants a 24h bias

1. **The documented edge is mostly at weeks to months, not 24h.** TSMOM persists over 1-12 months [FT]. FX momentum is strongest at 1-month holding and decays after that [FT]. Christoffersen-Diebold argue sign predictability is unlikely at the daily horizon [ABS]. A 24h bias should therefore be a slow signal (trend, carry, rate-differential momentum) *carried* to the day. It should not be expected to come from new daily information. Expect next-day hit rates only slightly above 50% **[UNVERIFIED]**. Judge the bias by return per unit of risk, not hit rate alone.
2. **Contemporaneous ≠ predictive.** The most striking published numbers (75% same-week direction from COT; R² 60% from order flow; RR co-movement) are concurrent. Check any bias input with a strict t → t+1 timestamp test (COT is released on Friday for Tuesday data).
3. **Use regimes for size, not sign.** Every robust regime/HMM result is about volatility and drawdown control (Ang-Bekaert, Kritzman et al., Nystrup, Shu-Mulvey, Quantpedia's "de-risks; does not time"). For direction the result is weak at best (Engel 1994; Dueker-Neely). Practical design: let a filtered-probability vol regime decide *how much* bias to express (including "no bias"), and let the trend/carry/rates stack decide *which way*.
4. **HMM hygiene is non-negotiable.** Use filtered probabilities only. Refit with an expanding window ending before each decision. Freeze model versions. Use persistence/jump penalties against flicker. Keep a sealed holdout. Report against a random-walk and a "vol-scaled always-long-trend" benchmark.
5. **Multi-timeframe: blend first, then gate on disagreement.** The published result (Goulding-Harvey-Mazzoleni) is that fast/slow *disagreement* marks turning points with worse trend performance, and that *blending* speeds earns the alpha. A sensible 24h rule: bias = sign of the blended trend. Cut size when the 2-week and 3-month signs disagree (a "correction/rebound" state). Requiring all timeframes to agree before acting is an untested retail heuristic **[UNVERIFIED]** and will mostly lower turnover and trade count.
6. **Stand aside on events.** Tier-1 releases and central-bank decisions produce surprise-driven jumps whose sign cannot be known in advance (ABDV; Faust et al.). A 24h bias should be marked "conditional" or reduced in size on those days. On month-end/fix days, consider bank rebalancing estimates as an overlay **[UNVERIFIED value]**.
7. **Positioning is a crowding and risk input, not a timer.** Extreme CFTC speculative longs in carry currencies signal crash risk if funding stress appears (BNP; BIS on August 2024). Positioning does not forecast next week's direction (Klitgaard-Weir). Treat the retail-contrarian rule as untested.
8. **For gold**, the WGC's own model treats momentum and investment flows as the short-run marginal driver, with the dollar and yields as opportunity cost. That supports the same trend + rates + risk stack used for FX [ABS].
