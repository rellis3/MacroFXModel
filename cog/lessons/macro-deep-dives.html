Macro Deep Dives

Three lesson modules on reading, validating, and trading macro regimes through the rates market: the six macro driver families and the growth/inflation regime framework, the US 10-year vs German Bund yield spread that leads EUR/USD by days to months, and a live 17–18 Feb 2026 case study where the spread moved a full trading session before the currency caught up.

COURSE NOTES — applied macro-regime & yield-spread trading frameworks ~30 min read Prereqs: The Taylor Rule, Interest Rate Parity & the Carry Trade
TL;DR
  • Six macro driver families (growth, inflation, policy, liquidity, sentiment, flows) combine into a four-quadrant growth×inflation regime — Goldilocks, Reflation, Stagflation, Deflation/Risk-off — and the transition between regimes, not the regime itself, carries the highest-value signal.
  • Regime detection uses rate of change, not levels, and any systematic edge — mean reversion, momentum, stat arb, or lead-lag — must clear the same validation gauntlet: in-sample → walk-forward → out-of-sample → Monte Carlo → sensitivity → paper trade, with walk-forward efficiency (OOS/IS) above 0.5.
  • The US 10Y − German Bund yield spread is the fundamental anchor for EUR/USD: because institutional flows (carry → funds → pensions → reserves → corporates) take days to quarters to execute, the yield move happens first and the FX adjustment follows.
  • Spread momentum, not just level, is the tradable signal — a widening spread that keeps widening implies more USD strength ahead as slower capital catches up. As of 02 Sep 2025 the spread sat at 149 bps, only the 21st percentile of its 5-year range, after narrowing 78 bps from the Dec-2024 peak.
  • A live 17–18 Feb 2026 case study shows the mechanism firing in real time: the DE−US spread fell all through Tuesday while EUR/USD ranged and mean-reverted; the currency didn't capitulate until Wednesday — a ~24-hour lead between the rates move and the FX confirmation.
  • The relationship is a tendency, not a law: it breaks down in risk-off events, central bank intervention, extreme positioning, and geopolitical shocks — check for those before trusting the signal mechanically.

Lesson 1 — Macro Drivers, Regime Detection & Validation

1.1 The drivers of asset prices (six macro factor families)

Macroeconomic variables create the fundamental currents that move markets. Understanding these drivers is the foundation of systematic macro trading.

Growth expectationsRisk appetite and corporate earnings expectations across all asset classes — GDP growth, employment trends, industrial production, leading indicators.
Inflation dynamicsReal returns, central bank policy, nominal-vs-real asset attractiveness — CPI, PCE, wage growth, inflation expectations.
Monetary policyLiquidity conditions and discount rates for all assets — Fed funds rate, QE/QT, forward guidance, global CB coordination.
Liquidity conditionsAvailability of capital flowing through the financial system — M2 growth, bank reserves, credit spreads, funding markets.
Risk sentimentAggregate risk appetite and potential for mean reversion — VIX, credit spreads, safe-haven flows, positioning data.
Global flowsCross-border dynamics and relative valuations — capital flows, currency movements, trade balances, reserve accumulation.

Memory aid: Growth, Inflation, Policy, Liquidity, Sentiment, Flows. Growth and inflation define the regime quadrant (§1.3); the other four modulate how a regime expresses itself.

1.2 The yield curve as economic barometer

The yield curve encodes market expectations about future growth, inflation, and monetary policy. Its shape is one of the most powerful predictive signals in macro finance. Term spread = Yield(10Y) − Yield(2Y).

Steep (normal)Expansion expected, positive carry. Asset map: cyclicals, banks, duration.
FlatTransition period, uncertainty — 6–12 months pre-slowdown. Asset map: quality, cash.
InvertedRecession signal, rate cuts expected — 12–24 months pre-recession. Asset map: duration, defensives; avoid cyclicals.
Bear steepeningRising long rates, inflation fears. Asset map: commodities, TIPS; avoid growth.
Bull steepeningRate cuts, early recovery. Asset map: risk assets, small cap.
💡
Revision point

Bear steepening = long end rises (inflation fear); bull steepening = short end falls (cuts arriving). Same shape change, opposite cause, opposite asset map.

1.3 The growth–inflation quadrant (regime classification)

Every macro environment can be classified into one of four regimes along two dimensions: growth trajectory and inflation trajectory. Each regime has distinct asset-class implications.

Growth rising, Inflation fallingGOLDILOCKS — OW: equities, credit, growth stocks, EM. UW: commodities, gold, cash.
Growth rising, Inflation risingREFLATION — OW: commodities, value, TIPS, energy. UW: duration, growth stocks, bonds.
Growth falling, Inflation fallingDEFLATION / RISK-OFF — OW: duration, quality, USD, defensives. UW: cyclicals, EM, high yield.
Growth falling, Inflation risingSTAGFLATION — OW: gold, energy, cash, TIPS. UW: equities, bonds, credit.
  • Goldilocks — the ideal environment: strong growth without inflationary pressure lets central banks stay accommodative; risk assets thrive.
  • Reflation — expansion with building price pressures; central banks begin tightening; real assets and value outperform.
  • Deflation / risk-off — contraction with falling prices; flight to safety dominates; central banks cut aggressively.
  • Stagflation — the worst environment: weak growth with persistent inflation leaves central banks trapped; real assets and cash are the only refuge.

1.4 Regime detection framework

Core principle: use rate of change, not levels — we care about trajectory, not absolute values. (A 55 ISM falling from 60 is deteriorating growth even though the level is expansionary.)

GrowthPrimary: ISM Manufacturing PMI, ISM Services PMI. Secondary: initial claims, LEI, industrial production. Signal: 3-month rate of change vs. 12-month trend.
InflationPrimary: Core CPI, Core PCE, 5Y breakevens. Secondary: PPI, wage growth, commodity indices. Signal: YoY change in 3-month momentum.

Implementation sketch from the lesson — the growth and inflation composites and their classification rule:

Growth composite0.4 · ISM_mfg + 0.4 · ISM_svc + 0.2 · (50 − claims_zscore)
Growth scorerolling 3-month mean of the composite minus rolling 12-month mean (3-month momentum vs. 12-month trend)
Inflation composite0.4 · core_CPI + 0.4 · core_PCE + 0.2 · breakevens
Inflation score3-month change in the composite (diff(3))
Classifygrowth>0 & inflation≤0 → GOLDILOCKS; growth>0 & inflation>0 → REFLATION; growth≤0 & inflation>0 → STAGFLATION; else → DEFLATION (threshold at zero for simplicity, or use percentiles).

Data note (from the lesson): the FRED API provides free access to these indicators — fred.stlouisfed.org or the fredapi Python package.

1.5 Regime transition signals

Regime transitions are often more important than the regime itself. Early detection of transitions provides the highest-value trading signals.

Goldilocks → ReflationEarly warnings: wage growth accelerating, commodity prices rising, breakevens widening. Duration: 3–6 months. Shift: rotate Growth→Value, add commodities, reduce duration.
Reflation → StagflationEarly warnings: PMIs rolling over while inflation sticky, yield curve flattening. Duration: 2–4 months. Shift: reduce equity beta, add gold, increase cash.
Stagflation → DeflationEarly warnings: credit spreads widening, inflation expectations falling, PMIs contracting. Duration: 1–3 months (often rapid). Shift: add duration aggressively, quality over junk, reduce commodities.
Deflation → GoldilocksEarly warnings: PMIs troughing, credit spreads narrowing, central bank easing. Duration: 3–6 months. Shift: add risk, reduce duration, favour cyclicals.
💡
Revision point

Stagflation→deflation is the fastest transition and is credit-driven — the early warnings are market prices (credit spreads, breakevens), not survey data.

1.7 Entry models — the four systematic strategy families

Different market inefficiencies require different exploitation methods.

⟲

Mean reversion

Extreme deviations from a central tendency tend to revert — z-score/RSI/Bollinger entries, best in range-bound, low-vol regimes.

↗

Momentum / trend

Winners keep winning via behavioural biases and slow information diffusion — 12-1 momentum, golden cross, breakout systems.

⇌

Statistical arbitrage

Related securities revert to a stable relationship — pairs, basket-vs-component, factor residuals; needs cointegration, not just correlation.

⏱

Lead–lag

Information propagates with a delay between related assets/markets — needs Granger causality, OOS stability, and an economic rationale.

⟲ Mean reversion — prices oscillate around a central tendency; extreme deviations tend to revert. Signals: z-score from MA (Z = (Price − MA) / σ, entry when |Z| > 2); RSI extremes (<30 oversold, >70 overbought); Bollinger penetration with volume confirmation; pairs spread deviation from cointegrated equilibrium. Best conditions: range-bound, low-volatility regimes; high mean-reversion coefficient assets; absence of strong fundamental catalysts.

↗ Momentum / trend following — winners keep winning; trends persist due to behavioural biases and slow information diffusion. Signals: time-series 12-1 momentum (Signal = Return(t−252 → t−21), skip the most recent month); 50/200-day golden cross; Donchian/ATR breakout systems; cross-sectional rank (long top decile, short bottom). Best conditions: trending markets, macro regime changes, high dispersion across assets, strong macro catalysts driving flows.

⇌ Statistical arbitrage — related securities should maintain stable relationships; temporary deviations create market-neutral opportunities. Types: pairs trading (long underperformer / short outperformer); basket vs. component (ETF vs. underlying); factor residuals (alpha after removing factor exposures); cross-asset (credit vs. equity, commodity vs. producer). Spread model: Spread = β₀ + β₁·Asset₁ − Asset₂ + ε. Key requirements: cointegration (not just correlation), an economic linkage explaining the relationship, sufficient liquidity in both legs.

⏱ Lead–lag relationships — information doesn't propagate instantly; some assets/sectors/markets lead others. Common pairs: copper → equities (industrial demand); credit → equity (credit often leads turning points); large cap → small cap (information flows to less liquid); futures → cash (informed traders use leveraged markets first). Measure: cross-correlation ρ(lag) = Corr(Xₜ, Yₜ₊ₗₐ𝒈). Validation required: Granger causality testing, out-of-sample stability, and an economic rationale for the lead.

1.8 Validation framework — rigorous strategy testing

"The quantitative structure that separates robust strategies from data-mined illusions. Every edge must survive this gauntlet."

Pipeline (in order): 1. In-sample dev — build and optimise on training data. 2. Walk-forward — rolling optimisation + test windows. 3. Out-of-sample — final holdout test (never touched). 4. Monte Carlo — assess robustness via simulation. 5. Sensitivity — parameter stability testing. 6. Paper trade — live market, no capital at risk.

Walk-forward optimisation — the gold standard. Repeatedly optimise on past data, test on the next window; final performance is the concatenation of all OOS test periods (mimics real trading — you never have future information). Walk-forward efficiency: WFE = OOS performance / IS performance. Target WFE > 0.5; below 0.3 suggests severe overfitting.

System development checklist — before backtesting: economic rationale documented · point-in-time data confirmed · survivorship bias addressed · transaction cost model built · OOS holdout data reserved. Before live trading: walk-forward analysis passed · statistical significance confirmed · Monte Carlo stress tested · paper trading completed (3+ months) · risk limits and kill switches set.

Lesson 2 — The Transatlantic Yield Spread (US 10Y vs. German Bund)

"The most important spread in global macro, and why it leads EUR/USD."

2.1 Snapshot (data through 02 Sep 2025)

  • US 10Y yield: 4.27% (▲ 7 bps) · German 10Y: 2.79% (▲ 16 bps)
  • Spread: +149 bps (▼ 9 bps) — 21st percentile vs. 5-year history
  • 5-year range: min 101 bps (2023-04-24) · max 227 bps (2024-12-24) · mean 169 bps
  • 2025 regime: narrowing — down 78 bps from the Dec-2024 peak. Markets pricing ECB cuts slower than previously expected while the Fed stays on hold.

2.2 The core principle — why the spread leads EUR/USD

Capital flows to where it earns the highest risk-adjusted return. When US yields rise relative to German yields, holding dollars becomes more attractive than holding euros. Global investors — pension funds, sovereign wealth funds, reserve managers, hedge funds — shift portfolios accordingly, creating structural USD demand that persists until the differential changes.

The key insight: portfolio flows take time to execute. Large institutions don't move billions overnight — they announce strategic changes, execute over weeks or months, and their flows show up in the currency market after the yield move that triggered them. This is why the spread leads.

2.3 The five transmission channels (memorise, with timescales)

1. Carry tradeHours to days — speculative capital borrows the low-yield currency (EUR) to buy USD assets; hedge funds/prop desks execute within hours.
2. Fixed-income reallocation1–4 weeks — bond fund managers shift allocations toward the higher yield as they rebalance and handle redemptions.
3. Pension & insurance flows2–6 months — liability-driven investors: higher US yields mean fewer dollars needed to fund future USD liabilities; investment-committee speed.
4. Reserve manager behaviourQuarters — central banks hold ~60% of reserves in USD; higher US yields justify higher allocations; slow but enormous notional.
5. Corporate treasuryVariable (months) — multinationals repatriate offshore earnings, choose funding currency; depends on corporate cash cycles.

Ordered fast → slow: Carry, Funds, Pensions, Reserves, Corporates.

The lead-lag chain: Fed hawkish signal → US yields rise → spread widens → carry trades (days) → fund flows (weeks) → real money (months) → EUR/USD falls → trend persists → until the spread reverses.

The trading edge: because institutional flows take time, a sustained spread move predicts continued EUR/USD movement even after the initial FX reaction. Spread momentum — not just level — is the powerful signal: a widening spread that keeps widening implies more USD strength ahead as slower capital catches up.

The tradable signal: it's the spread's momentum, not its level, that matters most — a widening spread that keeps widening implies more USD strength still ahead as the slower legs of institutional capital (funds, pensions, reserves, corporates) keep catching up.
Carry-trade mechanics live one lesson over. Channel #1 above is exactly the covered/uncovered interest-rate-parity trade: borrow the low-yield currency, hold the high-yield one, and the expected-return condition (and its violation) is what the Interest Rate Parity lesson derives from scratch. This lesson treats the carry trade as an observed transmission channel with a timescale; it doesn't re-derive the parity condition underneath it.

2.4 Practical applications — why the relationship is useful

  1. Observable in real time. Unlike GDP, inflation expectations, or current account data, the spread updates tick-by-tick. A spread gap on a Fed statement tells you — immediately — that USD-supportive flows are coming.
  2. It provides lead time. The lag between spread and FX creates a window to position ahead of slower institutional flows.
  3. It validates or rejects FX moves. EUR/USD moves sharply without spread confirmation → likely positioning/technicals/noise → tends to reverse (mean-reversion opportunity). Move with spread confirmation → fundamental backing, more likely to persist.
  4. It warns of regime changes. Prolonged divergence (spread widening for months but EUR/USD no longer falling) suggests the move is fully priced, other factors are offsetting, or a reversal is coming. Non-confirmation = "the easy money in the trend is over."
  5. It works in both directions. Symmetric: spread narrowing → capital flows out of USD into EUR-denominated assets → EUR strengthens.
High volatility / news-drivenHours to 1–2 days — fast money dominates, quick repricing.
Trending market1–2 weeks — institutional flows build gradually.
Range-bound / low vol2–4 weeks — flows sluggish, need accumulation.
Regime change1–3 months — strategic reallocations take time.

Pattern: more volatility ⇒ shorter lead.

2.5 Deep mechanics — from yield to FX, step by step

Scenario: Fed signals fewer rate cuts than expected.

  • T+0 — Powell suggests inflation is stickier; markets reprice; US 2Y jumps 15 bps within minutes.
  • T+0 → 1hr — term structure adjusts: US 10Y +8–10 bps; Bunds unchanged (no catalyst); spread widens 8–10 bps.
  • T+1hr → 1 day — fast money moves: macro funds and CTAs short EUR/USD for carry and momentum; −50–80 pips. The "obvious" quick move.
  • T+1 day → 1 week — asset managers rebalance Bunds → Treasuries; each purchase buys USD / sells EUR; EUR/USD drifts lower.
  • T+1 week → 1 month — real money (pensions, insurers) executes strategic rebalancing; large flows spread over weeks; EUR/USD grinds lower even as news flow quiets.
  • T+1 month+ — new equilibrium; the spread move is fully reflected; the trade is "done" until the next catalyst.

2.6 Why the 10-year specifically?

  • Duration sweet spot — long enough to reflect policy expectations, short enough to be liquid; the benchmark for institutional fixed income.
  • Real-money benchmark — pensions and insurers benchmark to 10Y; their rebalancing moves 10Y-duration assets; this is where the big flows are.
  • Global comparability — every major country has a liquid 10Y; the German Bund is the European risk-free rate.

Refinement: the 2Y spread is more sensitive to near-term policy and often leads the 10Y. Advanced practitioners watch both: 2Y for direction, 10Y for magnitude.

2.7 When the relationship breaks down (limitations)

Risk-off events (Lehman, COVID)USD rallies as safe haven regardless of yields; the spread can narrow (Treasuries rally) while USD still strengthens. Liquidity preference dominates yield preference; the relationship resumes once panic subsides.
Central bank intervention (e.g. Japan's JPY interventions 2022–24)Policy flows overwhelm fundamental flows; the relationship temporarily breaks.
Extreme positioningIf everyone is already short EUR, further spread widening may not push EUR lower — no one left to sell. The positioning overhang absorbs the signal. Monitor CFTC data.
Geopolitical shocks (e.g. Ukraine invasion)FX moves for non-yield reasons (energy security, trade, growth); the FX move may lead the spread — causality temporarily reverses.
⚠️
Critical rule

Never treat the spread–FX relationship as mechanical. It's a tendency, not a law. When correlation breaks down, ask why — the answer often reveals important information about market regime and sentiment.

2.8 What moves the spread itself (upstream drivers)

  • Monetary policy — Fed vs. ECB rate paths; divergence = widening; the primary medium-term driver.
  • Growth differential — stronger US growth pushes US yields higher; watch relative PMIs.
  • Inflation gap — higher US breakevens vs. Europe widen nominal spreads; inflation surprise = spread move.
  • Safe-haven flows — risk-off → Bund outperformance (European safe haven) → spread narrows temporarily.
  • Supply dynamics — heavy Treasury issuance vs. Bund scarcity affects relative pricing; fiscal policy matters.
  • Hedging costs — wide differentials make FX hedging expensive, reducing cross-border demand for higher-yielding bonds (a dampening feedback).
The mechanism behind "Fed vs. ECB rate paths." This lesson treats each central bank's rate path as an observed input to trade around. The prior question — what actually generates that rate path in the first place — is derived separately in the Taylor Rule lesson: a committee's policy rate as a function of its inflation gap and output gap. A widening gap between one economy's Taylor-implied-minus-actual rate and another's is a candidate leading explanation for why this spread might be about to move, before the spread itself has visibly shifted. That lesson works through this exact connection; it isn't re-derived here.

2.9 Bottom line + key takeaways (as given)

The yield spread is the fundamental anchor for EUR/USD — it tells you where capital wants to go, and because institutional flows take time, it provides early warning of currency moves. Use it to validate trades, time entries, and identify regime changes. Not right 100% of the time, but over the medium term it's the closest thing to a fundamental "compass" for the world's most traded pair.

  • The spread leads EUR/USD because institutional flows take weeks–months; the yield move happens first, the FX adjustment follows.
  • Five transmission channels: carry (days), fund flows (weeks), pensions (months), reserve managers (quarters), corporates (variable).
  • Spread momentum matters as much as level.
  • Use the spread to validate FX moves: no confirmation = often noise; confirmation = the move has legs.
  • Watch for breakdowns: risk-off, intervention, extreme positioning.
  • As of 02 Sep 2025: 149 bps (21st percentile) — relatively narrow, suggesting limited EUR downside unless the spread widens again.

Lesson 3 — Live Case Study: The Spread Moved First (17–18 Feb 2026)

A real-time example of the yield spread leading EUR/USD by ~24 hours.

3.1 The setup — two days, one signal

  • Tuesday 17 Feb: the DE–US yield spread declines early and falls steadily all session — the US yield advantage widening, a fundamentally bearish input for EUR/USD. But EUR/USD didn't reprice: it ranged and mean-reverted; spot traders saw noise. The rates market was already telling a different story.
  • Tuesday night: spread continues lower overnight; EUR/USD still hasn't responded; the lead extends to ~12–18 hours.
  • Wednesday 18 Feb: EUR/USD capitulates — hard selloff throughout the day, accelerating into the close. The spread keeps falling, confirming Tuesday's move was structural, not noise. What was a leading indicator Tuesday became a confirming indicator Wednesday.

3.2 Side-by-side state table

Tue morningYield spread ↘ declining · EUR/USD flat/choppy · Signal: divergence forming.
Tue afternoonYield spread ↘ still falling · EUR/USD ↗ mean-reverting · Signal: divergence widening.
Tue closeYield spread ↘ lower · EUR/USD recovered · Signal: max divergence.
Wed morningYield spread ↘ continued · EUR/USD ↘ starting to fall · Signal: convergence begins.
Wed afternoonYield spread ↘ accelerating · EUR/USD ↘ hard selloff · Signal: full alignment.

Lead time: ~24 hours — a full trading session to position before the currency caught up to what rates were already pricing.

3.3 The lesson (as stated)

Watching EUR/USD alone on Tuesday showed nothing actionable — rangebound, no momentum, no trend. Watching the spread showed a clear directional move not yet priced into spot. That gap between what rates are saying and what FX is doing — that's the edge. When the spread moves and spot doesn't follow, the question isn't "if" — it's "when."

⚠️ The warning was there: the divergence itself is the signal.
✓ Spread confirmed: once both aligned, the lag had closed.

3.4 Application rules — how to use this

  1. Watch for divergence. When the spread moves but EUR/USD doesn't respond, pay attention. The bigger the divergence, the more likely a snapback.
  2. Don't fight the spread. Long EUR/USD into a falling spread = fighting institutional capital flows. You might be right on short-term price action, but the fundamental current is against you.
  3. Use it for confirmation. Before entering a EUR/USD position, check the spread. Aligned = fundamental backing. Diverging = proceed with caution.
  4. Estimate your lead time. Normal conditions: hours to a couple of days (here ~24h). The more orderly the move, the cleaner the lead. In high-volatility regimes — risk-off, CB surprises, geopolitical shocks — the lag compresses toward coincident or can temporarily invert.

3.5 Next steps — quantitative lag modelling (the lesson's roadmap)

The case study demonstrates the lead-lag qualitatively; the next stage is to model it quantitatively — from observation to systematic measurement.

  1. Rolling lag correlation. Compute the optimal lag between spread changes and EUR/USD changes over a rolling window (e.g. 60-day). The lag isn't static — it expands in quiet markets and compresses in volatile ones. Tracking the rolling optimal lag tells you how much lead time to expect now.
  2. Granger causality testing. Formally test whether spread changes improve EUR/USD forecasts beyond EUR/USD's own history. Run on rolling windows to detect regime shifts. Causality can reverse — in risk-off episodes or when FX drives the narrative, EUR/USD may lead the spread. Detecting reversals is critical.
  3. Dynamic lag models. State-space or regime-switching frameworks that let the lag structure vary with volatility, liquidity, and macro regime. A static lag assumption will underperform.
  4. Regime conditioning is mandatory. Carry-dominant regimes: cleaner, longer lead. Risk-off: instruments move together or FX leads. Policy-divergence regimes: relationship strengthens. Liquidity crises: it can break entirely. Any quantitative model needs regime conditioning to avoid false signals.

What this repo actually has built, and what it doesn't

Before any EUR/USD entry: check the spread. Direction aligned → fundamental backing. Diverging → caution, or treat as a §2.4-point-3 mean-reversion setup. Divergence watchlist: spread moving while spot ranges = the Lesson 3 setup; the bigger and longer the divergence, the closer the snapback. Regime overrides: in risk-off conditions, suspend the normal spread logic — safe-haven flows dominate and causality may invert. Check positioning extremes (CFTC) before assuming spread moves will transmit.

This is not a hypothetical wishlist — the core pieces already exist in the codebase: the price↔yield-spread coupling in js/yieldCouplingCore.js computes rolling correlation, the standardized-gap residual, and the lead-lag offset (the exact "rolling lag correlation" §3.5 asks for) between an FX series and a signed bond-leg spread. js/compass.js wires EUR/USD's spread specifically to us10y vs. de10y (the German 10Y leg resolves to FRED series IRLTLT01DEM156N), so the US10Y−Bund pairing this lesson studies is a real, live-computed input, not just a chart concept. Separately, computeMacroRegime() in js/macro.js is a working four-quadrant Goldilocks/Reflation/Stagflation/Deflation classifier — but built from FRED market proxies (curve shape, VIX, HY OAS, NFCI, breakevens, TIPS) rather than the ISM/CPI-momentum construction in §1.4; treat the two as parallel, not identical, regime signals until one is validated against the other. CFTC positioning (the §2.7 "extreme positioning" filter) is already parsed in _worker.js and rendered via js/cot.js.

What's genuinely still missing: a Granger causality test (§3.5 item 2) — the repo has Engle-Granger cointegration (a different test, in js/hedgeSignalV2Engine.js) and the coupling engine's lag-scan, but no formal causality-direction test, and no regime-conditioned dynamic-lag model (§3.5 items 3–4) sits on top of the existing coupling primitive yet. Per this repo's own validation discipline, none of the above — built or not — should be treated as a proven EUR/USD edge until it clears a real walk-forward OOS test with a non-trivial trade count; what exists today is the measurement infrastructure the lesson's roadmap calls for, not a validated signal.

Data sources named in the lessons: FRED (fredapi) for growth/inflation indicators; CFTC for positioning; the 10Y yields for US and Germany for the spread. Sign convention care: Lesson 2 quotes US−DE (149 bps positive; widening = USD-supportive); Lesson 3's charts quote DE−US (falling = same thing); js/compass.js's EUR/USD leg config uses fxSign:-1 on the US10Y/DE10Y pair for exactly this reason. Fix one convention when building anything new on top of this.

Section 05

Key Takeaways

✓Six driver families feed one regime quadrant. Growth, inflation, policy, liquidity, sentiment and flows combine into a four-quadrant growth×inflation regime (Goldilocks/Reflation/Stagflation/Deflation) — and the transition between regimes, not the regime itself, carries the highest-value signal.
✓Detect regime by rate of change, not level. A 55 ISM falling from 60 is deteriorating growth even though the level is still expansionary — trajectory is what the framework scores.
✓The US10Y−Bund spread is EUR/USD's fundamental anchor. Because institutional flows (carry → funds → pensions → reserves → corporates) take days to quarters to fully execute, the yield move happens first and the FX adjustment follows.
✓Spread momentum, not just level, is the tradable signal. A widening spread that keeps widening implies more USD strength still ahead as slower capital keeps catching up.
✓The Feb 2026 case study showed the mechanism firing live. The DE−US spread fell all through Tuesday while EUR/USD ranged and mean-reverted; the currency didn't capitulate until Wednesday — a ~24-hour lead between the rates move and the FX confirmation.
⚠The relationship is a tendency, not a law. It breaks down in risk-off events (safe-haven USD demand), central bank intervention, extreme positioning, and geopolitical shocks — when correlation breaks, ask why rather than trusting the signal mechanically.
⚠None of this is a validated edge yet. Every systematic idea here (mean reversion, momentum, stat arb, lead-lag) must clear in-sample → walk-forward → out-of-sample → Monte Carlo → sensitivity → paper trade with WFE > 0.5 before it's trusted; per the repo notes above, the coupling/regime infrastructure exists, but the formal Granger-causality test and a real walk-forward OOS run on it do not yet.
Section 06

Self-test

Q1. Name the six macro driver families and the representative series for each.
Growth expectations (GDP growth, employment trends, industrial production, leading indicators); Inflation dynamics (CPI, PCE, wage growth, inflation expectations); Monetary policy (Fed funds rate, QE/QT, forward guidance, global CB coordination); Liquidity conditions (M2 growth, bank reserves, credit spreads, funding markets); Risk sentiment (VIX, credit spreads, safe-haven flows, positioning data); Global flows (capital flows, currency movements, trade balances, reserve accumulation).
Q2. Draw the growth–inflation quadrant with overweights and underweights per regime. Which regime is best for USD? For gold? For duration?
Growth rising + Inflation falling = Goldilocks (OW equities/credit/growth stocks/EM, UW commodities/gold/cash); Growth rising + Inflation rising = Reflation (OW commodities/value/TIPS/energy, UW duration/growth stocks/bonds); Growth falling + Inflation falling = Deflation/Risk-off (OW duration/quality/USD/defensives, UW cyclicals/EM/high yield); Growth falling + Inflation rising = Stagflation (OW gold/energy/cash/TIPS, UW equities/bonds/credit). Best for USD: Deflation/risk-off (+7.1% historically). Best for gold: Stagflation (+21.4%). Best for duration: Deflation (+11.4%).
Q3. Why does the framework use rate of change rather than levels?
Because the framework cares about trajectory, not absolute value — a 55 ISM falling from 60 is deteriorating growth even though the level is still expansionary. Regime detection is about the direction of travel, not a static snapshot.
Q4. What are the five yield-curve shapes and their asset implications? What lead time does inversion give before recession?
Steep/normal (expansion expected — cyclicals, banks, duration); Flat (transition, 6–12 months pre-slowdown — quality, cash); Inverted (recession signal — duration, defensives, avoid cyclicals); Bear steepening (rising long rates/inflation fears — commodities, TIPS, avoid growth); Bull steepening (rate cuts/early recovery — risk assets, small cap). Inversion leads recession by 12–24 months.
Q5. Which regime transition is fastest, what are its early warnings, and what's the prescribed positioning shift?
Stagflation → Deflation, 1–3 months (often rapid), credit-driven. Early warnings: credit spreads widening, inflation expectations falling, PMIs contracting (market prices lead, not survey data). Positioning shift: add duration aggressively, quality over junk, reduce commodities.
Q6. Write the growth-score and inflation-score constructions (weights and momentum windows).
Growth composite = 0.4·ISM_mfg + 0.4·ISM_svc + 0.2·(50 − claims_zscore); growth score = rolling 3-month mean of the composite minus rolling 12-month mean. Inflation composite = 0.4·core_CPI + 0.4·core_PCE + 0.2·breakevens; inflation score = 3-month change in the composite (diff(3)).
Q7. List the four entry-model families, one signal construction for each, and the conditions each works best in.
Mean reversion — z-score from MA, |Z|>2 entry; best in range-bound, low-vol regimes. Momentum/trend — 12-1 time-series momentum, Return(t−252→t−21); best in trending markets/regime changes. Statistical arbitrage — spread = β₀+β₁·Asset₁−Asset₂+ε; best with cointegration, economic linkage, and liquidity in both legs. Lead-lag — cross-correlation ρ(lag)=Corr(Xₜ,Yₜ₊ₗₐ𝒈); best when validated by Granger causality, OOS stability, and economic rationale.
Q8. What three validations does a lead-lag relationship require before use?
Granger causality testing, out-of-sample stability, and an economic rationale for the lead.
Q9. Define walk-forward efficiency, the target, and the overfitting threshold. List the six stages of the validation pipeline in order.
WFE = OOS performance / IS performance; target > 0.5; below 0.3 suggests severe overfitting. Pipeline: 1. In-sample dev, 2. Walk-forward, 3. Out-of-sample, 4. Monte Carlo, 5. Sensitivity, 6. Paper trade.
Q10. State the five spread→FX transmission channels in speed order with actors and timescales.
1. Carry trade (hours–days, hedge funds/prop desks). 2. Fixed-income reallocation (1–4 weeks, bond fund managers). 3. Pension & insurance flows (2–6 months, liability-driven investors). 4. Reserve manager behaviour (quarters, central banks — ~60% of reserves in USD). 5. Corporate treasury (variable, months — multinationals).
Q11. Why the 10Y tenor specifically (three reasons)? What is the 2Y refinement?
Duration sweet spot (long enough for policy expectations, short enough to be liquid); real-money benchmark (pensions/insurers benchmark to 10Y); global comparability (every major country has a liquid 10Y; the Bund is the European risk-free rate). The 2Y refinement: it's more sensitive to near-term policy and often leads the 10Y — "2Y for direction, 10Y for magnitude."
Q12. Give the four conditions under which the spread–FX relationship breaks down and what dominates in each.
Risk-off events (Lehman, COVID) — USD rallies as safe haven regardless of yields, liquidity preference dominates. Central bank intervention (e.g. Japan's JPY interventions 2022–24) — policy flows overwhelm fundamental flows. Extreme positioning — an overhang of existing short EUR absorbs the signal; monitor CFTC data. Geopolitical shocks (e.g. Ukraine invasion) — FX moves for non-yield reasons and causality can temporarily reverse.
Q13. What are the six upstream drivers of the spread itself?
Monetary policy (Fed vs. ECB paths), growth differential (relative PMIs), inflation gap (breakevens), safe-haven flows (risk-off narrows the spread via Bund outperformance), supply dynamics (Treasury issuance vs. Bund scarcity), and hedging costs (wide differentials dampen cross-border demand — a self-limiting feedback).
Q14. In the Feb 2026 case study: what did each instrument do on Tuesday, when was maximum divergence, and what was the total lead time?
Tuesday: the yield spread declined steadily all session (US yield advantage widening) while EUR/USD ranged and mean-reverted, not repricing at all — the divergence built and widened through the day. Maximum divergence occurred at Tuesday's close (spread lower, EUR/USD recovered). Total lead time: ~24 hours — a full trading session before EUR/USD capitulated on Wednesday.
Q15. What are the four application rules from Lesson 3?
1. Watch for divergence — the bigger it is, the more likely a snapback. 2. Don't fight the spread — trading against it means fighting institutional capital flows. 3. Use it for confirmation before any EUR/USD entry — aligned means fundamental backing, diverging means caution. 4. Estimate your lead time — hours to a couple of days normally, compressing toward coincident (or inverting) in high-volatility/risk-off/CB-surprise/geopolitical regimes.
Q16. What are the four items in the quantitative lag-modelling roadmap, and why does a static lag assumption underperform?
1. Rolling lag correlation (60-day rolling window). 2. Granger causality testing on rolling windows, watching for causality reversals. 3. Dynamic/regime-switching lag models (state-space frameworks). 4. Mandatory regime conditioning (carry-dominant = cleaner longer lead; risk-off = coincident or FX leads; policy-divergence = relationship strengthens; liquidity crises = can break entirely). A static lag assumption underperforms because the true lag itself is regime-dependent — it expands in quiet markets, compresses in volatile ones, and can even reverse direction, so any single fixed-lag model will misspecify most periods.
Q17. Numbers check: spread level/percentile/range/mean as of Sep 2025; USD reserve share; typical lead times by market condition.
Spread (Sep 2025): 149 bps, 21st percentile of the 5-year range (101–227 bps, mean 169 bps). USD reserve share: ~60% of global FX reserves. Lead times: high-vol/news-driven = hours to 1–2 days; trending market = 1–2 weeks; range-bound/low vol = 2–4 weeks; regime change = 1–3 months.
