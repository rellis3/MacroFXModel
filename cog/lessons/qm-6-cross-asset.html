Cross-Asset Synthesis, Part 1: The Framework

Part 1 of 2. How a global macro fund reads rates, credit, currencies, equities and volatility as one story: the growth-and-inflation regime map, the data dashboard, the order in which markets react, and why stock-bond correlation is a variable. Part 2 tests the framework against three real episodes and asks where edge actually lives.

COURSE NOTES — framework as taught, not a backtested strategy ~27 min read Difficulty: Intermediate Prereqs: Quant & Macro 5 (currency flows) and the earlier lessons in this course
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • One macro view can be expressed through rates, credit, currencies, equities, volatility or gold, each with a very different risk and reward. Choosing the expression, and watching what should confirm it, is the institutional habit. Looking at equities alone is watching one character of the film.
  • Growth (accelerating or decelerating) and inflation (rising or falling) make four regimes: Goldilocks, Reflation, Stagflation and Deflation. Each leans different asset classes. The money is in spotting the transition early, not in labelling the regime after the fact.
  • Markets that all tell the same story are coherent, and trends tend to persist. When they disagree, someone is wrong, the regime is changing, or the picture is genuinely uncertain. Divergences do not say what to do; they say investigate.
  • Correlations are variables, not constants. The stock-bond correlation is negative when growth fears drive markets and positive when inflation fears do; 2022 was the year the assumption broke. In a liquidity crisis, correlations head towards 1.
  • This is part 1 of 2. It builds the framework and tests it with simulations. Part 2 works three real episodes (2022, Q4 2023, March 2023), shows how one view is expressed and sized across the capital structure, and gives the honest verdict: understanding the framework is not an edge, and none of it is proved here.
A note on the numbers in this lesson. The specific correlations, levels and percentages in the course notes are exemplar data representing typical historical ranges, as the course material itself states. Every simulation in this lesson is labelled as a simulation and uses made-up numbers.
Section 01

Why this matters: one view, many expressions

A trader who only watches one market has one clue. A macro fund watches all of them, because the same underlying belief shows up in different places at different times, with different amounts of noise. Suppose the belief is "the Fed will turn dovish earlier than the market expects". A global macro fund asks five questions about it before it puts on a trade:

  1. What is the highest-conviction expression? Perhaps two-year rates, not equities.
  2. What is the best risk-adjusted expression? Perhaps rate options, for convexity.
  3. What should confirm that we are right? Credit tightening, a weaker dollar.
  4. What invalidates the thesis? Inflation re-accelerating.
  5. What is the time horizon? Weeks, months or quarters.

The same view can be expressed across the whole capital structure with very different risk and reward. Picking the expression, and watching the confirming signals, is what separates institutional thinking from retail thinking in this course's framing.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Why watching only equities is watching one character of a film

Think of the five layers as five witnesses to the same event. Government bond markets are cleaner, dominated by institutions, and mathematically constrained (a bond's price is tied to its yield by arithmetic), so they tend to price macro news first. Credit bridges company health and the macro picture; it is where cautious, downside-focused investors live. FX is policy divergence made tradeable. Equities have the broadest participation, so they are noisy, narrative-driven and have retail flows, which is why they are often the last to price a macro shift. Volatility is insurance pricing: how much people will pay to be protected.

If you only watch equities, the rates, credit and currency moves that already told the story hours-to-weeks earlier go unseen. The point is not that equities are wrong. They are the witness who arrives last and talks loudest.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine rain is coming. The ants hurry underground first, then the birds go quiet, then the leaves turn over, and only at the end do the people say "it looks like rain". Each one noticed a different sign, at a different time. If you only listen to the people, you hear it last. A macro trader listens to all of them.

The capital structure as an information flow

The course lays the layers out as a top-down information flow, from the market that is usually first to move down to the one that is usually last:

A discrepancy in the notes, left visible

The cascade order is not fixed

The course states the order two ways: Rates → Credit → FX → Equities in its first section and key takeaways, but Rates → FX → Credit → Equities in its lead-lag section. The case studies in Part 2 show the order changes with the episode: late 2023 went rates, then credit, then FX, then equities; March 2023 started in credit. Treat the cascade as a hypothesis about who tends to move first that you test, not as a rule.

Coherence and incoherence

  • High coherence: every asset class prices the same scenario. Trends tend to persist, and trend-following tends to work.
  • Low coherence: the stories conflict. Either someone is wrong (an opportunity), or markets are in transition (wait), or the uncertainty is genuine (reduce risk).
  • Coherence informs conviction and size, not just direction.

The course adds a quant note: coherence is measurable. A coherence score could track the stock-bond correlation against what the current regime would predict, the credit-equity correlation against its norm, and the alignment of FX with rate differentials, then aggregate the deviations into a single "regime uncertainty" reading. High- and low-coherence periods may suit trend-following and mean-reversion differently, which is testable.

Not tested here A cross-asset coherence score has not been tested on this desk. The idea that trend-following does better in high-coherence periods and mean-reversion in low-coherence ones is the course's, not a result.
How to test it: build the score from information available on each date only (rolling correlations against a pre-registered norm), split days into high and low terciles, and compare trend and mean-reversion returns after costs in each, on a period held out in advance. The honest outcome includes "no difference".
The reality check, in the course's own words: understanding the framework is not the same as having an edge. Markets are efficient at short horizons. The institutional edge is superior data infrastructure, systematic implementation, longer horizons and the capacity to hold through volatility. This part builds the framework; Part 2 returns to why that matters.
Section 02

The four-quadrant regime map

Growth and inflation are the two axes that matter most; Fed policy, earnings and credit conditions mostly flow from them. Growth is accelerating or decelerating (ISM, employment, GDP). Inflation is rising or falling (CPI, PCE, breakevens). Cross the two and you get four worlds.

Weights are a smooth blend: growth-up probability \(p_g=\sigma\!\big((\text{ISM}-50)/1.5\big)\) and inflation-up probability \(p_\pi=\sigma\!\big((\text{CPI}-3)/0.5\big)\), with \(\sigma\) the logistic function; each regime's weight is the product (for example Goldilocks \(=p_g(1-p_\pi)\)). 🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Two questions describe the weather of the economy: "is it speeding up or slowing down?" and "are prices rising or falling?" Two yes/no questions make four kinds of weather. In each kind of weather, some things do well, like ice cream on a hot day or umbrellas on a wet one. The trick is not naming today's weather; it is noticing the forecast changing.

Regime transitions: where the money is made

The course's point is that the label matters less than the move between labels. For each transition it names leading indicators (what moves first) and confirmation (what should follow if the move is real):

TransitionLeading indicatorsConfirmation
→ GoldilocksBull steepening, credit tightening, stable breakevensEquity breadth, volatility declining, EM FX strengthening
→ ReflationBreakevens rising, commodities breaking out, bear steepeningValue beats growth, banks rallying, TIPS beat nominals
→ StagflationGrowth data weakening with breakevens elevated, curve flattening from the short endCredit widening, equities falling, gold and commodities holding
→ DeflationBull flattening, credit blowing out, volatility spikingJPY surging, gold bid on a real-yield collapse, quality dominating

How a quant would identify regimes (from the course): rule-based thresholds (such as ISM above 50, or core CPI year-on-year above 3%), which are interpretable but arbitrary; Hidden Markov Models, which are elegant but can overfit; and machine-learning clustering on macro indicators. Robustness means testing across methods rather than trusting one.

The critical caveat: regime identification is inherently backward-looking. By the time you are confident of the regime, much of the move has happened. The edge, if there is one, is early transition detection through forward-looking indicators and systematic monitoring, which is a much harder problem than naming the regime.
Not tested here Regime-conditional allocation (the "Leans" column) has not been tested on this desk. It is the course's framework, and a table of leans is not a backtest.
How to test it: classify each month from data published by then (vintage values, not later revisions), hold the table's leans for the next month, and compare against an equal-weight benchmark after costs, in-sample and out-of-sample, with the thresholds fixed beforehand. Repeat with a second classifier (a different threshold or a hidden-state model) and require the result to survive both.
Section 03

The macro dashboard: data architecture

The course describes a dashboard built in six tiers, designed for medium-term positioning, weeks to months, not daily trading. Trading these signals daily is, in its words, a common retail mistake: markets are efficient at short horizons, and the edge, if any, sits at longer horizons against behavioural biases and structural flows.

Watch the formula build
The net-liquidity proxy, one subtraction at a time
\[ \text{NL} = \color{#fbbf24}{\text{Fed balance sheet}} \]

Start with the size of the Fed's balance sheet (FRED series WALCL, weekly). Expanding it (QE) injects liquidity; shrinking it (QT) withdraws it.

\[ \text{NL} = \text{Fed balance sheet} - \color{#fbbf24}{\text{TGA}} \]

Subtract the Treasury General Account (WTREGEN), the government's own cash balance at the Fed. When it rises it drains liquidity from the banking system; when it falls it injects.

\[ \text{NL} \approx \text{Fed balance sheet} - \text{TGA} - \color{#10b981}{\text{RRP}} \]

Subtract the overnight reverse repo facility (RRPONTSYD, daily). A high balance means excess liquidity is parked idle; a falling balance means it is being deployed. Rising net liquidity tends to help risk assets; falling is a headwind.

NLnet liquidity proxy: liquidity available to markets
WALCLFed balance sheet (weekly): QE up, QT down
TGATreasury's cash at the Fed: rising drains liquidity
RRPreverse repo balance: idle cash parked at the Fed
"Not mechanical." The course is explicit that net liquidity is a condition, not a signal. It tells you the wind direction, not when to buy.
🧮
Worked example (illustrative round numbers, not actual balances)

Why a rising TGA is a headwind even when the Fed is doing nothing

Suppose the Fed's balance sheet is $8.0 trillion, the TGA is $0.5 trillion and reverse repo holds $1.0 trillion. Then \(\text{NL} = 8.0 - 0.5 - 1.0 = 6.5\) trillion. The Treasury now rebuilds its cash by $0.3 trillion, with nothing else changed: \(\text{NL} = 8.0 - 0.8 - 1.0 = 6.2\) trillion, a fall of $0.3 trillion. Nothing about monetary policy moved, yet the proxy fell, because cash moved from the banking system into the government's account.

Lead-lag and divergences

Because information flows through markets in sequence, a move in one layer can be an early warning for another. The course gives a playbook by scenario, and tells you what to do when the layers disagree.

ScenarioWhat you would seeVersus its opposite
Growth scareBull flattener, credit widening, copper weak, defensives leadingRecovery: bear steepener, credit tight, cyclicals leading
Inflation scareBear steepener, breakevens rising, gold bid, TIPS outperformDisinflation: bull flattener, breakevens falling, growth stocks outperform
Fed pivot (dovish)Front end rallying, USD weak, gold bid, duration outperformingHawkish: front end selling, USD strong, real yields rising

Reading divergences

When asset classes disagree, someone is wrong, or you are missing information. The course's three examples:

⚠Equities rallying, credit widening. Credit sees risks equities ignore; historically credit is often right at turning points.
⚠Yields falling, equities flat. Bonds are pricing a growth fear equities have not acknowledged. Either bonds overreact or equities catch down.
⚠JPY strengthening, equities rallying. Haven flows against the risk narrative: possible intervention, a positioning unwind, or an early risk-off warning.

A divergence does not tell you what to do. It tells you to investigate: why the disagreement, and what would resolve it?

Divergence flag: equities and credit stop agreeing (simulation, not market data)

Testing lead-lag properly

"Credit leads equities" is a claim about timing, and timing claims can be tested. The course names four tools: cross-correlation across lags, Granger causality, VAR models, and rolling-window stability tests. Cross-correlation is the simplest:

Watch the formula build
The cross-correlation at lag k, one idea at a time
\[ \rho(k) = \operatorname{corr}\big(\color{#fbbf24}{X_t},\; Y_{t+k}\big) \]

Pair today's value of the candidate leader \(X\) with the follower \(Y\) a lag of \(k\) periods later. If \(X\) leads \(Y\), the correlation should be strongest at some positive \(k\), not at \(k = 0\).

\[ k^{*} = \color{#10b981}{\arg\max_k}\; \rho(k) \]

Scan a range of lags and find the one where the relationship peaks. A peak at \(k^* = 0\) means the two move together; a peak at \(k^* > 0\) is evidence that \(X\) leads.

\[ \text{rolling: recompute } \rho(k) \text{ on windows } [t-w,\, t] \]

Then check the answer is stable across windows. A lead that exists in one sample and vanishes in the next is not a lead.

Xtthe candidate leader at time t (say a rates move)
Yt+kthe follower k periods later (say credit or equities)
ρ(k)correlation between them at lag k

In this simulation Y is built as 0.7 times X from 5 days earlier plus noise, so the true lead is 5 days. The leader is a smoothly wandering series (an AR(1) with persistence 0.8), which is why neighbouring lags also correlate. The band is the usual \(2/\sqrt{n}\) rule of thumb, and it is too narrow for persistent series, so real tests use more careful methods. In real data the delay is not a fixed 5, and the course's own finding is that leads are strongest at regime turning points and weakest in stable trends.
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

If you clap and an echo comes back a moment later, you can find the delay by sliding a copy of your clap sound along until the two line up. That is lead-lag testing: slide one series along the other until they match best. The slide distance is the delay. If the match is not clearly better than sliding randomly, there is no echo.

⚠️
The critical finding in the course

The signal is most useful exactly when it is hardest to trust

Lead-lag relationships are often strongest at regime turning points and weakest during stable trends. That is when a leading signal is most valuable, and it is also when you have the fewest clean examples and the most nerves. The course concludes that this is where systematic testing beats discretion. The same fact is also a warning: a lead that only appears at turning points can look spectacular in a handful of memorable episodes and be useless as a rule.

Section summary (as given): information flows through markets in sequence; lead-lag reflects market structure (who participates and how fast they process); confirmation builds conviction (more asset classes agreeing, more confidence); divergences require investigation (mispricing, transition, or missing information).

Not tested here "Credit leads equities at turning points" has not been tested as a rule on this desk. The course's three episodes are three chosen cases, which cannot establish a lead on their own.
How to test it: take a daily credit-spread series and an equity index over a long sample, define turning points mechanically in advance (for example a 10% drawdown peak), measure the lag between the credit turn and the equity turn for every such event, and compare against the lags from randomly placed pseudo-events. Report the whole distribution of lags, including events where credit did not lead.
Section 05

Correlation regimes: diversification is a variable

For decades stocks and bonds mostly moved in opposite directions: when stocks fell, bonds rose, so a 60/40 portfolio hedged itself. That relationship was a feature of a particular regime, not a law. Inflation forced the Fed to hike into weakness in 2022, stocks and bonds fell together, and 60/40 had one of its worst years in decades. Correlation assumptions built on forty years of falling inflation broke when the regime changed.

RegimeStock-bond correlationWhy
Growth-drivenNegativeGrowth fears: stocks down, bonds up (flight to safety)
Inflation-drivenPositiveInflation pushes rates up: both fall
Liquidity crisisVariable → 1Everything is sold as cash is raised

The key question is therefore: what is driving markets right now, growth fears or inflation fears? The answer decides whether bonds hedge you or hurt you.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Two kinds of bad news, two kinds of correlation

Picture the stock and bond returns as being pushed around by two kinds of news. A growth surprise (good growth news) lifts stocks and pushes bond prices down (yields up), so it makes the two move in opposite directions. An inflation surprise (higher inflation) hurts stocks and also pushes bond prices down, so it makes them move in the same direction. The correlation you actually observe is the tug of war between the two. When growth shocks dominate, the correlation is negative. When inflation shocks dominate, it flips positive. Nothing changed about stocks or bonds; the mix of news changed.

\[ \operatorname{cov}(S,B) \;=\; -\,c\,\sigma_g^{2} \;+\; b\,d\,\sigma_\pi^{2} \]
S, Bstock return and bond return (bond price, so a yield rise is a loss)
σg2variance of growth surprises: pushes S and B apart (negative term)
σπ2variance of inflation surprises: pushes S and B together (positive term)
b, c, dsensitivities: stocks to inflation (b), bonds to growth (c), bonds to inflation (d)

This is a deliberately simple two-shock model, built for teaching (it is not in the course notes). Stocks respond \(+1\) to a growth surprise and \(-b\) to an inflation surprise; bond prices respond \(-c\) to growth and \(-d\) to inflation. The covariance is the sum of the two effects, and it changes sign when \(\sigma_\pi / \sigma_g = \sqrt{c/(bd)}\). With the values used in the simulation below (\(b = 0.8,\ c = 1,\ d = 0.6\)) that tipping point is a ratio of about 1.44: once inflation news is about 1.4 times as volatile as growth news, the stock-bond correlation turns positive.

How to measure it

The course's methodology: rolling windows of 20 to 60 days for tactical use or 120 to 252 days for strategic use (shorter is noisier but more responsive); an EWMA (exponentially weighted) correlation with decay \(\lambda \approx 0.94\); regime-conditional estimates (for example, VIX above 25 versus below 20); and stress-testing with historical crisis correlations.

Watch the formula build
The EWMA correlation, from a plain correlation to one that forgets
\[ \rho = \frac{\sum_i r_{1,i}\, r_{2,i}}{\sqrt{\sum_i r_{1,i}^2 \;\sum_i r_{2,i}^2}} \]

The ordinary correlation of two return series (with the means taken as zero for daily data): the co-movement, divided by the product of each series' own size. Every day counts equally.

\[ \rho_t = \frac{\sum_i \color{#fbbf24}{\lambda^{i}}\, r_{1,t-i}\, r_{2,t-i}}{\sqrt{\sum_i \color{#fbbf24}{\lambda^{i}}\, r_{1,t-i}^2 \;\sum_i \color{#fbbf24}{\lambda^{i}}\, r_{2,t-i}^2}} \]

Weight each observation by \(\lambda^i\), where \(i\) is how many days ago it happened. With \(\lambda = 0.94\), yesterday counts 94% as much as today, the day before 88%, and so on. Old data fades instead of dropping off a cliff.

\[ \text{effective window} \approx \color{#10b981}{\frac{1}{1-\lambda}} \]

A rule of thumb for how much history the weights effectively use. For \(\lambda = 0.94\) that is about 17 days; for \(0.97\), about 33. A larger \(\lambda\) is smoother and slower; a smaller one is faster and noisier.

Section 06

Key Takeaways

✓Trade the capital structure, not single assets. One view has many expressions with different risk and reward; the cleanest one is most directly tied to the thesis.
✓Information flows in sequence, but the sequence is a hypothesis. Rates and credit often move first; know where you are in the cascade, and test the order rather than assuming it.
✓Regimes determine allocation. Growth × inflation gives four worlds; the opportunity is in detecting transitions early, not in naming the regime afterwards.
✓Correlations are variables, not constants. Stock-bond correlation flips between growth-driven and inflation-driven regimes; diversification can fail when it is most needed.
✓Coherence builds conviction; divergence means investigate. Aligned markets raise confidence and size; disagreement is a question, not a signal.
Section 07

Self-test

Q1. Why did stocks and bonds fall together in 2022, and what does that say about the diversification assumption?
Inflation, not growth, was driving markets. When inflation forces the central bank to hike into weakness, higher rates hit both bond prices and equity valuations at once, so the stock-bond correlation turns positive. The negative correlation that made 60/40 a self-hedging portfolio was a property of a growth-driven regime, built on decades of falling inflation. Correlations are variables that depend on which fear is dominant, so "bonds hedge stocks" is a regime-dependent statement, not a law.
Q2. Equities are rallying but high-yield credit spreads are widening. What does the framework say, and what does it not say?
It says there is a divergence: credit may be seeing risk that equities ignore, and historically credit is often right at turning points, so it is a prompt to investigate. It does not say sell equities. A divergence tells you to ask why they disagree and what would resolve it. Credit can also be wrong, and the course notes that either the bonds overreact or equities catch down. The October 2023 episode (in Part 2) is the mirror case: a sell-off in rates with credit not confirming a hard landing was informative, and credit was right to stay calm.
Q3. In the course's regime table, which regime has the "worst" outcome, and why is the label less important than the transition?
Stagflation (growth falling, inflation rising): central banks are trapped, and the only places to hide are real assets and cash. But the label is backward-looking. By the time you are confident of the regime, much of the move has happened. The money is in detecting the transition early, using forward-looking indicators (for example bear steepening and rising breakevens for the move to Reflation) and the confirmations that follow.
Q4. In the simple two-shock model, why does the stock-bond covariance change sign, and when?
Growth shocks push stocks and bond prices in opposite directions (a negative contribution, \(-c\,\sigma_g^2\)); inflation shocks push both down together (a positive contribution, \(+bd\,\sigma_\pi^2\)). The sign of the sum depends on which kind of news is more volatile. It flips when \(\sigma_\pi/\sigma_g = \sqrt{c/(bd)}\), about 1.44 with the lesson's made-up sensitivities. The model is a teaching device: it shows that a changing mix of news can flip the correlation with no change in how stocks and bonds themselves behave.
