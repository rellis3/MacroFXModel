Currency Markets & Global Flows, or Why Calm and Stress Need Different Maps

The largest market on earth moves for different reasons at different speeds. Rate differentials rule for weeks and months; fear overrides them in a spike. This guide builds the map: the personalities, the Dollar Smile, why carry pays in pennies and loses in lumps, what the cross-currency basis says about dollar scarcity, and why central banks can slow a move but not turn a trend. Course notes, taught as a framework, not a backtested strategy.

The whole idea
Section 1

FX is the market everything else trades through

Every cross-border investment is also a currency position. That is why policy divergence shows up in currencies before stocks.

💵

~$7.5 trillion a day

The BIS survey figure the course quotes: the largest market by turnover, so it is liquid, efficient and hard to manipulate.

🌏

24 hours, 5 days

Sydney, Tokyo, London, New York. Decentralised and over-the-counter: no single exchange.

🔁

~88% have USD on one side

The dollar is the hub of nearly every cross. Swaps are the largest segment, so dollar funding stress shows up there first.

📡

The transmission mechanism

Level 3 in the course's hierarchy. JPY strength, EM weakness and basis blowouts have preceded VIX spikes.

EUR/USD = 1.0850 means 1 euro buys 1.0850 dollars. First currency is the base, second the quote; the number rising means the base is strengthening.

The Dollar Smile: strong at both ends, weak in the middle

Slide through the state of the world. A stylised curve, not an estimate.

The dollar is the reserve currency (about 58–60% of central-bank reserves), the invoicing currency for most trade, the funding currency for most global debt and a safe haven, so it is wanted for two opposite reasons.

  • Fed hawkishness is negative for risk assets (higher rates, lower valuations)
Right side · March 2020

  • Negative for risk assets

Ask why the dollar is strong, not just whether it is. Dollar cycles run roughly 7–10 years peak to peak, but timing is imprecise: understand the regime, do not call the turn.
Section 6

Carry: borrow cheap, buy the yield, collect the gap

The course's example: borrow JPY at 0.1%, hold MXN at 10%, about 9.9% a year. Exposure: the spot rate.

Borrow JPY · CHF · EUR
→
Buy AUD · NZD · EM
R = L × [ (i_h − i_f) + s ]
i_h − i_f: the pay
+
s: spot move (unpaid)
×
L: leverage

Break-even spot move: s* = −(i_h − i_f). In calm markets the differential dominates and the yield is steady; in volatile markets spot dominates and can overwhelm years of carry in days. In theory the high-yielder should fall by the gap (uncovered interest parity); in practice it often does not, thanks to a risk premium, momentum and slow adjustment. Pennies in front of a steamroller.

It actually happened

USD/JPY had climbed to about 162 by early July 2024, with Japan's rate near zero against a US rate of 5.25–5.50%. The Bank of Japan hiked to about 0.25% on 31 July; the US jobs report of 2 August was weak (114,000, unemployment 4.3%). On Monday 5 August USD/JPY traded below 142, the Nikkei 225 fell 12.4% (its worst day since October 1987) and the VIX spiked above 65 intraday, closing near 38.6. The next day the Nikkei rebounded about 10%. How much was carry, and how much other leveraged positions in thin August liquidity, is debated.

Reflexivity: yen strength hits stops, forcing more yen buying, which makes the yen stronger. Watch JPY in risk-off, COT positioning (extreme short JPY means crowded carry), FX vol and narrowing differentials.
Section 9

The basis: what dollars cost offshore

Covered interest parity says two routes to dollars should cost the same. The basis is the deviation.

Follow it through
1 + i_EUR · T
Deposit the euro at the euro rate and take it back after time T.
S (1 + i_USD · T) / F
Swap into dollars at spot, earn the dollar rate, swap back at the forward F agreed today. No free lunch means this equals Route A, so F = S (1 + i_USD T) / (1 + i_EUR T): the forward premium equals the rate differential.
F = S (1 + i_USD T) / (1 + (i_EUR + x) T)
The basis x is quoted in bps. Negative means getting dollars via swaps costs more than CIP says: non-US entities pay a premium for dollars. EUR/USD at −30 bps means European entities pay about 30 bps a year extra. Positive is rare and usually minor.

Why it exists: global dollar demand, post-2008 balance-sheet regulation that limits arbitrage, quarter-end balance-sheet reduction (a seasonal widening) and risk aversion blocking arbitrage in stress.

Section 11 · It actually happened

March 2020: everyone needed dollars

It actually happened

The dollar index rose from the mid-90s in early March 2020 to about 103 around 20 March, even though the United States was a pandemic epicentre. By the course's account the EUR/USD basis blew out from about −20 bps to beyond −100 bps in days. The Fed cut the price of its dollar swap lines with five other central banks on 15 March and opened temporary lines with nine more on 19 March; the basis then normalised quickly.

Section 12

Intervention buys time, not a new trend

Verbal warnings → rate checks → actual intervention. The BoJ/MoF, SNB and PBoC are the active interveners; the Fed rarely intervenes. A stylised model: the gap closes at the fundamental drift.

Try it

Illustrative, with arbitrary level 150. Push the drift to zero or negative and the gap never closes: that is the course's resolution of Japan 2022–2024.
Section 13 · It actually happened

Japan 2022–2024, and the SNB in 2015

It actually happened

On 22 September 2022 Japan intervened for the first time since 1998 with USD/JPY at about 145.9; it fell roughly five yen within hours, then climbed back to about 152 by 21 October, when it intervened again (about ¥9.2 trillion in total for September and October 2022). In 2024 it spent about ¥9.8 trillion over 26 April to 29 May after USD/JPY touched about 160, and about ¥5.5 trillion on 11 and 12 July; USD/JPY printed about 162 on 3 July. The lasting reversal came with falling US rate expectations and a BoJ hike.

When the authority stops

The SNB set a 1.20 floor on EUR/CHF on 6 September 2011 and abandoned it on 15 January 2015. EUR/CHF fell about 30% at the low, to roughly 0.85, before settling near 1.04.

Trading around it: know the lines in the sand, watch the verbal ladder, respect the firepower short term, do not overestimate it long term, and treat intervention-driven moves as entries only if fundamentals still favour the original direction.

Section 15

Where this needs qualifying, and what this desk has tested

Where this needs qualifying

FX relationships are well-established but regime-varying. The "50–80%" differential figure, the basis guideposts (rough, EUR/USD, from a regulatory era that has since evolved) and the Smile are frameworks and ranges, not laws. Several of the stylised charts here are illustrations, not estimates.

Desk test: nothing found A basis widening day was not an early warning of dollar strength. EUR and GBP basis, 1,830 days 2016–2026: r +0.018 [−0.030, +0.071], inside the placebo; only 5 days met the literal 20 bps threshold. Volatility likewise: r −0.003 [−0.036, +0.032].
Desk test: held up The year-end turn in dollar funding is real, and shrinking: −14.4 bps [−24.2, −4.8] against other quarter-ends; about −39 and −46 bps in 2017–18, under −10 bps in 2024–26. A calendar seasonal; it says nothing about spot direction.
Desk data: base rate The carry-unwind shape (yen firming while US yields rise) is too rare to score: 35 episodes in 18 years. The shape can be described; nothing can be claimed about the week after.
Not tested here Dollar-Smile regime labels as a conditioner, and intervention levels as "lines in the sand", have not been tested on this desk. A fair test fixes the regime rules or the intervention levels in advance, uses matched controls and placebo levels, and holds out a final period.

Quick check

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

The yen surges on a risk-off day despite a large rate disadvantage. What does the course read from it?

Haven flows and forced buy-backs of the funding currency dominate in stress. Fundamentals reassert once the panic subsides.
Question 2

The dollar index is up 5%. Which is the best first question?

Left-side strength (2022) and right-side strength (March 2020) mean very different things for other assets.
Question 3

Borrow JPY at 0.1%, hold MXN at 10%, leveraged 5×. The peso falls 10%. What is the net on equity?

Leverage scales both terms. The break-even spot move is still −9.9%, but the loss on the way is five times bigger.
Question 4

The EUR/USD basis moves from −8 to −30 bps in a day. What is the sensible reading?

The course reads 20–30 bps in a day as stress emerging; the level is elevated; quarter-end widenings are predictable and should be discounted.
Question 5

Why does intervention usually fail to reverse a trend?

Japan 2022–2024: sharp short-term reversals, but the trend only turned when US rate expectations shifted.
Your call
Scenario drill · Friday 2 August 2024

The yen is rallying and the jobs report was weak. You are levered carry.

You run a carry basket: short yen as the funding leg, long AUD and MXN against it, at about 5× your equity. USD/JPY was near 162 at the start of July and is now about 147. The Bank of Japan raised rates two days ago. The US payrolls report out this morning was much weaker than expected and unemployment rose to 4.3%. The VIX is around 23. Markets are about to close for the weekend. What do you do with the size?

  1. Hold. The rate gap still favours carry, and a 9% yen move is just a pullback.
    At 5× a further 10% spot fall costs about 50% of equity, which is five times the 9.9% of a year of carry in the lesson's example. The differential is also narrowing (a BoJ hike, US cuts being priced). "Still favours carry" is exactly what is true right up until the unwind.
  2. Add. The yen's rise looks overdone, so buy the dip in carry.
    Averaging into a funding-currency rally while the trigger is live is the unwind's reflexive step in reverse: you would be adding size just as stops start to be hit. The explorer shows the loss scales with leverage.
  3. Cut the leverage to a size where a further 10% adverse gap would be survivable, and accept the cost now.
    This is the arithmetic of the explorer. At 5× a 10% fall is −50% of equity; at 2× it is −20%. Over a weekend you cannot exit at your stop, so the survivable size is the size you hold when the gap arrives. Cutting early does cost the carry you would have earned and, as it turned out, gave up some of the rebound.
  4. Keep the size but set a tighter stop for Monday.
    A stop is not a guarantee in a fast, gapping market: it fills at the next available price. A tighter stop makes sense as one layer, but it does not change the size you are carrying into a weekend with this trigger live.

What happened. On Monday 5 August USD/JPY traded below 142, the Nikkei 225 fell 12.4% and the VIX spiked above 65 intraday before closing near 38.6. The next day the Nikkei rebounded about 10%, and on 7 August a Bank of Japan deputy governor said the bank would not raise rates while markets were unstable. Cutting on Friday would have given up some of that rebound and would have survived Monday; the lesson is about the size you can hold through the gap, not about predicting the low.

Sources: US Bureau of Labor Statistics, The Employment Situation, July 2024 (released 2 August 2024); Bank of Japan, Statement on Monetary Policy, 31 July 2024; Cboe, VIX historical data; Japan Exchange Group, Nikkei 225 daily closes.

Section 17

What to carry forward

01

FX is the transmission mechanism: policy divergence flows through currencies to global assets, often before equities price it.

02

Rate differentials dominate weeks to months; risk sentiment overrides in stress. Havens: USD, JPY, CHF. Risk currencies: AUD, NZD, CAD, NOK, EM.

03

The Dollar Smile: strong at both extremes, weak in synchronised growth. Always ask why the dollar is strong.

04

Carry works until it doesn't: R = L × [(i_h − i_f) + s]. Unwinds are reflexive and violent; JPY strength in a risk-off is the tell (5 August 2024).

05

Cross-currency basis equals dollar funding stress: a fast-widening negative basis marks scarcity; speed beats level; ignore quarter-ends (March 2020).

06

Intervention has limits: it buys time but cannot fight fundamentals forever (Japan 2022–2024). EM FX is the canary; use FX to confirm equity views. Dollar-Smile labels and intervention lines are untested here; a basis widening was not an early warning in the desk's test.

What this now lets you do

Read a currency move by horizon and regime instead of by headline: spot the carry unwind in the yen, tell left-side dollar strength from right-side, size a carry trade against the crash instead of the average, and treat a fast-widening basis as a funding question. Remember that the stylised charts and the guideposts here are frameworks, not tested rules.
