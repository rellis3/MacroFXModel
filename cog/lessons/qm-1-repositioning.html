Repositioning Your Approach to Financial Markets

Most of what you see in a price chart is noise, short-term trading pays friction on every trade, and the cheapest place to find out an idea is wrong is a spreadsheet, not your account. This lesson is the case for testing before trading.

COURSE NOTES — framework as taught, not a backtested strategy ~22 min read Prereqs: none
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • Noise dominates short horizons. Many kinds of participant, with different horizons and mandates, generate price moves that are essentially random. The signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) improves as the holding period lengthens.
  • Friction is the second penalty. Spread, slippage and market impact are paid per trade, so short holding periods meet low SNR and high cost. The holding period where expected edge first exceeds cost is the minimum viable holding period.
  • Testing before trading decouples time from capital. A systematic process (hypothesis, in-sample, out-of-sample, walk-forward, small live size) fails most ideas for the price of compute. Trading on intuition fails the same ideas with real money already deployed.
  • Know the numbers before you need them. A backtest gives you eleven statistics (CAGR, Sharpe, max drawdown and so on) before day one. Without them, an ordinary −15% drawdown looks like a verdict on the strategy.
  • Real-world episode: Knight Capital, 1 August 2012, found a software defect in the live market rather than before it, and lost more than $400 million in about 45 minutes. It was an operations failure, not a strategy that failed validation, but the cost structure is the same: a defect found live is paid for in capital.
Section 01

Why this matters

Most people come to markets the way they come to any new skill: they form a view, try it, and learn from the result. In most fields that works, because feedback is fast, cheap and informative. Markets break all three. Feedback is slow (a strategy needs months to show anything), expensive (every lesson is paid for in capital), and uninformative (one run of P&L is a single random path, so a good run and a bad run can both come from the same underlying edge).

This first lesson of the Quant & Macro course asks you to change the order of operations. Instead of "trade, then find out", it argues for "find out, then trade": measure how much of a price move is noise, work out what trading costs you, test ideas on history before they touch money, and arrive at day one already knowing what a normal drawdown for your strategy looks like. The rest of the course (what moves markets, central banks, bonds, currencies, putting it together) assumes you have made that switch.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Think of testing a recipe on a stove vs. testing it at a wedding

A cook who tries a new dish at home risks one dinner. A cook who tries it for the first time at a 200-person wedding risks the event, and learns exactly the same thing, only more expensively and with an audience. Trading a new idea with real capital is the wedding. Testing it on history, then on data it has never seen, then in small size, is the home kitchen. Both cooks find out the dish is bad; only one pays for it with something that cannot be re-made.

A note on the numbers. The course states that its specific correlations, percentages and figures are exemplar data representing typical historical ranges, not measurements. The mechanisms are well-established; the exact figures (the 52-idea funnel, the −22% maximum drawdown, the six-month timeline) are teaching examples. Treat them as illustrative.
Section 02

Noise: who is trading, and why most of the movement is random

A price is the running outcome of many different participants acting at once: central banks, pension funds, sovereign wealth funds, hedge funds, proprietary desks and algorithmic traders. They differ in time horizon (a pension fund thinks in decades, an algorithm in milliseconds), in mandate (one must hedge, another must track an index, another is paid to speculate) and in what they know. A move caused by a pension fund rebalancing at month-end tells you nothing about where the market is going; it only tells you someone had to trade.

The course calls the part of price movement that comes from this clash of unrelated motives market noise: movements that are fundamentally random and carry no predictive signal. It sits on top of whatever real information the price also carries. The question for anyone building a strategy is how much of what they see is the second thing and how much is the first.

\[ \text{SNR} = \frac{\text{Signal Power}}{\text{Noise Power}} \]
Signal Powerhow large the genuinely predictive part of a move is (squared size)
Noise Powerhow large the random part is (squared size)
SNRtheir ratio: above 1 the signal wins, below 1 the noise wins
SNRReadingWhat it means for a strategy
< 1 (low)Noise dominatesRandom fluctuations overwhelm the signal; any pattern you see is more likely luck than edge.
≈ 1 (neutral)Signal ≈ noiseEdge is uncertain; the strategy might work, but a short track record cannot tell you.
> 1 (high)Signal dominatesExploitable patterns emerge and can be measured.

The crucial fact is that SNR is not a fixed property of a market. It varies with the holding period. Short horizons are noise-dominated; longer horizons move through a transition zone into signal-extractable territory. Extending the holding period improves signal-extraction potential. Section 04 builds a small model that shows why, and lets you move the dials.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine listening for one quiet song in a crowded playground. For one second all you hear is shouting (noise). If you listen for a whole minute, the shouting jumbles together into a background hum, and the song, which keeps repeating, starts to stand out. Holding a trade longer is like listening longer.

Section 03

Friction: the second penalty on short horizons

Noise is only half of why short-term trading is hard. The other half is cost. Institutions reach markets through prime brokerage, whose cost structures differ materially from retail execution. At short horizons, costs compound geometrically across four components: spread (the gap between buy and sell prices), slippage (the price moves while your order is working), market impact (your own order moves the price against you) and opportunity cost (capital tied up in a trade that does not pay).

↔ Spread

Pay the ask to buy, receive the bid to sell. The gap is a toll, charged on every round trip.

🏃 Slippage

The price you get is not the price you saw. The longer an order takes to fill, the more it drifts.

💥 Market impact

A large order moves the price against you as it executes. It grows with size relative to available liquidity.

⏳ Opportunity cost

Capital committed to a trade that earns nothing is capital unavailable for something that would.

🧮
Alpha erosion, the course's example

5 bps of expected alpha per trade

A strategy with 5 basis points (0.05%) of expected alpha per trade is only profitable if each round trip costs less than 5 bps. At a cost of 4 bps it keeps +1 bp a trade; at 6 bps it loses 1 bp a trade, and because costs are certain while the alpha is only expected, the strategy has negative expected value from the first trade. At high trading frequency, where each trade carries a small edge but the cost is still paid in full, this is the common outcome: the edge is real and the strategy still loses money.

The double penalty. Short horizons suffer both low SNR (noise dominates) and high transaction-cost impact. The two compound: with little signal to find and a fixed toll to pay on each attempt, extracting alpha at short horizons is exceptionally difficult. The holding period where expected alpha first exceeds friction is the minimum viable holding period for any strategy.
Section 04

The maths: a toy model of signal, noise and cost

The course states the SNR idea and the break-even idea in words. To see them move together, here is a deliberately simple model, built for teaching and not taken from the course or fitted to any market. It assumes the edge accumulates in a straight line with time held, while the random wobble accumulates only with the square root of time.

Watch the formula build
From "edge per day" to the minimum viable holding period, one term at a time
\[ E(h) = \color{#fbbf24}{k\,h} \]

Expected gross edge of a trade held for \(h\) days: a drift of \(k\) basis points a day, collected for \(h\) days.

\[ E(h) = k\,h \qquad N(h) = \color{#fbbf24}{\sigma\sqrt{h}} \]

The random part. Independent daily wobbles of size \(\sigma\) do not add up in a straight line, because ups and downs partly cancel. After \(h\) days their typical size is \(\sigma\sqrt{h}\).

\[ \text{SNR}(h) = \frac{E(h)^2}{N(h)^2} = \color{#fbbf24}{\frac{k^2}{\sigma^2}}\,h \]

Power is squared size, so SNR is the signal squared over the noise squared. The edge grows with \(h\) and the noise only with \(\sqrt{h}\), so SNR rises in proportion to the holding period. This is the maths behind "longer holding periods improve signal extraction".

\[ \text{net}(h) = k\,h - \color{#fbbf24}{C} \]

Now subtract the toll. \(C\) is the round-trip cost of one trade in basis points, paid once however long you hold.

\[ k\,h^* - C = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \color{#10b981}{h^* = \frac{C}{k}} \]

Break-even: the holding period where expected edge exactly pays the cost. Shorter than \(h^*\), the trade loses on average; this is the minimum viable holding period.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Say you walk to a shop and every trip costs you a fixed bus fare, but you only collect a little pocket money for every hour you spend there. Stay five minutes and the fare is more than you earned. Stay long enough and the pocket money beats the fare. The "break-even stay" is the fare divided by the pocket money per hour.

🧮
Worked example

Edge of 4 bps a day, cost of 5 bps, noise of 60 bps a day

Break-even is \(h^* = C/k = 5/4 =\) 1.25 days. Hold for 1 day and you earn 4 bps gross against a 5 bps toll: net −1 bp. Hold for 5 days: gross \(4 \times 5 = 20\) bps, net \(20 - 5 = +15\) bps, and cost has shrunk from 125% of the edge (1 day) to 25% (5 days).

\[ \text{SNR}(5) = \frac{k^2}{\sigma^2}\,h = \frac{4^2}{60^2}\times 5 = \frac{80}{3600} \approx 0.022 \]
k²/σ²16 / 3600 per day
× 5five days held
≈ 0.022signal power is about 2% of noise power

So the trade clears its costs at 1.25 days and is still overwhelmingly noise at 5 days. SNR reaches 1 only when \(h = \sigma^2/k^2 = 3600/16 =\) 225 days. Break-even on cost and a clear signal are two different thresholds, and the second comes much later. This is only a model with a straight-line edge: a real edge can decay or saturate, and the numbers are chosen to make the shape visible.

Section 05

Two ways to build a strategy

Given that noise is large and friction is certain, how you decide whether an idea is any good matters more than how you come up with it. The course sets two approaches side by side.

💡
Systematic practitioner (data-driven)
  • Hypothesis generation from empirical observation
  • In-sample parameter estimation with regularisation
  • Out-of-sample validation on held-out data
  • Walk-forward analysis simulating live deployment
  • Monte Carlo simulation for tail-risk assessment
  • Paper trading before capital allocation
⚠️
Retail trader (intuition-based, no validation)
  • Hypothesis from intuition or narrative
  • No systematic parameter optimisation
  • Live market is the only test environment
  • Strategy invalidation requires months of live trading
  • Drawdown limits discovered through experience
  • Real capital at risk from day one

The difference is not intelligence or effort. It is where the test happens. The systematic route moves every test that can be run on history to before the first dollar is exposed; the intuition route has one test environment, the live account.

Time and capital, side by side

The last two rows are the compounding ones. A process that rejects an idea in two weeks tests about twenty-five times as many ideas a year as one that needs six months, and since most ideas fail, the testing rate matters more than the quality of any single idea.

Section 06

The validation funnel

The systematic route is a sequence of gates. Each gate costs compute (time on a computer), not capital, and each one removes ideas that looked fine at the previous stage:

Idea52 hypotheses
→
In-sample34 pass
→
Out-of-sample13 pass
→
Walk-forward6 pass
→
Live, small4 deployed

The course's worked numbers: 52 hypotheses generated → 34 pass in-sample → 13 pass out-of-sample → 6 pass walk-forward → 4 deployed live. That is 48 strategies rejected safely, with $0 lost to failed strategies. The intuition-based route skips every gate: 3 hypotheses are generated, all validation stages are skipped, 3 are deployed live untested, 100% of capital is exposed during what amounts to testing, and 0 were validated before deployment.

The survivors fall quickly even at generous pass rates. If each gate passes half of what reaches it, 52 ideas yield about 3.25 live strategies; the intuition path would have deployed all of them to find out which three were any good.
What the funnel does not promise. Passing every gate shows an idea survived the tests it was given, not that it has an edge. With enough hypotheses, some will clear any fixed gate by luck (the multiple-testing problem), and a walk-forward run on one data set is still one path. The funnel's actual guarantee is smaller and more useful: the failures are found for the price of compute. The survivors then go live small, because the last gate is the real market.
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

A funnel has a wide top and a narrow spout. Lots of ideas go in; each stage lets only some through. Because the stages cost nothing but thinking time, when an idea gets stuck you lose nothing but a bit of effort. The kid who skips the funnel pours all her ideas straight onto the floor to see which ones bounce.

Section 07

What you know before you go live

Running ideas through the funnel does more than filter. It produces a statistical profile of each survivor. The systematic practitioner enters live trading with 11 of 11 key metrics already known from the backtest; the intuition-based trader knows 0 of 11 and discovers them through live P&L, which the course calls "paying tuition to the market".

Reference: the eleven metrics and why each matters
MetricDefinition / why it matters
CAGRExpected annualised return; is the strategy worth the operational overhead and opportunity cost of capital?
Sharpe ratioReturn per unit of volatility; enables comparison across strategies and asset classes. Below 0.5 generally unacceptable.
Sortino ratioReturn per unit of downside deviation; more relevant than Sharpe when losses matter more than volatility (i.e. always).
Maximum drawdownLargest peak-to-trough decline; critical for survival: determines position sizing and whether you can psychologically and financially endure the strategy.
Drawdown durationTime to recovery; a 20% drawdown lasting 18 months will break most investors, so know it before deployment.
Win rate% of winning trades; psychological sustainability. A 30% win-rate strategy can be highly profitable but emotionally brutal without preparation.
Profit factorGross profit ÷ gross loss; below 1.0 = losing strategy; below 1.5 leaves little margin for execution slippage.
Max consecutive lossesLongest losing streak; even profitable strategies have 10+ consecutive losses. Without this knowledge, streaks feel like strategy failure.
ExpectancyAverage profit per trade after costs; negative expectancy = guaranteed long-term loss.
Calmar ratioCAGR ÷ max drawdown: "is the expected return worth the worst historical pain?"
Tail ratioRight tail ÷ left tail (95th percentile); are your big days wins or losses? Critical for the true risk profile.

Notice what these numbers have in common: nearly all of them are about what to expect when things go badly (drawdown, losing streaks, tails, recovery time). They are the context you need in order to keep running a strategy through the stretch where it looks broken. The next section shows why that matters.

Section 08

The blindfold problem

Suppose both traders are sitting in the same −15% drawdown. Their decisions will be very different, because only one of them can tell what −15% means.

💡
With backtest data

Historical max drawdown −22%; average drawdown duration 47 days; current drawdown 31 days old; 8 similar historical drawdowns, 8 of 8 recovered. Within expected parameters; continue executing; this is normal variance. The −15% is about 68% of the way to the historical worst (15 ÷ 22), and it is 16 days short of the average length.

⚠️
Without backtest data

Historical max ??? · expected duration ??? · is this normal ??? · will it recover ??? No framework for the decision; likely abandons the strategy at the worst possible time, locking in the loss.

The point is not that drawdowns are rare. It is that they are typical, and you cannot know how typical without simulating. The course recommends Monte Carlo simulation (for example 1,000 simulated equity paths) to show the range of outcomes before deployment. The chart below does exactly that for an edge that is real by construction: every path has the same average return and volatility (10% a year, 12.5% volatility, a Sharpe ratio of 0.8). Only the luck differs.

Worked example

Reading −15% with and without the map

With the backtest and simulation: the live drawdown is −15% against a historical worst of −22%, so you are at \(15/22 \approx 68\%\) of the worst case, and 31 days against an average of 47. Eight of eight similar historical drawdowns recovered. The decision is "execute the plan", and the plan can even say in advance what depth (say, beyond the historical −22%) would trigger a review. Without any of this, the same −15% is a single number with nothing to compare it to, and the only available action is to react to how it feels.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

If you know a hill on a hike is steep, a hard climb is normal and you keep walking. If someone blindfolds you and puts you on that same hill, every step feels like you are about to fall off a cliff, so you stop and turn back, right before the top. The backtest takes the blindfold off.

Section 09

It actually happened: Knight Capital, 2012

The course's argument is about where defects get found. A famous case of a defect being found in the most expensive place possible is Knight Capital. It was not a trading idea that failed validation: it was an operational failure. It is included because the cost structure it shows is exactly the one the timeline above describes.

It actually happened

A defect found in the live market, in 45 minutes

Knight Capital Group was one of the largest US equity market makers. On the morning of 1 August 2012 it rolled out new trading software to its servers. Seven of eight servers received the new code; the eighth still carried an old, supposedly retired routine, which the new release re-activated by accident.

When the market opened, the system began sending a flood of unintended orders. In about 45 minutes Knight built large unwanted positions in about 150 stocks and lost more than $400 million. Within days it raised about $400 million of emergency financing from a group of investors, and it later agreed to merge with Getco. In October 2013 the US Securities and Exchange Commission found that Knight had violated the Market Access Rule, which requires adequate risk controls on market access, and fined it $12 million.

Sources: U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission, In the Matter of Knight Capital Americas LLC, Exchange Act Release No. 34-70694, 16 October 2013.

Where this analogy stops

Knight's failure was a software-deployment and risk-control failure, not a strategy whose backtest was misleading. The parallel is only the structure of the bill: when a defect is first found live, the loss is capital, and at Knight's speed it arrived in minutes rather than months. A statistical edge that quietly fails to replicate, the funnel's main subject, is a slower and quieter version of the same cost.

Section 10

Where the course's picture needs qualifying

⚠️
Common pitfalls
  • Treating the example numbers as measurements. 52 → 34 → 13 → 6 → 4, "6 months" and "−22%" are teaching figures. Your funnel will have its own rates.
  • Equating "passed the funnel" with "has an edge". More hypotheses means more lucky survivors. See the Multiple Testing lesson.
  • Treating the break-even holding period as the target. At \(h^*\) a trade only pays its costs, with no margin for slippage, and the move is still mostly noise.
  • Reading simulated drawdowns as a ceiling. The simulation above uses well-behaved returns. Real strategies have fatter tails and clustered losses, so the true worst case is deeper than a Gaussian simulation suggests.
  • Using the backtest as a promise. "8 of 8 recovered" is a statement about history. It tells you what normal looked like; it does not guarantee the ninth.
Not tested here"Longer holding periods are where the edge is, net of costs, for the instruments and strategies on the desk." The course states it as a general principle; no test of it has been run in this repository, and the course's funnel rates (65%, 38%, 46%, 67%) have not been checked against any real research pipeline.
How to test it: take each candidate signal, record its per-trade gross edge at several fixed holding periods on a held-out sample, subtract the measured round-trip cost, and fix the pass rule (net edge above zero with a confidence interval that excludes it) before looking. Count every signal tried, not only the survivors.
Scenario drill · an illustrative drawdown

Month 3, and you are down 15%

You deployed a strategy after testing it. The backtest and simulation said: historical maximum drawdown −22%, average drawdown duration 47 days, and 8 of 8 similar historical drawdowns recovered. Three months in you are −15% from the peak, and the drawdown is 31 days old. You have checked that data, fills and costs match what the backtest assumed. What do you do?

  1. Stop trading it. A 15% loss is too much to sit through.
    The backtest said drawdowns of this size are normal, and this one is well inside the historical worst. Closing the strategy now converts a paper loss into a realised one, and removes you before any recovery. Without the map, this is what −15% usually produces.
  2. Double the size so the recovery is twice as fast.
    Size scales the drawdown too. If the historical worst was −22% at your planned size, then doubling roughly doubles it to about −44%, and you would be taking that risk after a loss, with less capital, on an edge you have no new evidence for.
  3. Keep executing at the planned size. Confirm that the live behaviour (hit rate, average trade, costs) matches the backtest, and write down in advance what drawdown or duration, beyond the historical worst, would trigger a formal review.
    This is the case for the whole lesson. The drawdown sits inside the range the testing predicted, so it carries no new information about the strategy. A review rule set beyond the historical worst keeps you honest: if the strategy breaks the map, that is a signal; if it follows the map, that is just variance.
  4. Halve the size until it recovers.
    Defensible, especially if the plan already contained a de-risking rule written before the trade. Improvised after the fact, it reacts to a number the backtest called normal, and it makes the strategy earn back less while it recovers. Better than quitting, worse than following a rule you set when you were calm.

Illustrative scenario. This is not a documented episode; the figures are the course's own example numbers (−22% historical worst, 47-day average, 8 of 8 recovered). The correct call in a real drawdown also depends on the specifics of the strategy and the account. The general point holds: the person with a pre-trade map can classify the drawdown as expected, and the person without one cannot.

Section 11

Key Takeaways

✓Signal vs noise. At short horizons noise dominates; extending the holding period improves signal extraction. In the toy model, SNR rises in proportion to \(h\).
✓Transaction cost drag. Costs compound at high frequencies; the crossover point of alpha versus friction sets the minimum viable holding period (\(h^* = C/k\) in the model). Short horizons pay both penalties.
✓Systematic validation. Walk-forward testing costs compute, not capital. Most hypotheses fail; the advantage is discovering the failure before deployment.
✓The double negative. Ad-hoc validation risks destroying time and capital at the same time; systematic approaches decouple them, so failed strategies consume only time.
✓Know your numbers first. Eleven statistics from the backtest turn a drawdown from a verdict into a data point. In the simulation, a real edge dipped 15% in about half of all five-year runs.
⚠Passing the funnel is not proof of edge, and the funnel's rates and the drawdown figures are the course's illustrative numbers. Break-even on cost and a clear signal are different thresholds, and the second comes much later.
✗Trading as the test. Making the live account the only test environment turns every idea into a capital event, and every drawdown into a decision made blindfolded. The claim that longer holds are where the net edge is has not been tested here.
Section 12

Self-test

Q1. Why do short holding periods carry a "double penalty"?
Two separate things go wrong together. First, SNR is low: over a short window the random part of a move is large relative to any predictive part, so signal extraction is hard. Second, transaction costs (spread, slippage, market impact, opportunity cost) are paid on every trade regardless of how long it is held, so many short trades pay the toll many times against a small edge. Either problem alone makes short-term trading harder; both together make alpha extraction at short horizons exceptionally difficult.
Q2. In the toy model with \(k = 4\) bps a day, \(C = 5\) bps and \(\sigma = 60\) bps a day, at what holding period does the trade break even, and is it a clear signal at that point?
Break-even is \(h^* = C/k = 1.25\) days. It is not a clear signal there: \(\text{SNR} = (k^2/\sigma^2)h = (16/3600)\times 1.25 \approx 0.0056\), far below 1. SNR does not reach 1 until \(h = \sigma^2/k^2 = 225\) days. Clearing costs and having a measurable signal are different thresholds, and the second comes much later. (The model is a teaching device, not a claim about any market.)
Q3. The funnel in the course goes 52 → 34 → 13 → 6 → 4. What does the systematic route lose to the 48 rejected ideas, and what does passing every gate not prove?
It loses time and compute, and $0 of capital, because every gate runs on history, held-out data or simulation. Passing every gate does not prove an edge exists: with many hypotheses some clear a fixed bar by luck (multiple testing), and walk-forward on one history is still one path. The funnel's job is cheap rejection, which is why survivors go live small.
Q4. Two traders are both −15% from their peak. One has a backtest (max drawdown −22%, 47-day average, 8 of 8 recovered), the other does not. Why does the same number lead to different decisions?
The first trader can compare −15% and 31 days with a distribution: it sits inside the historical range and well within the average duration, so it is "normal variance, continue executing". The second has no reference class: no historical maximum, no expected duration, no idea whether it will recover. A drawdown with nothing to compare it to feels like failure, so they are most likely to abandon the strategy at the worst moment and lock in the loss.
Q5. In the simulation, a strategy with a genuine Sharpe ratio of 0.8 dips 15% in about half of five-year runs. What does that say about judging a strategy by its recent live drawdown?
A drawdown of that size is the normal price of a real edge over a modest sample, so it carries little information about whether the edge is gone. With only one live path, the intuition-based trader cannot separate "unlucky run of a good strategy" from "broken strategy", which is why invalidation takes about six months of live trading in the course's example, against about two weeks when the test runs on history.

Further reading
  • Fischer Black, "Noise", Journal of Finance, 1986 — the classic argument that noise trading is what makes markets liquid and prices only loosely informative.
  • André Perold, "The Implementation Shortfall: Paper versus Reality", Journal of Portfolio Management, 1988 — the standard way to measure how much a trade costs relative to the paper price.
  • David H. Bailey, Jonathan M. Borwein, Marcos López de Prado and Qiji Jim Zhu, "Pseudo-Mathematics and Financial Charlatanism", Notices of the American Mathematical Society, 2014 — how backtest overfitting makes mediocre strategies look good.
  • David H. Bailey and Marcos López de Prado, "The Deflated Sharpe Ratio", Journal of Portfolio Management, 2014 — correcting a Sharpe ratio for the number of strategies tried.
  • Campbell R. Harvey, Yan Liu and Heqing Zhu, "…and the Cross-Section of Expected Returns", Review of Financial Studies, 2016 — hundreds of published return factors, and why the bar for a new one should be higher.
  • Robert Pardo, The Evaluation and Optimization of Trading Strategies, Wiley, 2nd ed. 2008 — the practitioner reference for walk-forward analysis.
  • Malik Magdon-Ismail and Amir Atiya, "Maximum Drawdown", Risk magazine, 2004 — the expected size of the worst drawdown for a given edge and horizon.
