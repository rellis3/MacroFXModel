COG: Reverse-Engineering a Trader's Black-Box Forecast

A worked case study in recovering an unknown formula from nothing but its own published outputs — and in this repo, a formula that turned out to be reproducible but not, on its own, a better trade.

ALREADY BUILT & TESTED — this repo's own case study ~16 min read Prereqs: Correlation & Linear Regression primer, Multiple Testing & the Look-Elsewhere Effect
TL;DR
  • COG (the trader/analyst this repo's education material calls "C.OG") publishes daily manual forecast levels for EURUSD/NQ/GOLD — median and 75th-percentile High-Low range, median and 75th-percentile Open-Close — with no published formula behind them.
  • This repo recovered his calc from his own numbers in two honest, separable steps: ratio diagnostics (no fitting — just dividing his published numbers by each other, checked for stability across days and instruments) and estimator fitting (finding which candidate σ, times which constant, reproduces his levels with the lowest variance).
  • The back-solved result, COG_CONST in js/cogReverseEngineer.js: his median High-Low (1.56) is essentially raw Feller driftless-Brownian theory (1.572) — but his 75th-percentile (1.93 vs Feller's 2.049) and Open-Close numbers (0.74 vs 0.7979 median, 1.24 vs 1.284 75th) run meaningfully tighter than pure theory predicts.
  • Reproducing his line was useful — it removes a manual-paste dependency and lets his numbers be checked and labeled programmatically (js/cogBands.js). It is not the same claim as his line being a good trade.
  • The already-run paired A/B (js/cogFadeEngine.js, js/cogLevelPoc.js) found COG's reproduced line is not a better tradeable fade than this repo's own Feller-based bands, out of sample. That is a real tested null, not an unbuilt candidate — a clean example of this repo's "built ≠ works ≠ has edge" discipline.
Section 01

Why this matters

Every trading desk eventually runs into the same problem from the other side of the table: someone — a competitor, a vendor, a well-followed trader, a black-box signal you're evaluating for a licensing deal — hands you their outputs and nothing else. No formula, no methodology note, no source code. Just numbers, published on a schedule, that look consistent enough to suggest there's a real calculation behind them rather than daily discretion. The question "is this actually one fixed formula, and if so, which one?" is a genuinely useful, transferable skill that has nothing specifically to do with FX or with COG — it's the same problem as identifying an unknown system from its input/output behavior, just applied to a trader's published range forecast instead of a control system or an audio filter.

The honest version of this problem has two traps on either side. Trap one: assume the numbers are unknowable magic and never try, which throws away a genuinely checkable, reproducible signal if one exists. Trap two — the more common and more dangerous one — see a plausible-looking pattern in a handful of published numbers and declare victory: "his 75th percentile is always about 1.9x his median, so that's his formula," based on a sample small enough that the pattern could just as easily be noise. The discipline that keeps you out of trap two is exactly what this lesson works through: separate what you can conclude from raw ratios (zero fitting, so zero curve-fit risk) from what requires an actual estimator fit (some fitting, so some overfit risk), and — critically — never let "I can now reproduce the number" quietly become "the number is worth trading." Those are two different claims, and this repo has the receipts on both: it built the reproduction, and it separately tested the trading claim, and they came back with different answers.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Guessing a recipe by tasting the dish, twenty times

Imagine a chef who publishes the finished dish every day but never the recipe. You can't see the ingredients or the method — only what came out. If you tasted the dish once, you'd have almost nothing to go on; any impression could be a fluke of that one dinner. But if you tasted it every day for a month, in different restaurants of theirs across three different cities, and one specific ratio — say, "how much saltier the deluxe version tastes compared to the regular version" — came out nearly identical every single time, regardless of city, that stability itself is evidence. Not proof of the exact recipe, but strong evidence there IS a fixed recipe rather than an improvising chef, and a clue about what the recipe's ratio actually is. That's exactly the logic of the ratio diagnostics below: dividing COG's own published numbers by each other and checking whether the ratio holds steady across ~20-40 days and across three unrelated instruments (a currency pair, an equity index, a metal). A steady ratio there is not a statistical model fit at all — it's just arithmetic on his own published numbers, which is exactly why it's the trustworthy first step, before any actual curve-fitting begins.

Section 02

The math, step by step

Three stages, each with a different amount of fitting risk attached to it — deliberately ordered from zero risk to the most.

oc_med, oc_75COG's published median and 75th-percentile Open-Close displacement, as % of price.

1. Ratio diagnostics — no fitting, just division. Before fitting anything, divide COG's own published numbers by each other, day by day, and look at the distribution of each ratio:

  • hl_75 / hl_med — his 75th-percentile-to-median mapping. This repo's own theoretical benchmark for this same ratio is Feller's \(BM_{P75}/BM_{P50} = 2.049/1.572 \approx 1.303\) (computed directly in cogReverseEngineer.js as FELLER_HL75_OVER_HL50). If COG's ratio sits near 1.303 and is stable, his percentile mapping shares the same underlying distribution shape as raw Feller theory; a different but still-stable ratio means a different distribution (fatter tails, a vol-of-vol widening, empirical rather than theoretical quantiles) — not an absence of formula, just a different one.
  • oc_med / hl_med — his Open-Close-to-High-Low relationship, benchmarked against Feller's \(HN_{P50}/BM_{P50} = 0.7979/1.572 \approx 0.508\) (FELLER_OC50_OVER_HL50 in the code).
  • oc_75 / oc_med — the Open-Close percentile mapping, the O-C analogue of the first ratio.

The reason this step matters so much is that it costs nothing in overfit risk: it's arithmetic on numbers COG himself already published, with no candidate estimator, no free parameter, nothing to tune. A ratio that stays close to constant across ~20-40 days and across three genuinely unrelated instruments (EURUSD, NQ, GOLD trade on completely different volatility regimes and drivers) is strong, cheap evidence that there is one fixed formula behind the numbers rather than daily discretionary judgment — because a discretionary trader's day-to-day "feel" for the range would have no reason to produce the same ratio on a currency pair, an equity index and a metal.

2. Estimator fitting — which σ, which constant. Ratio diagnostics tell you COG's numbers are internally consistent with a formula; they don't tell you what volatility input feeds that formula. For a menu of candidate σ estimators (this repo already carries several — HV20, GARCH, Yang-Zhang — from volBacktestEngine.js), compute the implied constant on each day:

\[ C_t = \frac{\text{hl\_med}_t / 100}{\sigma_t}, \qquad \text{Feller expects } C_t \approx BM_{P50} \]

The candidate σ whose implied \(C_t\) is the most stable (lowest CV across days) and whose raw value correlates well with COG's published hl_med is the likely estimator behind his numbers — implemented directly as fitEstimator() in cogReverseEngineer.js, which reports exactly those two numbers (cCv, r) per candidate, ranked. The mean of that stable \(C\) itself is informative on its own: near 1.57 says "raw Feller," meaningfully above it says "COG runs a wider range than pure driftless-Brownian theory," meaningfully below it says tighter.

3. The blend test — an explicit overfit trap, tested for on purpose. The natural next question is whether a weighted blend of the top two estimators fits even better than either alone. It's a fair question to ask, but it's exactly the kind of question that can quietly manufacture a false discovery: with only ~20-40 days of COG reference data per instrument, adding degrees of freedom (a mixing weight α on top of an estimator choice) makes it easy to find a blend that fits this particular small sample better than any single estimator purely by chance, the same look-elsewhere problem this curriculum's Multiple Testing lesson covers for scanning many pairs or many thresholds — here the "many things scanned" is every possible mixing weight α. cogReverseEngineer.js encodes the guard directly in code, not just as a caveat: a blend is only reported as a genuine win (blendWins) if it beats the best single estimator's correlation by a wide, pre-set margin (blendMinGain, default 0.03) and the winning mixing weight isn't sitting at a degenerate extreme (α between 0.05 and 0.95) — otherwise the honest default stays parsimony: report the blend's number, but don't let it replace the single best estimator unless it clears that bar comfortably.

The back-solved result. Applying this to COG's actual published manual (Gold/EURUSD/NQ, 19 May 2026 per the code's own comment) gives COG_CONST in cogReverseEngineer.js, compared against this repo's own FELLER theoretical constants:

BM_P50 (median H-L)COG: 1.56 vs Feller: 1.572 — essentially identical. His median range IS raw driftless-Brownian theory.
BM_P75 (75th H-L)COG: 1.93 vs Feller: 2.049 — about 5.8% tighter than pure theory.
HN_P50 (median O-C)COG: 0.74 vs Feller: 0.7979 — about 7.3% tighter.
HN_P75 (75th O-C)COG: 1.24 vs Feller: 1.284 — about 3.4% tighter.

Read together, this is a specific, discoverable, non-trivial finding about COG's methodology: his median High-Low number is close enough to raw Feller theory to look like it's simply computed straight from the driftless-Brownian range distribution with no adjustment at all. But his 75th-percentile and his Open-Close numbers are not — they consistently run tighter than what pure theory would predict, in a way that survived both the ratio-diagnostic check and the estimator fit. That's a real, characterizable deviation from the textbook distribution, not a rounding artifact — exactly the kind of thing that reverse-engineering an unknown formula is supposed to surface, and exactly the kind of thing that would have been invisible without doing the fit honestly (a single day's numbers alone couldn't distinguish "he runs 5-7% tighter on the tail" from ordinary sampling noise).

Worked example

Reading a ratio-diagnostic result

Say the ratio-diagnostics step, run across COG's published EURUSD, NQ and GOLD numbers, reports hl75_over_hl50 with a mean of 1.30 and a low CV (tight spread around that mean, day after day, on all three instruments) and oc50_over_hl50 with a mean of 0.51 and similarly low CV. Compare those directly to this repo's Feller benchmarks, computed straight from the constants above: \(BM_{P75}/BM_{P50} = 2.049/1.572 \approx 1.303\) and \(HN_{P50}/BM_{P50} = 0.7979/1.572 \approx 0.508\). A result that close, that stable, and that consistent across three unrelated instruments is a strong "match" reading: COG's percentile mapping and his O-C-to-H-L relationship are behaving like the same driftless-Brownian distribution this repo's own vol math already assumes — evidence his forecast isn't an arbitrary shape, it shares Feller's shape, at least for those two ratios.

Now contrast that with what the fit for the constants themselves actually showed: COG_CONST.BM_P75 / COG_CONST.BM_P50 = 1.93/1.56 ≈ 1.237 — noticeably below the Feller ratio of 1.303. That's a "mismatch" reading, and mismatch is not a failure of the method, it's information: it says COG's actual 75th-percentile line sits closer to his median than Feller's distribution would predict, i.e. his tail is genuinely compressed relative to raw theory, not merely different by sampling noise (the mismatch is small in absolute magnitude but shows up consistently, per the estimator-fit CV check, not as an isolated outlier day). The lesson for reading either kind of result the same way: a match tells you "shares this piece of the theory," a stable mismatch tells you "here's the specific way his formula deviates from the theory" — both are useful, and neither is established by a single day's number, only by the ratio holding (or consistently not holding) across the whole sample.

Scenario

25 days, three instruments: formula or discretion?

A researcher has pasted 25 days of COG's manual numbers for EURUSD, NQ and GOLD — hl_med, hl_75, oc_med, oc_75 for each day, each instrument. They want to know: is this one fixed formula, or is COG adjusting his numbers by feel each morning? Following the two-stage discipline above:

Step 1 — ratio diagnostics, zero fitting. Compute hl_75/hl_med, oc_med/hl_med, and oc_75/oc_med for every one of the 75 instrument-days (25 days × 3 instruments) and look at the distribution of each. If all three ratios come back with low CV — tightly clustered around a stable mean — and that stability holds within EURUSD alone, within NQ alone, and within GOLD alone (not just pooled across all three, which could hide instrument-specific drift), that's the strongest signal available from raw arithmetic alone that a fixed formula is behind the numbers.

Step 2 — estimator fit. Run fitEstimator() against a handful of candidate σ series (HV20, GARCH, Yang-Zhang) for each instrument and check whether one candidate's implied C comes back with a notably lower CV and a stronger correlation than the others, consistently across all three instruments. A single estimator winning cleanly on all three is a much stronger read than one estimator winning on EURUSD and a different one winning on GOLD — the latter looks more like each instrument's estimator happening to fit that instrument's 25 data points by chance than like a genuinely shared formula.

What a stable-ratio, low-CV result would prove — and wouldn't. It would be solid evidence COG runs a real, repeatable calculation rather than daily discretion, and it would pin down roughly what that calculation is. It would not, on its own, tell you whether trading against that calculation's levels makes money — that is a completely separate question requiring its own costed, out-of-sample test (exactly what cogFadeEngine.js and cogLevelPoc.js exist to run). And with only 25 days per instrument, any single ratio or estimator fit is still a small sample: a result that looks stable on 25 days deserves to be treated as directional evidence about the formula, not as a definitively "solved" black box — more days, if they become available, would only sharpen the read, not overturn the basic two-stage method.

Where this lives in MacroFXModel

Built, reproduced, and honestly tested — not the same thing as "works"

js/cogReverseEngineer.js is the pure core described above: ratioDiagnostics(), fitEstimator(), fitBlend(), fitVol() (fitting directly to COG's published annualized vol number rather than his hl_med, a cleaner target since it removes the range constant from the comparison), and reconstructionFit() — the direct verification that predicts COG's own levels from his own published vol using a candidate constant set and measures the % error, which is how the header comment's claim that COG_CONST reconstructs his numbers near-exactly (and Feller does not) is actually checked, not asserted. The exported COG_CONST and FELLER objects are the two constant sets everything else in this box compares against.

It imports COG_CONST from cogReverseEngineer.js rather than re-inlining the numbers, so the bot's live lines and the reverse-engineered constants can never silently drift apart (Lego Principle 1).

The tested result, stated plainly: reproducing COG's line is not the same as it being a good trade, and this repo already ran that second test. js/cogFadeEngine.js and js/cogLevelPoc.js ran the paired A/B this discipline requires — fade at COG's own reproduced line, on the exact days his reference data covers, with real costs and an out-of-sample split — against this repo's own Feller-based bands. The result: COG's line is not a better tradeable fade. cogFadeEngine.js's own header states the honest prior directly and the result matches it — his median High-Low essentially equals raw Feller (which itself roughly equals the realized median range), while this repo's own reversal-point research already found FX tends to turn before reaching that distance; a dynamic-median distance sweep across a range of multiples was already null, and COG's calibration sits inside that already-null range rather than outside it. A better-calibrated line, by itself, doesn't fix the overshoot-tail and fills problems that made the sweep null in the first place. This is a genuine, already-run tested null — not a candidate still waiting to be tried — and it's a clean illustration of this repo's own working discipline: built ≠ works ≠ has edge. The infrastructure (reverse-engineered formula, drop-in band brick, reproducible line) is sound and useful for understanding and for removing a manual-paste dependency. It does not, by itself, imply the strategy on top of it has edge — and here, tested honestly, it doesn't.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where black-box reverse-engineering quietly goes wrong

Mistaking small-sample coincidence for a real formula match. With ~20-40 days of reference data, a ratio or an implied constant can look stable purely by chance, especially if you only check it on one instrument. The reason this lesson leans on checking stability across three genuinely different instruments (a currency pair, an equity index, a metal) rather than one is precisely to rule this out — a coincidence that happens to hold on EURUSD alone is common; the same coincidence holding independently on NQ and GOLD too is much less likely to be chance.

Overfitting a "blend" to noise. The blend test in fitBlend()/reverseEngineer() exists precisely because a mixture of estimators, tuned freely, will find some mixing weight that fits ~20-40 days of data better than any single estimator — that's not evidence the true process is a blend, it's what an extra free parameter does to a small sample. This is the same look-elsewhere logic as scanning many pairs or many thresholds in the Multiple Testing lesson; the code's explicit blendMinGain margin and the α-not-at-an-extreme check are the practical guardrail, not decoration.

Conflating "we can reproduce his line perfectly" with "his line is worth trading." This is the central trap this whole case study is built to name. A reconstruction error near zero (from reconstructionFit()) proves the formula was correctly identified. It says nothing about whether fading, following, or doing anything else at that level beats costs out of sample — that's an entirely separate empirical question, with its own separate answer, which in this repo came back null. Treat "reproduced" and "profitable" as two different verdicts requiring two different tests, always.

Trusting a single day's ratio. Any one day's hl_75/hl_med can land anywhere due to that day's specific range; it's the distribution across many days (and the CV of that distribution) that carries the evidence, never an individual data point held up as "proof."

Section 04

Self-test

Q1. Why does the ratio-diagnostics step (dividing COG's published numbers by each other) carry essentially zero overfitting risk, unlike the estimator-fitting step?
Because it involves no candidate model and no free parameter — it's pure arithmetic on numbers COG himself already published (e.g. hl_75/hl_med for each day). There's nothing to tune and nothing that could be curve-fit to noise; the ratio either comes out stable across days and instruments or it doesn't. Estimator fitting, by contrast, picks the best-fitting candidate σ out of several options and computes an implied constant from it — a genuine fitting step, and therefore one that can, in principle, latch onto a candidate that merely happens to fit the available sample well.
Q2. What does a stable ratio (low CV) across ~20-40 days AND across EURUSD, NQ, and GOLD actually prove, and what does it not prove?
It's strong evidence COG's forecast is generated by one fixed formula rather than daily discretionary judgment — a discretionary trader's day-to-day "feel" would have no structural reason to produce nearly the same ratio across a currency pair, an equity index and a metal. It does NOT, by itself, prove which exact estimator or constant set produces that ratio (that's what the estimator fit is for), and it does NOT say anything about whether trading at the resulting levels is profitable — that's a separate, later question this repo tested separately and answered null.
Q3. What is the back-solved COG_CONST.BM_P50 value, how does it compare to Feller's BM_P50, and what does that specific comparison mean?
COG_CONST.BM_P50 = 1.56 versus Feller's BM_P50 = 1.572 — essentially identical (within about 0.8%). It means COG's published median High-Low range is, in practice, just the raw driftless-Brownian (Feller) theoretical median with no adjustment: his central forecast number isn't a proprietary tweak, it's standard range theory applied to whatever σ estimator he's using.
Q4. Which of COG's four back-solved constants deviate most from Feller theory, and in which direction?
BM_P75 (1.93 vs Feller's 2.049, about 5.8% tighter), HN_P50 (0.74 vs 0.7979, about 7.3% tighter), and HN_P75 (1.24 vs 1.284, about 3.4% tighter) all run consistently TIGHTER than pure Feller theory would predict — only BM_P50 (the median High-Low) matches Feller almost exactly. The deviation shows up specifically in the tail (75th percentile) and in the Open-Close numbers, not in the median High-Low.
Q5. Why does cogReverseEngineer.js require a blend of estimators to beat the best single estimator by a wide, pre-set margin (blendMinGain) before treating it as a real improvement?
Because with only ~20-40 days of COG reference data per instrument, a blend adds a free mixing-weight parameter (α) on top of an estimator choice, and an extra free parameter will almost always find some value that fits a small sample marginally better purely by chance — this is the same look-elsewhere risk as scanning many candidate pairs or thresholds. Requiring the blend to win by a wide, consistent margin (and requiring α not to sit at a degenerate extreme near 0 or 1) is the guard against mistaking that sampling noise for a genuine two-estimator formula.
Q6. This repo successfully reverse-engineered and can now reproduce COG's exact forecast line programmatically. Does that mean fading at his line is a validated trading edge? What did the actual test find?
No — and this is the central point of the whole case study. Reproducing the formula only proves the formula was correctly identified; it says nothing about whether trading against the resulting levels clears costs out of sample. This repo ran that separate test directly (js/cogFadeEngine.js, js/cogLevelPoc.js): a paired, costed, out-of-sample A/B against this repo's own Feller-based bands. The result was that COG's reproduced line is NOT a better tradeable fade — a genuine tested null, not an unbuilt candidate. "Built" (the reproduction), "works" (produces the exact numbers), and "has edge" (beats an incumbent OOS) are three different claims, and only the first two held here.
