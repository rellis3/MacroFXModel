What Moves Markets, Part 2: Regimes, Liquidity and Positioning

Risk regimes switch correlations on and off, liquidity sets the tide, institutions trade on a calendar, and crowded positioning decides how violently news lands. Part 2 of 2. A course-notes framework, not a backtested strategy.

01

Recap of part 1. Policy cascades from central banks to bonds, FX, equities and credit; check the levels above your asset.

02

Rate gaps pull capital across borders; the carry trade earns steadily and a 10% yen rally erases about two years of a 4.9% carry.

03

All figures are exemplars and the lead-lags are frameworks, not tested results.

Section 4 · It actually happened

March 2020: fundamentals said sell, liquidity said buy

→
Buys bonds from dealers
→
Dealers deploy the cash
→
Prices up, yields down
It actually happened

The S&P 500 was about a third below its February peak and the second quarter would be the worst quarter of GDP on record. On 23 March 2020 the Fed announced purchases in the amounts needed to support market functioning. The S&P closed that day at 2,237.40, its low of the sell-off, and was roughly 45% higher by early June. The course: $2.9T injected April to June, then $120B a month, with Nasdaq new highs. The vaccine news came in November.

🛁

The bathtub

QE raises the water level and everything floats; QT drains it. Track the Fed balance sheet, RRP and TGA weekly.

⚠️

Caveat

One episode, and fiscal support arrived at the same time. "Liquidity almost always beats fundamentals" is a rule of thumb.

Section 5

The institutional calendar: forced trades

A 60/40 fund after a strong stock month is overweight equities and has to sell: w′ = w(1 + rE) / (w(1 + rE) + (1 − w)(1 + rB)).

Try it
Equity return in the month+5%
Bond return in the month0.0%
Drifted weights from 60/40: equities 61.17%, bonds 38.83%
To rebalance: sell equities, buy bonds 1.17% of the portfolio, which is $1.17bn per $100bn
📅

OpEx

The third Friday (always the 15th to the 21st). Pin risk and dealer hedging dominate; real moves often come the week after.

🍂

Seasonality

September historically weakest; November–December strong; quarter-end window dressing. The "why" matters less than the "what", says the course.

The course says stocks +5% against flat bonds gives "62/38". The arithmetic gives 61.2/38.8; 62/38 needs about +8.8%.

Section 6

Positioning is potential energy

News explains moves afterwards. Crowding tells you how far a move can run once a catalyst arrives. COT index I = 100 × (x − xmin) / (xmax − xmin).

Try it
Net speculative position, thousand contracts (window −120 to +100)+40
COT index 72.7 · neutral: no extreme
GameStop, January 2021

Short interest above 100% of the float (about 140% in the course), retail buying, margin calls, forced covering, more buying: a reflexive loop. The course puts the rise at about +1,700% in weeks with the business unchanged. The size of the move tracked the crowding, not the news. An extreme case, not a base rate. The 90 / 10 cut-offs are my convention.

Section 7

Where this needs qualifying, and what this desk found

Where this needs qualifying

These are course-notes frameworks with exemplar figures, and the cleanest cases (2020, GameStop) are hindsight picks. The month-end drift is real arithmetic, but it has not been shown here to leave a footprint large enough to trade after costs.

Section 8

Quick check

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Ten equal assets: how much does the portfolio's volatility, relative to one asset, change when the average correlation goes from 0.3 to 0.9?

√((1 + 9ρ)/10): 0.3 gives √0.37 = 60.8%, 0.9 gives √0.91 = 95.4%. Diversification is a bet that correlations stay low, and it fails when it is most needed.
Question 2

A 60/40 fund's stocks gain 5% and bonds are flat. What weights does it hold, and what must it do?

0.60 × 1.05 = 0.63 against 0.40 bonds, a total of 1.03, so equities are 61.17%. Rebalancing means selling the 1.17% excess. A rally to 65% would need about +23.8%.
Question 3

What does an extreme crowded position tell you?

Crowding is stored energy and the magnitude tracks the crowding, as in GameStop. The direction depends on who has to act and on the catalyst, so positioning is context, not a direction signal.
Question 4

The S&P 500 closed at its low on 23 March 2020, the day the Fed promised purchases in the amounts needed. What does the course conclude, and what is the caveat?

The index rose roughly 45% by early June while the second quarter was the worst on record for GDP, and vaccine results came only in November. "Almost always" is a rule of thumb, not a law.
Your call
Scenario drill · 23 March 2020

The Fed has just promised to buy in the amounts needed

It is the morning of 23 March 2020. The S&P 500 is about a third below its February high, the VIX is above 60, and the data are terrible and about to get worse: lockdowns are spreading, unemployment claims are about to explode, and no vaccine is in sight. You are short equity index futures and have been right for a month. This morning the Federal Reserve announced that it would buy Treasuries and agency mortgage securities in the amounts needed to restore smooth market functioning. What do you do with the short?

  1. Add to it. The fundamentals are collapsing and the market has not priced the data yet.
    Fundamentals can be right and still lose to the tide. The announcement changes the supply of money and the willingness of dealers to hold risk, which is the course's point that liquidity beats fundamentals. Adding to a short on the day the biggest buyer in the system steps in is fighting the Fed, in the most expensive way.
  2. Hold the short until the economic data or a vaccine news item shows it is safe to cover.
    The data and the vaccine arrived long after the low. The first efficacy results came in November; the second-quarter GDP print came in July. A rule that waits for fundamentals to improve would have covered months after the rally began.
  3. Cover most or all of the short now and stay light, because the regime has changed from a liquidity drain to a liquidity flood, while volatility is still extreme.
    This follows the framework without pretending to call the bottom. Nobody knew 23 March was the low. The decision rests on a change in the liquidity regime, not a prediction, and with a VIX above 60 smaller size is the sensible way to be wrong.
  4. Flip to a large long right away to catch the rebound.
    The direction was right but the size is a bet on a call nobody could make. With volatility this high, a position sized like a normal day can be stopped out by a move that has nothing to do with the thesis. Defensible, not best.

What happened. The S&P 500 closed at 2,237.40 on 23 March 2020, its closing low of the sell-off, about 34% below its February peak, and then rose. By early June it was roughly 45% higher, with the second-quarter economy still contracting at a record pace. The course reads this as liquidity beating fundamentals. A fair caveat: this was one episode, and at the same time governments were announcing large fiscal support.

Sources: Federal Reserve Board, press release, 23 March 2020; S&P 500 daily closes, FRED series SP500; CBOE VIX daily closes, FRED series VIXCLS.

Section 9

What to carry forward

01

Know your regime: correlations spike in stress (ten assets: 61% of one asset's volatility at 0.3, 95% at 0.9). Three or more daily warnings means cut gross exposure first.

02

Liquidity sets the tide (March 2020). Track the Fed balance sheet, RRP and TGA weekly; do not fight the flow.

03

Flows are mechanical: month-end, OpEx, reconstitution. A 60/40 fund after +5% stocks sells about 1.2% of the portfolio. Do not be the liquidity for a forced trade unless you are paid.

04

Position before narrative: crowding plus a catalyst means a violent move, and crowding tells you how violent, not which way.

05

On this desk's data, crowded positioning predicting direction and a month-end drag both came back null. The mechanisms are real; the trades are not shown.

What this now lets you do

Before acting on a headline or a chart, ask which regime it is in, what the liquidity tide is doing, who is forced to trade today and who is leaning on which wall. Together with part 1, that is the whole six-question checklist.
