Recap of part 1 (read it first if you have not):
  • Policy shifts cascade in order: central banks, government bonds, G10 currencies, equities, credit. Each step down adds lag and noise, so check the levels above your asset before any position.
  • Interest-rate gaps pull capital across borders. The carry trade harvests the gap and gives years of it back in days when the funding currency rallies: a 10% yen rally against a 4.9% carry is about two years gone.
  • Every figure in the course is an exemplar and every lead-lag is a framework, not a backtested result; the verdict boxes show what has and has not been tested.
TL;DR
  • Markets switch between risk-on and risk-off, and in stress correlations converge towards 1: ten assets that diversify to about 61% of one asset's volatility at a correlation of 0.3 only get to 95% at 0.9. Check the regime (VIX level and structure, credit spreads, yen and franc, stock-bond correlation) before the trade thesis.
  • Liquidity, not just fundamentals, sets the tide. The course case is March 2020: the worst quarter of GDP on record, yet equities bottomed on the day the Fed promised to buy in the amounts needed (the press called it unlimited QE).
  • Institutions rebalance, expire options and window-dress on a calendar, and mandates force them to. A 60/40 fund whose stocks gain 5% against flat bonds drifts to 61.2/38.8 and must sell about 1.2% of the portfolio. Know the calendar so you are not the liquidity for someone's forced trade.
  • News explains moves after the fact; positioning tells you how violently a move can happen. Crowding is stored energy and a catalyst releases it. Real-world episode: GameStop, January 2021, short interest above 100% of the float and a price that rose many-fold in weeks with the business unchanged.
Section 01

Risk regimes: knowing which game you are in

Markets oscillate between risk-on and risk-off. In those regimes cross-asset correlations become highly predictable, because capital flows mechanically between safe and risky assets. The course's strongest sentence on the topic: knowing which regime you are in matters more than your specific trade thesis.

What leans which way

Choose a regime. Risk-off is the course's list: capital exits equities, EM, credit, oil and the Australian dollar and goes into Treasuries, the yen, the franc, gold and dollar cash. Risk-on is the mirror image, with the course's own examples at the top (equities, AUD/JPY as the best risk-on proxy, falling VIX, Treasury prices sold). The correlation problem

Modern Portfolio Theory assumes correlations are stable. In stress, correlations converge towards 1: everything falls together, exactly when diversification is needed. The course gives three bands: 0.2 to 0.4 normal markets, diversification works; 0.5 to 0.7 elevated stress, reduce exposure; 0.8 to 1.0 crisis, everything moves together, cash is king.

Watch the formula build
Why diversification melts as correlation rises (equal weights, equal volatilities)
\[ \frac{\sigma_p^2}{\sigma^2} = \color{#fbbf24}{\frac{1}{N}} \]

Start with the part that always diversifies. Each of \(N\) equal-weight assets contributes its own variance, scaled down by its weight squared. Alone this shrinks like \(1/N\).

\[ \frac{\sigma_p^2}{\sigma^2} = \frac{1}{N} + \color{#fbbf24}{\frac{N-1}{N}\,\rho} \]

Add the co-movement. Every asset has \(N-1\) partners, each adding \(\rho\) times the product of the weights. This term does not shrink as you add assets; it settles at \(\rho\).

\[ \frac{\sigma_p}{\sigma} = \color{#10b981}{\sqrt{\frac{1 + (N-1)\rho}{N}}} \]

Take the square root for volatility. At \(\rho = 0\) it is \(1/\sqrt{N}\); at \(\rho = 1\) it is 1: no benefit at all. As \(N\) grows, the floor is \(\sqrt{\rho}\).

σ_p / σportfolio volatility as a share of one asset's volatility (1 means no diversification)
Nnumber of equally weighted assets
ρthe average pairwise correlation, assumed the same for every pair
√ρthe floor you can never diversify below, however many assets you add
Diversification vs correlation: portfolio volatility as a share of one asset's
Average correlation ρ0.30
Number of assets N10
Portfolio volatility: 60.8% of one asset's · diversification benefit 39.2% · floor as N grows (√ρ) 54.8%
Band: normal
With N = 2 the same formula reproduces the stock-bond flip the course describes: at ρ = −0.30 the pair keeps 59.2% of one asset's volatility (a 40.8% benefit); at ρ = +0.40 it keeps 83.7% (a 16.3% benefit). Equal weights and equal volatilities are a simplification; a real 60/40 book is not that.
The correlation is clamped at \(-1/(N-1)\), the lowest value for which N equal assets can all be that negatively correlated. Bands follow the course's ranges; values in the gaps (for example 0.45) are treated as belonging to the band below.
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine ten friends carrying umbrellas, and each one gets wet at a random time. Most of the time someone is dry, so the group stays comfortable. Then a huge storm hits: everyone gets wet at the same moment. The friends did not become more numerous, they became the same. In a crisis, different assets stop being different friends and start behaving like one friend.

⚠️
Critical warning (from the course)

Correlations are not stable

In March 2020, correlations across risk assets spiked above 0.9 within 48 hours. In 2022 the −0.30 SPX/UST correlation flipped to +0.40 for much of the year, so stocks and bonds fell together. A "diversified" portfolio can become one concentrated bet. Verify the current regime's correlations before relying on historical averages.

Section 02

Central-bank liquidity regimes

The course calls liquidity the dominant variable in modern markets: "don't fight the Fed" isn't wisdom, it's mathematics. Liquidity is the amount of money in the financial system. More money means higher asset prices; less means lower.

💡
Bathtub analogy

Central banks control the faucet. QE raises the water level and everything floats higher; QT drains the tub and boats that were comfortably afloat find the bottom. A single boat's quality matters much less than whether the water is rising or falling.

QEQuantitative easing. The central bank creates new money and buys bonds from banks, putting cash into the banking system and eventually into other assets.
QTQuantitative tightening. The central bank lets bonds mature without replacing them, or sells them, and money is removed from the system.
TGA, RRPThe Treasury General Account and the overnight reverse-repo facility. The course says to track the Fed balance sheet, the RRP and the TGA weekly.

Section 03

It actually happened: March 2020, the liquidity firehose

It actually happened

Fundamentals said sell; liquidity said buy; liquidity won

By late March 2020 the picture looked as bad as markets get. Lockdowns had begun, unemployment was spiking, and earnings were collapsing; the second quarter would turn out to be the worst quarter of GDP on record. The S&P 500 was about a third below its February peak. On the morning of 23 March the Federal Reserve announced that it would buy Treasury and agency mortgage securities in the amounts needed to support the functioning of markets, which the press called unlimited QE. The S&P 500 closed that day at 2,237.40, its low of the sell-off. By early June it was roughly 45% higher.

The course's account of the rest: about $2.9 trillion injected in April to June, then $120 billion a month from July through December, with the Nasdaq making new highs month after month. The key tell it draws is the timing. The bottom came from the liquidity announcement, not from vaccine news (the first efficacy results came in November) or from any data. The course adds that combined Fed, ECB, BoJ and BoE balance sheets and the S&P 500 move together across QE zones (up) and QT zones (struggle).

⚠️
Where the textbook version breaks

"Liquidity beat fundamentals. It almost always does."

2020 is the course's flagship case. One episode cannot show "almost always", and fiscal stimulus and a very fast policy response arrived at the same time. In QT episodes the evidence is harder to read, because markets price expected policy before it happens.

Desk test: too small to tell Net liquidity (Fed balance sheet minus TGA minus RRP) as a tailwind. 786 Fridays, 2011 to 2026, first-published vintages only. Weeks with a rising 4-week change were followed by +1.09% on the S&P 500 over the next 4 weeks against +0.67% after falling weeks: a +0.42 percentage-point difference with an interval of −0.47 to +1.32, inside the placebo. The sign is right but the design could only have detected about 2.0 pp. That rules out a large effect and says nothing about a small one. The Nasdaq version is the same shape. Quoted as context, never as a forecast.
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

If the water in a swimming pool is rising, every toy floating in it goes up, the good toys and the broken ones. When the water is drained, every toy sinks towards the bottom. QE is someone turning the tap on; QT is someone pulling the plug. In March 2020 the pool was nearly empty, and someone turned the tap to full.

Section 04

The institutional flow calendar

Institutional money moves on predictable schedules: pension rebalancing, index reconstitution, options expiration and month-end flows. They create mechanical buying and selling pressure unrelated to fundamentals. The reason is mandates. A 60/40 fund whose equities rally to 65% must sell at month-end to rebalance, which is not a choice. Know the calendar so you are not the liquidity for someone else's forced trade.

Month-end rebalancing

  1. Stocks outperform during the month (the course's example is +5% against flat bonds).
  2. The portfolio is now overweight equities against its 60/40 target.
  3. The fund must sell stocks and buy bonds: sell winners, buy losers, the opposite of momentum.
  4. That creates counter-trend pressure in the final two to three days after a strong month.
Watch the formula build
How far a 60/40 portfolio drifts in a month, and what it must trade
\[ V_E = w\,\color{#fbbf24}{(1 + r_E)} \]

Start with the equity sleeve. It begins at target weight \(w\) and grows by the month's equity return \(r_E\).

\[ V_B = (1 - w)\,\color{#fbbf24}{(1 + r_B)} \]

The bond sleeve starts at \(1-w\) and grows by the bond return \(r_B\).

\[ w' = \frac{V_E}{\color{#fbbf24}{V_E + V_B}} \]

The new equity weight is its share of the new total.

\[ \text{trade} = \color{#10b981}{w' - w} \quad\text{(sell equities if positive)} \]

To return to target the fund sells the excess, as a share of the whole portfolio.

wtarget equity weight (0.60 in a 60/40 fund)
r_E, r_Bthe month's equity and bond returns
w′the equity weight after the drift, before rebalancing
w′ − wthe equity sale, as a share of the portfolio

Options expiration

  • Monthly options expire on the third Friday.
  • Pin risk. Prices gravitate towards strikes with high open interest into expiry. This is hedging mechanics, not news.
  • Gamma squeeze. Dealer hedging near expiry can amplify moves in either direction, in self-reinforcing loops.

Trading implication (as taught): OpEx week, especially the Friday, is dominated by hedging flows rather than information. Reduce directional bets into expiry; real moves often happen the week after.

Seasonality and quarter-end

  • September is historically the weakest month for the S&P 500 (a 74-year pattern in the course's 1950 to 2024 data). Theories: tax-loss selling ahead of October's mutual-fund fiscal year-end, institutional portfolio reviews, and pattern-following.
  • November and December are historically strong (the "Santa rally"): tax-loss selling is done, bonuses are invested, holidays lift optimism. The course says the "why" matters less than the "what".
  • Quarter-end window dressing. Managers buy winners so they show up in their reports.

Section 05

Positioning data and flow analysis

News explains moves retroactively; positioning shows where crowded trades are before they unwind. The same headline can be bullish or bearish depending on existing positioning. Positioning is potential energy.

💡
Room analogy

Everyone leaning on one wall of a room: any shout of "fire" causes a violent stampede to the other side. People spread evenly across the room move in an orderly way. The shout is the news; the lean is the positioning.

The narrative trap: same news, opposite headlines

Headlines written after the move
If stocks riseIf stocks fall
"Stocks rally on strong jobs data""Stocks fall on hot jobs (Fed fears)"
"Market shrugs off hawkish Fed""Hawkish Fed sends stocks tumbling"
"Earnings beat expectations""Guidance disappoints despite beat"

The narrative is written after the move to explain it, so it has no predictive value. Professionals ask: how is the market positioned, and will this news force repositioning? Not: what does this news mean?

The trap: a headline explains a move only after the fact. The same story is written as bullish or bearish depending on how the market was positioned beforehand. Trading the headline instead of the positioning behind it means reacting to the story, not the mechanism.

Where positioning data comes from

📋

CFTC COT report (Fridays)

Futures positioning by trader type, commercial hedgers versus speculators. Extreme speculator long or short often precedes reversals, in the course's account.

💧

Fund flows

ETF and mutual-fund flows. Massive inflows often mark tops (everyone who wants to buy has bought); outflows can mark bottoms.

🔻

Short interest

Very high short interest means squeeze potential if price rises.

⚖️

Options put/call ratio

Heavy puts read as bearish sentiment (a contrarian buy); heavy calls read as bullish sentiment (a contrarian sell).

Reading the extremes

  • Extreme long. Crowded long, vulnerable to negative catalysts: everyone who wants to buy already has, so who is left to buy?
  • Neutral. No extreme. Fundamentals and news flow drive direction.
  • Extreme short. Crowded short, vulnerable to a squeeze: forced covering accelerates rallies.

A crowded book tells you how far a move can run once something triggers it. It does not tell you which way the trigger will push. That distinction is the one the verdicts below keep making.

Watch the formula build
Putting a number on "extreme": the COT index
\[ x - x_{\min} \]

Take the current net speculative position \(x\) and measure how far it sits above its lowest reading in the look-back window.

\[ \frac{x - x_{\min}}{\color{#fbbf24}{x_{\max} - x_{\min}}} \]

Divide by the window's full range. The result is between 0 (the most short it has been) and 1 (the most long).

\[ I = \color{#10b981}{100 \times \frac{x - x_{\min}}{x_{\max} - x_{\min}}} \]

Scale to 0 to 100. Readings near the ends mean the book is crowded one way relative to its own history. The 90 and 10 cut-offs below are a common convention I chose for illustration; the course does not fix them.

Case study — GameStop, January 2021

A textbook positioning unwind

  • Setup. Short interest above 100% of the float, about 140% in the course's figure: more shares sold short than the company had available to trade. Extreme crowding.
  • Catalyst. Retail buying lifted the price, and shorts faced margin calls.
  • Unwind. Forced covering meant more buying, which meant more margin calls: a reflexive loop.
  • Result. The course puts the rise at about +1,700% in weeks with nothing about the business changed.

The course's lesson: extreme positioning is stored energy and a catalyst releases it. The size of the move is proportional to the crowding, not to the importance of the news. GameStop is an extreme case, not a base rate.

The pre-trade positioning checklist

  1. Who is already in this trade? (COT, flows, options positioning)
  2. What would force them out? (identify the unwind catalyst)
  3. Am I early or late? If everyone is talking about it, you are late; the move happens when positioning builds, not when it is recognised.
  4. What is my edge over the crowd? If you cannot identify one, you are the crowd.
Desk test: nothing found "Extreme speculator positioning predicts which way a market goes next." CFTC data, 8 instruments, 899 scored weeks each, 3,576 out-of-sample rows 2018 to 2026. Pooled out-of-sample rank-correlation −0.0317 (about 1.7 sigma), p = 0.094 against a p < 0.05 bar: fail. Per instrument the spread is wide and the ends disagree (GBP −0.228, gold +0.117, AUD +0.004). Positioning says who holds what and who has to act if they are wrong. It is not a direction signal.
Desk test: nothing found "Everyone is short bonds into the Fed, so a consensus decision squeezes them." 84 meetings since 2010. After crowded-short meetings the 30-year yield rose +4 bp over five sessions against −0.3 bp after the rest; on consensus decisions +9.1 bp. The shorts were paid, not squeezed. Check the CFTC percentile before repeating "everyone is short".
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

A see-saw with ten kids piled on one end is held up by a single plank. If one kid jumps off, the whole end crashes, and the kids on the other side go flying. The kids on the see-saw are positioning. The kid who jumps is the news. It is not the jump that decides how hard it crashes, it is how many kids were piled on. And the pile does not tell you who will jump, or whether they jump left or right.

Section 06

Putting it together

The six pieces answer six questions: who moves first (the hierarchy), why money moves (rate gaps), how risk is priced (the regime), how much water is in the tub (liquidity), who has to trade (the calendar) and who is leaning (positioning). Ask them in that order before a trade, and write down which answers you looked up rather than assumed.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where this framework is easy to misuse

Reading a framework as a forecast. The hierarchy says the order effects arrive in, not when or by how much. Several of its headline lead-lags are taught as averages with no direct test, and the shorter-horizon versions tested on this desk's data came back null.

Hindsight cases and exemplar figures. 2020, 2022 and GameStop are tidy examples picked because the story is clean. The correlation matrix, the QE magnitudes and the regime thresholds are typical ranges that drift with the era.

Mistaking a mechanism for a trade. The month-end drift is real arithmetic and the carry unwind is a real chain. Neither has been shown here to leave a footprint large enough to trade after costs. Knowing the flow exists helps you avoid being its victim; it does not by itself pay you.

Scenario drill · 23 March 2020

The Fed has just promised to buy in the amounts needed

It is the morning of 23 March 2020. The S&P 500 is about a third below its February high, the VIX is above 60, and the data are terrible and about to get worse: lockdowns are spreading, unemployment claims are about to explode, and no vaccine is in sight. You are short equity index futures and have been right for a month. This morning the Federal Reserve announced that it would buy Treasuries and agency mortgage securities in the amounts needed to restore smooth market functioning. What do you do with the short?

  1. Add to it. The fundamentals are collapsing and the market has not priced the data yet.
    Fundamentals can be right and still lose to the tide. The announcement changes the supply of money and the willingness of dealers to hold risk, which is the course's point that liquidity beats fundamentals. Adding to a short on the day the biggest buyer in the system steps in is fighting the Fed, in the most expensive way.
  2. Hold the short until the economic data or a vaccine news item shows it is safe to cover.
    The data and the vaccine arrived long after the low. The first efficacy results came in November; the second-quarter GDP print came in July. A rule that waits for fundamentals to improve would have covered months after the rally began.
  3. Cover most or all of the short now and stay light, because the regime has changed from a liquidity drain to a liquidity flood, while volatility is still extreme.
    This follows the framework without pretending to call the bottom. Nobody knew 23 March was the low. The decision rests on a change in the liquidity regime, not a prediction, and with a VIX above 60 smaller size is the sensible way to be wrong.
  4. Flip to a large long right away to catch the rebound.
    The direction was right but the size is a bet on a call nobody could make. With volatility this high, a position sized like a normal day can be stopped out by a move that has nothing to do with the thesis. Defensible, not best.

What happened. The S&P 500 closed at 2,237.40 on 23 March 2020, its closing low of the sell-off, about 34% below its February peak, and then rose. By early June it was roughly 45% higher, with the second-quarter economy still contracting at a record pace. The course reads this as liquidity beating fundamentals. A fair caveat: this was one episode, and at the same time governments were announcing large fiscal support.

Sources: Federal Reserve Board, press release, 23 March 2020; S&P 500 daily closes, FRED series SP500; CBOE VIX daily closes, FRED series VIXCLS.

Section 07

Key Takeaways

✓Know your regime. Risk-on and risk-off are distinct states; correlations rise in stress (ten assets: 61% of one asset's volatility at 0.3, 95% at 0.9). Check VIX structure and credit spreads daily; three or more warnings means cut gross exposure before analysing single positions.
✓Liquidity sets the tide. Track the Fed balance sheet, the RRP and the TGA weekly, and do not fight the flow. March 2020 is the flagship case.
✓Flows are mechanical. Month-end, OpEx and reconstitution create forced trades. A 60/40 fund after a +5% equity month sells about 1.2% of its portfolio. Do not provide liquidity to scheduled flows unless you are paid to.
✓Position before narrative. Extreme positioning plus a catalyst makes a violent move. Check COT, flows and options positioning before trading headlines.
⚠The figures are exemplars. Correlations, QE magnitudes and regime thresholds are typical ranges from the course, not constants. The month-end example also drifts to 61.2/38.8, not 62/38.
✗The mechanisms are real; the trades are not shown. Whether crowded positioning predicts direction and whether a month-end drag exists are separate questions from whether the mechanisms exist, and the course states them as lessons, not as measured results. On this desk's data each of those came back null; see the verdict boxes above.
Section 08

Self-test

Q1. Ten equally weighted assets have an average correlation of 0.3 in calm markets and 0.9 in a crisis. How much does the portfolio's volatility, as a share of one asset's, change, and why does that matter?
\(\sqrt{(1 + 9\rho)/10}\): at 0.3 that is \(\sqrt{0.37} = 60.8\%\), and at 0.9 it is \(\sqrt{0.91} = 95.4\%\). The diversification benefit shrinks from about 39% to under 5%. Diversification is a bet on correlations staying low, and it fails exactly when it is needed. The course's examples are March 2020 (above 0.9 within 48 hours) and 2022, when the −0.30 stock-bond correlation turned to +0.40.
Q2. The course says liquidity beat fundamentals in March 2020. What is the evidence in the case, and what are the caveats?
The S&P 500 closed at its low on 23 March, the day the Fed promised purchases in the amounts needed, then rose roughly 45% by early June while the second quarter was the worst quarter of GDP on record. The timing points to the announcement, not to data or to vaccine news, which came later. The caveats: it is one episode, fiscal support arrived at the same time, and "it almost always does" is a rule of thumb. On this desk's test of net liquidity as a tailwind (786 weeks), the sign was right but the effect was too small to tell from noise.
Q3. A 60/40 fund's stocks gain 5% in a month while bonds are flat. What weights does it hold, and what does it have to trade?
The equity sleeve is \(0.60 \times 1.05 = 0.63\), the bond sleeve is 0.40, the total is 1.03, so equities are \(0.63/1.03 = 61.17\%\) and bonds 38.83%. The fund sells \(61.17 - 60 = 1.17\%\) of the portfolio, about $1.17bn on $100bn, selling winners and buying losers. The course's example rounds the drift to 62/38, which would take about an 8.8% equity gain. On this desk's data, though, the last three sessions after the strongest months did not show a drag (+0.11%, interval −0.32% to +0.50%), so the mechanism is real arithmetic without a visible footprint.
Q4. The same jobs report is written up as "Stocks rally on strong jobs data" one day and "Stocks fall on hot jobs" another. What question do professionals ask instead, and what does a crowded position tell you?
They ask how the market is positioned and whether the news will force repositioning, because a headline is written after the move and has no predictive value on its own. A crowded position tells you how violent a move can be once a catalyst arrives (the magnitude scales with the crowding, as in GameStop), not which way. Positioning says who has to act if they are wrong, not what the market does next. This desk's one registered test of crowded positioning as a direction signal was a null.
