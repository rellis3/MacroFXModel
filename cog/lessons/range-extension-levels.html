Range Extension Levels

A systematic, non-statistical way to read where price might run out of steam: project a completed session's range forward as clean multiples, then treat the spots where two different sessions' projections land on top of each other as the day's highest-conviction levels.

COURSE NOTES — study notes on an applied trading lesson, not a backtested MacroFXModel strategy ~11 min read Prereqs: The Daily Forecaster Walkthrough
TL;DR
  • A range extension is not a statistical measure — it's the Asia session's own range (00:00–06:00 London time, measured on 5-minute candle closes) projected forward as simple multiples (1x, 2x… up to 10.5x). It's a forward-projecting structural read on how far price has expanded, deliberately not a backward-looking standard deviation.
  • Tool setup repurposes a TradingView Fibonacci retracement tool with its levels reset to a custom ladder (quarter-steps inside the range, half-steps out to 10.5x beyond it), applied to the Asia range on EUR/USD's 5-minute chart via the "Asian Range by Nico948" indicator on an OANDA feed.
  • Projecting extension levels runs in both directions: drawing the Fib tool from the range's highest close to its lowest close projects levels above price (potential short-consideration exhaustion zones); the reverse direction projects levels below price (potential long-consideration zones).
  • Alignment zones — where an extension level from today's Asia range and one from yesterday's fall within 2 pips of each other on EUR/USD — are the framework's highest-probability levels; a single session's level alone is lower conviction.
  • The lesson's daily checklist runs once per day, after the Asia session closes: mark today's range, project extensions above and below it, overlay yesterday's extensions, then scan for 2-pip alignment zones.

These notes convert applied course material on projecting session-range extensions into price levels — the raw study notes this page distills are in education/range-extension-levels-notes.md. Define the Asia session range (00:00–06:00 London time) on the 5-minute chart, measured from candle closes, not wicks. Project that range's size as multiples — 1x, 2x, 3x, up to 10.5x — both above and below the range, using a repurposed TradingView Fibonacci retracement tool. Each level marks how far price has extended relative to the Asia range; far extensions are candidate exhaustion zones (shorts above, longs below). The highest-probability levels are alignment zones, where extensions from today's and yesterday's Asia ranges land within tolerance (2 pips on EUR/USD) of each other. Levels are zones where price may react, not guarantees — always combined with risk management. This is educational content only, not financial advice.

Section 01

1. Understanding range extensions

The lesson flags one distinction as crucial — it "fundamentally changes how you interpret and apply the levels": a range extension is not the same thing as a mathematical standard deviation, even though both are ways of describing "how far price moved."

What it measures✗ Standard deviations measure statistical dispersion from a mean, using historical price data. ✓ Range extensions measure the size of a defined range (the Asia session) projected as multiples into price space.
Assumptions✗ Standard deviations assume a normal distribution and calculate variance across a dataset. ✓ Range extensions assume nothing stated — they measure how far price can extend from a known range.
Direction of inference✗ Standard deviations are a backward-looking statistical measure. ✓ Range extensions are a forward-projecting structural measure.

Why this matters, close to the lesson's own words: range extensions are anchored to today's volatility context. A 2x extension means price has moved twice the distance of the Asia range. This is a structural observation about market expansion, not a statistical probability statement.

The lesson introduces four building blocks on top of that distinction:

The Asia RangeThe foundational range from which all extensions are measured — 00:00–06:00 London time.
Extension MultiplesEach level (1x, 2x, 3x…) is how many times the Asia range's distance price has traveled.
Exhaustion ZonesExtension levels mark where price has moved significantly and may find exhaustion.
Alignment ZonesWhere extensions from multiple sessions overlap — the higher-probability areas.
Section 02

2. Tool setup

Step 01 — configure the Fibonacci tool (TradingView)

The Fib retracement tool is repurposed to project range extension levels rather than retracements. Set its levels to:

0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1,
1.5, 2, 2.5, 3, 3.5, 4, 4.5, 5, 5.5, 6, 6.5, 7, 7.5, 8, 8.5, 9, 9.5, 10, 10.5

The structure of that grid is worth noticing: quarter-steps (0.25 / 0.5 / 0.75) exist only inside the range itself (between 0 and 1); beyond 1 the ladder runs in half-steps out to 10.5. The lesson's pro tip: enable "Use one color" in the Fib settings for cleaner visualization, and save the configuration as a template for quick access.

Step 02 — identify the Asia range

The Asia range is the foundational reference — the range from which all extension levels are projected.

  • Time window: 00:00–06:00 London time.
  • Indicator: "Asian Range by Nico948" (TradingView).
  • Data feed: OANDA, for consistent timestamps.
  • Chart context: EUR/USD, 5-minute chart, with a shaded box highlighting the 00:00–06:00 consolidation period that establishes the range.
⚠️
Important, per the lesson

Ensure the chart timezone is set correctly — the Asia range must be consistently defined across all analysis. A mis-set clock silently shifts the entire range.

Section 03

3. Projecting extension levels

Project the Asia range as multiples both above and below current price to identify potential exhaustion zones. All analysis runs on the 5-minute timeframe for precision.

Step 01 — extensions above price (potential short entry zones)

Draw the Fib tool from the highest candle close to the lowest candle close within the Asia range. This projects the extension levels above the range — used to identify potential levels to short from.

Key point, per the lesson: use candle closes, not wicks. Closes represent where price was accepted; wicks represent rejection.

Step 02 — extensions below price (potential long entry zones)

Draw the Fib tool in the opposite direction — from the lowest candle close to the highest candle close. This projects the extension levels below the range.

Extensions above pricePrice has extended upward → levels mark potential exhaustion zones for short considerations.
Extensions below pricePrice has extended downward → levels mark potential exhaustion zones for long considerations.

Section 04

4. Finding alignment zones

The highest-probability levels occur where extensions from multiple Asia ranges overlap.

Multi-session confluence

  • Repeat the full extension-projection process on the previous day's Asia range.
  • Look for areas where levels from both sessions fall close together.

Alignment tolerance

  • EUR/USD: 2 pips.
  • If two extension levels from different sessions fall within 2 pips of each other, mark it as a confluence zone.
  • The lesson illustrates this with two overlaid Asia ranges, where the highlighted overlap area is described as "a high-probability confluence area."

Section 06

6. Knowledge check

Q. What's the key difference between range extensions and mathematical standard deviations?

✗ Range extensions use more data points for higher accuracy.

✗ Standard deviations project future levels while range extensions look backward.

✓ Range extensions project multiples of a defined range forward; standard deviations measure statistical dispersion from historical data.

✗ They are essentially the same calculation with different names.

Watch the second distractor — it's the true statement reversed, an easy trap. It's the standard deviations that look backward and the range extensions that project forward, not the other way around.

Section 07

7. Key takeaways

✓Range extensions ≠ standard deviations. Projecting multiples of a defined range forward, not calculating statistical variance.
✓Extensions measure market expansion. A 3x extension means price has traveled three times the Asia range distance.
✓Confluence amplifies probability. Alignment zones where multiple session extensions overlap offer higher probability than a single session's level alone.
✓Closes, not wicks. The range and its extensions are built from candle closes — acceptance, not the rejection a wick represents.
⚠These are zones, not guarantees. Extension levels identify where price may react — always combine with proper risk management.
⚠Nothing here is backtested yet. Which multiples matter, whether confluence measurably helps, and the right tolerance for other pairs are all open questions the lesson raises but doesn't answer (see §10 below) — course notes, not a validated MacroFXModel strategy.

Section 09

9. Real-time implementation notes

🎯
How this would run at the desk
  • Prep happens once per day, after 06:00 London — the Asia range can't be final until the window closes, so the checklist runs at/after Asia close.
  • Standing chart template: EUR/USD 5m on an OANDA feed, chart timezone verified, the custom Fib template saved, "Asian Range by Nico948" plotted.
  • Two drawings every day (highest-close→lowest-close, then the reverse), plus keep yesterday's two on the chart for the alignment scan.
  • Alignment scan: walk the ladder and tag any today-vs-yesterday level pair within 2 pips — those confluence zones are the priority levels for the day.
  • Directional read at a level: above the range = short consideration; below = long consideration. The framework supplies the level; entry trigger, stop, target and sizing are outside its scope — bring risk management.
  • Housekeeping: re-verify the chart timezone after any platform update or DST change, since a mis-set clock silently shifts the entire range.

10–11. Open questions & areas for further study

Questions to investigate in future

Open questions the lesson raises but doesn't answer — future study/research prompts, not conclusions:

  1. Which multiples matter most? The grid runs to 10.5x. Where do reactions actually cluster — 1x? 2–3x? Does reaction quality decay or improve with distance?
  2. Why would extension multiples mark exhaustion? What's the mechanism — statistical rarity of large moves, self-fulfilling attention on session-range multiples, or intraday mean-reversion after fast expansion?
  3. Does confluence measurably help? Test alignment-zone touches vs single-session-level touches: hit rate, reaction size, after costs.
  4. Tolerance scaling. 2 pips is given for EUR/USD only. What's the right tolerance for other pairs/instruments — fixed pips, %ATR, or a fraction of the Asia range?
  5. Closes vs wicks. The lesson asserts closes are better (acceptance vs rejection). Does the close-to-close range produce better levels than the wick high/low range in a fair A/B?
  6. Is the Asia session special? Would any 6-hour overnight window (or another session's range) project equally useful levels, or is 00:00–06:00 London specifically informative?
  7. Timezone sensitivity. London time shifts against UTC with DST. How sensitive are the levels to a one-hour shift in the window?
  8. More sessions of confluence. The lesson uses two Asia ranges (today + yesterday). Does adding a third session tighten or just thin out the zones?
  9. Extension distance as a market-state reading. If a 3x extension means "significant expansion," can the max multiple reached by a given time of day serve as a trend-day vs range-day indicator?
  10. Relation to volatility measures. The lesson says extensions are "anchored to today's volatility context." How do range-multiple levels compare with σ-based bands built from an explicit vol estimate — do they mark the same prices?

Areas of interest for deeper study

  • Session-range structure generally: how overnight/consolidation ranges relate to the following session's expansion (Asia → London/NY handoff).
  • Acceptance vs rejection as a candle-reading principle (closes vs wicks) — where else does this distinction apply (range definition, level breaks, stop placement)?
  • Confluence as a general method: the lesson's alignment idea (two independent projections agreeing within tolerance) is a pattern that could apply to any level family — pivots, prior highs/lows, volume levels.
  • Forward-projected vs backward-derived levels as two families of technical levels — the lesson's core distinction, worth keeping as a classification lens when studying other frameworks.
  • Exhaustion behaviour at stretched prices: what does "exhaustion" look like on the tape (failure to make new highs, wick clusters, momentum divergence), and how would one confirm it at an extension level?
