Currency Markets & Global Flows

How the largest market on earth is organised, what moves a currency at each horizon, and why carry, the dollar and dollar funding all behave differently once calm turns to stress.

COURSE NOTES — framework as taught, not a backtested strategy ~38 min read Prereqs: Lesson 4, Fixed Income Difficulty: Intermediate
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • FX is the world's largest market (about $7.5 trillion a day in the survey the course quotes, with the dollar on one side of about 88% of trades), so it is liquid, hard to manipulate and quick to price information. For an equity trader it is the transmission mechanism from central-bank policy to every other asset.
  • What moves a currency depends on the horizon. Over weeks to months the dominant driver is the rate differential (policy divergence); in a fear spike risk sentiment overrides it and the havens (USD, JPY, CHF) are bid whatever the rates say.
  • The Dollar Smile: the dollar is strong at both extremes (US exceptionalism on the left, global panic on the right) and weak in the middle (synchronised growth). Ask why the dollar is strong, because the answer changes what it means for other assets.
  • Carry earns the interest-rate gap in calm markets and gives it back in lumps: with spot risk, a 10% fall in a currency yielding 9.9% more cancels a full year of carry, and leverage multiplies both sides. Unwinds are reflexive, so JPY strength in a risk-off is the tell.
  • The cross-currency basis is the premium paid to swap into dollars. A negative basis that widens fast means dollar scarcity. The course's guideposts for EUR/USD run from 0 to −20 bps (normal) to below −100 bps (crisis).
  • Central banks can slow a move, not reverse a trend that fundamentals support. Real-world episodes: the 5 August 2024 yen carry unwind, the March 2020 dollar squeeze, and Japan's yen interventions of 2022–2024.
Section 01

Why this matters: the market everything else trades through

Every asset price is quoted in some currency, and every cross-border investment is a currency position as well as an asset position. A fund that buys Japanese stocks is also long or short the yen. That is why policy divergence between central banks shows up in exchange rates first and in stocks later: a currency is the cheapest, deepest place for large, sophisticated players to express a view on macro.

The course puts FX at Level 3 of its hierarchy: it often moves before equities fully price information, it reveals risk sentiment (JPY strength, EM weakness and basis blowouts have preceded VIX spikes), and it feeds straight into earnings (dollar strength hurts US multinationals' foreign earnings and helps importers). This lesson builds the map in the order a trader needs it: the plumbing, the personalities, the drivers by horizon, the dollar, then the three stress mechanisms (carry, the basis, intervention).

Course note: FX relationships are well-established but regime-varying. Dynamics can shift rapidly during intervention, crisis or regime change.

💡
Plain-English intuition

An exchange rate is the price of one country's money in another's

Think of each currency as a share in a country's economy and policy. When the Fed promises higher rates than the ECB, holding dollars pays more than holding euros, so money tilts toward the dollar and EUR/USD falls. When the world gets frightened, people stop caring about the yield and want the currencies that are safe and easy to sell, so the havens jump. Almost everything in this lesson is one of those two forces, the reward for holding a currency and the fear of holding the wrong one, fighting each other at different speeds.

Reference: quoting conventions, participants and instruments

Quoting conventions

  • Always pairs. EUR/USD = 1.0850 means 1 euro buys 1.0850 dollars. The first currency is the base, the second the quote; the number rising means the base is strengthening.
  • USD/JPY = 150.00 means 1 dollar buys 150 yen. Rising means the USD is strengthening and the yen weakening.
  • Most pairs quote USD first, but EUR, GBP, AUD and NZD quote USD second. That is historical convention, not logic.

Currency personalities: havens, risk currencies and commodity links

Each major currency has a stable character that you can use to cross-check a story. The first split is safe havens (USD, JPY, CHF), which strengthen in stress, against risk currencies (AUD, NZD, CAD, NOK, SEK and emerging markets), which strengthen with risk appetite and weaken in stress and are often commodity- or carry-linked. The yen is the purest haven: it rallies in risk-off even when Japan's fundamentals are weak.

Section 03

What moves currencies: the answer depends on the horizon

"Why did the euro move?" has no single answer, because the dominant driver changes with the time scale. The course sorts them into four horizons, and the useful point is where the signal-to-noise is best: weeks to months, where rate differentials and relative growth do the work.

Rate differentials: the dominant medium-term driver

The 2-year yield differential reflects the policy trajectory over the horizon most relevant for carry and macro positioning, so it is the number to watch. Over weeks to months, differentials typically explain a substantial part of major-pair moves: the course cites 50–80% depending on the period and pair.

Treat "50–80%" as a range people quote, not a constant. It varies by pair and period, it is an in-sample style statement, and the course itself lists when it breaks: safe-haven flows (JPY rallies in risk-off despite a rate disadvantage), intervention, and extreme positioning reversals.

Risk sentiment: the overrider

When fear rises, the order of events is predictable. Once the panic subsides, fundamentals reassert themselves.

Fear risesVIX spikes, credit spreads widen
→
Haven bidUSD, JPY, CHF strengthen regardless of rate differentials
→
Risk currencies sellAUD, NZD, EM fall; carry positions unwind
→
Panic fadesFundamentals (differentials, growth) reassert

Long-term anchors

  • Purchasing power parity (PPP): exchange rates should equalise the price of identical goods. Deviations can persist for years, but extremes tend to mean-revert eventually.
  • Current account: persistent deficits need foreign capital (a weaker currency or higher rates); surpluses give structural support.

Long-term valuation does not time anything, but it frames the context: extreme undervaluation can limit downside and extreme overvaluation can limit upside, eventually.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine a dog on a very long lead walking beside you. Minute to minute the dog zig-zags for no reason you can predict (noise). Over a few weeks it follows you, because the lead is short enough to matter (rate differentials). Over years the lead pulls it back to where a sensible dog should be (valuation). The loud bang that sends the dog bolting to its basket is risk-off.

Section 04

The dollar cycle and the Dollar Smile

Why the dollar is special

  • Reserve currency: about 58–60% of global central-bank reserves (gradually declining, still dominant), so there is structural demand.
  • Invoicing currency for most global trade, even between non-US countries.
  • Funding currency: most global debt is dollar-denominated, so dollar strength makes that debt harder to service.
  • Safe haven: in a crisis the world wants dollars, even when the crisis is US-originated.
💡
Plain-English intuition

Two completely different reasons to want dollars

Sometimes people buy dollars because America is the best place to earn a return: growth is strong, the Fed is hawkish, rate differentials point to the dollar. Sometimes they buy dollars because they are frightened and need the world's safest, most liquid money. In between, when everyone is growing and happy, nobody needs either reason, so the dollar is weakest. Plot the demand against the state of the world and you get a smile. The Dollar Smile framework is generally credited to Stephen Jen, then at Morgan Stanley, in the early 2000s.

Ask "why is the dollar strong?", not just "is it strong?"

Dollar cycles

Dollar cycles are multi-year, historically roughly 7–10 years peak to peak. The 1980s: strong early (Volcker), weak late. The 1990s: weak early, strong late (tech boom). The 2000s: weak (EM boom, commodity supercycle). The 2010s: bottomed around 2011 and strengthened through 2022.

Caution: cycle timing is imprecise. Do not try to call major turns; understand the current regime and what would change it.

How to test it: fix the regime rules in advance from data known at the time (for example the VIX level for the right side and a US-minus-world growth surprise for the left), label every day, then compare the next-5-day dollar-basket move and range across regimes against matched controls, with a shift placebo and a held-out final period.
Section 05

Carry trades: mechanics and risks

A carry trade borrows a low-yielding funding currency and invests in a high-yielding one. The example the course gives: borrow JPY at 0.1%, invest in MXN at 10%, and collect about 9.9% a year. The exposure is the spot rate, because you owe yen and hold pesos: a 10% fall in the peso exceeds a year's carry. This is the classic "pennies in front of a steamroller".

Watch the formula build
Where a carry trade's return comes from, one term at a time
\[ R = \color{#fbbf24}{i_h - i_f} \]

Start with the interest. You hold the high-yielding currency (rate \(i_h\)) and owe interest on the funding currency (rate \(i_f\)). In the course's example that is 10% − 0.1% = 9.9%.

\[ R = (i_h - i_f) + \color{#fbbf24}{s} \]

Add the spot move \(s\): the percentage change in the high-yielder, measured in the funding currency. Nobody pays you for this term. It can be positive or negative, and in a crisis it is the term that dominates.

\[ R_{\text{equity}} = \color{#fbbf24}{L}\,\big[(i_h - i_f) + s\big] \]

Leverage \(L\) multiplies both terms. At 5× a 9.9% differential becomes 49.5% of equity, and so does a 10% spot loss become 50%.

\[ s^{*} = \color{#10b981}{-(i_h - i_f)} \]

Set \(R = 0\) and solve: the break-even spot move is minus the differential. A fall in the high-yielder bigger than the gap, in one year, loses money before costs.

\[ \text{Carry Return} = \text{Interest Rate Differential} - \text{Hedging Cost} \]
Interest Rate Differentialthe yield gap you collect by holding the high-yielder
Hedging Costwhat you give up, or lose to spot moves, protecting the position

In practice the return has three components: the interest differential, the spot return, and the roll yield (forward points). In calm markets the differential dominates and the yield is steady; in volatile markets spot dominates and can overwhelm years of carry in days.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

You borrow a friend's sweets that cost almost nothing to borrow, and swap them for a different kind that pays you a few extra sweets every month. Most months that is free sweets. But if the price of the new kind suddenly drops, you owe your friend the original sweets back and you no longer have enough. Borrowing five times as many sweets makes the free ones five times bigger, and the sudden drop five times worse.

Try it yourself

The course's JPY/MXN trade, worked through

Predict first. You borrow yen at 0.1% and hold pesos at 10%, unlevered. How big a peso fall over the year just cancels the carry? Lock in a guess before reading on.

9.9%. The break-even spot move is minus the differential: \(10\% - 0.1\% = 9.9\%\). A 10% peso fall therefore leaves you at \(9.9 - 10 = -0.1\%\), a year of risk for nothing. The next step is what happens when you lever it.

Differential \(= 10\% - 0.1\% = 9.9\%\). With no leverage and a 10% peso fall:

\[ R = 1 \times (9.9\% - 10\%) = -0.1\% \]
9.9%the year of carry collected
−10%the spot loss, same size as the carry
−0.1%the net: a whole year's yield erased by one move

Now run it at \(L = 5\). Carry becomes \(5 \times 9.9\% = +49.5\%\) of equity, the 10% fall costs \(5 \times 10\% = -50.0\%\), and the net is \(-0.5\%\). In a harsher 15% fall the net is \(5\times(9.9-15) = -25.5\%\). The same trade that looked like a steady 49.5% a year loses a quarter of the equity in one move, and the spot loss alone is half of it.

Why carry works, usually

In theory the high-yielder should depreciate by the rate gap, so that the carry is exactly offset (uncovered interest parity):

\[ \mathbb{E}[s] = -(i_h - i_f) \]
E[s]the spot move you should expect if markets offered no free lunch
−(i_h − i_f)minus the rate gap: the high-yielder falls just enough to cancel the extra interest

In practice it often does not, at least not immediately, for three reasons the course gives: a risk premium (carry compensates real risk), momentum (flows toward yield strengthen the high-yielder further) and slow adjustment. The historical studies show positive long-run returns with significant crisis drawdowns, which is the shape you would expect from a premium for bearing crash risk.

Funding (borrow)JPY, CHF, EUR (historically)Low/negative rates; stable economies

The JPY carry trade: decades of near-zero rates made the yen the world's favourite funding currency, with trillions of carry positions built on borrowed yen. That is why the yen moves violently in risk-off: the same positions are being closed at once.

⚠️
Reflexivity

Yen strength hits stops, which forces more yen buying, which makes the yen stronger still. The funding currency being bought back is the thing the position is short, so the unwind feeds itself. This is why yen moves are explosive in risk-off.

Monitoring carry

  • JPY in risk-off: a sharp JPY rally means carry is unwinding, so be cautious about all risk.
  • COT positioning: extreme short JPY means crowded carry.
  • Volatility: carry works in low vol; rising FX vol is a headwind.
  • Differential narrowing (for example Fed cuts) makes carry less attractive.

It actually happened: 5 August 2024, the yen carry unwind

It actually happened

A weak-yen trade, a Bank of Japan hike and a bad jobs report

By early July 2024 USD/JPY had climbed to about 162, the weakest the yen had been against the dollar since 1986, with Japan's policy rate near zero against a US policy rate of 5.25–5.50%. That is a textbook carry set-up, and it had already drawn Japanese Ministry of Finance intervention in late April and early May and again on 11 and 12 July.

On 31 July the Bank of Japan raised its policy rate to about 0.25% and signalled that more could follow. On Friday 2 August the US payrolls report came in at 114,000 with unemployment at 4.3%, much weaker than expected, and the Sahm-rule recession indicator triggered. The yen strengthened sharply. On Monday 5 August USD/JPY traded below 142, the Nikkei 225 fell 12.4% (its worst day since October 1987), and the VIX spiked above 65 intraday before closing near 38.6. The next day the Nikkei rebounded about 10%, and on 7 August a Bank of Japan deputy governor said the bank would not raise rates while markets were unstable.

How much of the move was carry unwinding, as opposed to other leveraged positions being cut in thin August liquidity, is debated. What is not debated is the shape: USD/JPY falling about 12% from its peak in a month, risk assets selling at the same time, and the whole thing reversing partly within days. Core market figures here are widely reported; check them against your own data vendor before relying on them for anything but the story.

⚠️
Where the textbook version breaks

"Carry compensates a risk premium" is true on average and silent about your path

Long-run carry returns can be positive and still end an account that was levered on the calm years, because the crash does not wait for the average to arrive. The same logic as sizing against a calm-sample volatility applies: a leverage that looks conservative at the quiet-year variance can sit far above the growth-optimal size at the crisis variance. The course's own reading rule is the practical one: treat a sharp yen rally in a risk-off as a mechanical unwind signal, not a rate-based forecast to fight.

Desk data: base rate The carry-unwind shape (yen firming while US yields rise) is too rare to score. There were 35 episodes in 18 years, too few to score; five sessions later USD/JPY was higher 47% of the time, EUR/JPY 62%, AUD/JPY 47%. The shape can be described; nothing can be claimed about the week after.
Desk test: too small to tell The desk's G10 carry basket, and overlays such as a risk-off gate on carry. Out of sample (5.7 years, 7 USD crosses) the carry basket shows a Sharpe of +0.61, about 1.5 standard errors (the standard error of a Sharpe ratio on that sample is 0.41) and sitting in the 2021–26 rate-divergence era; in-sample it was −0.12. None of the overlay variants could be told apart from noise, and the risk-off gate ran with a design flaw, so the idea was not tested as meant. Do not cite the basket's out-of-sample Sharpe as an edge.
Section 06

Cross-currency basis: reading dollar funding stress (advanced)

The basis answers a different question from carry: not "which currency pays more?" but "what does it cost a non-US bank to get dollars?". It is the cleanest gauge of dollar scarcity, and it lives in the swap market, the largest segment of FX.

💡
Plain-English intuition

Two ways to turn euros into dollars-for-a-quarter should cost the same

A European bank can get dollars for three months in two ways. It can borrow them directly, or it can lend euros, swap them into dollars on the spot market, and agree today to swap back at a set forward rate. If the two routes cost different amounts, someone could borrow cheap and lend dear and pocket a free profit, so the forward rate should be set to make them equal. That is covered interest parity (CIP). The basis is how far the real market deviates from it. When everyone suddenly wants dollars, the swap route gets more expensive than CIP says, the basis goes negative, and that negative number is a price of dollar scarcity.

Watch the formula build
Where the forward rate and the basis come from, one term at a time
\[ \text{Route A (EUR):}\quad 1 + \color{#fbbf24}{i_{EUR}\,T} \]

A euro holder who just deposits the euro gets \(1 + i_{EUR}T\) euros back after time \(T\) (a fraction of a year).

\[ \text{Route B (via USD):}\quad \frac{S\,(1 + \color{#fbbf24}{i_{USD}\,T})}{F} \]

Or convert the euro to \(S\) dollars at spot, earn the dollar rate, and convert back at the forward \(F\) agreed today.

\[ F = S\,\frac{1 + i_{USD}T}{1 + i_{EUR}T} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{Forward Premium} \approx \color{#10b981}{\text{Interest Rate Differential}} \]

No free lunch means the two routes pay the same. Solve for \(F\): the forward premium or discount equals the rate differential. This is covered interest parity.

\[ F_x = S\,\frac{1 + i_{USD}T}{1 + (i_{EUR} + \color{#fbbf24}{x})\,T} \]

The basis \(x\) (in basis points per year, negative when dollars are scarce) is the shortfall in what the euro leg earns through the swap route. Route B pays less than route A by \(|x|\) a year, so the forward must be higher than CIP says.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine the whole world needs to borrow umbrellas (dollars) at once and there are not enough. The shop that lends umbrellas starts charging extra just to hand one over, even if you promise to return it. The "basis" is that extra charge. When the extra charge suddenly jumps, it is a sign that a lot of people are rushing for umbrellas because they think it is about to rain.

It actually happened: March 2020, everyone needed dollars

It actually happened

The dollar surged in the middle of a US-centred crisis

As the pandemic shock spread in March 2020, institutions around the world scrambled to raise dollars to meet dollar debts and margin calls. The dollar index rose from the mid-90s in early March to about 103 around 20 March, even though the United States was a pandemic epicentre. By the course's account the EUR/USD basis blew out from about −20 bps to beyond −100 bps in days, the crisis-zone reading in the table above.

The Federal Reserve responded. On 15 March, with the Bank of Canada, Bank of England, Bank of Japan, ECB and Swiss National Bank, it cut the price of its standing dollar swap lines and began weekly 84-day operations. On 19 March it opened temporary swap lines with nine more central banks (Australia, Brazil, Denmark, South Korea, Mexico, Norway, New Zealand, Singapore and Sweden). The basis then normalised quickly. The Fed had in effect backstopped global dollar funding.

On the desk's own futures-implied build the March 2020 lows were −110 bps for EUR, −87 for GBP, −246 for JPY and −132 for CHF. The lesson in the course's words: the basis warned of dollar stress before other indicators, and its normalisation showed that the intervention was working.

Section 07

Central-bank FX intervention

Central banks step in when a currency move is "disorderly" or economically harmful. The most active interveners are the Bank of Japan / Ministry of Finance, the SNB and the PBoC; the Fed rarely intervenes. Intervention can work in the short term, especially against one-sided positioning, but it struggles against fundamentals in the long term.

It actually happened: Japan 2022–2024, buying yen against a wide rate gap

It actually happened

Verbal warnings, rate checks, then actual intervention, and a trend that only turned with rates

On 22 September 2022 the Bank of Japan held its policy rate while other central banks tightened, USD/JPY touched about 145.9, and Japan's Ministry of Finance intervened for the first time since 1998. The rate fell roughly five yen within hours, to about 140. By 21 October it had climbed back to about 152, and the ministry intervened again, bringing the total for September and October 2022 to roughly ¥9.2 trillion (about $60 billion).

The pattern repeated in 2024. After USD/JPY reached about 160 on 29 April, the ministry spent roughly ¥9.8 trillion over the 26 April to 29 May window, with the operations on 29 April and 1 May, and a few weeks later spent about ¥5.5 trillion on 11 and 12 July. Each time the rate dropped several yen quickly; each time the pressure of the rate gap pulled it back toward the highs, and it printed about 162 on 3 July. The lasting reversal came only as US rate expectations fell and the Bank of Japan began to hike (Section 05).

The course's lesson: intervention slows moves and hurts speculators; it buys time but does not change fundamentals. The ministry's own published intervention data are the record of amounts and dates.

🎯
The opposite case: when the authority stops

Swiss National Bank, 15 January 2015

On 6 September 2011 the SNB set a floor of 1.20 on EUR/CHF and defended it with large purchases. On 15 January 2015 it abandoned the floor without warning, and cut its deposit rate further below zero the same day. EUR/CHF fell by about 30% at the low (to roughly 0.85) before settling near 1.04. Intervention that holds a level is a policy, and a policy can end; the move it was suppressing can then arrive in one step.

Trading around intervention

  • Know the "lines in the sand": levels where the authority intervened before.
  • Watch the verbal-escalation ladder.
  • Respect the firepower short term: do not fight it.
  • Do not overestimate it long term.
  • Intervention-driven moves can provide entries if fundamentals still favour the original direction.
Not tested here That earlier intervention levels act as "lines in the sand", and that intervention-driven dips are entries, has not been tested on this desk.
How to test it: take the Ministry of Finance's published intervention dates and amounts, record USD/JPY at each, then compare the reaction when the rate later revisits those levels against matched placebo levels at the same distance, and the 5- and 20-day return after an intervention-day drop against matched drops with no intervention. Fix the horizons and the holdout in advance.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

FX is a smoke alarm for the rest of the market. The yen jumping, the emerging-market currencies dropping, and the dollar-borrowing charge leaping are like the alarm chirping before you can see the fire. It does not tell you how big the fire is, and sometimes the alarm is just burnt toast, but it is worth checking the kitchen.

Section 09

Where the textbook version breaks

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Five ways this framework misleads

Treating regime-varying relationships as constants. The course itself flags that FX relationships are well-established but regime-varying. A haven that stops behaving like one (or a pair whose rate sensitivity flips) is a regime change, not a data error.

Reading "a strong dollar" as one thing. Left-side strength and right-side strength have opposite implications for risk assets. Always ask why.

Using the basis guideposts as fixed thresholds. They are rough EUR/USD levels from a particular regulatory era, the structure has evolved, and quarter-ends widen the basis by design.

Sizing carry on calm-sample volatility. The same arithmetic as the explorer above: a leverage that looks tame at quiet-year variance can be far above the survivable size at crisis variance.

Fighting the firepower, or trusting it forever. Intervention is real short-term firepower and no long-term anchor. The failure modes are on both sides of the line.

Scenario drill · Friday 2 August 2024

The yen is rallying and the jobs report was weak. You are levered carry.

You run a carry basket: short yen as the funding leg, long AUD and MXN against it, at about 5× your equity. USD/JPY was near 162 at the start of July and is now about 147. The Bank of Japan raised rates two days ago. The US payrolls report out this morning was much weaker than expected and unemployment rose to 4.3%. The VIX is around 23. Markets are about to close for the weekend. What do you do with the size?

  1. Hold. The rate gap still favours carry, and a 9% yen move is just a pullback.
    At 5× a further 10% spot fall costs about 50% of equity, which is five times the 9.9% of a year of carry in the lesson's example. The differential is also narrowing (a BoJ hike, US cuts being priced). "Still favours carry" is exactly what is true right up until the unwind.
  2. Add. The yen's rise looks overdone, so buy the dip in carry.
    Averaging into a funding-currency rally while the trigger is live is the unwind's reflexive step in reverse: you would be adding size just as stops start to be hit. The explorer shows the loss scales with leverage.
  3. Cut the leverage to a size where a further 10% adverse gap would be survivable, and accept the cost now.
    This is the arithmetic of the explorer. At 5× a 10% fall is −50% of equity; at 2× it is −20%. Over a weekend you cannot exit at your stop, so the survivable size is the size you hold when the gap arrives. Cutting early does cost the carry you would have earned and, as it turned out, gave up some of the rebound.
  4. Keep the size but set a tighter stop for Monday.
    A stop is not a guarantee in a fast, gapping market: it fills at the next available price. A tighter stop makes sense as one layer, but it does not change the size you are carrying into a weekend with this trigger live.

What happened. On Monday 5 August USD/JPY traded below 142, the Nikkei 225 fell 12.4% and the VIX spiked above 65 intraday before closing near 38.6. The next day the Nikkei rebounded about 10%, and on 7 August a Bank of Japan deputy governor said the bank would not raise rates while markets were unstable. Cutting on Friday would have given up some of that rebound and would have survived Monday; the lesson is about the size you can hold through the gap, not about predicting the low.

Sources: US Bureau of Labor Statistics, The Employment Situation, July 2024 (released 2 August 2024); Bank of Japan, Statement on Monetary Policy, 31 July 2024; Cboe, VIX historical data; Japan Exchange Group, Nikkei 225 daily closes.

Section 10

Key Takeaways

✓FX is the transmission mechanism. Policy divergence flows through currencies to global assets, often before equities price it.
✓Rate differentials dominate the medium term (weeks to months), but risk sentiment overrides in stress.
✓The Dollar Smile: strong at both extremes, weak in synchronised growth. Know the regime, and ask why the dollar is strong.
✓Cross-currency basis equals funding stress. A fast widening of a negative basis marks dollar scarcity; speed matters more than level.
⚠EM FX is the canary: it often leads broader risk moves. Use FX to confirm equity views; a divergence between FX and equities is a warning.
⚠Intervention has limits. It slows a move and hurts speculators, but cannot fight fundamentals forever. The basis guideposts and the "50–80%" differential figure are ranges, not laws.
✗Carry works until it doesn't. Unwinds are violent and reflexive; JPY strength in a risk-off is the unwind signal. Leverage turns a year of yield into one move's loss. The carry basket's out-of-sample record here is too small to tell.
Section 11

Self-test

Q1. The yen rallies hard on a day described as "risk-off", even though Japanese rates are far below US rates. Does the rate-differential view fail? What does the move signal?
No, the differential view is overridden, not wrong. In stress, haven flows and carry unwinds dominate: the yen is the world's favourite funding currency, so fear forces holders to buy it back to close carry trades, and the rate disadvantage is irrelevant for the duration of the panic. A sharp yen rally in a risk-off is therefore a mechanical unwind signal, and a reason for caution on all risk. Once the panic subsides, fundamentals reassert.
Q2. The dollar index is up 5% this month. What is the first question to ask, and why does the answer matter?
Why is it strong? On the left of the smile (US growth ahead, a hawkish Fed, differentials favouring USD, as in 2022) a strong dollar pressures EM and exporters but can sit alongside a strong US economy. On the right (panic and flight to safety, as in March 2020) the dollar is rising because everything else is selling, and it is a negative for risk assets. The same chart means different things for other assets.
Q3. You borrow JPY at 0.1% and hold MXN at 10%, leveraged 5×. The peso falls 10% over the year. What is the spot P&L on equity, the carry, and the net? What fall would take the net to zero?
Carry \(= 5\times 9.9\% = +49.5\%\), spot \(= 5\times(-10\%) = -50.0\%\), net \(= -0.5\%\). The net is zero at a spot move of \(-9.9\%\), the negative of the differential, whatever the leverage (leverage scales both sides equally, so it changes the size of the gain or loss, not the break-even). The sharp edge of leverage is that the loss is five times bigger on the way to a margin call.
Q4. The EUR/USD basis goes from −8 bps to −30 bps in a day. The level is in the "elevated" band. What does the course read into the speed, and what would make you discount it?
A 22 bps move in a day is in the 20–30 bps range the course reads as funding stress emerging, and an EUR/USD level of −30 is "elevated: dollar demand pressure". It is more worrying still if the dollar is rallying at the same time (a funding squeeze rather than rate-driven strength). Discount it if the date is a quarter-end, when the basis widens predictably as banks shrink balance sheets for reporting dates.
Q5. In the intervention model, the ministry pushes the rate down 5 yen and the underlying drift is +0.25 yen per day. How long until the gap is closed, and what if the drift is zero or negative?
Five yen at 0.25 yen a day is 20 days (\(J/d\)). If the drift is zero or negative the gap never closes, because fundamentals now point the same way as the intervention. This is the course's point: intervention buys time and can hurt speculators in the short run, but the trend reverses only when the rate-differential pull does. (The model is illustrative: it ignores any change in positioning that the intervention itself causes.)
Q6. AUD/USD rallies strongly on a day when headlines say markets are in a risk-off panic. What do you do with the narrative?
Question it. AUD is a risk currency and a China-demand proxy, and it normally weakens in a real risk-off. A rally means either the day is not actually risk-off, or something specific (China stimulus, a commodity move) is overriding sentiment. Cross-check with the haven currencies (JPY, CHF), EM FX and the VIX before accepting the story.
