Repositioning Your Approach, or Test It Before You Trade It

Most of a price chart is noise, short-term trading pays a toll on every trade, and the cheapest place to find out an idea is wrong is a spreadsheet, not your account. Course notes: a framework as taught, not a backtested strategy.

The whole idea
Section 1

Why most of what you see is noise

Price is the running outcome of very different participants acting at once.

🏦

Many horizons

Central banks, pension funds, sovereign wealth funds, hedge funds, prop desks and algorithms think in decades, quarters and milliseconds.

📋

Many mandates

Some must hedge, some must track an index, some are paid to speculate. Their trades often say nothing about direction.

🎲

Noise

Price movement that is fundamentally random and carries no predictive signal. It sits on top of whatever real information a price carries.

📶

SNR

Signal power ÷ noise power. Below 1 noise dominates, about 1 the edge is uncertain, above 1 exploitable patterns emerge.

SNR depends on the holding period: short horizons are noise-dominated, longer horizons move through a transition zone into signal-extractable territory.

Spread + slippage

Pay the ask, receive the bid; the price drifts while the order works.

The course's example: a strategy with 5 bps expected alpha per trade turns into negative expected value as soon as round-trip cost passes 5 bps. Common at high frequency.

  • h* = C ÷ k = 1.25 days

Where this needs qualifying

A straight-line edge is a teaching device; real edges decay or saturate. The course states the SNR idea in words; the model only makes the shape visible.

Section 4

Two ways to build a strategy

Systematic (data-driven)
  • Hypothesis from empirical observation
  • In-sample estimation with regularisation
  • Out-of-sample validation on held-out data
  • Walk-forward analysis
  • Monte Carlo for tail risk
  • Paper trading before capital
Intuition-based (no validation)
  • Hypothesis from intuition or narrative
  • No systematic parameter optimisation
  • Live market is the only test
  • Invalidation takes months of live trading
  • Drawdown limits found by experience
  • Capital at risk from day one
⏱

Time to invalidation

~2 weeks vs ~6 months

$0 vs 100%

🔁

Strategies tested a year

50+ vs 2–3

🧾

Cost of a failed strategy

Time only vs time + money

Section 6

Eleven numbers you know on day one

The systematic practitioner has 11 of 11 from the backtest. The intuition-based trader has 0 of 11 and pays "tuition to the market".

The eleven metrics
CAGR: expected annualised return; worth the overhead? Sharpe: return per unit of volatility; below 0.5 generally unacceptable. Sortino: return per unit of downside deviation. Calmar: CAGR ÷ max drawdown, "is the return worth the worst historical pain?"
Maximum drawdown: largest peak-to-trough decline; sets sizing and survivability. Drawdown duration: time to recovery; 20% for 18 months breaks most investors. Max consecutive losses: even profitable strategies have 10+ in a row.
Win rate: a 30% win rate can be very profitable and emotionally brutal. Profit factor: gross profit ÷ gross loss; below 1.0 loses, below 1.5 leaves little room for slippage. Expectancy: average profit per trade after costs. Tail ratio: right tail ÷ left tail at the 95th percentile.
Section 7

The blindfold problem

The same −15% drawdown, two very different decisions.

With backtest data
  • Historical max drawdown −22%
  • Average duration 47 days; this one is 31 days old
  • 8 similar historical drawdowns, 8 of 8 recovered
  • Within expected parameters: continue executing
Without it
  • Historical max ???
  • Expected duration ???
  • Is this normal ???
  • Likely abandons at the worst possible time, locking in the loss
The −15% is 15 ÷ 22 ≈ 68% of the way to the historical worst. With the map it is a data point; without it, a verdict.

Section 8 · It actually happened

Knight Capital, 1 August 2012

It actually happened

Knight Capital Group, a large US equity market maker, rolled out new trading software to eight servers. Seven got the new code; one still carried an old routine that the release re-activated by accident. In about 45 minutes after the open it built large unwanted positions in about 150 stocks and lost more than $400 million. Days later it raised about $400 million of emergency financing and later agreed to merge with Getco. In October 2013 the SEC fined it $12 million under the Market Access Rule. (Source: SEC, Release No. 34-70694, 16 October 2013.)

Where this analogy stops

This was a deployment and risk-control failure, not a strategy whose backtest misled. The parallel is the bill: a defect first found live costs capital, and at Knight's speed it arrived in minutes, not months.

Section 9

Where the course's picture needs qualifying

🔢

The numbers are exemplars

52 → 4, "6 months", "−22%" are teaching figures. Yours will differ.

🎰

Passing is not proof

More hypotheses means more lucky survivors (multiple testing). The funnel's real guarantee is cheap rejection.

⚖️

Break-even is not a target

At h* a trade only pays its costs, with no slippage margin, and the move is still mostly noise.

📜

History is not a promise

"8 of 8 recovered" says what normal looked like, not that the ninth will recover.

Not tested here That longer holding periods are where the net edge is, for the instruments and strategies on the desk. The course states it as a general principle; no test of it has been run in this repository. A fair test: per-signal gross edge at several fixed holds on held-out data, minus measured round-trip cost, with the pass rule fixed first and every signal tried counted.
Section 10

Quick check

Nothing is scored beyond this device. The explanation is the useful part.

Question 1

Why do short holding periods carry a "double penalty"?

Noise dominates short moves, and spread, slippage and impact are paid on every trade regardless of how long it is held. Both problems compound.
Question 2

In the toy model, k = 4 bps/day, C = 5 bps. The break-even holding period is:

h* = C ÷ k = 5 ÷ 4 = 1.25 days. At that point SNR is still tiny: it reaches 1 only at σ² ÷ k² = 225 days (with σ = 60).
Question 3

What does the validation funnel (52 → 34 → 13 → 6 → 4) cost, and what does it NOT prove?

Every gate runs on history or simulation. With many hypotheses some clear a fixed bar by luck, so survivors go live small and the final gate is the market.
Question 4

Why does the same −15% drawdown lead to different decisions with and without a backtest?

A backtest does not prevent drawdowns; it tells you which ones are normal. Without that reference class, the usual reaction is to abandon at the worst moment.
Question 5

A strategy has a genuine Sharpe of 0.8. In about what share of simulated 5-year runs does it dip 15% at some point?

50.4% in the simulation. A drawdown of that size is the ordinary price of a real edge over a modest sample, not evidence the edge is gone.
Your call
Scenario drill · an illustrative drawdown

Month 3, and you are down 15%

You deployed a strategy after testing it. The backtest and simulation said: historical maximum drawdown −22%, average drawdown duration 47 days, and 8 of 8 similar historical drawdowns recovered. Three months in you are −15% from the peak, and the drawdown is 31 days old. You have checked that data, fills and costs match what the backtest assumed. What do you do?

  1. Stop trading it. A 15% loss is too much to sit through.
    The backtest said drawdowns of this size are normal, and this one is well inside the historical worst. Closing the strategy now converts a paper loss into a realised one, and removes you before any recovery. Without the map, this is what −15% usually produces.
  2. Double the size so the recovery is twice as fast.
    Size scales the drawdown too. If the historical worst was −22% at your planned size, then doubling roughly doubles it to about −44%, and you would be taking that risk after a loss, with less capital, on an edge you have no new evidence for.
  3. Keep executing at the planned size. Confirm that the live behaviour (hit rate, average trade, costs) matches the backtest, and write down in advance what drawdown or duration, beyond the historical worst, would trigger a formal review.
    This is the case for the whole lesson. The drawdown sits inside the range the testing predicted, so it carries no new information about the strategy. A review rule set beyond the historical worst keeps you honest: if the strategy breaks the map, that is a signal; if it follows the map, that is just variance.
  4. Halve the size until it recovers.
    Defensible, especially if the plan already contained a de-risking rule written before the trade. Improvised after the fact, it reacts to a number the backtest called normal, and it makes the strategy earn back less while it recovers. Better than quitting, worse than following a rule you set when you were calm.

Illustrative scenario. This is not a documented episode; the figures are the course's own example numbers (−22% historical worst, 47-day average, 8 of 8 recovered). The correct call in a real drawdown also depends on the specifics of the strategy and the account. The general point holds: the person with a pre-trade map can classify the drawdown as expected, and the person without one cannot.

Section 11

What to carry forward

01

At short horizons noise dominates; extending the holding period improves signal extraction (SNR rises with h in the toy model).

02

Costs compound at high frequency; the crossover where expected alpha exceeds friction is the minimum viable holding period, h* = C ÷ k in the model.

03

Systematic validation costs compute, not capital. Most hypotheses fail; the advantage is finding out before deployment.

04

Ad-hoc validation destroys time and capital at once (the double negative); a systematic process decouples them.

05

Eleven backtest statistics turn a drawdown from a verdict into a data point; a real edge dipped 15% in about half of simulated five-year runs.

06

Passing the funnel is not proof of edge, and the course's figures are illustrative. Break-even on cost and a clear signal are different thresholds. The claim that longer holds carry the net edge is untested here.

What this now lets you do

Ask of any idea: how much of this move is noise, what does it cost me to trade it, how will I find out it is wrong without risking capital, and what would a normal bad stretch look like? Those four questions are the order of operations the rest of the course assumes.
