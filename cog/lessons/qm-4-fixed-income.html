Fixed Income Fundamentals for Traders

Why a bond's price and yield are the same fact seen from two ends, how duration turns a yield move into a price move, and what the curve, credit spreads and real yields are saying before stocks, gold and currencies catch up.

COURSE NOTES — framework as taught, not a backtested strategy ~39 min read Level: Intermediate (the duration and convexity maths in Section 03 leans Advanced) Prereqs: Central Bank Policy
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • Price and yield move in opposite directions, always. A bond pays a fixed coupon, so if its price falls, the yield you earn by buying it now rises. This is arithmetic, not opinion.
  • Duration is the bond's sensitivity to yields: price change \(\approx -D \times \Delta y\). A 10-year note with \(D\approx 8\) loses about 4% when yields rise half a point. Convexity is the curvature that makes that estimate slightly too gloomy for rises and too small for falls.
  • The yield curve (yield against maturity) encodes what the market expects for policy, growth and inflation. The course teaches that an inversion (2Y above 10Y) has preceded every US recession in 50+ years, with a variable 6 to 24 month lead.
  • Credit spreads are the fear gauge, real yields (TIPS) are the opportunity cost of holding gold and growth stocks, and 2-year yield differentials are the course's main fundamental driver of currencies.
  • Where the desk has tested these as predictors, they came back null: an inverted curve did not separate equity returns from a control, credit did not lead equities at 1 to 20 sessions, and yield moves did not forecast FX or index direction. The relationships are real in the same window; the "bonds first" lead is not established.
  • Real-world episode: in 2022 the 10-year yield rose from about 1.5% to about 4.2% (the course's rounded numbers). Duration arithmetic on a 10-year note gives roughly a 20% loss, in the same year real yields crushed gold and growth stocks. In March 2023 the same maths broke a bank: Silicon Valley Bank was forced to sell long bonds bought when yields were low.
Section 01

Why this matters

Almost every other market in the course is priced off the bond market. Mortgage rates, equity valuations, gold, and the dollar all lean on yields, and the central banks of Lesson 3 act on the front end of the bond curve first. If you read the bond market, you read the plumbing that the other markets sit on.

The course puts the claim bluntly: bonds are institution-dominated and obsessed with downside (will I be paid back or not?), so they react to macro news before narrative-driven equity traders do. That claim is a framework, not a result. This lesson teaches the bond maths, which is mathematical fact, and then each relationship the course builds on top of it, with the evidence status of every one stated where the claim appears.

How hard is this lesson? The ideas are intermediate, but Section 03 (duration and convexity) is the most mathematical in the whole Quant & Macro series and leans Advanced. You can read the plain-English paragraphs and the sliders and skip the derivations; the rest of the lesson only needs the one-line rule \(\Delta P/P \approx -D\,\Delta y\). The specific figures, as in the rest of the course, are exemplar numbers representing typical ranges, not live data.
💡
Plain-English intuition

Why a bond's price falls when yields rise

You hold a bond that pays $50 a year, which is 5% on $1,000. Then the market changes and brand-new bonds pay $60 a year. Nobody will pay full price for your $50 stream when $60 is on offer, so the price of your bond has to fall until the $50 looks as good as the new $60 does. Its price fell so that its yield could rise to the market's level.

Think of a seesaw. Price is one end, yield the other. The coupon is fixed, so it cannot move; only the price can, and when the market's required return goes up, the price end goes down. Everything else in this lesson is about how far the price end moves (duration), and what the shape of yields across maturities (the curve), the extra yield on risky borrowers (credit spreads) and the inflation-adjusted yield (real yields) are telling you.

Section 02

Bond maths essentials

The fundamental law: price up means yield down, and price down means yield up. Always. A bond is a fixed list of cash payments: a coupon every six months, and the face value back at the end. What you pay for that list is its price; the single discount rate that makes the list worth that price is its yield. Pay more for the same payments and the rate you earn must be lower.

\[ \text{Yield} \approx \frac{\text{Coupon}}{\text{Price}} \]
Yieldthe return you earn buying at today's price, as a percentage
Couponthe fixed annual cash payment, a percentage of face value
Pricewhat the bond costs now; the only moving part, so yield moves inversely to it
≈this is the simplified "current yield"; the exact version (yield to maturity) is below

This simplified yield ignores the gain or loss you will take at maturity. Bought at 95, you pay 95 and get 100 back, so your true return is higher than coupon over price. The full version is yield to maturity (YTM): the one rate that discounts every remaining payment back to today's price. It is "the yield" people quote.

Face value / parRepaid at maturity, typically $1,000. "At par" means priced at 100.
CouponThe annual interest, as a percentage of face value. It is fixed when the bond is issued.
MaturityThe date the bond repays its face value.
YTMYield to maturity: the total return if held to maturity, accounting for price, coupon and time.

Headline translation. "The 10-year yield hits 4.5%" means investors now demand 4.5% to hold 10-year Treasuries. If yesterday it was 4.3%, yields rose and prices fell: people already holding the bond lost money, and new buyers get a better rate.

Watch the formula build
Where a bond's price comes from, one payment at a time
\[ P = \color{#fbbf24}{\frac{k}{1+r} + \frac{k}{(1+r)^2} + \cdots + \frac{k}{(1+r)^N}} \]

Start with the coupons. A bond with an annual coupon rate \(c\) pays \(k = cF/2\) every six months. Each future payment is worth less today, so each is divided by \((1+r)\) once for every period it is away, where \(r = y/2\) is the six-month yield.

\[ P = \sum_{t=1}^{N}\frac{k}{(1+r)^t} + \color{#fbbf24}{\frac{F}{(1+r)^N}} \]

Add the face value \(F\), returned in the last period \(N\) (twice the years to maturity, for semiannual coupons) and discounted the same way.

\[ y \uparrow \;\Rightarrow\; (1+r)^t \uparrow \;\Rightarrow\; \color{#10b981}{P \downarrow} \]

Raise the yield and every denominator grows, so every payment is worth less today. Price and yield are opposites by construction. The calculators below use this exact formula with \(c\) equal to the starting yield (a par bond).

\[ P(y) = \sum_{t=1}^{N}\frac{k}{(1+y/2)^t} + \frac{F}{(1+y/2)^N} \]
P(y)the bond's price as a function of its yield
kthe coupon paid each half-year, \(cF/2\)
y/2the half-year yield, because coupons arrive twice a year
Nthe number of half-year periods left: twice the years to maturity
🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine you own a lemonade stand that always pays you $5 a week. Someone opens a stand next door that pays $6 a week. If you want to sell yours, you have to take less money for it, so the new buyer still gets a good deal. A cheaper price for the same $5 means a better "return per dollar" for the buyer. That return per dollar is the yield, and it goes up exactly when the price goes down.

Section 03

Duration and convexity

Knowing price and yield move oppositely is not enough for trading; you need to know how far. That is what duration measures. In the course's words, duration is roughly the percentage price change per 1% yield move. A longer maturity means a higher duration, which means more rate sensitivity.

Watch the formula build
From the price curve to the duration rule, and its correction
\[ \frac{dP}{dy} \]

Start with the slope of the price-yield curve: how many dollars of price one extra unit of yield removes. It is always negative.

\[ D = \color{#fbbf24}{-\frac{1}{P}}\,\frac{dP}{dy} \]

Divide by the price and flip the sign. Now the slope is a percentage price change per unit of yield, and positive. That is (modified) duration.

\[ \frac{\Delta P}{P} \approx \color{#fbbf24}{-D\,\Delta y} \]

For a small yield move \(\Delta y\), the price change is the slope times the move. This is the course's rule of thumb. It is a straight line drawn tangent to the curve.

\[ C = \frac{1}{P}\frac{d^2P}{dy^2} \]

The curve is not straight. Its bend is measured by the second derivative, again divided by price. That is convexity \(C\).

\[ \frac{\Delta P}{P} \approx -D\,\Delta y + \color{#10b981}{\tfrac{1}{2}\,C\,(\Delta y)^2} \]

Add the bend. Because \((\Delta y)^2\) is positive whichever way yields move, convexity always adds to the price: it softens falls and boosts rises.

\[ \text{Price change} \approx -\text{Duration} \times \text{Yield change} \]

Yield changehow much yields moved, in the same units (0.5% = 0.5 percentage points)

Checked against the exact formula for a 4.5% par bond, the true durations are 1.89 (2Y), 7.98 (10Y) and 16.37 (30Y), and a 0.5-point yield rise cuts the price by 0.94%, 3.90% and 7.73%. The course's rounded durations are fine for the 2Y and 10Y; the 30Y's 18 depends on the coupon (a 3% coupon 30-year bond is nearer 19.7), so read "~18" as a typical value, not a constant.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Duration says how long the bond makes you wait for your money, and the longer you wait, the more a change in the "going rate" hurts. A bond that pays you back tomorrow barely cares about rates. A bond that pays you back in 30 years cares a lot. Convexity is the bonus: the long bond gains more when rates fall than it loses when rates rise by the same amount.

Worked example

A 10-year par bond at 4.5%, with yields up one point

Take a 10-year bond with a 4.5% coupon, priced at par (100) with a 4.5% yield. Its exact duration and convexity, from the formula above, are \(D = 7.98\) and \(C = 76.2\). Yields now rise by one percentage point, \(\Delta y = +0.01\).

\[ \text{Duration term} = -7.98 \times 0.01 = -7.98\% \]
7.98duration of this 10-year bond
0.01the one-point yield rise, as a decimal
−7.98%the straight-line price loss
\[ \text{Convexity term} = \tfrac{1}{2}\times 76.2 \times (0.01)^2 = +0.38\% \]
76.2convexity of this bond
(0.01)²the yield move squared: 0.0001
+0.38%the correction in the holder's favour

Together: \(-7.98\% + 0.38\% = -7.60\%\). Repricing the bond exactly at a 5.5% yield gives 92.39, a change of \(-7.61\%\). The duration-only estimate was off by 0.37 points, and adding convexity brings it to within 0.01. Run the same bond in the other direction (yields down one point) and the exact price gain is \(+8.38\%\), not 7.98%: the gain is larger than the loss, which is what "positive convexity" means.

🎯
Why 2022 was historic

In 2022 the 10-year yield rose about 2.5 points (from roughly 1.5% to roughly 4%, in the course's rounded numbers). With a duration near 8, that is a loss of about 20%: the worst bond market in decades. Round 3 above is that arithmetic. The exact figure is a little smaller (17.8% for a par 4.5% 10-year, because of convexity); a real note also earns its coupon along the way, which narrows the loss further.

Growth stocks have duration too

The course's second duration idea is that equities are bonds with a messier cash-flow list. A growth company's value rests mostly on earnings far in the future, so a higher discount rate hits it harder, the same way it hits a 30-year bond harder than a 2-year. High-duration equities: growth, tech, unprofitable companies. Low-duration: value, and banks, which often benefit from higher rates. This is why the Nasdaq and 30-year Treasuries (for example the TLT ETF) often trade together, and the course puts growth-stock duration at 25 or more.

The simplest equity model shows where such a number can come from. A stream of cash flows growing at a constant rate \(g\), discounted at \(r\), is worth \(P = D_1/(r-g)\).

\[ D_{equity} = -\frac{1}{P}\frac{dP}{dr} = \frac{1}{r-g} \]
D_equitythe stock's sensitivity to the discount rate, same definition as for a bond
rthe discount rate (a risk-free yield plus a risk premium)
gthe growth rate of the cash flows, held fixed here
1/(r−g)the closer \(g\) is to \(r\), the longer the effective wait, so the higher the duration (4% gap means 25)

That is a textbook result for a constant-growth stream, shown here to explain the "25 or more" and not as a valuation tool. It also shows why the mathematics is "mechanical": higher rates mean lower price-to-earnings multiples through the discount rate.

⚠️
Convexity, and the cases where duration is not enough

Positive and negative convexity

The price-yield relationship is curved, not linear. With positive convexity (most bonds), prices rise more when yields fall than they fall when yields rise, which is good for holders. With negative convexity (for example mortgage-backed securities, because homeowners prepay when rates fall), the bond behaves worse than duration predicts in both directions. For most trading purposes duration is enough. Convexity matters for large bond books, mortgage-backed securities, and big yield moves, as the 30-year line in the chart above shows.

Section 04

The yield curve

The curve plots yields (vertical) against maturity (horizontal): 3 months, 2 years, 5, 10, 30. Its shape is the market's collective expectation for future rates, growth and inflation. The course's line is that this is "the bond market talking, and it's usually smarter than the stock market".

Why inversion predicts recession (the mechanism)

1 · Fed tightensPolicy hikes push short rates (the 2Y) up
→
2 · Market looks aheadIt expects the hikes to slow the economy and force cuts later
→
3 · Long rates averageA 10Y yield reflects the expected average of future short rates, so expected cuts hold it down
→
4 · 2Y > 10YThe inverted curve is the market pricing that the Fed will be forced to cut

The course states that an inverted curve has preceded every US recession in the past 50+ years, with a lead time that is variable, historically 6 to 24 months: a warning sign, not a precise timer.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Normally you get paid more to lend money for a long time than for a short time, like a bigger prize for waiting longer. When the curve inverts, lenders accept less for the long wait, because they think times will be bad later and the grown-ups in charge of rates will have to cut them. So a "backwards" curve is the market whispering that it expects trouble.

Key spreads and curve moves

  • 2s10s: the most popular measure; negative means inverted; watched by media and the Fed.
  • 3M–10Y: some research says it has better recession-predictive power; the Fed's preferred measure.
  • 2s30s: the very long end; more volatile, and it can diverge from 2s10s.
MoveWhat's happeningSignal (as taught)
Bull steepenerShort rates fall faster than longFed cuts being priced; early recession or crisis response
Bear steepenerLong rates rise faster than shortGrowth or inflation expectations rising; recovery or inflation fear
Bull flattenerLong rates fall faster than shortFlight to quality; growth fears; long bonds as haven
Bear flattenerShort rates rise faster than longFed tightening; hikes expected to slow the economy; inversion risk

Curve to equity positioning (as taught)
  • Steepening (bull or bear): generally favours cyclicals, value and banks.
  • Flattening toward inversion: caution; reduce cyclicals, favour defensives.
  • Deeply inverted: recession risk elevated, timing uncertain; reduce risk gradually and don't panic.
  • Rapid un-inversion: often happens right before the recession actually starts, so the steepening after an inversion can be the final warning.

These are the course's positioning rules of thumb. None is tested here; treat them as a vocabulary for thinking about the curve, not as signals.

⚠️
Where the textbook version breaks

"Every recession" is a small sample, and the lead time is wide

Fifty-odd years holds only a handful of US recessions, so "inversion preceded every one" rests on a few observations. Inversion is also not the same as "a recession follows": the claim runs from recession back to inversion, not forward, and false alarms are possible. The 2022 inversion lasted roughly two years, far longer than the course's usual window shows; whether a recession has been dated by the NBER is something to check, not assume. The 6 to 24 month lead is wide enough that "inverted today" is a poor entry timer.

Desk test: nothing found curve-inversion: NULL for the tradeable half. 12,581 daily 10y-minus-2y observations (1976 to 2026) collapse to ten inversion episodes. A recession within 24 months followed 6 of 10 (60%, 95% interval 31% to 83%), an interval that contains a coin flip; within 12 months only 3 of 10. Forward Nasdaq returns against a month-matched control did not separate at any horizon, and steepening episodes did worse than inversions, so curve shape is not what divides the periods. The recession association can be quoted descriptively, always with the denominator.
Section 05

Credit spreads and risk appetite

A credit spread is the extra yield investors demand to hold corporate debt instead of government debt. Treasury at 4%, corporate at 6%: the spread is 200 basis points, the compensation for default risk. Spreads widening means fear is rising; tightening means confidence is rising.

\[ \text{Credit spread} = y_{\text{corporate}} - y_{\text{Treasury}} \]
Credit spreadthe extra yield for default risk, in basis points (1 bp = 0.01%)
y_corpthe yield on a corporate bond of similar maturity
y_Treasurythe "risk-free" yield on a Treasury of the same maturity
IGInvestment grade: BBB− and above.
HYHigh yield ("junk"): BB+ and below.
OASOption-adjusted spread: the spread after stripping out the value of embedded options, the standard way to quote an index.
CDXA tradeable credit-default index (see the reference block below).

Why credit is supposed to lead equities (the course's reasoning). Bond investors are more institutional, more downside-focused and less swayed by narrative. Bonds have a defined downside (paid back or not), which breeds natural scepticism, and credit liquidity can seize faster, giving early signals. The classic pattern as taught: spreads start widening, equities ignore it for days or weeks, then equities sell off. Watching credit is advance warning.

🎯
The course's 2007 example

High-yield spreads began widening significantly in summer 2007; equities made new highs in October 2007. Credit warned for months before the 2008 collapse.

Section 06

TIPS and real yields

A nominal yield bundles two things: what you earn after inflation, and what you are compensated for expecting inflation to be. Pull them apart and you get the real yield, the true after-inflation return. Nominal 4.5% with 2.5% expected inflation means a real yield of roughly 2%.

\[ \text{Real yield} \approx \text{Nominal yield} - \text{Expected inflation} \]
Real yieldthe return after inflation: what a bond truly pays
Nominal yieldthe quoted Treasury yield, no inflation adjustment
Expected inflationthe market's expected average inflation over the bond's life

TIPS (Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities) have a principal that adjusts with inflation, so the TIPS yield is approximately the real yield directly. That gives a direct readout of the market's inflation expectation:

\[ \text{Breakeven inflation} = \text{Nominal yield} - \text{TIPS yield} \]
Breakeventhe market's implied average inflation over that horizon
Nominal yieldthe ordinary Treasury yield for the maturity
TIPS yieldthe real yield on the inflation-protected bond of the same maturity

Course example: the 10-year at 4.5% and the 10-year TIPS at 2.0% give a 10-year breakeven of 2.5%. The name comes from what it tells you: hold the TIPS rather than the nominal bond, and you come out ahead only if inflation averages more than that breakeven.

Why real yields matter. They are the opportunity cost of holding assets that pay nothing. High real yields mean bonds pay a real return, so why hold gold (no yield), speculative growth, or crypto? Low or negative real yields mean bonds don't beat inflation, which makes the non-yielders attractive. The course says gold performs well when real yields are falling or negative and struggles when they rise, "documented over decades".

Section 07

Yields as the FX driver

Core mechanism. Money flows to the highest return. If US rates are 5% and European rates are 3%, capital tends to flow to US dollar assets and EUR/USD falls. The course calls this the fundamental FX driver over weeks to months, more important than GDP, trade balances or politics in most regimes.

Why the 2-year tenor. It reflects the central bank's policy trajectory over the relevant horizon; it is less noisy day to day than the 3-month; and it is more policy-driven than the 10-year, which embeds term premium and inflation uncertainty. FX desks at major banks watch 2-year differentials closely. The rule of thumb: track the 2-year differential between two countries for fundamental FX pressure, and watch the spread, not absolute levels.

Using rates for FX views (as taught). Build a rate-differential watchlist (US 2Y against Germany, Japan, UK and Australia at minimum). Watch for divergence: differentials move but FX does not, which means FX may catch up or something else is driving. Rate expectations shift around FOMC, ECB and BoJ meetings. Combine differentials (the fundamental bias) with technicals (entry timing).

Section 08

Bonds leading equities

Why bonds often lead (the course's argument). The bond market is dominated by institutions (central banks, pensions, insurers, banks) that move on macro faster. They are obsessed with downside (paid back or not), so they are naturally sceptical and quicker to react. And Treasury markets are among the most liquid in the world, so information is priced quickly.

Yields and equity valuations. Higher rates mean lower price-to-earnings multiples; that is mathematical, via the discount rate (see the \(1/(r-g)\) result in Section 03). The rate of change matters most: a rapid move from 3% to 5% is more disruptive than sitting at 5% for a year. Don't use simplistic rules like "10-year above X% means sell stocks"; the impact depends on why rates are moving (growth optimism versus inflation fear) and on what is priced in.

The bond-first framework and the 5-minute scan

Before any equity position, ask five questions
  1. What are yields doing? Rising is a headwind for growth and long-duration assets.
  2. What is the curve saying? Flattening or inverting means caution.
  3. What are credit spreads doing? Widening is a risk-off warning.
  4. What is MOVE doing? Elevated means expect equity volatility.
  5. Any divergence? Bonds warning while stocks rally means be sceptical.

Section 09

It actually happened: 2022, then March 2023

It actually happened

The rate shock that hit bonds, gold, growth stocks, and then a bank

The course reads 2022 as every fixed-income signal arriving at once. In its rounded numbers: the 10-year yield surged from about 1.5% to about 4.2%; real yields flipped from deeply negative to positive, crushing gold and growth stocks; 2s10s inverted; high-yield spreads roughly doubled; MOVE hit multi-year highs; the dollar surged on large differentials against Europe and Japan; and equities followed, with the S&P about −25% and the Nasdaq about −35%, the duration assets hit hardest. Its lesson: every signal was visible in fixed income first; watched from the bond market, none of the equity damage was surprising.

The bond maths did a second, less expected job. Silicon Valley Bank had put deposits into long-dated Treasury and agency securities, mostly bought in 2020 and 2021 when yields were very low. After yields rose, those holdings were worth far less than their cost, and as long as the bank could hold them to maturity the loss stayed on paper. On 8 March 2023 the bank announced it had sold about $21 billion of securities at a loss of about $1.8 billion and would raise $2.25 billion of new equity. Depositors tried to withdraw about $42 billion on 9 March, and on 10 March California regulators closed the bank. It is the duration rule at work: a fixed stream bought at low yields, forced to be sold at high ones.

The 2022 figures are the course's rounded numbers; check FRED (DGS10, DFII10, T10Y2Y, BAMLH0A0HYM2) for actuals. The SVB figures are from the Federal Reserve's April 2023 review.

⚠️
Where the textbook version breaks

The relationships are mechanical; the leads are not

Duration, breakevens and spreads are identities: the numbers always add up. The claims that bonds lead everything else, that an inverted curve means equities fall, or that rate differentials forecast currencies are statements about the future, and those are the parts that vary by regime. "Every signal was visible in fixed income first" is a statement about 2022, seen with hindsight; the same signals in other years did not all point the same way.

Common pitfalls
⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where fixed-income reading goes wrong

Mixing up the direction. A rising "bond price" and a rising yield cannot both be true. Check which one a chart or headline is showing before reading it.

Treating duration as exact. It is a straight-line estimate. For large moves, long bonds, or mortgage-backed securities, use the convexity term or reprice.

Reading inversion as a timer. The lead is 6 to 24 months and varies, and the curve can stay inverted for a long time.

Using one rule on "why" a yield is rising. A rise from growth optimism and a rise from inflation fear (or from real yields versus breakevens) have different effects on equities, gold and FX. Decompose first.

Quoting the course's numbers as data. The levels and percentages are exemplar figures. Look up the actual series before relying on them.

Scenario drill · 8 March 2023

A bank sells its "safe" bonds at a loss

Silicon Valley Bank has just announced that it sold about $21 billion of securities at a loss of about $1.8 billion and will raise $2.25 billion of new equity. Its holdings are Treasury and agency securities, bought mostly in 2020 and 2021 when yields were very low; yields have since risen several points. A colleague says: "It is all government paper, so how can it lose money?" What does this lesson tell you?

  1. It cannot be the bonds. Government securities carry no risk, so this must be hidden credit losses in the loan book.
    Government bonds carry almost no default risk, but they carry full price risk. Duration turns a multi-point rise in yields into a large fall in the price of long bonds, whatever the credit quality.
  2. It is duration. Long bonds bought at low yields fall in price by roughly duration times the yield rise; a loss that was only on paper becomes real the moment the bonds must be sold to meet withdrawals.
    This is the lesson's rule applied. On illustrative numbers, a portfolio with a duration of 6 hit by a 3-point rise falls by roughly 18% before convexity. Held to maturity the loss may never be realised; a forced sale realises it.
  3. The loss is only on paper until the bonds are sold, so it does not matter.
    Right about the accounting, wrong about the risk. A paper loss matters when the holder is forced to sell: withdrawals turn an unrealised loss into a realised one, and the market reads the sale as a signal about the rest of the book.
  4. Convexity will protect the bank, since bond prices rise more than they fall.
    Convexity cushions the fall slightly, as the worked example shows (0.38 points on a 7.98-point loss). It does not offset a multi-point rise in yields on a long portfolio.

What happened. After the announcement, customers tried to withdraw about $42 billion on 9 March. On 10 March 2023 the California Department of Financial Protection and Innovation closed the bank and appointed the FDIC as receiver. Rising rates had cut the value of long-dated securities bought when yields were low, and a run on deposits forced the sale that made the loss real.

Sources: Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System, Review of the Federal Reserve's Supervision and Regulation of Silicon Valley Bank (Barr review), 28 April 2023.

Section 10

Key Takeaways

✓Price and yield are opposites. Master this or nothing else makes sense. A fixed coupon plus a falling price is a rising yield, by arithmetic.
✓Duration is rate sensitivity. \(\Delta P/P \approx -D\,\Delta y\); a 10-year note loses about 4% when yields rise half a point. Growth stocks are long-duration assets, which explains much of 2022.
✓Convexity is the correction. The curve bends above the duration line, so duration overstates losses and understates gains, and the error grows with the size of the move and the length of the bond.
✓Real yield = nominal − breakeven. Decompose every nominal move: gold and growth stocks respond to the real yield, so a nominal rise that is all breakeven is a different story.
✓Read direction and speed. For credit spreads, +50 bp in a week matters more than a slow drift; for the curve, the shape and the move (steepener or flattener, bull or bear) carry the message.
⚠The curve is a warning, not a timer. The course says inversion preceded every US recession in 50+ years, with a variable 6 to 24 month lead; that is a handful of episodes, and the claim does not run forward with certainty. The desk's pre-registered test found six recessions in ten inversions and no separation of equity returns from a control.
⚠Rate differentials drive FX in the same window, with exceptions. The 2-year spread is the course's key metric, but haven flows, intervention, balance-of-payments crises and extreme positioning can override it.
✗"Bonds lead, equities follow" is a framework, not a result. The cascade and the 5-minute scan describe the state of the market today; they have not been shown to forecast tomorrow's move. The desk's tests of credit leading equities and of yields predicting FX and index direction both came back null.
Section 11

Self-test

Q1. A bond pays a fixed $50 coupon on $1,000, and its price rises from 1,000 to 1,100. What has happened to its yield, and why?
The yield has fallen. The coupon is fixed, so a higher price means each dollar invested earns less: roughly 50/1,100 = 4.5% instead of 5% on the simple measure (the exact YTM is lower still because you also lose 100 at maturity). Price and yield are the same fact seen from two ends: pay more for the same payments and the return you earn must be lower.
Q2. A 30-year bond has a duration of about 18, and a 2-year bond a duration of about 2. Yields rise by 0.5 points. Roughly what happens to each, and why do they differ?
The 30-year loses about 9% (\(18 \times 0.5\)) and the 2-year about 1% (\(2 \times 0.5\)). Duration is the sensitivity of price to yield, and it grows with maturity: the longer the wait for the payments, the more a change in the discount rate changes what they are worth today. Exact repricing of a 4.5% par bond gives 7.7% and 0.9%, because convexity trims the loss on the long bond.
Q3. Why is the duration estimate too pessimistic when yields rise, and too modest when yields fall?
Duration is the slope of the price-yield curve at today's yield, so it is a straight tangent line. The actual curve is convex, bending above its tangent. A rise in yields moves you down the curve, but the curve falls less steeply than the tangent, so the true loss is smaller than the estimate. A fall in yields moves you up, where the curve climbs more steeply than the tangent, so the true gain is bigger. The convexity term \(\tfrac12 C(\Delta y)^2\) is positive either way, and corrects both.
Q4. The 10-year yield is 4.5% and the 10-year TIPS yield is 2.0%. What is the breakeven, and if the nominal yield rises 0.5 points while the TIPS yield is unchanged, what changed?
Breakeven inflation = 4.5% − 2.0% = 2.5%. If the nominal yield rises to 5.0% with TIPS unchanged at 2.0%, the whole 0.5-point rise is breakeven (it is now 3.0%): the market is pricing higher inflation, not a higher real return. Real yields, which matter most to gold and growth stocks, did not rise.
Q5. Walk through why an inverted curve is read as a recession warning, and name two reasons the signal is not a precise trading tool.
The Fed tightens, pushing short rates up. The market expects the hikes to slow the economy and force cuts later. Long yields reflect the expected average of future short rates, so expected cuts hold them down; the 2-year ends up above the 10-year. The two weaknesses: the lead time is wide and variable (6 to 24 months), so inversion is a poor entry timer; and the claim rests on a small number of episodes and runs from recession back to inversion, so an inversion is not a guaranteed recession. A third: the market may already have priced it.
Q6. HY spreads go from 350 bp to 520 bp over two weeks while equities hold near highs. What does the course's framework say, and what should you remember about its status?
By the course's bands, 520 bp is in the stressed region (500 to 700), and a 170 bp move in two weeks is fast; the divergence alert says to be sceptical of the equity rally and to add equity risk preferably only when credit is stable or tightening. The status: that credit leads equities is a framework, not a tested result; credit and equities move together in the same window, and a lead at 1 to 20 sessions was not found in the desk's test, so use the spread as a gauge of present stress rather than a forecast.
