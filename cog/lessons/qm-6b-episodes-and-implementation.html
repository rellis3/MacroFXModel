Cross-Asset Synthesis, Part 2: Episodes and Implementation

Part 2 of 2. The framework from part 1 tested against three real episodes (the 2022 bear market, the Q4 2023 everything rally and March 2023), how one view is expressed and sized across the capital structure, and the honest verdict on where edge actually lives.

COURSE NOTES — framework as taught, not a backtested strategy ~22 min read Difficulty: Intermediate Prereqs: Cross-Asset Synthesis, Part 1: The Framework and the earlier lessons in this course
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • Three episodes are worked in full: the 2022 bear market (rates led, FX confirmed, credit warned, equities followed), the Q4 2023 "everything rally" (peak coherence), and March 2023 (credit led, rate volatility led equity volatility, and a weekly review would have missed it).
  • A view is not a trade. The same view ("the Fed cuts sooner than expected") can be expressed seven ways, from long 2-year Treasuries to receiver swaptions to gold, with very different carry, convexity and noise. The cleanest expression is the one most directly tied to the thesis, and the position size is target risk divided by volatility times correlation to the view, a heuristic that explodes as the correlation falls and needs a cap.
  • All three episodes are told with hindsight. They are three stories, not a test: none shows how often the same rules would have misfired in other years.
  • The honest part: understanding the framework is not an edge. Markets are efficient at short horizons. The edge the course points to is data infrastructure, systematic rules, longer horizons and the ability to hold through drawdowns, and none of it is proved here.
Part 1 in three lines.
  • The layers of the capital structure (rates, credit, FX, equities, volatility) price one macro story at different speeds. When they agree the market is coherent; when they disagree, investigate. The order of moves is a hypothesis, not a rule.
  • Growth and inflation make four regimes (Goldilocks, Reflation, Stagflation, Deflation); the money is in spotting the transition early, and a macro dashboard is built for weeks to months, with real-time alerts only for stress signals.
  • Correlations are variables: the stock-bond correlation is negative when growth fears drive markets and positive when inflation fears do, and heads towards 1 in a liquidity crisis. Part 1 has the details.

Section 01

Three episodes, three lessons

The course tests the framework against three episodes. Read them as a set: the first is a regime shift that all the signals showed in advance, the second is peak coherence, and the third is a fast crisis where timing and cadence mattered most. Source note: the dates and order of events below are public record. The level figures in the tables are the course's rounded values (labelled exemplar data) and are approximate.

Episode 1: the 2022 cross-asset bear market

It actually happened

The year the stock-bond hedge broke, and every signal was visible first

Setup, late 2021. Inflation expectations were at multi-decade highs (the course gives a 5-year breakeven near 3.2%), 10-year real yields were deeply negative (about −1.0%), Fed language was drifting away from "transitory", and the curve was bear-flattening as the front end priced hikes. The regime was moving from Goldilocks to Reflation, with stagflation risk building.

The cascade. On 30 November 2021 Fed Chair Powell said it was time to retire the word "transitory", and the 2-year yield began to climb (the course: roughly 0.5% towards 1.0%) while equities initially ignored it. By January 2022 the 2-year was through 1%, real yields had risen from about −1.0% towards zero, the Nasdaq entered a correction, and long-duration growth stocks led the decline, as duration arithmetic predicts. Russia invaded Ukraine on 24 February 2022, adding a commodity and inflation shock (oil spiked well above $100). The Fed's first hike, 25 basis points, came on 16 March; the 2s10s curve briefly inverted around the start of April. Then came 50 basis points in May and 75 in June, with the 2-year near 3%, real yields positive for the first time since early 2020, high-yield spreads widening from about 300 to over 500 basis points, and the stock-bond correlation firmly positive. In September and October the 2-year peaked (the course: about 4.5%), the 10-year real yield reached about 1.7%, the dollar index passed 114, the UK gilt market was in turmoil and the MOVE index spiked. The S&P 500 finished about a quarter below its January peak at the October low (roughly −25% on closes, near −27% at the intraday low).

The lesson as taught. Rates led, FX confirmed, credit warned, equities followed. The stock-bond correlation flip was the signal that the diversification assumption had broken. Equity-only watchers were blindsided; full-capital-structure watchers had months of warning.

What worked: short duration; long commodities early (energy through June); long USD; long volatility (VIX and MOVE); value over growth (energy and financials versus tech); cash, once rising short rates made it competitive.
What failed: 60/40 (one of its worst years in decades); risk parity (leveraged bond exposure amplified the losses); growth stocks (crushed by rising real yields); "TINA" (cash became an alternative); EM assets (devastated by dollar strength).

Systematic lessons: regime-conditional allocation outperformed; trend-following (CTAs short bonds and equities) did well; a simple real-yield rule would have flagged the growth-to-value rotation; carry-adjusted positioning justified defensiveness as cash yields rose. The edge was not prediction. It was systematic response to observable signals.

⚠️
Where this case study needs care

Hindsight makes every cascade look obvious

The course claims every signal was visible. That is true of the ones that worked, written after the fact. At the time there were also plenty of signals pointing the other way (a strong labour market read as "soft landing", an inverted curve that "always" precedes recession for 18 months, transitory-inflation forecasts), and the lesson does not say how many false alarms the same rules would have produced in other years. One episode is a story, not a test.

Episode 2: the Q4 2023 "everything rally" (peak coherence)

It actually happened

When rates, credit, FX, equities and volatility all said the same thing

Setup, summer to autumn 2023. Inflation had fallen from about 9% to about 3% (core was stickier), the labour market stayed resilient, and the Fed had stopped raising rates after July with "higher for longer" messaging. The curve was deeply inverted but no recession had arrived. The debate was a soft landing (Goldilocks) against a delayed hard landing (Deflation): coherence was low.

The October trough. The 10-year yield spiked to about 5% on term premium, supply and resilient data. The S&P 500 fell about 10% from its July highs, but credit spreads were only modestly wider and the VIX was around 20: elevated, not panicked. Crucially it was a rates-driven sell-off, not a credit-driven one: high-yield spreads did not blow out, so credit was not confirming a hard landing. That divergence was informative.

The pivot. On 27 October the course reads the core PCE print (0.3% on the month, 3.7% on the year, down from 3.8%) as soft, shifting the inflation narrative towards "mission accomplished". On 1 November the FOMC held and Powell was less hawkish than feared (the course quotes "proceed carefully"); markets read it as peak rates. On 3 November weak payrolls pulled the 10-year from about 4.9% to about 4.5% within days and the front end rallied. In November and December came the everything rally: bonds up, credit tighter, equities surging, the dollar weaker and volatility collapsing. The December FOMC's projections then showed rate cuts in 2024.

The lesson as taught. Coherence is a signal. When every asset class tells the same story, the risk of being wrong is lower. Sequencing was textbook: rates first (FOMC and data), credit confirmed (no stress), FX confirmed (weak dollar), equities followed. Waiting for "certainty" missed the move; tracking coherence gave early confirmation.

⚠️
Where this case study needs care

Coherence after the fact is easy to see

Every row in the confirmation table is the same direction, which is the point, but the table is read from the year-end. At the start of November the question was whether the October credit divergence meant rates were wrong or credit was. The lesson is a statement about how to use coherence (more layers agreeing is more confidence), not proof that coherence predicts. Also note this is the same episode in which waiting would have missed the move, and the lesson does not tell you how many times waiting for coherence cost the same trader a loss.

Episode 3: March 2023 banking stress (SVB)

It actually happened

Five days in which a hiking cycle turned into emergency cuts priced

Setup. The Fed was still hiking (25 to 50 basis points were expected in March), the 2-year was near 5% and the 10-year near 4%. Banks' held-to-maturity bond portfolios carried large unrealised losses from the 2022 rate surge, and regional banks with concentrated deposits and a duration mismatch were vulnerable.

The cascade. On Wednesday 8 March Silvergate announced a liquidation, which was treated as idiosyncratic. After the close that day Silicon Valley Bank disclosed a $1.8 billion loss on securities sales and a capital raise; its stock fell about 60% on Thursday 9 March, regional-bank credit default swaps spiked, and depositors tried to pull about $42 billion in a day. Credit reacted first: this was the early warning. On Friday 10 March the FDIC took SVB into receivership, then the second-largest bank failure in US history. The 2-year yield collapsed, about 30 basis points on 10 March and a further 57 on Monday 13 March on FRED closes, in a flight to safety, and regional-bank stocks cratered. On Sunday 12 March Signature Bank was closed, and the Fed announced the Bank Term Funding Program (BTFP), lending against underwater bonds at par, together with measures protecting depositors: the policy firewall. Stabilisation followed with aftershocks (concern about First Republic; the separate Credit Suisse rescue). The Fed still hiked 25 basis points on 22 March, threading the needle.

The lesson as taught. Credit leads at stress points; the MOVE index can lead the VIX in rates-driven crises; regime shifts can be violent and fast (hiking to emergency cuts priced in about 48 hours); and the policy response matters. For systematic work: crisis signals must be monitored in real time. A weekly review would have missed this.

⚠️
Where this case study needs care

A single fast crisis tests your cadence, not your model

The lesson here is operational: alerts, not weekly reviews. It does not show that "credit leads" or "MOVE leads VIX" is a rule; the course's own lead-lag finding is that leads appear at turning points, which makes them easy to cherry-pick. The 2-year yield also whipsawed afterwards (FRED closes: 4.03% on 13 March, 3.93% on 15 March, 4.17% on 21 March), so a trader who simply extrapolated the collapse would not have been rewarded.

Section 02

Expressing a view across the capital structure

A view is not a trade. Take "the Fed will cut sooner than expected" and look at seven ways to express it:

ExpressionInstrumentRisk profileCarryConvexity
Direct ratesLong 2Y TreasuriesLow volatility, cleanPositive (coupon)Low
Curve2s10s steepenerLower volatility than outrightOften negativeLow
Rate optionsReceiver swaptionsDefined risk, asymmetricNegative (premium)High
FXShort USD / long EURHigher volatility, rate-differential sensitiveVariableLow
EquityLong growth (QQQ)High volatility, many other factorsLow / variableLow
Equity optionsLong QQQ callsDefined risk, levered upsideNegative (premium)High
GoldLong GLDReal-yield proxyZeroLow
  • High conviction + short horizon: direct 2Y or front-end rate options are cleanest.
  • Moderate conviction + longer horizon: a steepener or gold may offer better risk-adjusted returns.
  • Equities are the noisiest expression.

What decides the expression: conviction (high means direct; low means relative value or optionality); time horizon (short means liquid; long can accept an illiquidity premium); risk budget (limited means options for defined risk); carry profile (can you afford negative carry while you wait?); correlation to the existing book; and liquidity and exit needs.

The "cleanest" expression

The cleanest expression is the one most directly tied to the thesis, with the fewest confounders:

ThesisCleanestNoisier
"Inflation surprises higher"Long breakevens (TIPS vs nominals)Short bonds, long commodities, long value
"Credit stress coming"Long CDX HY protectionShort equities, long VIX, long quality
"Dollar will weaken"Short DXY / long EUR/USDLong EM equities, long gold, long commodities

Sizing several expressions

The course's systematic version weights multiple expressions by signal strength, risk contribution (volatility-adjusted sizing), the correlation between expressions and costs, with this sizing heuristic:

\[ \text{Position size} \;=\; \frac{\text{Target risk}}{\text{Instrument volatility} \times \text{Correlation to view}} \]
Target riskhow much risk you want this view to carry, as a share of capital
Instrument volatilityhow much the instrument itself moves; wilder means smaller size
Correlation to viewhow tightly the instrument tracks the thesis; looser tracking means more size to carry the same view
🧮
Worked example

Two instruments, one view

Target risk is 1% of capital. Instrument A has 10% volatility and tracks the view perfectly (correlation 1). Then size \(= 0.01 / (0.10 \times 1) = 10\%\) of capital. Instrument B has the same 10% volatility but tracks the view with correlation 0.5. Then size \(= 0.01 / (0.10 \times 0.5) = 20\%\): twice as much, to carry the same slice of the view.

Section 03

Implementation reality: where edge actually lives

The hard truth (the course's own words): reading cross-asset signals and trading them profitably on a discretionary daily basis is extremely difficult. Markets are highly efficient at short horizons; most retail traders, and many professionals, who try to "trade the macro" lose money. Institutional edge comes from infrastructure, time horizon and systematic implementation.

Why daily discretionary macro is hard

  • Information is priced fast. By the time you see a signal, so has everyone.
  • Noise dominates short horizons. Daily moves are mostly noise; signal emerges over weeks and months.
  • Behavioural biases compound. Overtrading, confirmation bias, recency bias.
  • Transaction costs erode returns with frequency.
  • Narrative is seductive. A post-hoc story for any move is not tradeable.

Where edge can exist

  • Longer time horizons (weeks to months), where behavioural biases and structural flows create opportunity.
  • Systematic implementation: rules remove emotion and enforce discipline.
  • Superior data and infrastructure: the same signals, faster or at higher resolution.
  • Capacity to hold through drawdowns that force others out.
  • Structural advantages: market access, leverage, information.
  • The retail edge: no benchmark, no redemptions, no career risk. You can be patient and hold through volatility that would get a fund manager fired. Use it.

Strategy families the signals map to (all testable)

✓Regime identification models: classify, then allocate by historical regime performance.
✓Cross-asset trend-following.
✓Carry strategies with systematic exits.
✓Relative value and mean reversion in relationships (stock-bond correlation, credit-equity).
✓Risk parity with regime-conditional correlations.

Each can be backtested, validated out-of-sample and stress-tested: evidence beyond narrative. What the course's data-modelling section promises to cover: building data pipelines (FRED, alternative data), signal construction and normalisation, rigorous backtesting (walk-forward, no lookahead, cost modelling), regime detection (HMM, rules, ensembles), portfolio construction (signal combination, correlation-aware sizing, risk budgeting) and monitoring and execution (alerts, rebalancing, slippage).

Scenario drill · 10 March 2023

A bank has just failed, and you are short the front end

The Fed is in the middle of a hiking cycle, and the market expected a 25 to 50 basis point hike on 22 March. You hold a short position in the 2-year Treasury, sized in calm conditions for a market that is still pricing hikes. Silicon Valley Bank was taken over by the FDIC this morning. The 2-year yield has dropped sharply, regional-bank credit has been under stress since Thursday, and rate volatility is jumping. It is Friday close and the weekend is ahead. What do you do?

  1. Hold the short. One bank is not the system, and the Fed's hiking path is still intact.
    Credit moved first and rate volatility is spiking, which is the lesson's signal that the regime is shifting from "hiking" towards "emergency cuts priced". A position sized for calm rates volatility is now much too large for the new volatility, and the weekend is a gap risk in which policy can change the picture.
  2. Add to the short: yields have fallen too far, too fast and will snap back.
    It is a fade of a move in a stress event with a policy response not yet known. If the authorities stabilise things the yields may bounce, but if they do not you are adding to a loser in the fastest market in years. Adding is a view on timing you cannot support.
  3. Cut the position well down before the weekend, treat it as a possible regime break, and rebuild only when the policy response and credit signals confirm.
    This respects both lessons of the episode: credit and rate volatility led, and a regime shift can arrive in 48 hours. Reducing is not a bet on the new direction; it is admitting your sizing assumed a world that may have just ended. You can re-enter once more layers agree.
  4. Flip to a large long in 2-year Treasuries to ride the flight to safety.
    The direction of the next few days was plausible, but it is a full-size bet on one outcome in a gap-prone weekend, and the 2-year whipsawed afterwards. Right direction for the wrong reason can still be wrongly sized.

What happened. On Sunday 12 March regulators closed Signature Bank, and the Federal Reserve announced the Bank Term Funding Program, lending to banks against Treasuries and other eligible securities valued at par; depositors at the failed banks were protected. The 2-year yield fell much further on Monday 13 March and stayed very volatile, and the Fed raised rates by 25 basis points on 22 March.

Sources: Federal Reserve Board press release, 12 March 2023 (Bank Term Funding Program); Board of Governors, Review of the Federal Reserve's Supervision and Regulation of Silicon Valley Bank, 28 April 2023.

Section 04

Key Takeaways

✓Credit leads at turning points, in the course's episodes. Skeptical, downside-focused investors move first.
⚠Infrastructure enables edge, and crises need real-time alerts. Pipelines, alerting and quantitative testing are what turn a framework into something usable; a weekly review would have missed March 2023.
⚠Three episodes are three stories, not a test. Each is told with hindsight; none shows how often the same rules misfire.
✗Daily discretionary macro is extremely hard. Time horizon is the edge: longer horizons, systematic rules, patience. Understanding the framework is not an edge, and none of it is validated here. Nothing in this lesson has been tested on this desk.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. In October 2023 the 10-year yield hit about 5% and the S&P 500 fell about 10%, yet high-yield spreads barely widened. What did that divergence suggest, and what does the later rally show and not show?
It suggested a rates-driven sell-off rather than a credit-driven one: credit was not confirming a hard landing, so the divergence was informative, and credit was right to stay calm. After the soft inflation read, the Fed hold and weak payrolls, every layer (credit, dollar, volatility, equities) agreed, which is what the course calls peak coherence. What it shows is how to use coherence: more layers agreeing is more confidence. What it does not show is that coherence predicts. The confirmation table is read from the year-end, and the lesson does not count how many times waiting for coherence cost the same trader a loss.
Q2. What would a weekly review have missed in March 2023, and what does that imply for how a macro dashboard is run?
The credit warning on 9 March (bank CDS and the SVB stock collapse), the FDIC takeover on 10 March, the historic 2-year yield move, the weekend policy response on 12 March and the near-48-hour swing from hikes priced to cuts priced were all over before a weekly check. The implication is that slow-moving tiers (policy and liquidity) can be reviewed weekly or monthly, but credit stress, rate volatility and curve alerts need to be automated and real-time, with thresholds set in advance.
Q3. With target risk 1% and instrument volatility 10%, what is the position size at a correlation to the view of 1, and of 0.5? What is the trap in the formula?
Size = target risk / (volatility times correlation): \(0.01/(0.10\times 1) = 10\%\) of capital at correlation 1, and \(0.01/(0.10\times 0.5) = 20\%\) at 0.5. Halving the correlation doubles the size, and at a correlation of 0.1 the size would be 100% of capital. The trap is that dividing by the correlation makes the size explode as the instrument tracks the view more loosely. It is the course's heuristic, not a derived result, so any real use needs a hard cap on size and a floor on how loosely an instrument may track the view.
Q4. Why does the course say that understanding this framework is not the same as having an edge?
Markets are efficient at short horizons: information is priced fast, daily moves are mostly noise, behavioural biases compound, costs scale with trading frequency, and any story can be told after the fact. What the course points to as potential edge is different: longer horizons where biases and structural flows matter, systematic rules that remove emotion, better data and infrastructure, and the ability (especially a retail trader's, with no benchmark or redemptions) to hold through drawdowns. Even those need to be tested before they are trusted.
