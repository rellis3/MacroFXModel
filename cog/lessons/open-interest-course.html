Open Interest — Applied Practice

Six lessons of applied study notes on reading real exchange positioning data: what open interest actually is, where to read it for free, how to convert a futures strike into the level your broker's chart really shows, and the mechanical dealer-hedging story that turns a pile of open contracts at one strike into a wall, a magnet, or a squeeze.

COURSE NOTES — applied trading practice, not from-scratch theory ~26 min read Prereqs: Black-Scholes-Merton Option Pricing
TL;DR
  • Open interest (OI) is the count of derivative contracts still open (not volume — contracts traded today). Rising OI means new money is entering; falling OI means positions are being closed — the same price move means opposite things depending on which one moved with it.
  • The CME Options Open Interest Heatmap is free, public, updated once daily, and is the same positioning data institutional desks look at — strike on the left, expiration across the top, calls and puts as separate columns.
  • CME strikes are quoted in futures terms, not spot — a level has to be converted (spot = futures strike − basis, basis = futures − spot) before it means anything on a broker's chart, with a special inversion step for JPY pairs.
  • The open interest matrix turns a heatmap into four concepts: the call wall (resistance), the put wall (support), max pain (the strike price gravitates toward near expiry), and magnetism (how strongly). A 3×-plus outlier versus neighboring strikes is the rule of thumb for a "real" wall.
  • Gamma and dealer hedging is the mechanism, not folklore: dealers who sold options must rehedge as price moves, and that mechanical buying/selling is what creates — and then exhausts — the pressure at a wall. A wall breaking flips that same hedging into a squeeze in the breakout's direction.
  • Five trading frameworks turn all of this into a daily process: wall-to-wall range trading, max-pain reversion near expiry, wall-break momentum, and OI as a confirmation filter on top of a technical setup — all built on a 10–15 minute daily prep ritual.

Lesson 01 — Foundations of Open Interest

Definition

Open interest (OI) is the total number of outstanding derivative contracts (futures or options) currently open — not closed, exercised, or expired. Every contract has two parties, a buyer and a seller. A new contract being created moves OI up by one; an existing contract being closed moves it down by one.

OI vs volume — the critical distinction

Most retail traders miss this distinction entirely: volume measures activity, OI measures commitment.

MetricMeasuresResets daily?Tells you about
Volumecontracts traded todayyes — starts at zeroactivity and liquidity
Open Interestcontracts still heldno — cumulativepositioning and commitment

The four trade scenarios (mechanics)

  1. New buyer + new seller → new contract born → OI +1.
  2. Long closes + short closes → contract ceases to exist → OI −1.
  3. Existing long sells to a new buyer → transfer → OI unchanged (volume up).
  4. Existing short buys from a new seller → transfer → OI unchanged (volume up).

Key inference: rising OI = new money entering the market; falling OI = positions being liquidated. Volume alone can't tell you which — high volume could be either.

Lesson example

Same price move, opposite meanings

Price rises 100 → 105 in both cases below — the OI reading is what tells them apart:

Case A: OI +15,000  → new longs entering, trend likely to continue
Case B: OI −15,000  → shorts covering, rally may be exhausted

Rising price + rising OI = new longs entering (a healthy trend). Rising price + falling OI = short covering (a weak rally).

Market scale

  • Global equities ≈ $100T; global bonds ≈ $130T; global derivatives ≈ $700T+ notional — roughly 7× equities and bonds combined.
  • This is where institutions hedge risk, express macro views, take leveraged exposure, and where market makers dynamically hedge their books.
  • Three drivers of institutional activity: hedging (large put positions show where smart money sees risk), leverage/exposure (futures on margin), and market making (dealer hedging around key strikes creates predictable price behaviour).

Why retail misses this (the blind spot)

  1. Education gap — retail education is chart patterns and indicators.
  2. Complexity barrier — strikes, expirations, and Greeks put people off.
  3. Platform limitations — retail platforms emphasise price; OI needs the exchange.
  4. CFD/spot focus — traders don't realise futures/options data applies to them.

The information asymmetry: institutions analyse positioning while retail draws lines on charts, unaware of the option structures sitting at key levels. The data itself is public and free — the edge is knowing where to look.

What OI reveals (the claimed edge)

  • Support & resistance — large put OI acts as support; large call OI acts as resistance. Levels where real money is positioned, not arbitrary lines.
  • Price magnets — near expiration, price gravitates toward max-pain strikes.
  • Trend confirmation — OI contextualises price moves (see the case A/B example above).
  • Gamma acceleration — breaks through concentrated-OI levels accelerate via dealer hedging.

The destination tool for all of it: the CME Options Open Interest Heatmap — free, public, used by institutions, updated daily. Strikes run down the side, expirations run across the top, "C"/"P" mark calls and puts, and cyan intensity marks OI concentration. Lesson 02 covers reading it.

Lesson 02 — Reading the CME

Why the CME

CME Group (CME + CBOT + NYMEX + COMEX) is the world's largest derivatives exchange. FX, indices, commodities, and rates — institutional positioning happens here, and the heatmap is the same data desks at Goldman, JPM, and hedge funds use. It's free, requires no account, and updates daily.

Anatomy of the heatmap

  • Strike — leftmost column; exercise price in the contract's native format.
  • Expiration — column headers; contract code + DTE.
  • C / P — call OI and put OI sub-columns per expiry.
  • OI value — outstanding contracts at that strike/expiry.
  • Colour intensity — cyan; brighter/darker means more OI, i.e. bigger positions.
  • DTE — days to expiration; near-term expiries have the most immediate impact because gamma intensifies toward expiry (Lesson 05).

Read it as a matrix: strike on the left, scan across expiries. Big numbers plus bright highlighting mean significant institutional positioning at that level.

Strike format warning

Strikes are in futures contract format, not spot — this is the entire reason Lesson 03 exists. FX strikes are the full exchange rate (futures level). Index strikes are futures points, not the cash index. Commodity strikes are the commodity's own unit ($/barrel, $/oz).

Useful features

  • Expiration filter — focus on the front month or a specific date.
  • Call/Put combined toggle — total OI per strike regardless of direction.
  • Settlements link — yesterday's official settlement prices.
  • Volume & OI toggle — OI is usually more informative for levels.

Data timing

The heatmap updates once daily, typically early morning US time, reflecting the previous day's close. Not real-time — but sufficient for identifying key levels and structural positioning.

Lesson 03 — Futures to CFD Conversion

The problem

The CME shows futures prices; brokers show spot. Related, but not the same number. Draw a raw CME strike straight onto a spot chart and the level is in the wrong place — price never quite gets there, or it blows straight through.

The basis

Basis = futures price − spot price. It exists because futures expire while spot is immediate; the difference reflects cost of carry, primarily the interest-rate differential between the two currencies. Three drivers:

  1. Interest rate differential — futures price in the carry (premium or discount).
  2. Time to expiration — basis shrinks toward zero as expiry approaches (futures and spot converge at expiry). Far-dated contracts carry a larger basis.
  3. Supply & demand — positioning can push futures away from fair value temporarily.

The practical upside: there's no need to compute a theoretical fair value — just measure the current futures−spot difference.

The formula, worked

EUR/USD basis conversion

Basis      = Current futures price − Current spot price
Spot level = Futures strike − Basis
6E futures:      1.1520
Broker spot:     1.1500
Basis:           1.1520 − 1.1500 = +0.0020 (+20 pips)
Strike 1.1600 →  1.1600 − 0.0020 = 1.1580   ← draw the line here

JPY special case (inversion)

The CME quotes yen as JPY/USD (yen per dollar) — the inverse of retail USD/JPY. Convert with 1 ÷ rate. 

6J at 0.006700  → USD/JPY equivalent = 1 ÷ 0.006700 = 149.25
Broker USD/JPY  = 149.00 → basis = +0.25
CME strike 0.006667 → inverted = 150.00 → spot level = 150.00 − 0.25 = 149.75

Simplification: invert all strikes of interest first, then subtract the one basis figure from each.

Data sources

  • Futures price: the CME site ("Last" or "Settlement") or TradingView (6E, 6B…).
  • Spot price: your broker's feed — that's the price you actually trade at.
  • Capture both at the same moment — basis fluctuates intraday; prices taken hours apart give a wrong basis. Tip: a TradingView layout with futures and spot side by side.

Other markets

MarketSymbolTypical basisNotes
S&P 500ES5–20 pointsfutures usually at premium to SPX cash
NasdaqNQ10–40 pointssame dynamic; premium varies with rates
GoldGC$2–10slight premium common (contango)
CrudeCL$0.20–2.00contango or backwardation — check the curve

Retail index CFDs usually track the futures, not the cash index, so the basis is often just a few points — verify per broker.

Workflow

  1. Heatmap → pick the strikes with significant OI.
  2. Note current futures price.
  3. Note broker spot at the same time.
  4. Basis = futures − spot.
  5. Each strike − basis = spot level.
  6. Plot.

Discipline points

  • Recalculate at least once per session — the basis changes intraday and especially near expiry; a stale basis puts levels 10–20 pips off.
  • Efficiency: most traders track only 3–5 strikes at a time — the largest call wall, the largest put wall, and max pain. Don't convert everything.

Cheat sheet

  • Standard pairs: Spot level = CME strike − (futures − spot)
  • JPY: invert CME price → basis in USD/JPY terms → invert strike → subtract basis
  • Indices/commodities: same formula; basis often smaller for index CFDs

Lesson 04 — The Open Interest Matrix

The framework — four core concepts

  • Call wall — a strike with massive call OI → resistance; price tends to stall or reverse there.
  • Put wall — a strike with massive put OI → support; price tends to find a floor there.
  • Max pain — the strike where option holders lose the most; price gravitates there near expiration.
  • Magnetism — the pull of price toward high-OI strikes as expiry approaches.

Identifying walls — relative outliers

You're looking for strikes with outsized OI relative to surrounding strikes, not just big absolute numbers. Lesson example: 1.1550 with 9.8K calls vs. 2–3.5K at neighbours = call wall; 1.1300 with 8.5K puts vs. 1.6–2.9K = put wall.

The 3× rule of thumb:

OI vs. surrounding strikesWall strength
1.5×weak — minor level
2×moderate — worth watching
3×+strong — high-probability level

Context matters: a 3× wall in a liquid market beats the same reading in an illiquid one.

Max pain

Definition: the strike at which option holders (calls and puts combined) would lose the most if the underlying expired there — a.k.a. the maximum pain point, or op-ex price. It's computed by finding, per candidate strike, the total value of options expiring worthless (the course's simplified form): 

For each strike:
  Call pain  = Call OI × max(0, Strike − Current price)
  Put pain   = Put OI  × max(0, Current price − Strike)
  Total pain = Call pain + Put pain
Max pain strike = strike with LOWEST total pain
(in practice: use online calculators or estimate visually from the heatmap)

Why price gravitates there: option sellers (market makers, institutions) profit when options expire worthless. The course notes flag a live debate over whether this is active "pinning" or a by-product of gamma hedging — but the empirical tendency is observable either way.

Limitations (as taught):

  • Most relevant within 2–3 days of expiration; earlier, other factors dominate.
  • It shifts as OI changes — a snapshot, not a fixed target.
  • One input among many, not a guaranteed target.

Quick estimation: look for the strike with roughly equal call and put OI on either side — often close to max pain.

The complete structure map

Lesson example: put wall 1.1300 · max pain 1.1450 · call wall 1.1600 · current price 1.1400 → expected range 1.1300–1.1600, gravitational pull toward 1.1450.

ObservationImplication
Price below max painupward bias toward max pain, especially into expiry
Put wall below pricestrong support; shorts may struggle below it
Call wall above pricestrong resistance; longs may struggle above it
Price between wallsrange-bound behaviour likely until catalyst or expiry
Worked example

Systematic heatmap read (front-month EUR/USD)

Steps applied to a sample grid:

  1. Call wall: largest value in the Calls column → 1.1600 (8,450 contracts; 2.7× the next-largest strike).
  2. Put wall: largest value in the Puts column → 1.1350 (7,800 contracts; 2.3× the next-largest strike).
  3. Max pain: strike where call OI ≈ put OI → 1.1500 (2,400 calls vs. 2,200 puts).
  4. Range: 1.1350–1.1600, centred on 1.1500.

The 80/20 rule: the top 1–2 call strikes, plus the top 1–2 put strikes, plus a rough max-pain zone, is 80% of the value from 20% of the effort. Don't analyse every strike.

Put/Call ratio and skew

P/C OI ratio = total put OI ÷ total call OI.

P/C ratioInterpretationTypical behaviour
< 0.7very bullish positioningmay be overextended; watch for reversal
0.7–1.0moderately bullishnormal uptrend conditions
1.0–1.3neutral to cautiousbalanced market or hedging
> 1.3bearish / heavy hedgingdownside protection in demand; fear or support

Extremes can be contrarian: very high put ratios (fear) often mark bottoms; very low ratios (complacency) often mark tops — but in strong trends, "extreme" readings can persist. Context matters.

OI skew = asymmetry of OI across strikes. Put OI concentrated far below price plus call OI near price = negative skew (downside hedging); the reverse = positioning for upside.

Dynamic analysis — tracking OI changes

  • OI increasing at a strike → new positions; the level is becoming more significant; wall strengthening.
  • OI decreasing → positions closing; the level is losing significance; wall weakening.
  • OI shifting strikes → repositioning; a new expected range.
  • Wall break + OI drop → positions closed; the wall may not hold on retest.
Lesson scenario

A wall that defends once, then fails

Put wall OI goes 7,800 → 8,200 (strengthening, support looks more reliable) → price bounces off it → next day OI is 6,100 (−2,100 — positions banked on the bounce) → the next test may break through. Data-lag caveat: OI is from the previous close — you are always reading yesterday's positioning; a big intraday move may already have changed the picture underneath you.

The pre-session OI checklist (Lesson 04 §9)

  1. Pull the heatmap for your product — front month (highest liquidity).
  2. Identify the call wall (resistance).
  3. Identify the put wall (support).
  4. Estimate max pain (balanced call/put OI).
  5. Compute basis & convert strikes.
  6. Plot converted CW / PW / MP on the chart.
  7. Note the P/C ratio and skew.
  8. Compare to yesterday — are the walls strengthening or weakening?

Lesson 05 — Gamma & Dealer Dynamics

The core concept

Market makers who sell options must continuously hedge by trading the underlying. That hedging is mechanical and predictable — it is what creates the support/resistance observed at high-OI strikes.

The Greeks you need

This course treats delta and gamma as operational facts about hedging behaviour rather than derived quantities — for the formal definition of both as partial derivatives of the option-pricing formula (and why gamma for an at-the-money option provably rises as time-to-expiry shrinks), see the Greeks section of Black-Scholes-Merton Option Pricing. The operational version used here:

Delta (Δ)Option value change per $1 move in the underlying (a 0.50-delta call gains $0.50 per $1 up-move). Also ≈ the probability of expiring in the money.
Gamma (Γ)How fast delta changes per $1 move. Highest for at-the-money options near expiration. High gamma means constant rehedging, which means more market impact.

Theta (time decay) and vega (volatility sensitivity) aren't critical for this analysis. Why gamma matters more than delta here: delta says how much to hedge now; gamma says how much the hedge must change as price moves. Strikes with large OI near the current price create the strongest effects.

Delta hedging flows

  • Dealer sells a call → exposed to upside → hedges by buying the underlying. Price rises → call delta rises → dealer buys more → supports the rally.
  • Dealer sells a put → exposed to downside → price falls → put delta rises in magnitude → dealer sells → accelerates the decline.

This looks like dealers amplify everything — true when short gamma. The sign of dealer gamma exposure decides the regime.

Long vs. short gamma

Dealer positionPrice risesPrice fallsMarket effect
Short gamma (sold options)must buymust sellamplifies moves — trends accelerate
Long gamma (bought options)must sellmust buydampens moves — mean reversion

In most conditions dealers are net short gamma (retail plus institutions are net option buyers).

The gamma flip: there is often a price level where aggregate dealer gamma flips sign — above it price tends to accelerate, below it price tends to stabilise (or vice versa). It's estimable from the OI distribution — a key structural level to watch.

Plain-English intuition

Walls don't push back — they run out of fuel

The counterintuitive part of this whole mechanism: dealer hedging accelerates the move toward a wall, then stops right at the wall. A wall isn't a barrier slowing price down on approach — it's the point where the buying (or selling) that was fuelling the move disappears, because the dealer's hedge is finally complete. That's why a sharp move into a wall can suddenly stall with no obvious reversal trigger: the reversal isn't caused by anything pushing back, it's caused by the fuel running out.

Why call walls are resistance (the mechanism)

Trace-through from the lesson (large call OI at 1.1600, dealers short those calls, price 1.1550 and rising):

  1. Approaching 1.1600 → call delta increases → dealers buy to hedge → buying supports the rally.
  2. At/through 1.1600 → calls go ATM/ITM, delta → 1.0 → dealers are fully hedged — no more buying needed.
  3. Buying pressure evaporates → without fuel the rally stalls → resistance.

Key point: the resistance is not dealers actively selling at the level — it's the disappearance of the buying that was supporting the move. The market loses its fuel.

Why put walls are support (mirror image)

Falling toward a big put strike: dealers sell to hedge growing put delta (accelerating the decline) → at the strike, delta → −1.0, hedging complete → selling evaporates → the decline stalls → support.

Gamma vs. time — why effects intensify near expiry

Gamma for at-the-money options rises exponentially as DTE → 0.

  • 2+ weeks out: gamma flat; hedging gradual; wall effects muted.
  • ~1 week: gamma rising; walls exert stronger influence.
  • Final 48 hours: gamma peaks; hedging intense; max-pain magnetism strongest; walls most effective.

Practical takeaway: focus on the nearest expiration with significant liquidity — monthly expiry (typically the third Friday) and weeklies are the key dates.

When walls break — the gamma squeeze

Price breaks a big call wall with momentum:

Calls flip ATM → ITM → delta jumps toward 1.0
Dealers who thought they were hedged now need MORE long exposure
Rapid catch-up buying → price accelerates higher

A gamma squeeze is hedging chasing price through a broken high-OI strike, accelerating the breakout. The same positioning that created resistance becomes fuel once breached. The bigger the wall, the bigger the potential squeeze. (Puts: the same in reverse — forced selling below a broken put wall.)

  • Wall holds → price stalls, hedging complete, momentum dies → look for reversal/consolidation.
  • Wall breaks → squeeze ignites → expect acceleration to the next major level.

The complete mental model

The feedback loop: price move → delta change → hedging → more price movement. OI tells you where the loop engages; gamma tells you how powerful it will be.

Lesson 06 — Trading Frameworks

Five frameworks; use the ones that fit your style, and integrate them gradually.

Framework 5 first — the daily prep ritual (10–15 min, non-negotiable)

  1. Heatmap — front month (or the weekly if within ~5 days of expiry).
  2. Key levels — largest call OI (call wall), largest put OI (put wall), max pain zone; note secondary walls.
  3. Basis — current futures vs. broker spot.
  4. Convert — CW/PW/MP → spot equivalents (spot = strike − basis).
  5. Plot & label — CW / PW / MP lines on the chart.
  6. Context — where is price in the structure? Near a wall? Between walls? Above/below max pain?

Daily record: CW level · PW level · MP level · DTE · P/C ratio · OI changes vs. yesterday.

Framework 1 — Wall-to-Wall Range Trading

  • Concept: between the put wall (support) and the call wall (resistance) the market tends to oscillate; dealer hedging defends both ends. Trade the range until it breaks.
  • Use when: walls are clearly defined (3×+), price is mid-range, there's no major catalyst, and there are 5+ DTE (moderate gamma).
  • Long near the put wall / short near the call wall on momentum exhaustion.
  • Stop: beyond the wall (10–20 pips FX). Targets: the max-pain zone, then the opposite wall.
  • Invalidation: a decisive close beyond a wall → range broken → switch to Framework 3.
Lesson example trade

PW 1.1350 / CW 1.1550 / MP 1.1450 → long 1.1360, stop 1.1330, T1 1.1450 (90 pips), T2 1.1530 (170 pips) — risking 30 pips to make 90–170.

Framework 2 — Max Pain Reversion

  • Concept: price gravitates toward max pain as expiry approaches; strongest in the final hours before settlement.
  • Use when: within 2 days of expiry (ideally the final 24h), price is meaningfully extended from max pain (50+ pips FX, 20+ points indices), and there's no major news catalyst that could override positioning.
  • Long below MP on stabilisation / short above MP on exhaustion. Stop beyond the nearest wall. Target max pain (or a partial there).
  • Magnetism strength: 5+ DTE weak · 2–4 DTE moderate · 1 DTE strong · expiry day maximum. Don't fight a clear trend far from expiry.
  • Timing: same-day or overnight trade only — the effect is time-specific. Wrong → cut quickly; right → price should move relatively fast.

Framework 3 — Wall Break Momentum

  • Concept: a decisive break of a major wall flips dealer hedging from resistance to acceleration — trade the gamma squeeze in the break direction.
  • Use when: a convincing break occurs (not a touch), ideally with a fundamental catalyst; a clean break, not choppy grinding.
  • Confirming a real break: weak = barely crosses, low volume/thin session, immediate reversal attempt → avoid/wait. Strong = 20+ pips beyond the wall, elevated volume/active session, no immediate reversal, catalyst present → high-probability continuation.
  • Stop: back inside the broken wall. Target: next major OI level or a technical target.
  • Size matters: a 10,000-contract wall break beats a 3,000-contract one — prioritise the largest concentrations for the most explosive moves.

Framework 4 — OI Confirmation (filter, not signal)

Use OI to validate or invalidate technical setups:

Technical setupOI alignmentAction
Long at supportput wall nearby✓ take it — high conviction
Long at supportno significant put OIcaution — reduced conviction
Long breakoutcall wall directly above✗ avoid — resistance ahead
Long breakoutno OI until much higher✓ take it — clear air
Short at resistancecall wall nearby✓ take it — high conviction
Trend continuationtoward max pain✓ aligned with magnetism
Trend continuationaway from max paincaution — fighting the magnet

Principle: OI doesn't replace technical analysis — it enhances it. Aligned → conviction up; conflicting → skip or reduce size.

Position management with OI

  • Targets: walls and max pain as natural targets. Three-tier scale-out: ⅓ at max pain, ⅓ halfway to the opposite wall, final ⅓ at the wall or trail.
  • Stops: beyond walls = structural protection; a stop in open space is just a line.
  • Sizing: OI aligned with the setup → larger; conflicting → smaller; none → minimum or skip.
  • Adjustment triggers: a significant OI change mid-trade (a new wall appears, an old wall shrinks) → reassess — the structure you traded may be gone.
Common pitfalls

The six pitfalls

  1. Treating OI as absolute — probabilities, not guarantees; always use stops.
  2. Ignoring time to expiry — a wall at 2 DTE is not the same as one at 20 DTE; adjust expectations.
  3. Stale data — recalc the basis; big overnight moves change positioning.
  4. Over-complicating — 2–3 key levels, not every strike across every expiry.
  5. Fighting fundamentals — walls don't stop a central bank announcement.
  6. Confirmation bias — read the full picture, not just the levels that agree with your view.

Self-test

Q1. Define open interest and state the three ways a contract stops counting.
Open interest is the total number of outstanding derivative contracts (futures or options) currently open. A contract stops counting toward OI when it is closed (the long and short both exit, so the contract ceases to exist), exercised, or expires — in every case the contract no longer exists as an open position, so it's removed from the count rather than merely changing hands.
Q2. Two parties trade one contract: list the three possible OI outcomes and what each says about positioning.
(1) A new buyer trades with a new seller → OI +1 — brand-new commitment, new money entering. (2) An existing long closes against an existing short closing → OI −1 — a position is being fully unwound, liquidation. (3) An existing position holder trades with a new counterparty (existing long sells to a new buyer, or existing short buys from a new seller) → OI unchanged, only volume rises — this is a transfer of an existing position, not new commitment.
Q3. Price rises with falling OI — what's happening and what does it imply for the rally?
Falling OI while price rises means existing short positions are closing (short covering) rather than new longs entering — the rally is being driven by shorts buying back to exit, not by fresh bullish commitment. That implies the rally may be exhausted rather than healthy: once the covering is done, the buying pressure that produced the move disappears, since no new money backed it.
Q4. Decode 6BZ5 and WE3U5.
6BZ5 = 6B (GBP/USD futures) + Z (December) + 5 (2025) — a December 2025 GBP/USD contract. WE3U5 = a weekly contract, Week 3 of September (U) 2025.
Q5. Futures 1.0842, spot 1.0825 — convert strike 1.0900. Then: 6J 0.006250, broker USD/JPY 159.60 — convert strike 0.006211.
EUR/USD-style: basis = 1.0842 − 1.0825 = +0.0017. Spot level = 1.0900 − 0.0017 = 1.0883.

JPY case: invert the 6J price, 1 ÷ 0.006250 = 160.00 (USD/JPY equivalent). Basis = 160.00 − 159.60 = +0.40. Invert the strike, 1 ÷ 0.006211 ≈ 160.99. Spot level = 160.99 − 0.40 ≈ 160.59.
Q6. Why can't the basis be set once and forgotten? Give two reasons.
(1) The basis fluctuates intraday — it reflects the interest-rate differential and time-to-expiry, both of which shift, plus temporary supply/demand pressure on the futures contract, so a value captured hours earlier can already be stale. (2) The basis shrinks toward zero as expiry approaches (futures and spot converge), so a basis measured weeks out is systematically too large once expiry is close — using a stale basis puts converted levels 10–20 pips off exactly when precision matters most (near expiry, when gamma effects are strongest).
Q7. Define max pain, give the simplified calculation, and state when it is and isn't predictive.
Max pain is the strike at which option holders (calls and puts combined) would lose the most if the underlying expired there — the strike where option sellers keep the most premium. Simplified calculation: for each candidate strike, call pain = call OI × max(0, strike − current price), put pain = put OI × max(0, current price − strike), total pain = call pain + put pain; max pain is the strike with the lowest total pain. It is most relevant within 2–3 days of expiration, when magnetism is strongest; earlier than that other factors dominate, it shifts as OI changes (it's a snapshot, not a fixed target), and it's one input among many, never a guaranteed target.
Q8. State the 3× rule and why relative OI beats absolute OI.
The 3× rule: a strike with roughly 1.5× the OI of its neighbours is a weak/minor wall, 2× is moderate and worth watching, and 3×+ is a strong, high-probability level. Relative OI beats absolute OI because what matters is whether a strike is an outlier against its own surrounding strikes, not whether its raw number looks big in isolation — a large absolute OI figure in a naturally high-volume product may be unremarkable next to its neighbours, while a smaller absolute number that dwarfs everything nearby is the real concentration of positioning.
Q9. Explain why a call wall is resistance without saying "dealers sell there" (the fuel-exhaustion mechanism).
As price approaches a big call strike, the calls dealers sold gain delta, so dealers buy the underlying to stay hedged — that buying is what supports the rally on the way up. Once price reaches or passes the strike, those calls go in the money and their delta saturates near 1.0, meaning dealers are now fully hedged and have no further need to buy. The buying pressure that was fuelling the rally simply stops — not because anyone starts selling, but because the source of buying runs out. Without that fuel, the rally stalls, and that stall is what shows up on a chart as resistance.
Q10. Short-gamma vs. long-gamma dealers: what does each regime do to moves, and which is typical? What is the gamma flip?
A short-gamma dealer (one who sold options) must buy as price rises and sell as price falls to stay hedged — this amplifies moves, accelerating trends. A long-gamma dealer (one who bought options) must sell as price rises and buy as price falls — this dampens moves, producing mean reversion. Dealers are typically net short gamma, since retail and institutions are net option buyers in aggregate. The gamma flip is the price level where aggregate dealer gamma exposure changes sign — trading above it behaves like a short-gamma (accelerating) regime, below it like a long-gamma (dampening) one, or vice versa; it's estimable from the OI distribution and is itself a structural level worth watching.
Q11. Walk through the gamma squeeze after a put-wall break.
As price falls toward a big put strike, dealers who sold those puts sell the underlying to hedge growing (more negative) delta — accelerating the decline. If price decisively breaks through the put wall rather than stalling there, those puts move further in the money and delta pushes toward −1.0 faster than dealers' existing hedge accounts for, forcing rapid additional selling to catch up. That catch-up selling is the squeeze: the same positioning that was providing "support" on the way down becomes the fuel accelerating the decline once the wall is broken, mirroring the call-wall breakout case exactly but in the downward direction.
Q12. Why do OI effects intensify near expiration, and which options carry the most gamma?
Gamma for at-the-money options rises as time to expiration shrinks toward zero — the closer to expiry, the faster delta swings for a given price move, so dealers must rehedge more urgently and more often. That means the mechanical buying/selling that creates wall and max-pain effects gets stronger the closer the market gets to expiration, peaking in the final 48 hours. At-the-money options near expiration carry the most gamma of any option; deep in-the-money or out-of-the-money options, and options far from expiry, carry much less.
Q13. For each of Frameworks 1–4: precondition, stop logic, and invalidation.
Framework 1 (Wall-to-Wall Range): precondition — walls 3×+ defined, price mid-range, no catalyst, 5+ DTE; stop — beyond the wall (10–20 pips FX); invalidation — a decisive close beyond a wall (switch to Framework 3).
Framework 2 (Max Pain Reversion): precondition — ≤2 days to expiry, price extended 50+ pips FX / 20+ points indices from max pain, no major catalyst; stop — beyond the nearest wall; invalidation — a clear trend far from expiry (don't fight it), or the trade not resolving same-day/overnight.
Framework 3 (Wall Break Momentum): precondition — a convincing (not choppy) break, ideally with a fundamental catalyst; stop — back inside the broken wall; invalidation — a weak break (barely crosses, low volume, immediate reversal attempt).
Framework 4 (OI Confirmation): precondition — a technical setup already exists (this framework doesn't generate entries on its own); stop/target follow the base technical setup; invalidation — OI conflicts with the setup (reduce size or skip rather than a hard stop rule).
Q14. What distinguishes a weak wall break from a strong one (four criteria)?
A strong break shows: (1) at least 20+ pips beyond the wall, not a bare touch; (2) elevated volume / an active session, not thin liquidity; (3) no immediate reversal attempt back through the level; and (4) a catalyst present (a fundamental reason for the move). A weak break shows the opposite of each: barely crosses the level, low volume or a thin session, an immediate reversal attempt, and no catalyst — that combination should be avoided or waited out rather than traded as a breakout.
Q15. Yesterday's put wall bounced price; today its OI is down 27%. Expectation on the next test, and why?
A 27% drop in OI at a wall that just defended a bounce indicates the positions responsible for the defence were closed — likely banked for profit after the bounce — rather than reinforced. Per Lesson 04's OI- change framework, that's "OI decreasing → positions closing → the level is losing significance → wall weakening." The expectation on the next test is therefore a higher chance the wall fails to hold this time, since the same structural support that produced the first bounce is materially smaller now.
Q16. Where do stops belong relative to walls, and why is that structurally better than an arbitrary level?
Stops belong just beyond the relevant wall — outside a put wall for a long, outside a call wall for a short (or back inside a broken wall for a momentum trade). That's structurally better than an arbitrary distance because the wall itself is the level where dealer hedging flow changes character: price trading through it means the structure that was expected to hold has actually failed, which is a real invalidation signal, not just "price moved against me by some amount." A stop in open space, with no OI structure behind it, is just a line — it has no reason tied to the market's actual positioning.

Future research ideas & areas of interest

Things to investigate off the back of these lessons — not conclusions, a queue to work through:

  1. Wall behaviour in FX: track converted 6E/6B call and put walls daily — how often does price touch them, and what happens after a touch (stall/reverse vs. break)? How does that compare against round numbers or pivots at similar distances?
  2. Max-pain magnetism: around monthly expiries, measure how close settlement lands to max pain vs. where price was 24–48h earlier. Is the final-48h pull measurable in FX futures?
  3. OI-change dynamics: does the "wall weakened after a defence → next test breaks" pattern (Lesson 04's scenario) show up in the data? Requires a daily OI history — the CME heatmap only shows the current snapshot, so history has to be self-collected. Start capturing early.
  4. Gamma flip estimation: SPX-style GEX (OI × per-strike gamma, with a dealer-positioning assumption) is well known for indices — what would an FX version look like from the heatmap data, and does realised volatility differ either side of the estimated flip level?
  5. Basis stability: how much does the 6E basis actually wobble intraday and into expiry? That sets the error bar on every converted level — are walls lines, or zones?
  6. P/C ratio as a sentiment input: do the lesson's ratio bands (<0.7, >1.3) line up with anything measurable in subsequent price behaviour? Could feed the existing day-type/regime features.
  7. Confluence with existing level sources: does an OI wall that coincides with a level this codebase already computes (pivot, VAH/VAL, round number, prior high/low) behave differently from either alone? A natural fit for the confluence scorer.
  8. CME vs. OTC: FX options trade heavily OTC — how representative is CME listed OI of total FX option positioning? Where do the OTC strike concentrations get published (e.g. DTCC data, bank flow notes), and can they be compared?
  9. Dealer positioning assumption: the mechanism assumes dealers are net short the big OI strikes. What data exists (CFTC COT, CME participant breakdowns) to check who is long/short at the wall strikes?
  10. Weeklies vs. monthlies: the course says monthlies carry the OI — how much structure do FX weeklies add in the final week, and do weekly walls behave like monthly ones?
  11. Reading list: literature on option-expiration pinning (Ni, Pearson & Poteshman 2005), dealer gamma imbalance and returns (Barbon & Buraschi), SqueezeMetrics' GEX white paper, SpotGamma methodology notes — and whatever exists specifically for FX rather than equities.
