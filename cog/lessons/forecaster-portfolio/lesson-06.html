The arithmetic of diversification

What ten legs with near-zero correlation buy, and where the arithmetic stops.

SubjectPortfolio construction evidence
MethodCorrelation, random matrices, tail dependence
Reading timeAbout 45 minutes

A single edge is a source of return; a portfolio of edges is a source of return with less risk, provided the edges are different from one another. The Forecaster Portfolio holds ten legs in two books, and this lesson measures how different they are: the correlation between them, how many independent sources of risk they amount to, and whether their independence survives the worst days.

The evidence is the record's own correlation structure, shown as images and summarised in the text. Legs are numbered, never named. The record's figures are shown before trading costs; a later costing module adds them. The lesson also sets out the theory behind each measurement, from Markowitz's arithmetic to random-matrix theory and tail dependence, so that the Forecaster Portfolio's numbers can be read against what the theory leads one to expect.

§01 · Diversification

Why a portfolio

The arithmetic of combining sources of return, and what the record's legs achieve.

Markowitz's insight of 1952 was that the risk of a portfolio depends not only on the risk of its parts but on how they move together. For N sources of return with equal volatility σ and average pairwise correlation ρ̄, the variance of an equal-weight portfolio is σ² multiplied by 1/N plus (1 − 1/N)ρ̄. The first term falls towards zero as sources are added; the second does not. Average correlation sets a floor beneath which no amount of diversification can push risk, which is why diversification is often described as the only free lunch in finance and why the lunch is smaller than it looks when the sources are related.

Figure 1.1 shows the arithmetic. With independent sources, volatility falls as one over the square root of N: ten sources leave 31.6% of a single source's volatility, forty leave 15.8%. With an average correlation of 0.1 the curve flattens early; at 0.3 it stops at 54.8% however many sources are added, so that forty correlated sources are worth about five independent ones. Everything a portfolio can buy is decided by the correlations, not by the count.

The Forecaster Portfolio was built on that arithmetic. Its ten legs are separate sources of return, grouped into two books: Book A holds Legs 1 to 5 and Book B holds Legs 6 to 10. The legs are numbered rather than named, and nothing about what any leg does is described in this series. What can be measured is how they move together, and the answer is that they barely do: the average pairwise correlation of their daily returns is 0.000.

Figure 1.2 is built from the record's own correlation matrix. It adds the legs one at a time, in order, and shows the volatility of the equal-weight combination as a share of a single leg's. After five legs the combination carries 0.489 of one leg's volatility; after all ten, 0.316. The curve lies on top of the line for independent legs, because with an average correlation of zero the arithmetic of Markowitz delivers the whole of its promise: the portfolio has 31.6% of a leg's volatility, a reduction of 68.4%, with each leg's expected return intact.

Equal weights are the simplest case, and the arithmetic generalises. If legs differ in volatility, weighting each by the inverse of its volatility gives every leg the same risk budget, so that no single leg dominates the portfolio's variance; weighting so that each leg contributes equally to total risk, with correlations taken into account, is the principle of equal risk contribution. Both are ways of making the count of legs mean what it appears to mean. A portfolio of ten legs in which one carries half the risk is, in the terms of this lesson, a portfolio of far fewer than ten.

A single number summarises how much a portfolio has gained from its structure. Choueifaty and Coignard's diversification ratio divides the weighted average of the legs' volatilities by the volatility of the portfolio itself. For independent legs of equal volatility it equals the square root of N; for perfectly correlated legs it equals one. For the record's ten legs, with average correlation 0.000, the ratio is about 1 divided by 0.316, which is 3.16, the largest value ten legs can achieve. §03 asks the related but distinct question of how many independent sources of risk the ten legs really amount to.

The consequence for the risk-adjusted return is direct. If ten legs had equal expected returns and equal volatility and were independent, the portfolio's Sharpe ratio would be √10, about 3.16, times a single leg's. The legs are not equal, and the next chapters examine how far from independent they are, but the direction of the effect is what matters here: the portfolio's Sharpe ratio of 3.51, reported in Lesson 8, is the property of the combination, and no single leg is expected to show it.

This is also why Lesson 2 insisted on breadth. The research programme that produced the Forecaster Portfolio searched many directions and kept the few that produced edges; a portfolio needs not only edges but edges that differ, and the value of each new leg is set by its correlation with the legs already held rather than by its own return alone. A tenth leg correlated 0.5 with the others would add little; a tenth leg uncorrelated with them adds almost a full independent source.

Average correlation sets the floor. At zero, ten legs deliver the whole of what diversification can promise.

§02 · Correlation

The correlation structure

Reading the matrix, and knowing which of its numbers are real.

A correlation matrix for ten legs holds forty-five distinct pairs. Figure 2.1 shows the record's, redrawn: each cell is the correlation of two legs' daily returnsover [sic: no space on the page] the record. Blue cells mark pairs that move together, red cells pairs that move apart, and pale cells pairs with no measurable relation. The matrix is almost entirely pale. The strongest pair, Legs 4 and 5, reaches 0.420; the most negative pair reaches −0.170; everything else lies close to zero.

The two books are visible in the structure. Within Book A the average correlation is 0.049, within Book B 0.008, and across the books −0.023. The books are not merely administrative groupings; they are sources of return that are as unrelated to each other as the legs within them, which is what §04 relies on when it asks whether one book's worst days are the other's.

What does near-zero correlation buy? Figure 2.2 answers in variance. For ten equal-weight legs, portfolio variance as a share of one leg's is 10% plus 90% of the average correlation. At the record's average of 0.000 it is 10%, the independent value; at 0.3 it would be 37%. Each tenth of average correlation costs nine percentage points of variance reduction, and the record gives up almost none of it.

Pearson correlation, the measure in the matrix, captures the average linear relation between two legs. Two legs can be linearly uncorrelated and still related, moving together only on their largest days, or related through a curve rather than a line; rank correlation, which compares the ordering of returns rather than their size, is less sensitive to a few extreme days, and the two measures are compared when a pair is suspected of hiding dependence in its tails. For the record the two tell the same story in the matrix as a whole, and §04 goes to the tails directly.

A matrix of near-zero pairs also answers a question that a single correlation cannot: whether the legs share a common factor. If one hidden driver moved every leg, pairwise correlations would be modestly positive throughout, and the first eigenvalue of the matrix, examined in §03, would stand far above the others. The record's matrix shows neither. Its two negative pairs are as small as its positive ones, and should be read the same way: as values within estimation noise rather than as hedges, since a correlation of −0.170 is no further from zero than the noise allows.

Not every number in a correlation matrix is real. A correlation estimated from T observations carries a standard error of about (1 − r²)/√T, whichfor [sic: no space on the page] the record's 1,181 days is about 0.029 near zero. A measured correlation of 0.05 is therefore indistinguishable from nothing, and a matrix of forty-five pairs estimated from the same data will show several values of that size by chance alone. Only pairs well beyond twice the standard error can be treated as structure; by that test the record has one, Legs 4 and 5 at 0.420, and §03 shows that the same conclusion follows from the mathematics of random matrices.

Correlation is also a limited description. It measures the average linear relation between two legs over the whole record, with every day weighted equally; it says nothing about whether the relation strengthens on the worst days, which is the question of §04, or whether it changes between regimes, the question of §05. The matrix is the starting point of the analysis of diversification, not its conclusion.

Forty-five pairs, one of them clearly real. The rest lie within the noise of estimation, which is exactly what independent sources should look like.

§03 · Random matrices

Random matrices and effective bets

Which structure in the matrix is real, and how many independent sources of risk the legs amount to.

§02 ended with a test of single correlations. A matrix can be tested as a whole, through its eigenvalues. Any correlation matrix can be decomposed into independent directions of variation, each with an eigenvalue measuring how much of the total variance it carries; ten legs give ten eigenvalues summing to ten. If the legs were independent and their correlations known exactly, every eigenvalue would equal one. Estimated from a finite record, they spread out even when nothing is correlated, because sampling noise alone creates apparent structure.

Random-matrix theory says how far they spread. Marchenko and Pastur showed that for N uncorrelated series observed over T periods, the eigenvalues of the estimated correlation matrix fall within an interval set by the ratio N/T, between (1 − √(N/T))² and (1 + √(N/T))². For ten legs over 1,181 days the noise occupies 0.82 to 1.19. An eigenvalue inside it carries no information; an eigenvalue above it marks structure that noise cannot explain.

03 · NOISE — The Marchenko-Pastur ceiling (screenshots 11, 34)

λ± = (1 ± √(N/T))²      (N = 10, T = 1,181: 0.82 to 1.19)

Takeaway: For N uncorrelated series over T periods, the eigenvalues of an estimated correlation matrix lie between (1 − √(N/T))² and (1 + √(N/T))²; anything above is real structure.

Figure 3.1 shows the record's spectrum against the noise. Nine of the ten eigenvalues lie inside it. One stands above, at 1.481, and it belongs to the direction shared by Legs 4 and 5, the pair that §02 identified from its single correlation of 0.420. The smallest eigenvalue is 0.533, below the floor, which is the complementary signature of the same pair: two legs that move together also leave a direction in which they cancel. Everything else in the matrix is consistent with independence. This is a stronger statement than the average correlation of zero, because an average can conceal offsetting structure; the spectrum shows there is one piece of structure and no more.

How many independent sources of risk, then, do ten legs amount to? The question has more than one answer, and they must be distinguished. The participation ratio treats the eigenvalues as a distribution and asks how many equal ones would give the same spread: for the record it is 9.47. The entropy measure asks the same question with a different weighting, counting small directions more generously: 9.72. The variance ratio, the number of independent legs that would give the equal-weight portfolio its measured variance, is 10.02, marginally above ten because the small negative correlations reduce variance slightly below the independent value.

Figure 3.2 shows why the definitions matter, on four stylised matrices. For independent legs all three give ten. For a single common factor at correlation 0.3, the participation ratio collapses towards three while entropy stays near six, because one large direction dominates the squared sum more than the logarithmic one. For two blocks of five legs at 0.5, both fall to about three, the two blocks plus the remainder. The record's structure, one strong pair and nothing else, costs about half a bet by either measure. When a portfolio is said to carry a number of effective bets, the definition must be stated; the Forecaster Portfolio carries about nine and a half by the participation ratio, which is the most conservative of the three, and that is the figure this series uses.

The contrast with a portfolio of equities shows what the spectrum is measuring. For a universe of stocks the first eigenvalue typically stands far above the noise and carries a large share of total variance, the market mode in which all stocks rise and fall together, with a few further eigenvalues marking sectors; such a portfolio of a hundred names may amount to a handful of effective bets. The Forecaster Portfolio's spectrum has no market mode. Its largest eigenvalue is barely above the noise and belongs to a single pair, so its ten legs are closer to ten independent bets than most portfolios of a hundred assets.

Effective bets also connect to the fundamental law of Lesson 3. There, the information ratio grew with the square root of breadth, the number of independent bets a year. A portfolio's breadth is not the number of positions it holds but the number of independent sources of risk among them multiplied by how often each is refreshed, and the spectrum is how the first factor is measured. Ten legs with a participation ratio of 9.47 contribute almost their full count to breadth; ten legs sharing a common factor would contribute far less, whatever their individual quality.

Random-matrix methods are now standard in institutional portfolio construction. Estimated correlation matrices are cleaned by shrinking the eigenvalues inside the noise interval towards their average before the matrix is used for weighting, a practice that Laloux, Cizeau, Bouchaud and Potters introduced and that Ledoit and Wolf developed into general shrinkage estimators; without it, a portfolio optimised on a noisy matrix allocates confidently to structure that does not exist. For the Forecaster Portfolio the cleaned matrix and the raw one tell the same story, which is itself evidence: there is almost nothing to clean.

One eigenvalue above the noise, nine inside it. Ten legs behave as about nine and a half independent sources of risk.

§04 · Tails

Does diversification survive the worst days?

Correlation describes the average day. The question that matters is the worst one.

Every measure so far weights every day equally. An allocator's concern is different: whether the legs that are independent on average remain independent on the days when losses are largest, because that is when diversification is needed and when, in many portfolios, it fails. Correlations that rise in stress are the rule rather than the exception in markets, for reasons §05 develops, and a correlation matrix estimated over all days cannot show whether a portfolio shares that weakness.

The direct test counts coincidences. Take the worst 5% of days for one series, exactly 59 days out of 1,181 with ties broken by date, and the worst 5% for another. If the two were independent, the number of days on both lists would follow a known distribution with an expected value of k²/T, which is 2.95. The record shows four such days between its two books. A count of 4 or more would occur under independence with probability 0.34, about one time in three, so the count gives no evidence of dependence in the tails: the books' worst days coincide about as often as those of two unrelated series would. Figure 4.1 shows the full distribution with the observed count marked.

The count has a formal counterpart. The lower tail-dependence coefficient of two series is the probability that one lies in its worst fraction q of days given that the other does, in the limit of small q. Under independence it equals q; under a normal distribution it tends to zero as q shrinks whatever the correlation, which is why the normal model understates tail risk; under distributions with heavier joint tails, such as the Student-t, it stays positive. The record's count is an estimate of this coefficient at q of 5%: four of 59 days, a rate of 6.8%, against the 5% that independence implies.

Figure 4.3 traces the same estimate across the whole lower tail, from the worst 2% of days to the worst quarter, against the line independence implies and the central 90% of what 2,000 random re-pairings of the two books' days produce. A re-pairing keeps each book's own returns exactly and destroys only their alignment in time, so its spread is the honest measure of what chance alone does with tails of each size. Each tail holds exactly its share of the days, as in the count, so the curve at 5% is the count. Nowhere does the curve rise above what chance produces. The figure finds no tail dependence, and at the widest tails it finds the opposite, books whose bad days tend to avoid each other.

The honest summary is that the record's diversification survives its worst days. Four coincidences against an expected three is a difference chance produces about one time in three, the implied correlation of about 0.08 is close to zero, the quantile curve never rises above what chance produces and at the widest tails falls below it, and on each book's worst days the other book made money. Lesson 15 follows the portfolio's worst stretches across many reorderings of its own days, and Lesson 8 reports what the worst days did to the portfolio as a whole. This chapter establishes what the direct measures can and cannot say: no dependence between the books' worst days is detectable in four and a half years, and none large enough to undo the arithmetic of §01.

Four joint worst days against three expected, about one chance in three: no detectable tail dependence, and each book made money on the other's worst days.

Markets as a whole show the stronger version of this behaviour. Longin and Solnik found that correlations between international equity markets rise in bear markets and not in bull markets, so that diversification across countries weakens precisely in the downturns it was bought for. A portfolio whose legs are built from distinct sources of return, rather than from assets exposed to the same economic cycle, is the structural answer, and the joint-day count is how that answer is checked rather than assumed.

§05 · Regimes

Regimes and correlation

Why correlations rise in stress without anything about the legs changing, and how that is tested.

Correlations in markets are not constants. They rise in stress, and §04 found a trace of the same tendency between the record's two books. The reason is mechanical and worth deriving, because it shows what protects a portfolio and what does not. Suppose two legs share a common factor, each return being its own exposure to the factor plus an independent part. Their correlation is the product of the two exposures and the factor's variance, divided by the product of their total volatilities. If the factor's volatility rises while everything else stays the same, the shared part of each leg grows relative to its independent part, and the correlation rises, with no change in the legs' logic or exposures at all.

06 · COMMON FACTORS — Correlation from a shared factor (screenshots 21, 37)

r_i = β_i f + ε_i   ⇒   ρ_ij = β_i β_j σ_f² / √( (β_i² σ_f² + σ_i²)(β_j² σ_f² + σ_j²) )

Takeaway: Two legs with exposures to a common factor are correlated in proportion to the factor's variance; when the factor turns volatile, correlation rises by itself.

Figure 5.1 shows the arithmetic. Two legs whose shared factor accounts for a tenth of their independent variance on calm days are correlated 0.09. If the factor's volatility doubles, their correlation rises to 0.29; if it triples, as common factors do in a liquidity stress, to 0.47. Nothing about the legs changed; what changed was the environment. The same figure shows two legs with a fifth of that factor exposure, whose correlation stays below 0.2 even at four times the calm volatility. The protection is not in the legs' quality but in how little they share.

This is the mechanism behind the liquidity spirals of Lesson 4 and the regime problem of Lesson 5: in stress, one factor, forced liquidation by leveraged holders, moves many markets at once, and everything exposed to it becomes correlated. Figure 5.2 translates the effect into portfolio risk for ten legs. With the shared factor of Figure 5.1, the equal-weight portfolio's volatility rises from near the independent value to about two thirds of a single leg's when the factor triples; with a fifth of that exposure it barely moves. A portfolio's diversification in stress is decided by its exposure to common factors, which is exactly what the record's spectrum in §03 measured: no market mode, and one shared direction confined to a single pair.

Measuring correlation in stress has a pitfall of its own. If correlations are estimated only on volatile days, they come out higher than the true dependence even when nothing has changed, because the volatile sample over-represents the shared factor; Forbes and Rigobon showed that much of what had been reported as contagion between markets was this bias, and gave the correction, which rescales a correlation measured in a volatile period by the ratio of variances. The joint-worst-day count of §04 does not suffer from it, because it conditions on rank rather than on magnitude, which is one reason this series prefers counts of coincidences to correlations measured in stress.

07 · MEASUREMENT — The Forbes-Rigobon correction (screenshots 23, 24, 38)

ρ* = ρ √(1 + δ) / √(1 + δ ρ²),      δ = σ²_stress / σ²_calm − 1

Takeaway: A correlation measured in a volatile period overstates dependence; dividing out the rise in variance recovers the underlying value.

Institutions also model the change directly. Engle's dynamic conditional correlation model lets each pair's correlation evolve from day to day as a function of recent co-movement, so that a rise in stress shows up as a rising estimate rather than as a bias; regime-switching models go further and let correlations jump between a calm state and a stressed one with estimated probabilities of each. Both are descriptive rather than protective: they measure how much correlation has risen, not how to prevent it, and the protection remains structural, in how little the legs share.

The bluntest institutional test is the correlation stress scenario, in which every pairwise correlation is set to one and the portfolio's risk recomputed. For ten legs of equal volatility the equal-weight portfolio's volatility then rises from 31.6% of a leg's to 100%, a multiple of 3.16, and that multiple is the price of relying on diversification: it measures what the portfolio would lose if every source of return collapsed into one. A portfolio built from distinct sources never pays the full price, but a careful allocator sizes positions knowing what the full price would be.

The tests that institutions apply follow from the mechanism. Correlations are estimated conditionally, on days when a stress indicator is active, and corrected for the variance bias; rolling windows show whether the structure of §02 holds through time; and the resampling of Lesson 15 asks how the portfolio would have fared across many orderings of its own days, including orderings in which its stress days cluster. For the Forecaster Portfolio, the strongest evidence is structural: an average correlation of 0.000, a spectrum without a market mode, and books that offset rather than compound each other's worst days. The record has not yet been through every kind of stress, which Lesson 5 explained a record of its length cannot have been, so the conclusion is bounded by the regimes it has seen.

Correlation rises in stress by arithmetic, not by malice. The defence is to share as little as possible with the factors that stress inflames.

§06 · Inheritance

What the portfolio inherits

How the legs' skewness, kurtosis, Sharpe ratios and drawdowns combine, and what Lessons 8 and 15 inherit from this one.

A portfolio inherits its properties from its legs, but not one for one. Lesson 1 showed how skewness and kurtosis diminish as returns are aggregated over time; the same arithmetic applies across legs. For independent legs of equal weight and volatility, the portfolio's skewness is a single leg's divided by √N and its excess kurtosis a single leg's divided by N. Figure 6.1 shows the decline: ten legs leave 31.6% of a leg's skewness and 10% of its excess kurtosis. A portfolio of independent legs is therefore closer to normal than any of its parts, which is why the distribution examined in Lesson 9 is a property of the combination.

γ_p = γ / √N,      κ_p = κ / N      (independent legs, equal weight and volatility)

Takeaway: Across N independent legs, skewness falls as 1/√N and excess kurtosis as 1/N, as they do across time.

Drawdowns aggregate least kindly. A portfolio's deepest drawdown is not an average of its legs' drawdowns, because the legs' worst periods do not coincide when they are independent and the portfolio's own worst period is a path property of the combination. It is usually far shallower than the legs' own, which is diversification seen through time, but it can only be measured on the portfolio's own path, which is what Lesson 8 does for the record's deepest fall of 17.25% and what Lesson 15 does across simulated paths. The tail dependence of §04 enters here: coincident worst days are the mechanism by which legs' drawdowns can overlap, and the count of six against three is the measure of how often they did.

What this lesson passes forward is a set of measured structural facts about the record: ten legs in two books; an average correlation of 0.000; one shared direction, Legs 4 and 5, and nothing else above the noise; about 9.47 effective bets by the participation ratio; four joint worst days against three expected, no more than chance gives; and books that offset each other's worst days. Lesson 7 turns to how the system reached this structure through five builds, and Lesson 8 to what the combination produced.

Across legs as across time: skewness shrinks by √N, kurtosis by N, and the Sharpe ratio grows by √N when the legs are independent.

The expected depth of a drawdown has a known shape. For a strategy with a stable Sharpe ratio S and volatility σ, the expected deepest drawdown over a long record grows with σ divided by S and with the logarithm of the record's length: higher volatility deepens it, a higher Sharpe ratio shallows it, and a longer record uncovers a deeper one simply by giving bad luck more chances. A portfolio of independent legs improves the first two factors at once, because its volatility falls by √N while its Sharpe ratio rises by √N, so that the ratio σ/S that governs drawdown falls by a factor of N. Ten independent legs can therefore be expected to suffer drawdowns an order of magnitude shallower, relative to their return, than any one of them.

This is diversification seen through time. Across legs, diversification reduces the variance of each day; through time, it reduces the chance that the legs' worst stretches line up. The two are the same arithmetic applied in different directions, and the record's structural facts, near-zero correlation and modest tail dependence, bear on both. Lesson 8 measures what the combination actually produced over its 1,181 days, and Lesson 15 asks how much of that was the particular ordering of its days rather than the structure itself.
