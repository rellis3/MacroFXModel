§01 · The Structure

The dynamics beneath the curve

What an equity curve compresses, and what the same returns reveal when they are decomposed.

An equity curve is the cumulative sum of a strategy's returns, drawn through time. It is the most compact description of a record and, for that reason, the most compressed. Each value on the line aggregates every return before it, so the curve preserves two quantities faithfully: the value the account reached and the average rate at which it got there. Almost everything else about the return process is folded into those two numbers.

What is folded away is precisely what a researcher needs in order to understand the system. The curve does not display the distribution of daily outcomes, the clustering of volatility, the dependence of returns on their own recent past, or how gains were distributed across months and years. Nor does it show the depth and duration of declines from previous highs, which are properties of the path rather than of its endpoints. Two strategies with the same cumulative return and the same average slope can differ in every one of these respects.

The compression is not a defect of the chart; it is what a cumulative sum does. Summation nets out the order of returns within any stretch, so a period of steady gains and a period of sharp alternating gains and losses can produce the same net rise. Yet the order is exactly what determines drawdowns, and the dispersion is what determines risk.

An equity curve preserves the end value and the average slope. The dynamics of the return process lie beneath it.

Recovering those dynamics means reading the same returns through other transformations. The running maximum of the curve, subtracted from the curve itself, yields the drawdown series: the depth of every decline and the time taken to recover from it. Aggregating returns by calendar month and year yields their distribution at the horizons over which allocators evaluate performance. The share of days spent below a previous high measures how gains were arranged in time. Each transformation isolates one dimension of the return process that the cumulative line compresses.

This lesson applies those transformations to the Forecaster Portfolio's record. It begins with a prior choice that changes every number that follows, the convention by which returns are accumulated, and then reads the equity curve for what it does capture, the drawdown for the depth and duration of declines, the time spent below previous highs, and the distribution of returns across months and years.

Figure 1.1 sets out how the record is read in the chapters that follow. From the equity curve, the cumulative return day by day, two things are derived: the running high, the best value reached so far, and the drawdown, the curve less that high, from which the time under water follows. Separately the daily returns are aggregated into months, which give the heatmap and the annual figures. Every statistic in this lesson is one of these views of a single path.

THE RESEARCH VIEW In systematic research an equity curve is where decomposition begins rather than an object of interpretation in itself. Risk, consistency and path dependence are measured on transformations of the return series, each chosen to expose one property of the process that generated it.

A track record is a time series of returns and a set of conventions for reading it, and the conventions matter as much as the series. Institutional reporting fixes most of them. Under the Global Investment Performance Standards described in Lesson 4, returns are time-weighted, so that the record measures the strategy rather than the timing of flows into and out of it; the period is the full history from the first live date, never a chosen window; and every figure is traceable to the data behind it. This record follows those conventions: 4.69 years of daily returns from the first day of the record, with no window chosen and no day omitted.

Two further choices shape every number in the chapters that follow. The first is the unit: the record is kept in percentage returns on the capital at risk each day, so that it describes the edge at the scale the record simulates, and §02 explains why those returns are summed rather than compounded. The second is the resolution at which the record is shown. Lesson 5 explained that resolution is a decision, and that a daily record can be presented more coarsely without losing its statistics; the figures in this lesson are drawn weekly or coarser, while every summary statistic is computed from the full daily record.

Read with those conventions, the structure of the record is simple. The cumulative return rose to +489.07% over 1,181 days. Its straightness can be measured: a straight line fitted to the daily cumulative return explains 0.985 of its variation, and the K-ratio, the slope of that line divided by the uncertainty in the slope and scaled by the length of the record, is 8.18, a measure institutional analysts use to score the consistency of an equity curve. The chapters that follow decompose that straight-looking line into the dynamics it conceals.

§02 · The Presentation

Simple, not compounded

The smaller-looking choice, and why it is the honest one.

There are two ways to add up a series of daily returns, and they can produce figures so far apart that a reader would struggle to believe they describe the same strategy.

The first is the simple return. Each day's gain or loss is measured against a fixed starting account and added to a running total. If a strategy makes one percent on Monday and one percent on Tuesday, its simple return over the two days is two percent. The account size the returns apply to never changes.

The second is the compounded return. Each day's gain is reinvested, so the next day's return applies to a larger account. One percent on Monday grows the account; one percent on Tuesday is then one percent of that larger figure. Over two days the difference is a rounding error. Over years it is not.

01 · THE TWO CONVENTIONS — Simple against compounded (screenshots 07, 33)

C_T^simple = Σ_{t=1}^{T} r_t        C_T^comp = Π_{t=1}^{T} (1 + r_t) − 1

Takeaway: Simple adds each day's return to a fixed base. Compounded multiplies, so every gain enlarges the base the next day works on. The two agree while returns are small and the horizon short, and part company as either grows.

The figure makes the gap concrete. At a steady gain of 0.1% a day, the two conventions are almost indistinguishable after a hundred days, but after a thousand the simple total is 100.0% while the compounded one is 171.7%. Nothing about the underlying days has changed. The only difference is the assumption about what happens to each gain once it is made.

That assumption is the heart of the matter. Compounding is not wrong in general. For a fund whose capital actually grows and is redeployed, compounded returns are the correct record of what investors experienced, and reporting them is standard. The question is whether the assumption holds for the strategy in front of you.

The divergence is not a curiosity. It is the difference between two different claims about the world. A compounded figure asserts that every gain could have been reinvested at an ever larger size, indefinitely. For most strategies that assertion is false, and Lesson 4 showed why.

Every edge has a capacity. Beyond a certain size, a strategy's own trading moves prices against it, and the impact grows faster than the position: under the square-root regularity of market impact, doubling a trade raises its cost per unit by about forty-one percent. An account that compounds without limit is an account that assumes this cost never arrives. It always does.

A compounded figure assumes the account can grow forever. Capacity guarantees that it cannot.

So this series reports simple returns, on a notional account of $200,000. The compounded figure for the same daily returns would be far larger, and it is not shown here at all, because it describes something that could not actually have been earned. A number that cannot be earned has no place in a case study about honest measurement, however impressive it looks.

A CONSEQUENCE WORTH NOTING Because simple returns add, a gain and a loss of the same size cancel exactly. That property will matter in §04, where it makes recovery from a drawdown symmetric: a fall of a given percentage needs exactly that percentage of gain to recover.

The notional $200,000 is a presentation device. Because simple returns scale linearly, the figure can be read in either direction: every 1% of simple return is $2,000 on the notional account, and the right-hand axis of each chart carries the percentage directly, so the result can be read in money or in percent.

There is a second reason a record is stated in percentages rather than in money. Lesson 4 showed that the profit an edge can produce is not proportional to capital: market impact grows with capital to the power 1.5, so net profit rises, peaks and falls. Figure 2.2 restates the curve. A record in percentages, uncompounded, reports the edge itself, the return on each unit of capital at the scale the record simulates; what that edge can carry is a separate question, answered in a later costing module with the record's own costs.

The choice between summing and compounding is not cosmetic. A compounded record reinvests each day's gain, so that capital grows and later returns are earned on a larger base; a summed record holds capital at risk constant. The two diverge by an amount that Lesson 1 derived: along a compounded path, growth is approximately the mean return less half the variance, and at the record's volatility of 29.74% the volatility drag is about 4.4% a year. A compounded presentation of the same daily returns would therefore not only reach a different end value but would do so by a different route, falling more steeply in drawdowns, when capital is largest, and rising more steeply in recoveries.

Institutions report both, for different purposes. The summed record is the statistician's object: its daily returns are the stationary series on which the Sharpe ratio, the significance tests of Lesson 11 and the resampling of Lesson 15 are computed, and its cumulative sum is linear in the edge. The compounded record is the investor's experience of a particular sizing rule, one in which exposure is scaled with capital. The series reports the summed record because its subject is the evidence for the edge; whether to compound, and at what fraction of capital, is a sizing decision governed by the Kelly arithmetic of Lesson 1, and it is left to the reader.

There is a related convention about the base. Returns in this record are expressed on the capital at risk, so that a day's return is a return on the whole account rather than on the margin posted, and leverage is implicit in the volatility rather than hidden in the base. Lesson 5 explained that futures returns are naturally excess returns over the collateral rate; the record is stated before any interest on collateral, which an investor would add, and before trading costs, which a later costing module subtracts.

§03 · The Equity Curve

Where it ended

Reading the most familiar chart for what it can and cannot tell you.

An equity curve has two properties worth reading. Its slope over any stretch is the average return during that stretch: steeper means faster. Its roughness is the volatility, the size of the day-to-day swings around that average. Neither captures the internal structure of the path: the depth and duration of its declines, or how its gains were arranged in time.

Slope and roughness trade off against each other in a way the eye struggles to separate. A steep, jagged curve and a gentler, smoother one can end at the same place with very different experiences along the way. The Sharpe ratio, taken up in Lesson 9, is the number that measures slope against roughness directly. For now it is enough to see that the curve alone cannot tell you which of the two you are holding.

Read as a summary, the curve is plain. From a notional $200,000, the account ends at $1,178,143, a simple return of +489.07% across 1,181 trading days. The rise is steady rather than concentrated in one burst, which is itself informative: a curve that climbs evenly suggests an edge that operated across conditions, where one that leaps in a single stretch suggests a result that rests on a single episode.

One detail of how the curve is drawn matters when reading it: it is built from weekly values.

A professional reading an equity curve looks for three things beyond its ending. Flat stretches, where the strategy stopped earning for a while, and how long they lasted. Acceleration, where the slope steepens, and whether it coincides with a change the reader can identify. And shifts in character, where the roughness changes abruptly.

Figure 3.1 shows the same record at three resolutions: weekly, monthly and quarterly. All three end in the same place and tell the same broad story, but each coarser view loses the falls that happened within its periods. The quarterly line barely shows the deepest drawdown of §04 at all. This is why Lesson 5 insisted that a record's resolution be stated: the smoothness of a curve is partly a property of how often it was drawn.

Reading an equity curve well is a matter of knowing what its scale shows. Because the record is summed, the curve is drawn on a linear axis, and a constant edge appears as a straight line; on a compounded record the same edge would appear as a straight line only on a logarithmic axis. Local slope is the local rate of return, and the eye reads it accurately; local roughness is the local volatility, which the eye reads poorly, because a curve that is drawn weekly, as this one is, smooths away most of what happened inside each week. §04 and §05 measure what the smoothing hides.

Two numbers summarise the curve's shape beyond its end value. Its straightness, the share of variation explained by a straight line, is 0.985; its K-ratio, which rewards both a steep slope and a steady one, is 8.18. Neither is a measure of return. A curve can be steep and ragged or shallow and smooth, and the Sharpe ratio of 3.51 reported here combines the two in a single figure, average return per unit of volatility. The straightness statistics ask a different question, whether the return arrived evenly through time, and the monthly view of §06 answers it in detail.

The curve is also one path among many, which was the subject of Lesson 1. Its end value is an estimate of the process that produced it, with a standard error that Lesson 1 computed; its shape is one draw of the ordering of its days, and Lesson 15 asks what other orderings would have produced. The right way to read the exhibit, then, is as a single realisation with known precision, rather than as a promise about the next 1,181 days.

RESOLUTION SHAPES WHAT A CHART CAN REVEAL A line through weekly values is smoother than the daily path it summarises. Sharp moves inside a single week are averaged into the week's close and disappear from view. This is a deliberate choice here, since daily resolution is never published, but it is a general lesson: the smoothness of any curve is partly a property of its sampling frequency.

One property the curve conceals matters more than the rest. It records the value of the account, but not the depth or duration of its declines from previous highs. An account that falls ten percent and recovers within a week and an account that falls ten percent and stays there for three months can produce curves that look nearly identical at this scale. As risk events, they are entirely different.

The depth and duration of declines have a series of their own: the drawdown, the subject of §04.

A concrete case makes the distinction plain. Picture two accounts that each fall 10% and each end the year at the same value. The first falls and recovers inside a single week. The second falls and stays below its previous high for three months before recovering. At the scale of a multi-year equity curve, the two dips can look almost identical. In risk terms, those three months dominate the year.

§04 · The Drawdown

The path of drawdowns

How deep the falls went, how long they lasted, and why recovery is symmetric here.

A drawdown is the distance between where an account is and the highest value it had reached before. On a day when the account sets a new high, the drawdown is zero. On every other day it is negative, and its size is the loss a holder would be sitting on, measured from the best moment they had seen.

That definition makes the drawdown the principal measure of path risk. It answers the two questions that govern capital allocation to a strategy: how far it fell from a high, and how long it took to recover.

02 · THE MEASUREMENT — Drawdown, defined (screenshots 16, 34)

M_t = max_{s ≤ t} C_s        D_t = C_t − M_t ≤ 0        maximum drawdown = min_t D_t

Takeaway: The drawdown is the gap between the account and its highest earlier value. It is always zero or negative, and its most negative value is the maximum drawdown.

The deepest fall in the record was −17.25% below a previous high. The longest spell spent below a previous high lasted 54 trading days. Those two figures, depth and duration, are the ones to hold in mind when reading any return figure elsewhere in the series, because they describe what the return actually demanded of whoever held it.

The character of the drawdowns matters as much as their extremes. The chart shows frequent, shallow dips and a small number of deeper ones, rather than long, grinding declines. A strategy whose losses are brief and recovered quickly asks something very different of its holder from one whose losses linger, even when the two share the same maximum drawdown.

Depth can also be set against return. Over the record the account gained +489.07% while its deepest fall was −17.25%. The ratio between annual return and maximum drawdown is the Calmar ratio, one of the measures Lesson 9 introduces, and it answers a practical question: how much return did the strategy deliver for each 1% of its worst loss?

03 · A PROPERTY OF SIMPLE RETURNS — Recovery is symmetric (screenshots 18, 19, 35)

simple: a fall of x% needs a gain of x%        compounded: a fall of f needs a gain of f / (1 − f)

Takeaway: On a simple-return account, a fall and its recovery are the same size, because the base the returns apply to never shrinks. In compounded terms a fall of half needs a gain of double.

(In the page screenshots 18 and 19 the formula is cut off at both ends; it is shown whole in close-up 35.)

This symmetry is easy to overlook and worth stating plainly. On a compounded account, a fall of fifty percent needs a gain of one hundred percent to recover, because the lost capital no longer earns anything. On a simple-return account the base is fixed, so recovering from the deepest fall needed exactly as large a gain, in percent, as the loss. The drawdown here is therefore a direct measure of the ground to be made up, with no compounding penalty hidden inside it.

There is one more subtlety, and it is easy to miss. The maximum drawdown is a single number drawn from a single history. Had the same days arrived in a different order, the deepest fall would have been different, possibly shallower and possibly deeper. Lesson 15 reorders these same days thousands of times and finds that the real drawdown sits near the middle of what those orderings produce, but that roughly one ordering in twenty would have fallen noticeably further. The recorded maximum is a guide to the scale of risk, not a ceiling on it.

Recovery has its own arithmetic, and it depends on how the record is kept. In a simple, uncompounded record a loss of 17.25% from the high is recovered by gaining 17.25%. In a compounded record the same loss leaves 82.75% of the capital, which must then grow by 20.85% to return to the high; a loss of 50% would need a gain of 100%. Figure 4.2 shows the two curves diverging as losses deepen. The record's deepest fall sits where the difference is still modest, which is one more consequence of its depth being limited.

Drawdown has a theory, and it tells us how deep a fall to expect from a given edge. For a process with a stable Sharpe ratio S and volatility σ, the expected deepest drawdown over a long record grows with σ divided by S and with the logarithm of the record's length; Magdon-Ismail and co-authors gave the exact expectation for Brownian motion with drift. The practical content is that drawdown depth is governed by the ratio of volatility to Sharpe ratio: halving volatility at the same Sharpe ratio halves the expected deepest fall, and doubling the Sharpe ratio at the same volatility does the same. The record's deepest fall of 17.25% at a volatility of 29.74% and a Sharpe ratio of 3.51 is close to what that theory implies, and Lesson 15 confirms the point by placing it at about the 51st percentile of the falls that random reorderings of the record's own days produce.

Institutions summarise drawdown in several ratios, and the record's values are reported in Lesson 9. The Calmar ratio divides annual return by the deepest drawdown; the Martin ratio divides it by the Ulcer index, which averages the squared depth of every drawdown day rather than taking only the deepest, so that it rewards shallow, short falls as well as rare deep ones. Both ratios depend on the record's length, because a longer record has more chances to produce a deep fall, which is why they are compared only between records of similar length.

The episode shown in Figure 4.1 is worth reading twice. Its depth, 17.25%, is the number an allocator would cite; its shape, 39 days down and four days back, is the more informative fact. Falls that arrive gradually and recover sharply are characteristic of an edge that continued to operate through a difficult period rather than one that stopped working; the opposite shape, a sudden fall followed by a long, grinding recovery, is the signature of a strategy that lost something it had relied on. The distinction is qualitative here and quantitative in Lesson 16, which asks how quickly a forward record could tell the two apart.

§05 · Time Under Water

The arithmetic of new highs

Why a strong strategy still spends most of its days below a previous high.

The third figure in the drawdown panel is the least intuitive. The account spent 75.3% of its days below a previous high. The figure is not a loss rate: it follows from the arithmetic of new highs, and it is typical of strategies of this strength.

04 · THE MEASUREMENT — Time under water (screenshots 21, 22, 36)

TUW = (1/T) #{ t : D_t < 0 }

Takeaway: The share of days spent below a previous high. A new high is set only when a day's gain clears the whole of any open drawdown, so even a strongly rising strategy sets them on only a minority of days.

Consider what a new high requires. After any loss, the account is below its peak. To set a new high, it must first recover the entire loss and then add more. On a day with a gain smaller than the open drawdown, the account rises, but it is still under water. So even a strategy whose trend is firmly upward will spend most of its days climbing back towards a peak it has already reached, and only a minority of days rising above it.

The way to know whether 75.3% is ordinary or not is to compare it with strategies whose strength is known exactly.

The numbers are worth reading closely. At a Sharpe ratio of 1.0 the synthetic strategies spend 90% of their days under water; at 2.0, 83%; at 3.5, 73%. The share falls as strength rises, but slowly, and even the strongest case spends most of its days below a previous high.

It is also worth separating two figures that are easily confused. Winning days, the share of days with a gain, was 49.87% in this record. Time under water, the share of days below a previous high, was 75.3%. They measure different things. A winning day can still be a day under water, if its gain does not clear the open drawdown. That is why a strategy can win on about half of its days and still spend three quarters of them below its peak.

The comparison settles the question. A synthetic strategy with a Sharpe ratio of 1.0 spends around nine days in ten under water. Even at a Sharpe ratio of 3.5, which is roughly the strength of the record in this series, a strategy spends nearly three quarters of its days below a previous high. The Forecaster Portfolio's figure of 75.3% sits right where a strategy of its strength would be expected to sit.

Most days below a previous high is not a sign of weakness. It is what a rising strategy looks like from the inside.

READING TIME UNDER WATER Time under water describes how gains are arranged in time, not how often the strategy loses. It is high whenever gains arrive as a sequence of recoveries from small declines, the normal texture of a strategy with a positive but noisy edge. Its informative use is comparative, against strategies of the same Sharpe ratio as in Figure 5.1, rather than as an absolute figure.

The implication is that the frequency of new highs is a poor measure of a strategy's quality. The informative quantities are the depth and duration of its drawdowns, which §04 measured directly, not the share of days spent below a peak, which is high for almost every strategy with a positive edge.

Time under water is not one number but a distribution of spells. The record spent its days below the running high in 149 separate spells. The median spell lasted 2 trading days, 73 of spells ended within five days, 13 lasted longer than twenty, and the longest, the episode of §04, lasted 54. Figure 5.2 shows the distribution. Most of the time below the high was a matter of days; the long spells were few, and the longest was the one that produced the deepest fall.

Time under water and drawdown depth are different dimensions of the same experience, and they do not move together. A record can spend long periods a small distance below its high, which is uncomfortable but costless, or short periods far below it, which is the reverse. The record's distribution of spells in Figure 5.2 is of the first kind in its typical spell and the second kind in its longest: a median spell of two days, and a single spell of 54 days that was also the deepest fall. Allocators care about both, because a long shallow spell tests patience while a deep short one tests capital.

The theory of §04 extends to duration. For a process with a stable positive Sharpe ratio, the fraction of time spent below the running high is a decreasing function of the Sharpe ratio alone, which is the curve of Figure 5.1, and the expected length of the longest spell grows with the record's length. A high-Sharpe record is therefore one that is usually at or near a new high, and the record's days at a high, and its longest spell, are consistent with its Sharpe ratio rather than exceptional given it. This is the sense in which time under water is a property of the edge rather than of luck.

Institutions report the longest recovery time alongside the deepest drawdown, and some set tolerances on both: a maximum acceptable depth and a maximum acceptable time without a new high, beyond which an allocation is reviewed. Lesson 16 turns the second tolerance into a statistical test, asking how long a forward record can go without a new high before the absence itself is evidence that the edge has decayed.

§06 · The Monthly View

How evenly it was earned

Consistency, the worst month, and the order in which to read everything.

The daily curve and the drawdown describe the path. The monthly table describes how evenly the result was distributed through time, and that is a different question. A strategy can earn a large annual return in two exceptional months and give some of it back in the other ten, or it can earn the same total steadily. The totals match; the propositions do not.

The table rewards a second reading for its spread as well as its count. Most months sit fairly close together, with a small number of strong months and a handful of losing ones. The losing months are scattered rather than gathered into one long run, which is consistent with the drawdown chart's picture of brief, recovered falls rather than a single extended decline.

That consistency is part of what makes a result credible, and part of what makes it holdable. A strategy that earns its return across most months gives its holder regular confirmation that it is working. One that earns it in rare bursts asks for long patience between them, and patience is precisely what most people find they do not have once real money is involved.

HOW ALLOCATORS READ THE TABLE A professional allocator reads the monthly table early, and reads the worst month first. It is the nearest thing the table offers to the cost of a bad stretch, and it is the figure most likely to decide whether a strategy could be held through the period that produced it.

Taken together, the three charts in this lesson give a complete first reading of the historical track record. The equity curve says where it ended. The drawdown says what it cost to get there, in depth and in time. The monthly view says how evenly the result was earned. Each one is necessary, and none of them is sufficient on its own.

Figure 6.1 gathers the 55 months into one distribution. 48 of them were positive; the mean month returned +8.89%; the best month gained 31.11% and the worst lost 11.47%. The shape is the monthly counterpart of the daily skewness Lesson 1 measured: a long right tail of strong months and a short left tail, with the losing months clustered close to zero.

The monthly series has statistics of its own, and they corroborate the daily ones. Across the 55 months the mean return was +8.89% with a standard deviation of 8.51%, which gives a monthly Sharpe ratio, annualised by the square root of twelve, of 3.62; the daily figure is 3.51. The two differ because aggregation to months changes the measured volatility when returns are serially dependent, and Lesson 9 examines the dependence directly. Over rolling twelve-month windows the record never lost: its weakest twelve months returned +60.0% and its strongest +156.0%. Every one of its 18 of 18 quarters was positive, a fact Lesson 1 showed to be consistent with the record's Sharpe ratio rather than a separate finding.

The heatmap invites a reading that should be resisted. With fewer than five observations of each calendar month, any pattern across the columns, strong springs or weak autumns, is within the noise of estimation, and the series does not read seasonality into the record. What the heatmap shows reliably is the texture of the return: how evenly it arrived, where the losing months fell and how they were surrounded, and that the strongest months, including the best at +31.11%, were spread across years rather than concentrated in one.

Annual returns deserve the same care. The years differ, and the first and last are partial, so the annual figures are read as the record's return through each environment of Lesson 5 rather than as a forecast of a typical year. Their consistency, each year positive and none dominated by a single month, is what the heatmap and the distribution of Figure 6.1 establish between them: the record's return was earned broadly, through time, rather than in a few fortunate stretches.
