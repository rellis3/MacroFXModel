The anatomy of data

Why the construction of data is part of the evidence.

Every statistic in a research record is a statistic about its data. Before any question about an edge can be asked, a researcher must decide how prices are recorded, how series that end are joined to series that begin, which instruments are included, and at what resolution the record is kept. Each decision changes the numbers that follow, and some change them decisively.

This lesson examines those decisions in general terms: how continuous series are constructed and why the construction matters, what futures data offers as a research record, the two classic errors of survivorship and look-ahead, the role of resolution, and how the macroeconomic environment runs through every series. The Forecaster Portfolio's historical track record was built on futures data because futures data is cleaner; the resolution and construction of its data are proprietary.

§01 · Construction

What a price series is

Why the way a series is built changes the returns it reports.

A price series looks like a simple record of what happened. For many instruments it is nothing of the kind. Contracts with a fixed expiry trade for a limited life, and a research record that spans years must join many of them into one continuous series. Each join, or roll, replaces an expiring contract with the next, and the two rarely trade at the same price. A series that simply joins the contracts' own prices records every price exactly as it traded, which is its great strength; but it contains a gap at every roll, and any return, volatility or indicator computed across that gap records a price move that no investor ever earned or lost.

There are two responses. One alters the data: earlier prices are shifted or rescaled so that the joined series looks continuous. The other leaves every price exactly as it was recorded and makes the code aware of the boundaries instead: the trading logic knows which contract it holds, executes each roll as real trades at the prices quoted at the time, and the signal logic never reads across a boundary as if nothing had happened. This series follows the second approach. It is the more realistic of the two, because every price in the record is one that traded, and it is the approach the Forecaster Portfolio's historical track record was built on: contract data left untouched, with every roll handled in the code.

The institutional standard avoids treating any constructed series as a record of trading. The raw prices of each contract are kept exactly as they traded and never edited; they are the single source of truth. Trading is simulated contract by contract: a position is held in a specific contract, and each roll is an explicit pair of trades, closing the expiring contract and opening the next, executed at the prices actually quoted at that moment, buying at the offer and selling at the bid, or as a single spread trade where one is available. Profit and loss is computed from those fills, so the gap between contracts never enters it, and no fill can occur at a price that was not available. Rolls are timed by liquidity, as trading activity migrates from one contract to the next, rather than at a moment chosen with hindsight.

Why does the gap exist at all? Because two consecutive contracts are not the same instrument. Each is a claim on the same asset at a different date, and the cost of carry ties each to the spot price through its own time to delivery. At any moment the later contract differs from the nearer one by the net carry over the interval between their expiries: the cost of financing and holding the asset for those extra months, less the income or convenience of holding it, plus whatever premium hedgers pay or expectations add for the later date. Where carry is positive the later contract trades higher, a term structure in contango; where income or convenience outweighs financing, it trades lower, in backwardation. The gap is therefore a product of the term structure, and it is as real and as persistent as the term structure itself.

Its size follows from the same arithmetic. With net carry of c a year and a spacing of Δ years between contracts, the gap is approximately cΔ of the price: quarterly contracts in a market carrying −6% a year stand about 1.5% apart, which is the gap used throughout this chapter; monthly contracts in the same market stand about 0.5% apart; six-monthly ones about 3%. The gap changes with rates, inventories and expectations, so it is not a constant to be subtracted once but a quantity that must be observed on each roll day. Figure 1.1 follows the two contracts through the days around a roll. The front and next contracts move together, separated by the gap; a series stitched from whichever is the front jumps by the gap on the roll day, as if the market had moved; a position that is rolled does not jump at all, because the old contract is closed at its own price and the new one opened at its own price. Figure 1.2 shows how the gap scales with carry and spacing.

Handling the roll in the code has five parts, and each is a rule fixed before the research begins. The first is scheduling: which contract is held, and when the position moves to the next. Common rules roll a fixed number of days before expiry, or when trading activity in the next contract overtakes the current one, or a set number of days before any delivery or notice date that a position must not cross; the activity rule is the most realistic, because it follows the liquidity, and it must be computed from activity already observed, never from activity that came later.

The second is execution. On the roll day the simulation closes the old contract and opens the new one as two trades at the prices quoted at that moment, selling at the bid and buying at the offer, or as one calendar spread trade where such a market exists, which often costs less than two separate legs. A large position may be rolled over several days to limit impact, exactly as it would be in practice. The third is accounting: profit and loss is the change in the value of the contract actually held, so the record carries the old contract's moves until the roll and the new contract's moves afterwards, and the gap between them never appears, except as the real cost of the two trades.

02 · ACCOUNTING ACROSS THE ROLL — Profit and loss without the gap (screenshots 04, 05, 30)

ΔV_t = q (F^old_t − F^old_{t−1})  before the roll,      ΔV_t = q (F^new_t − F^new_{t−1})  after;   the gap never appears

Takeaway: Before the roll, value changes with the old contract; after it, with the new one. The gap is never a return, only the cost of the two trades is.

The fourth is signal continuity. Any indicator with a look-back window would see the gap as a price move if it read across the boundary, so the code computes signals from the history of the contract about to be held, which trades for months before it becomes the most active and so has a history of its own, or from returns that were earned inside contracts and chained by real trades. Where an incoming contract's early history is thin, the code warms the signal up on that contract for a fixed period before relying on it. The fifth is validation: every roll is checked for a gap that is implausibly large relative to recent volatility, every fill for a price that was quoted, and the whole record for the round trip of §06, in which returns chained back into prices reproduce the raw prices exactly within each contract.

Derived views have a separate role. A return index computes each day's return within a single contract, using the same contract's price on both days and, on a roll day, the new contract for both, and chains the returns. It is calculated deterministically from the raw prices, so it involves no manual interference with the data, and in the simulated market it is identical to the ratio-adjusted series, as it must be. Views of this kind serve analysis that must span many contracts, such as long-horizon returns and volatility, and they never supply a fill.

03 · CONSTRUCTION — The return index (screenshots 05, 31)

r_t = F_{k,t} / F_{k,t−1} − 1,      I_t = I_{t−1} (1 + r_t)

Takeaway: Each return uses one contract k on both days; returns are chained into the index I, so gaps between contracts never enter it.

Signals need the same care as fills. Any indicator whose look-back window spans a roll sees the gap as a price move unless the window is drawn from a single contract or from returns that exclude the gaps. A careful system therefore computes its signals from the history of the contract it will actually hold, which typically trades for months before it becomes the most active, or from a return index; either way the raw data remain untouched. The execution model and the signal logic are both roll-aware, and the record is realistic at every step.

What does altering the data do, and what happens if a researcher does neither? For analysis that must span the joins, there are two standard derived views, and each preserves something different. Difference adjustment shifts all earlier prices by the gap at each roll, so that the joined series is continuous and every price change, measured in price units, is preserved. Ratio adjustment scales all earlier prices by the ratio of the two prices at each roll, so that every percentage return is preserved instead. Neither leaves the historical prices as they were actually traded; each is a construction chosen for a purpose.

04 · CONSTRUCTION — Difference and ratio adjustment (screenshots 06, 32)

P̃_t^diff = P_t + Σ_{j>t} (F_j^new − F_j^old),      P̃_t^ratio = P_t Π_{j>t} F_j^new / F_j^old

Takeaway: Difference adjustment adds every later roll gap to earlier prices; ratio adjustment multiplies earlier prices by every later roll ratio.

A simulated market shows how much the choice matters. Ten years of prices are built from 40 consecutive quarterly contracts, each trading 1.5% below the one before it at the moment of the roll. The underlying market returns -3.7% a year. If returns are computed naively across the joins, the series appears to return -9.6% a year: about six per cent a year of apparent loss is created entirely by treating the gaps as returns. The ratio-adjusted series reproduces -3.7% exactly. The difference-adjusted series reports -4.4%, and its early prices lie -40% below the prices at which the market actually traded, which distorts every percentage return measured in the early years; on average its daily percentage returns differ from the true ones by 0.247%, a large error for a series whose daily volatility is about one per cent.

Data quality is weakest at exactly the moment of the roll. Trading activity drains from an expiring contract in its final days, so its prices become sparse and less representative, while the next contract may not yet be fully active. Daily records may report an official settlement price, determined by a published procedure, or the last traded price, which can be stale when trading is thin; mixing the two across contracts introduces spurious returns. Careful construction rolls before activity collapses, uses one price definition consistently, and checks every roll for gaps that are implausibly large relative to recent volatility.

The lesson generalises. Returns belong inside contracts: computed within the contract actually held and chained across rolls by the trades that were made, they need no adjusted series at all. Where a derived view is wanted for long-horizon analysis, ratio adjustment or the return index preserves percentage returns, while difference adjustment preserves price changes and distorts percentages; neither is a record of trading, and a long difference-adjusted history can even produce negative early prices.

Roll timing is a further choice. The code can roll on a fixed calendar, a set number of days before expiry, or when trading activity migrates from one contract to the next, and each rule produces slightly different gaps and slightly different returns. None is universally correct. What matters is that the rule is fixed before the research begins, applied consistently, and recorded, so that every result can be traced to the construction that produced it.

This is the first sense in which data is part of the evidence. Two researchers studying the same market with different roll rules can report different returns for the same strategy, and neither need have made an error; what distinguishes a credible record is that the data were never altered, the roll rule was fixed in advance and applied by the code, and every fill was at a price that was quoted. The Forecaster Portfolio's record meets that standard; the detail of how its code handles each boundary is proprietary.

Leave the data untouched. Make the code aware of every contract boundary, and let returns come only from trades that were made.

§02 · Futures data

Futures as data

How futures are priced, what their returns contain, and why the data is cleaner.

A future is a commitment to trade an asset at a fixed price on a later date. Its price is tied to the price of the asset today by the cost of carry: the cost of financing a position in the asset until the delivery date, plus any costs of holding it, less any income or benefit that holding it provides. The relation is exact in the absence of arbitrage, and it determines how futures prices behave relative to the underlying market at every moment of their life.

05 · PRICING — The cost of carry and convergence (screenshots 09, 33)

F_{t,T} = S_t e^{(r + u − y)(T − t)},      b_t = F_{t,T} − S_t → 0  at  T

Takeaway: The future equals spot grown at the net carry rate r + u − y to the delivery date; the basis b shrinks to zero as delivery approaches.

The difference between the future and spot prices is the basis, and the basis must vanish at expiry, when the future becomes a claim on the asset itself. When the net carry is positive, the future trades above spot, a term structure described as contango, and converges downward; when the benefits of holding the asset outweigh the costs, the future trades below spot, backwardation, and converges upward. Figure 2.1 shows both paths around the same spot price over a year to expiry.

Convergence gives futures returns a precise anatomy. A position in futures requires only a margin deposit, and the rest of the capital can be held as collateral earning interest. The total return of a fully collateralised position is then approximately the sum of three parts: the collateral return, the change in the spot price, and the roll yield, which is the convergence of the basis earned by holding a future as it approaches expiry. In contango the roll yield is negative, because each future is bought above spot and falls towards it; in backwardation it is positive. Figure 2.2 decomposes a year's return in each case with spot unchanged and collateral earning 4%: carry of +5% leaves a total of −1%, while carry of −5% leaves +9%. Figure 2.3 shows that the roll yield moves one for one with the slope of the term structure.

Two refinements sharpen the picture. First, a future is not quite a forward. Gains and losses on a future are settled every day through variation margin, while a forward settles only at delivery. Cox, Ingersoll and Ross showed that when interest rates are correlated with the asset's price, daily settlement makes the two prices differ: gains received when rates are high can be reinvested at those rates, and losses paid when rates are low are financed cheaply, so a long future is worth more than a long forward when the correlation is positive. For most research purposes the difference is small, but it is a reminder that the cost-of-carry relation holds exactly only under stated assumptions.

Second, the roll yield may carry a risk premium rather than being a mechanical by-product of carry. Keynes proposed the theory of normal backwardation: producers who hedge by selling futures pay speculators a premium for taking the other side, so that futures prices lie below the expected future spot price and long positions earn a return for bearing that risk. Whether and where such premia exist is an empirical question, but the theory establishes that the term structure can reflect the balance of hedging demand as well as the costs of carry.

Margin also turns price risk into liquidity risk. Because losses are settled daily, a futures position that moves sharply against its holder demands cash immediately, and those demands arrive in precisely the volatile periods when cash is hardest to raise. This is the mechanism by which the liquidity spirals of Lesson 4 propagate through futures markets: variation margin calls force sales, sales move prices, and moving prices generate further calls. A research record in futures data therefore needs to account not only for returns but for the capital required to sustain positions through their worst days.

These properties explain why futures data is cleaner for research than many alternatives. The instruments are standardised, so one market's history is a sequence of like-for-like contracts rather than a changing mixture of securities. They are centrally cleared, and each day ends with an official settlement price determined by a published procedure. There are no dividends, splits or other corporate actions to adjust for, because the instrument carries none. A short position is as simple to hold as a long one, so the record of a strategy that trades in both directions is not distorted by the costs and constraints of borrowing. And because a future is a claim rather than a company, its history is not thinned by bankruptcies and delistings, a source of the survivorship bias examined in §03.

The economics are also cleaner. Because only margin is posted, the return of a futures position is naturally an excess return over the collateral rate, which separates the return of the strategy from the return on cash. Leverage becomes an explicit choice, the ratio of notional exposure to capital, rather than a by-product of financing arrangements. The collateral assumption still matters, since the same strategy reports a higher total return when rates are high, and a careful record states which return it reports. The Forecaster Portfolio's historical track record was built on futures data for these reasons; how that data was constructed, and at what resolution, is proprietary.

A futures return has three parts: collateral, spot and roll. The roll yield is the basis converging, and it can add or subtract several per cent a year.

§03 · Bias

Survivorship and look-ahead

Two classic errors that make a record look better than anything that could have been achieved.

Two errors in the handling of data have done more to flatter research records than any other. Both are easy to commit, both bias results in the same direction, and both are invisible in the final numbers unless the construction of the record is examined. Survivorship bias removes the failures from the evidence; look-ahead bias lets decisions use information that was not available when they were made.

Survivorship bias arises whenever a dataset contains only what survived. A database of funds that lists only those still operating, an index whose history includes only today's members, or a collection of strategies that excludes those abandoned after losses: each omits the cases that failed, and failures are disproportionately the ones with poor returns. The average of what remains overstates the average of what existed. Brown, Goetzmann, Ibbotson and Ross showed that survivorship alone can create apparent skill and apparent persistence in performance, and Elton, Gruber and Blake estimated its effect on reported mutual fund returns.

07 · SELECTION — Survivorship bias (screenshots 12, 13, 35)

bias = E[R | survived] − E[R] > 0

Takeaway: When failures leave the record, the average of the survivors exceeds the average of everything that existed.

A simulation makes the mechanism exact. Take 1,000 strategies with no edge at all, each with annual volatility of 15%, and follow them for ten years, closing any strategy whose drawdown reaches 30%, as managers and investors close failing strategies in practice. About 18% survive. The average annual return of all the strategies is −1.2%, slightly below zero only because of the volatility drag described in Lesson 1; the average of the survivors is 4.1%. Figure 3.1 shows the process: failures leave the record at their worst moments, and what remains looks like a population with an edge. Figure 3.2 shows that the inflation grows as the closure rule tightens, reaching about 5.3% a year at a 30% limit.

The same bias operates inside research itself. A researcher who tries many variants of an idea and keeps only those that performed well is closing failing strategies before they ever reach a record, and the variants that remain carry exactly the inflation of Figure 3.2. This is why Lesson 2 insisted on a complete research record, every direction tried and every result kept, and why Lesson 12 corrects the Forecaster Portfolio's significance for the breadth of its own search. Survivorship is not only a property of databases; it is a property of any process that discards its failures.

Futures data is less exposed than most, because an individual market's price history is not ended by a bankruptcy or a delisting in the way a company's is. Survivorship enters instead through the choice of markets. A study that includes only the markets that are active today excludes those whose trading dwindled, and those that dwindled often did so after the conditions a strategy depended on disappeared. A careful record defines its universe with the information available at each date, not with the list of markets that exist at the end.

Look-ahead bias is subtler, because it rarely looks like cheating. It occurs whenever a decision in the record uses information from after the moment at which the decision would have been made. The most common form is execution at a price that was only known after the signal was computed: a signal calculated from a day's closing price cannot be filled at that same close. The effect can be enormous. On a simulated random walk with no structure at all, a rule that holds each day in the direction of that same day's return, filled at the close it was computed from, shows a Sharpe ratio of about 21.2; filled honestly on the following day, the same rule shows 0.19. Figure 3.3 shows the comparison.

Takeaway: A position at time t may depend only on the information available at t; any dependence on later information is look-ahead.

Other forms are harder to see. Economic data are published after a lag and revised later, sometimes substantially; Croushore and Stark built real-time datasets precisely so that research could use the figures that were actually available at each date, rather than the revised figures known today. Index membership, corporate events and even the identity of the most active contract are known with certainty only in hindsight. And a back-adjusted price series, as §01 showed, contains at every date the sum of roll gaps that had not yet occurred: any rule that compares those adjusted prices with a fixed threshold is using information from the future.

Figure 3.4 sets out the timeline that every record must respect: an event happens, it is published after a lag, a decision may use only what has been published, and later revisions cannot be used for earlier decisions. The defences follow directly. Every datum carries the time at which it became available, every decision is made from data stamped earlier than the decision, every fill is at a price that was quoted after the decision, and every universe is defined with the information of its own date. The architecture of §01, with raw data untouched and trading simulated contract by contract at quoted prices, is the foundation on which all of these defences rest.

Survivorship removes the failures from the evidence. Look-ahead lets decisions see the future. Both make a record look better than anything that could have been achieved.

§04 · Resolution

The resolution of a record

What finer data reveals, what it cannot, and where it starts to distort.

A record can be kept at many resolutions: annual, monthly, weekly, daily or finer still, each multiplying the number of observations. It is natural to assume that more observations always mean more knowledge. For some quantities they do; for the most important one they do not; and beyond a certain point additional resolution introduces errors of its own. Figure 4.3 shows the same market at four resolutions: a coarse record hides the risk that happened inside each period. The resolution of the Forecaster Portfolio's own data is proprietary; this chapter develops the general principles that govern the choice.

The first principle is due to Merton. The precision with which an average return can be estimated depends on the span of time the record covers, not on how often it is sampled. Over a span of T years, the standard error of the estimated mean return is the annual volatility divided by the square root of T, whatever the number of observations: daily data over ten years estimates the mean no better than annual data over the same ten years. With volatility of 16%, ten years leave a standard error of about 5.1% a year, as large as a typical edge of 5%. No increase in resolution can shrink it; only a longer record can.

09 · PRECISION — What resolution can and cannot estimate (screenshots 16, 17, 37)

SE(μ̂) = σ / √T   (independent of n),      SE(σ̂) / σ ≈ 1 / √(2n)

Takeaway: The error of the mean depends on the span T alone; the relative error of the volatility falls with the number of observations n.

Volatility behaves in the opposite way. Its precision improves with every observation, because each observation carries information about the size of moves regardless of the span it covers. From annual data over ten years, the relative error of an estimated volatility is about 22%; from daily data over the same span, about 1.4%. Figure 4.1 shows the two curves parting: finer data transforms the estimation of risk and leaves the estimation of return untouched. This asymmetry explains much of quantitative practice, in which risk is measured precisely and expected returns are known only roughly, and it is why the significance tests of later lessons depend on the length of the record rather than its resolution.

The second principle sets a limit on the first. At very high frequencies, observed prices are not the efficient prices of theory: they bounce between bid and offer, move in discrete increments and respond to the mechanics of each trade. This microstructure noise adds a component to every observed return. Realised variance, the sum of squared returns over a period, then measures the true variance plus twice the number of observations times the noise variance, so the finer the sampling, the larger the error. Aït-Sahalia, Mykland and Zhang showed how to choose the sampling frequency, and how to combine frequencies, so as to balance the precision gained from more observations against the noise they bring.

10 · NOISE — Realised variance under microstructure noise (screenshots 17, 18, 38)

E[RV_n] = σ² T + 2n ω²

Takeaway: Each observation adds noise of variance ω², so the expected realised variance grows with the number of observations n.

Figure 4.2 shows the effect as a signature plot, a standard diagnostic. With a true volatility of 16.0% and noise of two basis points per observation, volatility measured from five-minute returns is about 16.5%, from one-minute returns 18.3%, and from ten-second returns 27.0%. The market is no more volatile at ten seconds; the measurement is inflated by noise. A record kept at too fine a resolution can therefore overstate risk, and a strategy evaluated on such a record can appear to face hazards that are artefacts of the data.

The third principle connects resolution to decisions. Shannon's sampling theorem states that a signal must be sampled at least twice per cycle to be represented faithfully; components faster than that are not merely lost but folded back into slower ones, a distortion called aliasing. The analogue in research is that data must be fine enough to observe the behaviour a strategy responds to, while data much finer than the horizon of its decisions adds noise without adding usable information. Coarser data delays the response to changing conditions; finer data increases turnover, costs and exposure to the artefacts of microstructure. The right resolution matches the horizon of the decisions and the costs of acting on them.

Resolution also interacts with every bias of §03. Finer records multiply the opportunities for look-ahead, because more timestamps must be aligned, and they magnify the influence of small errors in prices at rolls. Coarser records are more robust to these errors but conceal intra-period risk, such as a deep drawdown that recovered before the period closed. A careful researcher states the resolution of a record, the reasons for it, and the risks it hides. For the Forecaster Portfolio that resolution is proprietary; the figures in this series are presented at coarse resolution, for the reasons of Lesson 4.

More observations sharpen the estimate of risk. Only more time sharpens the estimate of return.

§05 · Macro

Macro in the data

How rates, inflation and liquidity run through every series, and why a record holds fewer regimes than days.

No price series is a closed system. Every one of them is produced inside a macroeconomic environment: a path of policy rates, an inflation regime, a cycle of growth and recession, and a state of market liquidity that can change in a week. The environment is not noise added to the series; it is a determinant of the series, and a research record that spans only part of it has seen only part of what the markets can do.

For futures data the transmission is unusually direct, because the cost of carry puts the financing rate inside the price. From the relation of §02, the logarithm of a future relative to spot is the net carry rate multiplied by the time to expiry, so a rise of one point in rates raises a one-year future by about 1% relative to spot. Figure 5.1 shows the basis against the financing rate for three yields: when rates exceed the income or convenience of holding the asset, the term structure stands in contango and long positions pay a negative roll yield; when they fall below it, backwardation returns and the roll yield turns positive. A tightening cycle therefore shifts whole term structures, and with them one of the three components of every futures return.

Takeaway: The sensitivity of a future to the financing rate equals its time to expiry; policy rates move term structures mechanically.

Inflation and growth enter through the other terms. Inflation raises the expected drift of nominal prices and the cost of storage; growth and scarcity raise the convenience of holding physical assets and so deepen backwardation; expectations of either change the slope of term structures before the facts arrive. Because these effects differ across markets, a portfolio of futures carries a varied macro exposure through its carry alone, before any view about direction is taken.

Liquidity is the third channel, and the most abrupt. Lesson 4 described market impact under normal conditions, with depth available at every price. In stress, depth thins and spreads widen precisely when forced selling arrives, so the impact of a given trade can multiply within days. Volatility regimes are the visible face of the same shift: the clustering of §02 operates not only from day to day but across months, as calm and turbulent spells succeed one another. This has a consequence for evidence that is easy to overlook. A record is usually described by its length in days, but what it has witnessed is better described by the number of distinct macro episodes it contains, and those are few. Figure 5.3 shows the simulated market of Lesson 3 over ten years: its rolling volatility has a median of about 14%, and spells of at least a month above one and a quarter times that median, the turbulent regimes, occur 5 times, lasting about 2.7 months on average. Ten years of daily data, over two and a half thousand observations, contain only a handful of such episodes.

If a regime lasts a year or two, a record of 5 years contains perhaps three or four independent ones, however many days it holds. This is Lesson 1's lesson again: observations within a regime are not independent evidence about behaviour across regimes, and only the passage of time, through new regimes, adds that evidence. Lessons 6 and 10 ask how the Forecaster Portfolio's record behaves under stress, and Lesson 16 how much a forward record adds; both questions are, at root, questions about regimes.

The choice of where a record begins is itself a macro decision, and a cousin of the survivorship bias of §03. A record that starts just after a stress episode, or just as a favourable regime begins, inherits the flattering half of a cycle; one that starts before it carries the whole. Because a researcher sees the past when choosing a start date, the honest rule is to report the record from the first date the data allow, as the Forecaster Portfolio's historical track record does, and to treat any later start as a selection that must be declared and justified.

Regimes also govern diversification. Correlations between markets are not constants; they rise in stress, when the liquidity channel of Figure 5.2 forces many holders to sell many things at once, so that a portfolio that appears diversified in calm regimes may behave as a single position in turbulent ones. Lesson 6 measures this for the Forecaster Portfolio's own legs. The point for data is that correlations estimated over a record of few regimes describe those regimes, not all regimes, and a record that has seen no stress has not yet measured its own diversification.

The practical discipline follows. A research record states the macro environment it was built in: the direction of rates, the inflation regime, the stress episodes it contains and those it does not. Results are read against that statement rather than in isolation, and a strategy's sensitivity to each channel, to carry, to liquidity and to volatility regime, is examined explicitly rather than assumed away. The macro environment of the Forecaster Portfolio's historical track record is a matter of public record; what the system does with it is proprietary.

A record is measured in days but witnesses in regimes. Ten years of data may contain only a handful of them.

§06 · Quality

Data quality as evidence

Why cleaning, validation and an audit trail belong in the research record.

Everything in this lesson converges on one claim: the data behind a research record is part of its evidence, and the care taken over it should be stated and verifiable in the same way as the tests performed on it. Three practices make that possible: cleaning that measures its own effect, validation that flags rather than alters, and an audit trail that makes the dataset itself a fixed, verifiable object.

Errors in price data are ordinary. Exchanges correct bad prints, feeds drop days, holidays differ across markets so that one series has a price where another has none, and an expiring contract's last trade may be hours stale. Each error is small in the record and large in the statistics. A single bad print of k daily standard deviations in a record of n days raises the measured variance by about k²/n and the excess kurtosis by about k⁴/n. In a thousand-day record, one print of five standard deviations raises measured volatility by +1.2%; one of ten raises it by +4.9% and adds about 10 to the excess kurtosis; one of fifteen raises volatility by +10.7%. A single wrong number can therefore make a calm series look dangerous, or hide a real tail among false ones. Figure 6.1 shows the effect.

Validation therefore screens every series before it is used. Returns beyond a threshold set from recent volatility are flagged; prices that fail to move for days are flagged as stale; gaps in the calendar are reconciled across markets; volumes are checked for consistency with prices; and where a second source exists, the two are compared. The screens flag and never alter: a flagged value is examined, and if it is corrected the correction is itself recorded, with its reason. Silent correction is a form of look-ahead, because the corrected series is one that no one could have traded at the time.

A simple round trip tests the construction of §01: returns computed from the record are chained back into prices, and the result must reproduce the raw prices exactly within each contract. Any difference marks a roll handled wrongly or a price altered without record. Checks of this kind are cheap, and they catch the construction errors that most flatter or damage a record.

The audit trail makes the dataset verifiable. Once cleaned and validated, the data are stored in an immutable form and hashed, as Lesson 4 described for a strategy specification: the hash fixes the dataset at a moment, and any later change produces a different hash. Versions are kept rather than overwritten, so that any result can be traced to the exact data that produced it. A data manifest records what the dataset contains, the rules by which it was constructed and cleaned, the resolution and the hashes, without naming anything proprietary. Regulated institutions have formalised the same practices. After the financial crisis, the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision issued its principles for risk data aggregation and reporting, known as BCBS 239, requiring banks to be able to show that the data behind their risk measures are accurate, complete, timely and adaptable, with governance and audit trails to prove it. The principles were written for balance sheets rather than research records, but the standard they set is the one this chapter describes: a number is only as credible as the demonstrable quality of the data behind it.

Reproducibility closes the loop. A result in a research record should be reproducible from the stated data, the stated rules and the stated code, which means pinning all three together: the dataset by hash, the construction and cleaning rules in the manifest, and the research code under version control alongside them. A result that cannot be regenerated from pinned inputs is a report of what happened once, not evidence. Institutions apply this through independent reproduction of key results before capital is committed, and it is the standard to which the figures in this series are held.

The Forecaster Portfolio's data follow this discipline. Its sources are unnamed, its resolution and construction are proprietary, and nothing in this series describes either; but the practices of this chapter, never editing in place, flagging rather than altering, fixing the dataset by hash and keeping every version, are the general standard against which any research record, including this one, should be read. The remaining lessons examine the record that these data produced, beginning, in Lesson 6, with how its ten legs combine into a portfolio.

Data are part of the evidence. A record is only as credible as the dataset behind it, and that dataset should be fixed, verifiable and never silently changed.
