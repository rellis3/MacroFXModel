An edge is an asset that wears out as others learn of it. The economics of decay, capacity and crowding explain why systematic firms keep their logic proprietary, and how evidence can still be shown without it.

The economics of proprietary logic

Why the value of an edge depends on who else knows it.

SeriesCase Study: The Forecaster Portfolio
SubjectThe economics of edges
MethodEdge valuation, market impact, effective independence
Reading timeAbout 45 minutes

An edge in a financial market is a wasting asset. Its value depends on how much it earns and, above all, on how long it lasts, and how long it lasts depends on how many others come to know it. Market impact limits how much capital any edge can support, and imitators who trade the same edge share both its return and its liquidity, so that crowded strategies fail together.

This lesson develops the economics behind the Forecaster Portfolio's decision to keep its logic private: the valuation of a decaying edge, the square-root law of market impact and the optimal size it implies, the arithmetic of correlated imitation, the structural advantage of small capital in edges too small for institutions, the cost of disclosure, and how evidence can be made public while the recipe is not. Every model in it is general; none describes the system.

§01 · Decay

An edge is a wasting asset

Why the value of an edge is proportional to how long it lasts.

An edge in a financial market is an asset. Like any asset it produces a stream of returns, and like many assets it wears out. Unlike machinery, it does not wear out through use alone; it wears out as others discover it. Every trader who finds the same pattern and acts on it takes part of the return, and the pattern weakens as capital accumulates against it. The economics of systematic research begin from this fact: an edge is a wasting asset, and its value depends above all on how long it lasts.

The formal statement is simple. Suppose an edge earns α a year at first and decays exponentially, halving every h years, and suppose future returns are discounted at a rate r. Its present value is the integral of the decaying, discounted stream, which is α divided by the sum of the decay rate, ln 2 / h, and the discount rate. For α of 10% and r of 5%, an edge with a one-year half-life is worth about 13.5% of the capital deployed, one with a three-year half-life about 35.6%, and one with a ten-year half-life about 83.8%. A permanent edge would be worth 200%.

Takeaway: An edge earning α a year that halves every h years, discounted at r, is worth α divided by the sum of its decay rate and the discount rate.

Why do edges decay at all? Grossman and Stiglitz gave the foundational answer in 1980. If prices fully reflected all available information, no one would have an incentive to pay the cost of gathering it, and prices could not then reflect it. Markets can therefore be efficient only up to the cost of information: edges exist as compensation for the cost of finding them, and they shrink as more participants pay that cost. Decay is the market's way of bringing returns into line with the cost of discovery.

The empirical evidence is consistent. McLean and Pontiff studied nearly a hundred published return predictors and found their average predictability about 26% lower after the end of the original sample, and about 58% lower after publication. The first figure reflects the optimism of in-sample research; the second, the arrival of capital once the edge was known. Publication, in effect, starts a clock.

Imitation divides the return directly. In the simplest case, an edge of fixed size traded identically by several competitors is split among them: one imitator halves each holder's share, four reduce it to a fifth, nine to a tenth. Figure 1.2 shows the decline. In practice the division is worse than proportional, because imitators also compete for the same liquidity, the subject of §02.

Decay leaves a signature in a strategy's own record. An edge that is being competed away shows a declining risk-adjusted return over successive periods, a shrinking response to the same conditions, and rising costs as others trade in the same direction. None of these is conclusive on its own, because a single record is one path among many, as Lesson 1 showed; but together they are the evidence a disciplined firm watches for, and Lesson 16 returns to how quickly such decay can be detected.

Decay also sets the pace of research. A firm that holds several edges, each decaying, must discover new ones at the rate at which the old ones wear out simply to keep its returns constant. If edges halve every three years, roughly a fifth of the value of the collection is lost each year and must be replaced. This is the research treadmill, and it connects the economics of this lesson to the mathematics of Lesson 2: a search whose duration cannot be scheduled must nonetheless keep pace with a decay that never stops.

The treadmill has an exact form. Little's law, a result from the theory of queues, states that the average number of items in a stable system equals the rate at which they arrive multiplied by the average time each spends in it. Applied to a research firm, the number of live edges it can sustain equals its rate of discovery multiplied by the average life of an edge. A firm that finds 2 new edges a year, each lasting 5 years on average, sustains about 10 live edges. If competition halves the average life, the firm must double its rate of discovery merely to hold its breadth, and with it, by the fundamental law of Lesson 3, its information ratio.

Uncertainty about durability matters as well. The value of an edge, α/(ln 2/h + r), is a convex function of its decay rate, so by Jensen's inequality an edge whose half-life is uncertain is worth more in expectation than one with the same average decay rate known for certain. That expected value, however, is carried by the chance of an unusually long life, and the realised value varies widely. A portfolio of edges diversifies this uncertainty in the same way that it diversifies returns.

Edges leak through identifiable channels: publication, the movement of staff between firms, counterparties who observe order flow and infer the logic behind it, and competitors who reverse-engineer a strategy from its returns or its holdings. Firms that keep their logic proprietary manage each channel. Research is compartmentalised so that no individual holds the whole; execution is spread across counterparties and varied in timing so that order flow reveals little; and what is published about performance is chosen so that it cannot be inverted into the rules that produced it. The last of these is the principle this series follows, and §04 sets out why.

The implication for a research firm is direct. The value of an edge lies less in its initial strength than in its durability, and durability is largely a matter of how many others come to know it. Protecting the half-life is one of the most valuable things a firm can do with an edge it has found, and it is the economic reason systematic firms keep their logic proprietary.

An edge is worth roughly its return multiplied by how long it lasts. Keeping the logic proprietary protects the second factor.

§02 · Capacity

The limits of capacity

Why an edge can support only so much capital, and why the best size keeps a third of it.

Even an edge that no one else knows has a limit. Trading moves prices against the trader, and the cost of that movement, market impact, grows with the size of the trade. An edge that earns a clear profit on a small position can be consumed entirely by impact on a large one. Capacity, the amount of capital an edge can support, is therefore a property of the edge and the market together, not of the edge alone.

The shape of market impact is one of the best-established regularities in market microstructure. Across markets and decades, the price impact of executing a large order grows approximately with the square root of its size relative to market volume, scaled by the asset's volatility. Almgren, Thum, Hauptmann and Li estimated it directly from institutional executions; Tóth and co-authors showed why it arises from the way liquidity is distributed around the price. Because impact per unit traded rises with size, total impact cost grows with size to the power of 1.5.

Combine that with an edge that earns α on each unit of capital, and the profit from deploying capital C is αC less a cost proportional to C^1.5. Profit rises at first, peaks and then falls, reaching zero where impact consumes the edge entirely. Setting the derivative to zero gives the optimum and a striking result: at the optimal size, impact takes exactly two thirds of the gross profit, and the net edge on each unit of capital is α/3.

03 · CAPACITY — The square-root law and the optimal size (screenshots 06, 07, 30)

P(C) = α C − η C^{3/2},      C* = (2α / 3η)²,      P(C*) / C* = α / 3

Takeaway: Gross profit grows in proportion to capital while impact cost grows with capital to the power 1.5. At the optimum, a third of the gross edge is kept.

Figure 2.1 builds the result. With a gross edge of 10% and impact scaled so that the optimum falls at 100 units of capital, gross profit there is 10.00 units, impact cost 6.67 and net profit 3.33. The square-root law is not the only model of impact, and the contrast is instructive. Kyle's 1985 model of an informed trader facing market makers implies impact linear in order size: prices move in proportion to the quantity traded, because market makers infer information from order flow. Empirically, large institutional orders move prices less than a linear law would predict, and the square root describes them better across a wide span of sizes. The difference matters for capacity, because under a linear law impact cost grows with the square of size and the optimum keeps half of the gross edge rather than a third.

Capacity can be stretched, within limits, by the way orders are executed. Spreading a large trade over time reduces impact but exposes the position to price movements while it is being built or unwound. Almgren and Chriss formalised this trade-off: the optimal execution schedule balances the expected cost of impact against the variance of the price path, and a more risk-averse trader executes faster at a higher expected cost. Execution is therefore part of an edge's economics, not an afterthought, and a strategy's true capacity depends on how patiently it can trade.

Turnover is the hidden variable in capacity. Impact is paid every time the portfolio trades, so a strategy that turns its positions over τ times a year pays impact τ times. With profit αC − τηC^1.5, the optimal capital falls with the square of turnover: doubling turnover cuts capacity to a quarter, and a strategy that trades monthly supports about 1/144 of the capital of one that trades annually. In the example of this chapter, capacity falls from 100 units at annual turnover to 25 at twice a year and about 0.69 at monthly turnover.

Impact is also not wholly permanent. Part of the price movement caused by a trade reverts as liquidity replenishes, and Bouchaud, Gefen, Potters and Wyart described this with propagator models in which the impact of each trade decays as a power of the time since it was made. Transient impact rewards patience: a strategy that trades slowly lets earlier impact decay before adding more, while one that trades quickly pays for its own recent trades. The economics of capacity therefore depend jointly on how large a strategy is, how often it trades and how its trades are spread in time.

Capacity is additive across independent markets in a way that edge is not. An edge that operates in many unrelated markets can deploy capital in each up to that market's own optimum, so total capacity grows with the number of markets while the impact in each stays within its own limit. This is breadth in the sense of Lesson 3, applied to capital rather than to information, and it is one reason why systematic strategies that span many markets can operate at a scale that a single-market strategy cannot.

Berk and Green turned the tension into a theory of the industry. In their model, investors allocate capital to managers with evident skill until diseconomies of scale, such as impact, compete the excess return away. Skill persists, but returns to investors do not, because capital flows to wherever returns are high. Capacity and fees, rather than an absence of skill, bring net performance back towards the market. The model explains why capable managers close strategies to new money, and why capacity is managed as carefully as the edge itself.

Capacity also shapes the case for keeping the logic proprietary. An edge known to others is not only shared; the combined capital trading it moves along the same impact curve, so every holder's costs rise together. A private edge is a private capacity. Disclosure adds other traders' capital to the same curve and pushes every holder past the optimum.

At the optimal size, impact takes two thirds of the gross profit. Every unit of someone else's capital on the same curve takes more.

§03 · Crowding

Correlated imitation

Why many similar strategies behave like a few, and why crowded trades unwind together.

Imitation rarely produces exact copies. Competitors who study the same markets with similar methods arrive at strategies that differ in detail but whose returns are highly correlated. Correlation changes the arithmetic of independence in two distinct ways, and both matter for how edges are evaluated and how they fail.

The first concerns diversification. The variance of an average of N strategies with pairwise correlation ρ equals that of N/(1 + (N − 1)ρ) independent strategies. With a hundred strategies at a correlation of 0.9, the effective number by this measure is only 1.11: a hundred strategies diversify almost no better than one. Adding correlated strategies adds capital, not independence.

The second concerns selection. When the best of many trials is chosen, what matters is the expected maximum, and for equally correlated normal trials it is the independent value scaled by √(1 − ρ). The best of a hundred independent trials is expected to lie about 2.51 standard deviations above the mean; at a correlation of 0.9 the expectation falls to about 0.79, the same as the best of about 2.8 independent trials. By this measure a hundred trials at 0.9 behave like about three.

Correlation among competitors also shapes how edges fail. When many funds hold similar positions they share not only returns but liquidity. If one must reduce its exposure, whether because of losses elsewhere or investor withdrawals, its selling moves prices against all the others, whose losses may force them to sell in turn. By the square-root law, when k equal holders liquidate at once each pays impact on the combined size, and each holder's cost rises by a factor of √k: 2.0 times for four holders, 3.16 times for ten. Crowded exits rarely stop at one round of selling. Brunnermeier and Pedersen showed how market liquidity and funding liquidity reinforce each other: losses tighten the funding available to leveraged traders, forcing sales that worsen market liquidity, which produces further losses and tighter funding. The result is a liquidity spiral, in which prices overshoot and correlations among otherwise unrelated positions rise sharply because the same holders are selling all of them.

Institutions respond by treating crowding as a measurable risk. Common indicators include the correlation of a strategy's returns with those of peers, the overlap of reported holdings, short interest and the cost of borrowing securities, and the behaviour of the strategy on days when similar strategies are known to have suffered. Stress tests model the forced liquidation of crowded positions at impact costs scaled for simultaneous selling, and liquidity-adjusted risk measures charge each position for the time and cost of exiting it. An edge that others do not hold is, among other things, an edge that does not require these defences.

Competition for the same edge can be solved exactly. Suppose k firms trade one edge against the same square-root impact curve, each choosing its own capital given the others'. This is Cournot competition, and its symmetric equilibrium has a closed form. As firms join, total capital rises towards zero-profit capital, at which impact consumes the edge entirely, and industry profit falls as 1/(2k + 1). With two firms the industry earns 86.4% of what a single firm would; with five, 50.7%; with ten, 29.2%, of which each firm earns about 2.9%; with twenty, 15.7%. Total capital with ten firms is already 204% of the single-firm optimum.

The equilibrium explains why imitation is so destructive. Each entrant earns something, so entry continues; but each also adds capital to the shared impact curve, so every incumbent's profit falls. The edge is not so much taken as dissipated: the return it generated is transferred, through impact, to the providers of liquidity on the other side of the trades.

Crowded exits share the structure of a bank run. Diamond and Dybvig showed that when depositors fear that others will withdraw, withdrawing first is individually rational even when staying would be collectively better. Holders of a crowded position face the same incentive: the first to sell pays the least impact, and the fear of others selling is itself a reason to sell. Institutions therefore watch for the conditions in which such runs become likely. Kritzman, Li, Page and Rigobon proposed the absorption ratio, the share of total variance explained by a small number of common factors, as a measure of how tightly markets are coupled; a rising absorption ratio signals that positions increasingly move together, and that an exit by some holders will be felt by all.

August 2007 made the mechanism concrete. In the second week of the month many quantitative equity strategies suffered sharp, simultaneous losses without any major market news. Khandani and Lo attributed the episode to the rapid unwinding of similar portfolios: liquidation by one or more large holders pushed prices against every fund with overlapping positions and triggered further de-risking. Strategies that had looked diversified across firms proved to be one crowded trade.

For a research firm, the lesson is that an edge's risk depends on who else holds it. A privately held edge carries its own impact and its own drawdowns; a widely held one carries everyone's. Keeping an edge proprietary protects not only its return but its independence, and with it the reliability of every risk estimate built on the assumption that the edge's behaviour is its own.

A crowded edge is not one edge held many times. It is one position, held together, that must be sold together.

§04 · Scale

The structural advantage of small capital

Why edges too small for institutions are the natural territory of the individual trader.

The economics of capacity have a consequence that is easy to miss when they are viewed from the side of a large firm. An institution does not merely prefer edges with large capacity; it cannot use any other kind. Every strategy it runs carries fixed costs of research, staff, data, infrastructure and compliance, and a strategy is worth running only if its net profit covers them. Combined with the result of §02, that at the optimal size an edge keeps a third of its gross return, this sets a precise lower limit on the capacity an institution can use.

If a strategy's fixed costs are F a year and its gross edge is α, the net profit at optimal capital C is αC/3, and the strategy covers its costs only if C is at least 3F/α. With fixed costs of $2 million a year and an edge of 10%, the minimum is $60 million. An edge that cannot absorb that much capital is, to the institution, economically invisible: whatever its quality, it cannot pay for itself. An individual trader, or a small firm managing less than five million dollars with near-zero marginal fixed costs, faces no such limit.

How much of the opportunity set does the limit exclude? Capacities are not evenly spread. Across many domains in finance, sizes follow power laws, with many small and few large; if the capacities of available edges follow a Pareto distribution with index near one, the share of edges able to absorb at least K falls in proportion to 1/K. On that assumption, a trader who needs only five million dollars of capacity can use about 20% of edges, a firm needing sixty million about 1.7%, and a firm needing five hundred million about 0.2%: the small trader has access to roughly 12 times as many edges as the first firm and 100 times as many as the second.

The excluded edges are also better protected. By §01, an edge's value depends on its half-life, and its half-life on how many others trade it. Edges that institutions cannot use attract less institutional capital, less published research and fewer imitators, so they tend to last longer. Size becomes a filter on competition, and the small trader operates on the side of the filter with fewer competitors.

Return on capital compounds the advantage. For an edge whose institutional optimum is one hundred million dollars, deploying five million moves the price so little that almost the entire gross edge is kept: the net return on capital is about 8.5%, against 3.33% at the optimum, roughly 2.6 times as much. Figure 4.3 shows the whole curve. The institution maximises total profit and accepts a lower return on each dollar; the small trader, who cannot deploy more, earns close to the gross edge on everything it deploys.

The binding constraint changes with scale. For an institution, capacity binds: it has more capital than its edges can absorb, and each additional dollar must search for a new edge large enough to take it. For a small trader, capacity rarely binds; breadth does. By the fundamental law of Lesson 3, the information ratio grows with the square root of the number of independent edges, and a trader with five million dollars can spread capital across many small edges, each deployed far below its own optimum, so that the portfolio earns close to the gross edge of each while diversifying across all of them. The constraint becomes the number of edges the trader can find, validate and run, which is why the research discipline of Lessons 1 and 2 matters more at small scale, not less.

Changes in market structure have widened this territory. Electronic trading, narrower spreads, lower commissions and smaller contract sizes have reduced the fixed and per-trade costs that once made small-scale systematic trading uneconomic, while regulatory position limits and reporting thresholds bind only at institutional size. As the costs of a small trader's strategy have fallen, so has the minimum capacity it needs, and the set of edges it can exploit profitably has grown accordingly.

Macro-finance explains why such opportunities persist. Shleifer and Vishny's limits to arbitrage showed that arbitrage is carried out by specialised, capital-constrained agents, so mispricings that are too small, too risky or too slow to correct for them can persist. Duffie's theory of slow-moving capital adds that capital does not flow instantly to opportunities: institutional mandates, approval processes and fund structures move money in months and years, not days. Pástor and Stambaugh showed that active management faces decreasing returns to scale across the whole industry, and Pástor, Stambaugh and Taylor found diseconomies both within funds and across the industry. Together these describe a market in which opportunities below institutional scale are structurally under-exploited.

Small capital has a further advantage that institutions cannot buy: it has no outside investors to redeem. Coval and Stafford documented how funds facing outflows are forced to sell holdings at depressed prices, and §03 showed how crowded exits behave like bank runs. A trader investing only their own capital is never forced to sell by someone else's withdrawal, and can hold through the episodes in which institutions are compelled to exit, or provide liquidity to them.

The advantage has limits, and they belong in the analysis. A small trader pays higher costs per trade in commissions and spreads, holds fewer independent positions, bears operational and data costs that are large relative to capital, and faces tighter constraints on leverage. Edges below institutional scale can also be crowded by other small traders, and the same economics of decay and imitation apply among them. The structural advantage is real, but it rewards the discipline of this series, validation before deployment, as much as any institution's.

An institution cannot use an edge that cannot pay for its fixed costs. Below that size lies territory that only small capital can reach.

§05 · Disclosure

Disclosure as a cost

What it costs to reveal an edge, and why some things can be shown and others cannot.

Every disclosure about a strategy is an economic decision. It may win the trust of investors, satisfy a regulator or advance a reputation, but it also transfers information to competitors, and information about an edge is the edge. The previous chapters supply the tools to price that transfer. An edge's value depends on its half-life; disclosure shortens the half-life by making replication easier; so disclosure has a cost that can be written down.

Model the edge of §01, earning α and halving every h years, and suppose that disclosing a fraction d of its logic adds a replication hazard γd to its natural rate of decay, where γ measures how easily a competitor who knows that much could reproduce it. The value of the edge falls from α/(ln 2/h + r) to α/(ln 2/h + γd + r). With α of 10%, a natural half-life of 5 years and a discount rate of 5%, the undisclosed edge is worth 53.0% of the capital it employs. With a moderate hazard, disclosing half the logic leaves 27% of that value and full disclosure 16%; where imitation is easy, half disclosure alone leaves 9%.

Figure 5.1 shows the shape of the cost. It is steepest at the start: the first disclosures, which reveal what kind of edge exists and where it operates, do most of the damage, because they tell competitors where to search. Later disclosures refine a search that has already been narrowed. This is why firms that keep their logic proprietary are careful even about seemingly harmless descriptions of what they do.

Disclosure need not be deliberate. A published return series can itself be inverted. Sharpe's returns-based style analysis regresses a fund's returns on the returns of candidate factors to infer its exposures, and Hasanhodzic and Lo showed that a substantial part of many hedge funds' returns can be replicated from common factors in this way. The precision of such a regression grows with the number of observations: the standard error of each estimated exposure falls with the square root of the sample size. Reporting weekly rather than daily returns inflates a reverse-engineer's standard errors by a factor of about 2.2, and monthly reporting by about 4.6. Figure 5.2 shows the effect; it is one reason the evidence in this series is presented as summary statistics and at coarse resolution.

It helps to think of disclosure as information in the strict sense. A competitor trying to reproduce an edge searches a space of candidate specifications, and every disclosure removes part of that space: naming the kind of market halves or quarters it, naming the horizon removes more, and each further detail narrows what remains. The proprietary content of an edge is what is left undetermined after everything published has been taken into account. This is why the first disclosures are the most expensive, as Figure 5.1 showed: they eliminate the largest share of candidates, and they tell the competitor that a search in that region is worth undertaking at all.

Regulation strikes the same balance explicitly. In the United States, institutional managers above a size threshold report their holdings of listed equities quarterly on Form 13F, within forty-five days of the end of each quarter, and may request confidential treatment for positions still being built. The delay and the quarterly resolution are deliberate: they give the public a view of what large managers own while limiting how far that view can be used to trade against them. The rules embody the principle of this chapter: transparency about outcomes and holdings at a resolution that does not transfer the edge.

If disclosure is costly, why do managers disclose anything? Milgrom's unravelling argument explains which disclosures happen voluntarily. When quality is verifiable and investors are sceptical, silence is interpreted as bad news: a manager who withholds results is assumed to have poor ones. Every manager above the worst therefore has an incentive to reveal, and in equilibrium performance is disclosed. The argument applies to outcomes, which can be verified and whose disclosure does not destroy their value. It does not apply to logic, whose disclosure destroys the edge, and whose absence investors therefore do not treat as a signal of low quality.

The result is a hierarchy of disclosure, shown in Figure 5.3. Outcomes, such as returns and drawdowns, can be published because they are verifiable and hard to invert at coarse resolution. Validation, the tests and statistics that establish whether the outcomes are reliable, can be published because it is about the outcomes, not the rules. Structure can be described by naming its layers without revealing what any layer does. Logic and parameters cannot be disclosed at all without transferring the edge. The value at risk rises steeply along the hierarchy, and the boundary this series observes lies between structure and logic.

The hierarchy also answers a natural objection: that an edge described only by its outcomes cannot be trusted. It can, if the outcomes are supported by validation of the kind that systematic research has developed for exactly this purpose. §06 sets out how evidence can be made convincing while the recipe stays proprietary.

Outcomes and validation can be public. Logic and parameters cannot be disclosed without transferring the edge.

§06 · Evidence

Evidence without the recipe

How outcomes can be made convincing while the logic stays proprietary.

If the logic cannot be disclosed, what can establish confidence? The answer is evidence about outcomes that is hard to fake and hard to misread: tests that could have failed, carried out in ways that could not have been tailored after the fact. Systematic research has developed a set of such tests, and this series presents them in sequence: significance corrected for the breadth of the search, stability across parameter choices, performance on data not used in development, and behaviour across simulated paths. Each is a statistic about outcomes. None reveals the rules.

Their force as evidence comes from independence. As Lesson 1 showed, independent tests multiply the odds of a real edge: from a prior of 4%, a test with a likelihood ratio of sixteen raises the probability to 40%, and a second independent test raises it to 91%. Weaker tests need several passes to reach the same conviction. Figure 6.1 shows the arithmetic. What matters is not the number of tests but how many examine distinct features of the record, which is why the validation of Module 4 is designed so that each lesson asks a different question.

Some evidence carries weight because of when it was produced. A test designed after the data have been seen can be tailored to them, the garden of forking paths of Lesson 1; a test declared in advance cannot. The strongest form is a holdout: data set aside at the start of research and examined once, at the end, by a rule fixed beforehand. Lesson 14 examines the Forecaster Portfolio's holdout. A forward record, accumulated after a strategy was fixed, is a holdout that grows with time.

Commitment can be made verifiable without disclosure. A cryptographic hash function maps any document to a short fingerprint from which the document cannot be recovered, and for which no different document with the same fingerprint can practically be found. A manager who publishes the fingerprint of a sealed specification today can later prove, by revealing the specification, that it was fixed before the outcomes on which it is judged; until then the fingerprint discloses nothing. A random nonce appended to the specification prevents anyone from confirming a guess about its contents. Science uses the same structure in pre-registration: the commitment is public, the content private, and the order of events provable.

Time does the rest. The standard error of an estimated Sharpe ratio, following Lo, is approximately the square root of (1 + S²/2)/T for a record of T years, so precision improves only with the square root of the record's length. For a strategy with a Sharpe ratio of 1, the 95% interval is about ±2.4 after one year, about ±1.4 after three and about ±0.76 after ten. Figure 6.3 shows the curve. Long, verified records carry weight not because they reveal more about the strategy but because they reveal more precisely what the strategy has done.

Outcomes themselves can be verified independently of the manager. Administrators calculate returns from positions held by custodians, auditors test the administrator's records, and brokers and exchanges confirm the trades. None of them needs to know why a position was taken in order to confirm that it was held and what it earned. The chain of verification separates the question of what happened, which can be answered publicly, from the question of why, which need not be.

Evidence must also be judged against what could have been shown. Allocators see the strategies that survived long enough to be presented, not the many that were tried and abandoned, so a record that clears the conventional threshold of statistical significance may simply be the best of a large, unseen population. Harvey, Liu and Zhu made the point for academic finance: after decades of searching for factors that explain returns, they argued that a new factor should clear a t-statistic of about 3 rather than the traditional 2. Lesson 12 applies the same correction to the Forecaster Portfolio, using the breadth of its own search.

Industry standards formalise the presentation of outcomes. The Global Investment Performance Standards, maintained by the CFA Institute, require firms to group comparable accounts into composites, to present their full performance history rather than selected periods, and to disclose how returns were calculated, with independent verification of compliance available to firms that seek it. The standards govern what is shown and how, never the logic that produced it, and they illustrate the principle of this chapter in institutional form.

At the research frontier, cryptography offers a stronger form of evidence still. A zero-knowledge proof allows one party to convince another that a statement is true without revealing anything beyond its truth: in principle, that a strategy used only information available at the time of each decision, or that it satisfies a stated risk limit, without disclosing the strategy itself. Such proofs are not yet part of institutional practice, but they express precisely the ideal toward which the methods of this chapter point: complete evidence about what a strategy does, and nothing about how.

Evidence can be public because it concerns outcomes. A test that could have failed, fixed before the data, carries weight without revealing the rules.
