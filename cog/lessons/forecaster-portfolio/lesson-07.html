Systems are not found; they are built, in versions, and each version is judged on the same research data as the last: the years 2022 to 2025, with 2026 held out untouched (Lesson 14). The Forecaster Portfolio reached its current form through five builds. Their Sharpe ratios, each measured over the whole record, rose from 2.25 to 3.51, with one step backwards, and the final build is the historical track record this series examines. Those five numbers are evidence of a research process; they are also the outcome of a selection, since the build kept is the build that did best.

This lesson reads the sequence both ways. It sets out what versioning means in systematic research, examines the five builds against the noise in each measurement, calculates how much choosing the best of several candidates flatters the winner, and shows why a disciplined research record keeps its failures. Nothing about what changed between builds is described; the subject is the shape of the evidence, not the contents of the system. The record's figures are shown before trading costs; a later costing module adds them.

§01 · Versions

Research as a sequence of versions

Why systematic strategies are built in builds, and what a build is evidence of. (screenshots 02, 03)

A systematic strategy is software, and like software it exists in versions. Each build is a complete specification, fixed at a date, run on the research data, and judged by the same measures as its predecessors. The discipline of versioning is what makes a research programme a record rather than a story: at any later time one can say exactly what the system was, what it produced, and what was changed to make the next build. It is also what allows the corrections of later lessons, because the breadth of a search can only be corrected for if the search was counted.

A build changes for a small number of reasons, and all of them recur across firms whatever the system does. A layer is added or replaced, in the sense of Lesson 3. A rule that worked in research proves fragile in simulation and is simplified. Costs, capacity or the handling of data, the subjects of Lessons 4 and 5, force a change in how the system trades rather than in what it believes. Or a build is reverted, because a change that looked like an improvement did not survive examination. The Forecaster Portfolio went through five such builds; what changed in each is proprietary, and this lesson treats the builds as the five results they produced.

The comparison between builds is always on the same data, the research years of 2022 to 2025, which is both the strength and the hazard of the method. The strength is that differences between builds are measured far more precisely than the standard error of either build alone would suggest, because the two see the same markets on the same days and most of the noise is common to both. The hazard is that every build sees the same past, so each one learns a little more about that particular history, and a sequence of improvements on the same data can be a sequence of better fits rather than better systems. Distinguishing the two is the work of Lessons 12 to 14.

The gain in precision from comparing on the same data can be written down. If two builds' daily results have correlation ρ, the standard error of the difference between their Sharpe ratios is the single-build standard error multiplied by √(2(1 − ρ)). For unrelated builds that is 0.59; for builds whose results are correlated 0.9, as successive versions of one system typically are, it is about 0.19. Figure 1.1 shows the curve. This is why a change of a few tenths in a Sharpe ratio can be a real finding between two versions of the same system and nothing at all between two unrelated strategies, and why the tests of §02 use the unrelated-build figure only as a conservative bound.

Institutional practice formalises the sequence. Each build has a specification and a version number; its results are generated by a fixed pipeline from fixed data, so that any result can be regenerated; the reasons for each change are recorded before the change is tested; and a build that is reverted stays in the record. The last point matters most for this lesson. A record that shows only the builds that improved is a survivorship record in the sense of Lesson 5, and it flatters in exactly the way that excluding failed strategies flatters a database. The Forecaster Portfolio's record keeps the build that fell, and §02 shows why that is the more informative sequence. (screenshots 04, 05)

A build is a complete specification fixed at a date. A sequence of builds is evidence only if every build, including the ones reverted, stays in the record. (screenshot 05)

§02 · Five builds

Five builds, one that fell

The sequence read against the noise in each number. (screenshot 06)

The five builds produced Sharpe ratios of 2.25, 2.24, 2.69, 2.87 and 3.51, in order. The last is the historical track record. Each figure measures its build over the whole record, holdout included, so that all five stand on the same 1,181 days; every comparison that chose between them was made on the research years alone, and the fifth build was carried unchanged into the holdout's single test. Figure 2.1 shows the sequence with the noise drawn around it: each build's Sharpe ratio carries a standard error of about 0.419, the figure established in Lesson 1, and the difference between two builds carries, at most, about 0.59. Read against that noise, the sequence says less than it appears to and more than a single number would.

Build 2 fell. Its Sharpe ratio of 2.24 was 0.01 below Build 1, a difference of about 0.02 standard errors, which is no difference at all; the change made in Build 2 neither improved nor damaged the system in any way the data could detect, and the build was superseded. The record keeps it because a research process that never goes backwards is one that is not being tested. Builds 3 and 4 rose by 0.45 and 0.18, each within the noise taken alone. Only the whole journey, from 2.25 to 3.51, a rise of 1.26 or about 2.1 standard errors, stands clearly outside what chance would produce if all five builds were equally good.

Even that statement is bounded in two directions. The standard error of a difference used here assumes independent builds, and because the builds share data it overstates the noise, so the journey is more significant than 2.1 standard errors suggests. Against that, the builds were made by researchers who saw the results of each before designing the next, so the sequence is not a set of independent experiments but a search, and §03 shows what searching does to the best result.

02 · COMPARISON — The standard error of a difference between builds (screenshots 06, 07, 20)

SE(Ŝ_a − Ŝ_b) ≈ √(SE_a² + SE_b²) = 0.419 √2 ≈ 0.59    (an upper bound: builds share data)

Takeaway: Two independent Sharpe ratios with standard error 0.419 differ by chance by about 0.59; builds that share data differ by less, so 0.59 is an upper bound on the noise.

Why show a fall at all? Because a research record is evidence only in proportion to what it could have revealed. A sequence that admits no reversals could not have shown a failure even if one had occurred, and so says nothing about whether the researchers were willing to find one. The single step backwards in the Forecaster Portfolio's sequence is small, but it is the point at which the record demonstrates that reversals were possible, and it is why the rises on either side of it can be read as results rather than as a narrative. (screenshot 08)

The sequence also contains a quantitative lesson about expectations. The mean Sharpe ratio across the five builds is 2.71. A reader who saw only Build 5 would take 3.51 as the system's quality; a reader who saw the whole sequence knows that the same research programme, applied to the same data, produced a spread of results, of which 3.51 is the best. That is the fact §03 prices.

Build 2 fell by 0.01, which is nothing; Build 5 rose 1.26 on Build 1, which is not. The record keeps both, because a sequence that cannot fall cannot be evidence. (screenshot 08)

§03 · Selection

Selection flattery

What choosing the best of several candidates adds to its measured result, whether or not the edge is real. (screenshot 09)

Lesson 2 introduced the expected best of N: when the best of several noisy estimates is chosen, its expected value exceeds the truth by the standard error multiplied by the expected maximum of N standard normal draws. The same arithmetic applies to builds. If five builds were independent draws of equal true quality, the best of them would be expected to exceed that quality by 1.16 standard errors, which at the record's standard error of 0.419 is about 0.49 of Sharpe. Figure 3.1 shows the curve: the flattery rises quickly with the first few candidates and slowly thereafter, reaching about 0.70 for the thirteen research families of Lesson 2.

This is the figure the series registers and uses. An earlier draft of the plan carried a larger number for the same quantity; it did not survive recomputation, and under the rule that every figure is recalculated before use it was replaced. The point is not the exact value but its logic: selection flatters by an amount that depends only on how many candidates were compared and how noisy each measurement was, and it flatters a worthless edge exactly as much as a real one.

03 · SELECTION — The expected flattery of the best of N (screenshots 09, 10, 21)

E[ max_{i≤N} Ŝ_i ] − S ≈ SE · E[ max_{i≤N} Z_i ]        (N = 5:  1.16 SE = 0.49)

Takeaway: Choosing the best of N independent candidates of equal quality inflates the winner by the standard error times the expected maximum of N normal draws: about 0.49 of Sharpe for five.

The five builds are not independent draws, and the difference matters in the direction of less flattery. Each build shares most of its logic and all of its data with its predecessor, so their results are highly correlated, and Lesson 4 showed that the expected maximum of correlated candidates is the independent value scaled by √(1 − ρ). Figure 3.2 shows the effect: at a correlation of 0.9 between builds the flattery of the best of five falls from 0.49 to about 0.15. Sequential improvement is a weaker search than five independent attempts, which is why a sequence of builds flatters less than a table of five unrelated strategies would. (screenshots 10, 11)

Against that, the sequence is only part of the search. Behind the five builds stand the thirteen research families of Lesson 2 and the parameter variants of Lesson 13, and the correction that matters for the record is the one that counts all of them. Lesson 12 applies it: the deflated Sharpe ratio asks whether 3.51 remains significant once the full breadth of the search is priced, and the answer there is yes by a wide margin. This chapter's contribution is the mechanism and its bound: among builds alone, selection could account for at most about 0.49 of the final 3.51, and in practice for less.

§04 · Stability

Stability as evidence

What a sequence of improvements looks like when it is learning rather than fitting. (screenshot 12)

A sequence of rising results can mean two things: a system that is being improved, or a system that is being fitted ever more closely to one history. The two are distinguished by what else moves. Figure 4.1 gives the first test, the probability that a rise of a given size would occur by chance between two builds of equal quality. A rise of 0.45, as from Build 2 to Build 3, occurs by chance about 22% of the time; a rise of 1.26, as from Build 1 to Build 5, about 1.7% of the time. Individual steps are weak evidence; the accumulation is strong. (screenshot 13)

The second test is whether improvements are broad or narrow. A change that improves the system in one market, one period or one parameter setting while leaving the rest untouched is the signature of fitting; a change that raises results across markets and periods together is the signature of a better system. Lesson 13 examines the Forecaster Portfolio's final build across 64 parameter variants and finds all 64 positive, with Sharpe ratios between 3.27 and 3.51: a plateau rather than a peak, which is what a broad improvement leaves behind.

The third test is the hardest and comes last: data the builds never saw. Lesson 14 examines the holdout, the period set aside before research began and examined once at the end, and Lesson 16 examines the forward record. A sequence of builds that improves in sample and holds out of sample is learning; one that improves in sample and fails out of sample was fitting. The five builds are the in-sample half of that comparison, and this lesson deliberately leaves the other half to the lessons that can supply it.

There is a subtler hazard in any sequence of builds judged on the same data, and it has a name in statistics: repeated significance testing on accumulating evidence. Armitage, McPherson and Rowe showed that if a hypothesis is tested again after each new look at the data, and research stops as soon as a test succeeds, the probability of a false positive rises well above the nominal size of each test, because the sequence offers many chances to succeed by luck. A programme that builds, tests and keeps whichever build first clears a threshold is running exactly that procedure. The remedies are the ones Lesson 4 described as evidence without the recipe: a holdout fixed before the sequence begins, which the sequence cannot look at, and a declared stopping rule. The Forecaster Portfolio's holdout is the subject of Lesson 14. (screenshot 14)

Bayesian reading makes the same point in a different form. Before the sequence, a reader's belief about the system's quality is spread widely; each build moves it a little, and the prior of Lesson 1, centred on a Sharpe ratio of 1.0, is pulled towards the measured values as the evidence accumulates. Five builds on the same data are not five independent pieces of evidence, but the shape of the sequence, rising, with one reversal, and consistent across variants, is the shape that learning produces and that fitting rarely does: fitting tends to rise without reversal and to collapse across variants.

One step up is weak evidence. A sequence that rises, reverses once, and holds across sixty-four variants is the shape of learning. (screenshot 14)

§05 · Corrections

The corrections ahead

Where each of this lesson's questions is answered with the record's own data. (screenshot 15)

This lesson has been about the shape of the evidence rather than its content, and it closes by mapping the shape onto the lessons that supply the content. The question of whether 3.51 is distinguishable from noise at all is Lesson 11, significance. The question of how much the search behind it flatters, of which this lesson's builds are one part, is Lesson 12, the deflated Sharpe ratio across the full breadth of the research programme. The question of whether the final build is a peak or a plateau is Lesson 13, which examines the 64 parameter variants. The question of data the builds never saw is Lesson 14, the holdout, and the question of how the structure behaves across other orderings of its own days is Lesson 15. Lesson 16 asks how quickly a forward record could detect decay in what the builds found.

Each of those lessons is a separate test of a distinct feature of the record, which is the design principle of Lesson 1: independent evidence compounds, and tests that probe the same feature do not. The five builds of this lesson are where several of those features were first set, and the reason the series records them as five results rather than as a single final number is that the later corrections need to know how many candidates there were. A record that reported only its best build could not be corrected for the others. (screenshot 16)
