Central Bank Policy Deep Dive

How the Fed decides, how it talks, what its dots and balance sheet really say, how the other big central banks differ, and why the tradeable object is always the gap between what the Fed signals and what the market has already priced.

COURSE NOTES — framework as taught, not a backtested strategy ~38 min read Prereqs: Lesson 2: What Moves Markets
Prefer pictures to prose? Try the Visual Guide — the same ground covered here, broken into short interactive slides.
TL;DR
  • Markets move on changes in expectations about the Fed, not on the decisions themselves. Edge, if there is any, is seeing where policy is heading before it is fully priced: what the Fed watches, how its members think, and what their words reveal.
  • The Fed has a dual mandate (maximum employment, price stability with a 2% PCE target), 12 FOMC voters out of 19 participants, and 8 scheduled meetings a year. A hawkish-leaning committee tightens faster and higher; dissents map the range of views.
  • Communication has a fixed rhythm: blackout, statement (2:00 pm ET), press conference (2:30 pm ET), minutes three weeks later. In the statement the change in wording is the signal, so diff it against the last one.
  • The dot plot is a set of conditional projections, not commitments. The tradeable thing is dots versus market pricing, read with the dispersion and the shift from the last SEP.
  • The balance sheet is liquidity: Net liquidity ≈ Fed balance sheet − TGA − RRP. QE adds reserves gradually; QT can drain them until something breaks suddenly (September 2019). Other central banks differ (ECB, BoJ, BoE, PBoC), and policy divergence drives FX.
  • Real-world episodes: the 2021-22 pivot from "transitory" to the first hike (16 March 2022) was telegraphed months earlier in the wording; the 17 September 2019 repo spike showed QT hitting scarce reserves; and 22 September 2022 showed policy divergence meeting a Japanese intervention. Course framework throughout; patience is the edge is not itself tested here.
Section 01

Why this matters

The Federal Reserve sets the price of dollar money, and almost every other price is quoted against it. Bond yields, the dollar, equity valuations and credit spreads all contain an assumed path for the Fed funds rate. So a trader does not really trade "the Fed decision". A trader trades the difference between the path the Fed is heading for and the path the market has priced.

Getting that difference right needs three things from this lesson: (1) the inputs the Fed watches, (2) how its members think, and (3) how their communication reveals intent. We then add the balance sheet (the second tool, which acts on liquidity), the other major central banks (because FX is a relative price), and a framework for trading around Fed events, including the honest option of sitting them out.

This is a course-notes lesson. The structure of the Fed and the mechanics of its balance sheet stay relevant across cycles. Specific stances, rates and committee compositions change, so verify current data before using any number here as a live input.

💡
Plain-English intuition

A central bank is a thermostat that talks before it acts

Think of the Fed as a thermostat for the economy. It reads the temperature (inflation, jobs), decides whether the room is too hot or too cold, and moves the dial (the policy rate, the balance sheet). The odd thing is that this thermostat announces where it plans to move the dial, months ahead, in careful coded language. Because everyone can hear the announcements, the market adjusts before the dial moves.

So the dial turning is rarely the news. The news is a change in the plan, a surprise in how fast, how far, or how sure the Fed sounds. That is why the lesson spends so long on language, projections and pricing, and only then on the tools themselves.

The dual mandate

The Fed answers to two goals set by Congress, and they sometimes pull in opposite directions:

  1. Maximum employment. The lowest sustainable unemployment that does not trigger inflation. The "natural rate" \(u^*\) is estimated, not fixed.
  2. Price stability. Since 2012 an explicit 2% inflation target, measured by PCE (not CPI). In 2020 the Fed shifted to average inflation targeting: inflation could run above 2% for a while to make up for earlier shortfalls. (As taught; the Fed has reviewed its framework since, so check the current statement of longer-run goals.)
🧭
Trading implication

Low unemployment and high inflation means there is no trade-off between the two mandates, so the Fed tightens aggressively. High unemployment and high inflation (stagflation) forces a painful choice, so watch which mandate the Chair prioritises.

Who votes: the FOMC composition

The hawk–dove spectrum

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine a class voting on how hot to keep the room. Some kids hate being cold (doves: they worry about people being out of work), some hate being too hot (hawks: they worry about prices rising). The teacher (the Chair) usually gets her way, but if lots of kids disagree she has to be careful. Counting who is hot-or-cold tells you what the room will probably do next.

Section 03

The FOMC communication cycle

Fed communication follows a fixed rhythm, and each piece has a different weight. In order of importance:

  1. FOMC statement — the official decision and forward guidance.
  2. Press conference — the Chair's interpretation; the Q&A reveals nuance.
  3. Minutes — detailed discussion, released three weeks later.
  4. Individual speeches — personal views that can signal future consensus.
  5. Interviews and Congressional testimony — often more candid.
The meeting cycle: drag the day to see what is public and what is not
Day relative to the decision (day 0 = Wednesday)Day 0
Decision day (Wed) — 2:00 pm ET statement, 2:30 pm ET press conference. Phase 1 is the algorithmic reaction; Phase 2 is human digestion.
The course notes put the start of the blackout 10-14 days before the meeting. The Fed's published rule is the second Saturday before the meeting begins, which for a Wednesday decision is 11 days earlier, so the band here starts at day −11 and ends at midnight after the meeting. Speeches just before it are the last chance to signal.
One cycle of eight a year. Quiet before, loud for ninety minutes, then a long digestion, then the minutes as a second, smaller event.

Reading the statement: the word changes are the signal

The statement is rewritten lightly each meeting, so the level of the language tells you little. The change versus the previous statement is the message. A good habit: paste the two statements side by side and read only the differences.

The press-conference edge

  • Prepared remarks (first 10-15 minutes): tone (confident or cautious) and emphasis reveal priorities. New phrases that are not in the statement are an important signal.
  • Q&A: less scripted. Hesitation or deflection, and repetition of a theme (strong conviction), are tells; "we'll see" and "it depends" mean genuine uncertainty.
The trap: the first market move on the press conference is largely algorithmic, scanning for keywords. It often reverses. Real positioning arrives over the following hours and days, as people digest the nuance.

Reading the minutes efficiently

  1. Skip to Participants' Views; the staff review is background.
  2. Look for the quantifiers. They run \(\text{a few} < \text{several} < \text{many} < \text{most} < \text{all}\), and they reveal how wide the consensus is.
  3. Find the "however" sentences. The clause after "however" usually reveals the true concern.
  4. Check the balance of risks: which scenarios were explicitly considered.
✅
FOMC meeting prep checklist
  1. Review the previous statement (the baseline language).
  2. Check market pricing (CME FedWatch gives the probability of each outcome).
  3. Read recent Fedspeak since the last meeting.
  4. Note data releases since the last meeting (CPI, NFP, PCE).
  5. Identify the key questions the market needs answered.
  6. Plan scenarios in advance: hawkish surprise → X; dovish surprise → Y; as expected → Z.
Section 04

The dot plot and rate expectations

Four times a year (March, June, September and December) the Fed publishes its Summary of Economic Projections (SEP), which includes the dot plot. Each dot is one participant's view of where the Fed funds rate should be at each year-end, plus a "longer run" dot, their estimate of the neutral rate. Voters and non-voters both submit dots.

Critical: dots are not forecasts and not promises. They are conditional projections: where rates should go if the economy evolves as that member expects. When the economy surprises, the dots move.

How the market's side of the comparison is built

To compare dots with the market you need the market's number. Fed funds futures give it directly: the contract settles to the average effective Fed funds rate over its month, so its price encodes the expected rate.

Watch the formula build
From a futures price to the probability of a move, one step at a time
\[ r_{\text{implied}} = \color{#fbbf24}{100 - P} \]

The futures price \(P\) is quoted as 100 minus the rate. A contract at 95.50 is pricing an average rate of 4.50%.

\[ \Delta r = r_{\text{implied}} \color{#fbbf24}{- r_{\text{now}}} \]

Subtract the rate today. The difference is the average change the market expects by that month.

\[ \text{prob} = \color{#10b981}{\frac{\Delta r}{s}} \]

Divide by the usual move size \(s\) (25 basis points). That gives a rough probability of one move; above 100% means more than one move is priced. This is a simplification: a meeting mid-month needs day-weighting, and the sign says hike or cut.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

A futures price is a bet on the future interest rate written backwards: price 95.5 means "the crowd expects the rate to be 4.5". Compare that with today's rate and you can read off how likely the crowd thinks a change is. If today is 4.33 and the crowd says 4.50, they think a quarter-point hike is more likely than not.

Three tools show market pricing, from simple to professional: the free CME FedWatch tool (probabilities per meeting), Fed funds futures (implied rate = 100 − price), and OIS (overnight index swaps), more liquid for longer horizons.

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Grown-ups at the Fed use special code words so they can warn you what is coming without promising it. "Inflation remains elevated" is like a parent saying "we'll see about that ice cream" in a firm voice: you can tell the answer is probably no. Learn the code words and you can hear the answer before it is spoken.

Language shifts precede policy shifts

It actually happened

From "transitory" to the first hike, 2021-22

By mid-2021 US inflation was running well above 2%, and for months the Fed described it as transitory. Then the wording started to move. On 30 November 2021 Chair Powell told the Senate Banking Committee it was "probably a good time to retire that word". At the 15 December 2021 meeting the statement no longer called pandemic-related price pressures temporary: it said supply and demand imbalances "have continued to contribute to elevated levels of inflation", the Committee sped up its bond-purchase taper, and the median dot showed hikes in 2022. On 26 January 2022 the statement said the Committee "expects it will soon be appropriate to raise the target range". On 16 March 2022 it raised the range to 0.25-0.50%, the first hike since 2018, and said it anticipated "ongoing increases".

The course lesson drawn from the sequence: the word change was the signal, months before the hike. The reaction function did not change on the day of the hike. The language had already moved.

Desk test: nothing found The statement is priced in half an hour. Tested on 82 FOMC meetings: the first-30-minute reaction agreed in sign with the next-day dollar move only 46% of the time (t = 0.32), and the statement's change in hawkishness did not predict the next-day move (t = −0.75), though it was priced inside 30 minutes (t = 2.04). So "language precedes action" describes months, not the day after: do not carry the statement's tone forward as a next-day lean.

Trial balloons

A trial balloon is a deliberate test of the market's reaction. A non-voting member or lesser voice floats an idea, or a "leak" goes to a well-connected journalist (the course names Nick Timiraos of the WSJ). The Fed watches the reaction: if it is acceptable the idea is adopted more broadly, and if it is severe it is walked back as "one person's view".

  • Watch for a lesser-known official suddenly getting attention.
  • Watch for WSJ stories citing unnamed "Fed officials".

Section 06

The Fed's balance sheet: QE and QT mechanics

The second tool is the balance sheet. It grew from about $900 billion before 2008 to nearly $9 trillion at the 2022 peak. The core mechanism: the Fed buys assets and pays with newly created reserves. The reserves enter the banking system, there is more liquidity, and asset prices tend to rise. QT (quantitative tightening) reverses the process.

Balance sheet anatomy

  • Assets (what the Fed owns): Treasuries about 60-70%; mortgage-backed securities (MBS) about 25-30%; loans and other about 5%. Growing assets = QE = liquidity injection = bullish for risk assets.
  • Liabilities (what the Fed owes): currency in circulation about 25%; bank reserves about 35-40%; reverse repo (RRP) about 20-25%; Treasury General Account (TGA) about 5-10%. Reserves + RRP + TGA are the "liquidity" that can flow to markets.

How QE and QT transmit

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

QE is the Fed using a magic printing pen to buy bonds from banks and handing them fresh digital cash, which then goes looking for something to buy. QT is the opposite: the Fed lets its bonds expire and the cash disappears. It is easy to pour water into a bath and hard to know how little water you can leave before the pipes start to bang. The Fed finds that out by hitting it.

It actually happened

The pipes bang: the repo spike of September 2019

The Fed had been running down its balance sheet, and on 31 July 2019 it decided to end the runoff in August, two months earlier than planned. Six weeks later, on 17 September 2019, overnight lending rates in the repo market spiked far above the Fed's target range, and the New York Fed stepped in with repo operations to add reserves. On 11 October 2019 the Fed announced that it would buy Treasury bills "to maintain over time ample reserve balances" and run repo operations at least into the following year. The Fed said these were reserve-management measures, not a new round of stimulus.

The lesson the course draws: QE effects are gradual, QT can cause sudden stress when reserves get scarce, and the Fed often does not know where the pain point is until it hits it. It lists March 2023 bank stress as a second instance. A caveat on the numbers: the course quotes "below about $3 trillion of reserves has historically caused stress", but in September 2019 reserves were far lower, roughly $1.4 trillion, so the threshold moves with the demand for reserves. Treat $3 trillion as a post-2020 rule of thumb, not a law.

The net liquidity equation

The headline balance sheet can mislead, because the Treasury's checking account (the TGA) and the money-market funds' overnight parking spot (the RRP) both sit on the liability side. Money in either is out of the banking system. So the course tracks a simple composite:

Worked example (illustrative numbers, in $ trillion)

Net liquidity can rise during QT

Week 1: balance sheet 6.60, TGA 0.80, RRP 0.40, so net liquidity is \(6.60 - 0.80 - 0.40 = 5.40\). Week 2: the Fed runs off another 0.05 (balance sheet 6.55), the Treasury spends 0.10 of its account (TGA 0.70), and money funds move 0.10 out of the RRP back into markets (RRP 0.30).

\[ \text{NL}_2 = 6.55 - 0.70 - 0.30 = 5.55 \qquad \Delta\text{NL} = 5.55 - 5.40 = +0.15 \]

How fast does QT drain reserves? A live model

A runoff cap sets how many dollars leave the balance sheet each month. Where the cash comes from decides the pain. If money-market funds have a big RRP buffer, they absorb part of the drain by moving cash out of the RRP, and reserves fall less. Once the buffer is empty, every dollar of runoff comes straight out of bank reserves.

Slide the cap up and the line is reached sooner. Slide the RRP share up and the same cap hurts reserves less, until the buffer runs out. This is the mechanism behind "gradually, then suddenly".

Balance sheet monitoring

  • H.4.1 release: every Thursday at 4:30 pm ET, the Fed's weekly balance sheet and the primary source. Track the week-over-week trend, not the level.
  • Reserves: the course says below about $3 trillion has historically caused stress (see the caveat in the 2019 story).
  • TGA: watch quarterly refunding plans. Big TGA buildups (debt ceiling, heavy issuance) drain liquidity.
  • RRP: high means excess cash parked; draining means cash that can support markets.

A related composite (Fed net-liquidity momentum with the yield curve and credit stress) has been tested as a direction signal; see the ledger entries for the macro-confluence tests before quoting any liquidity lean.
How to test it: take weekly H.4.1 data (balance sheet, TGA, RRP), build the net liquidity change using only values published by each Thursday, and compare the next-week return of the S&P 500 and the dollar when net liquidity rose versus fell. Fix the horizon and the sign in advance, require a placebo from shuffled weeks, and report both halves of the sample; any edge must survive that before it is worth sizing.

Section 07

Other major central banks and policy divergence

FX is a relative price, so the Fed alone is never enough. A currency pair moves on the difference between two central banks' paths. Here is the course's one-line profile of each, followed by how divergence drives FX.

ECBSingle mandate (price stability). Must manage fragmentation risk across about 20 economies (the euro area has grown over time).
BoJSignature policy as taught: Yield Curve Control (YCC), which targets the 10-year yield in a band around 0%, unique among major central banks. Decades fighting deflation, so structurally dovish: they want inflation. Owns about half of JGBs and is a major holder of Japanese equities through ETFs. 8 meetings a year but could adjust YCC at any time. The wildcard, most likely to surprise.
BoEFaces stagflation risk: high inflation plus weak growth limits its options.
PBoCDifferent toolkit: reserve requirement ratios (RRR), administered rates and FX management. Moves often outside scheduled meetings. China stimulus matters globally.
⚠️
A dated claim

YCC is history; the lesson is the mechanism

The course describes the BoJ's YCC as live. The BoJ ended negative rates and YCC in March 2024, so read the BoJ paragraph as a description of the mechanism and of the 2016-24 regime, then check the current policy. What the course keeps: BoJ moves cause extreme yen moves, a widening of the YCC band was a de facto tightening, and BoJ policy surprises were the most likely shock among the majors. The Fed, ECB and BoE rates and ranges below are dated facts from the decisions themselves.

Watch for four BoJ signals: YCC band changes (widening = de facto tightening), FX intervention (by the BoJ or Ministry of Finance), the governor's commentary on inflation expectations, and surprises generally. A removal of YCC would be seismic globally.

  1. Keep a central bank meeting calendar; dates cluster into event-heavy weeks.
  2. For each bank note the current rate, direction of travel, and relative hawkishness.

  4. Expect surprises mainly from the BoJ and off-schedule PBoC moves.
Section 08

Trading around Fed events

The core question: trade or avoid? Fed events are high volatility, not necessarily high edge. Before trading an FOMC, ask: do I have a view that differs from market pricing? Can I size for the volatility? Is this a real edge, or a gamble on a binary outcome? Sitting out is valid. Some of the best traders reduce exposure before meetings and re-engage afterwards.

Pre-FOMC dynamics

  • Week before: volatility compresses ("the calm before the storm").
  • 1-2 days before: light positioning; the market sits on its hands.
  • Morning of: low-conviction moves; everyone waits for 2:00 pm.
  • Pre-FOMC drift: a documented slight positive equity bias in the 24 hours before announcements. Well known, probably mostly arbitraged away, and it persists only weakly.
  • Positioning: reduce gross exposure if you have no edge; if you position for a surprise, size smaller than normal; options strategies can benefit from the volatility expansion.

FOMC trading checklist

Before: check market pricing (what would surprise?); decide trade-or-sit-out; define scenarios and sizes in advance; set alerts for 2:00 and 2:30 pm ET.

During and after: do not react to the Phase 1 spike; compare the outcome to your pre-defined scenarios; check cross-asset confirmation; consider waiting until Day 2-3 for a cleaner entry; calendar the minutes release three weeks out.

Desk data: base rate "The first move reverses" has the same base rate as any big day. On 84 FOMC days (2016 onwards), the share of day-0 moves that gave back at least half within 20 sessions was the same as for non-FOMC days with a move of equal size: 2-year 38% vs 39%, 10-year 50% vs 45%, dollar 48% vs 40%, S&P 500 45% vs 51%. The reference class that matters is "big days", not "Fed days", so the patience advice above is a course framework, not something this result supports on its own.
Desk test: held up A base rate for the days after a meeting. Over 81 FOMC meetings, the dollar basket averaged +26bp over the five sessions after the meeting, positive 65% of the time, against −3bp and 49% in non-FOMC windows. It sat at the 99.8th percentile of 10,000 placebos and held in both halves (2016-20 and 2021-26). It is a calendar base rate, never a call on a single meeting, and it is not forward-proven.

Section 09

How practitioners actually use this

Central-bank watching is a daily discipline at macro desks, but it is mostly preparation, not prediction. Practitioners keep the scorecard, the statement diff, the SEP comparison and the speech log so that when a meeting arrives they already know which outcomes would surprise the market, and what they would do in each case. The value is in having scenarios and sizes decided before the 2:00 pm release, not in guessing the decision.

The market's number is the benchmark. A professional compares what the Fed seems to be signalling with what futures, OIS and FedWatch show, and treats only the gap as information. The balance sheet gets a weekly check (H.4.1 each Thursday), and the other central banks get a calendar and a relative-hawkishness note, because FX moves on differences.

⚠️
Common pitfalls

Where central-bank reading quietly goes wrong

Treating the dots as promises. They are conditional projections from up to 19 people, and they have been wrong by large margins. Use the shift from the last SEP and the gap to market pricing, not the level.

Reading the statement without the previous one. The level of the language tells you almost nothing. Only the diff does. And statement tone is priced very fast, so a tone read is not a next-day lean.

Trading the first spike. Phase 1 is algorithmic and often wrong. Wait for the press conference and, ideally, for Day 2-3.

Reading the balance sheet headline. Total assets can fall while net liquidity rises, because of the TGA and RRP. Track the composite.

Using stale structure. Committee membership, the Chair, the framework, and even whole regimes (the BoJ's YCC) change. The mechanics last; the specific stances do not. Verify current data.

Believing that understanding the Fed is an edge by itself. Everyone watches the Fed. Understanding it is the entry ticket; the edge, if any, is in a differentiated view of the gap, in sizing, or in patience.

Scenario drill · 22 September 2022

The Fed hiked 75bp yesterday, and the BoJ has just held

You have been long USD/JPY on the policy-divergence theme since the spring: the Fed is hiking fast, and the Bank of Japan keeps its policy rate and its 10-year yield control unchanged. Yesterday the Fed raised its target range by 75bp to 3.00-3.25% and signalled "ongoing increases". Today the Bank of Japan has just left policy unchanged, and USD/JPY has jumped to about 146, the highest in 24 years. Japan's Ministry of Finance has been warning about the yen's moves. What do you do with the position?

  1. Add. The divergence just widened again, so the trend is your friend.
    The thesis is intact, but the risk is not symmetric. The course calls the BoJ and Japanese authorities the wildcard; a government that has been warning about the currency can move it by several yen in minutes, and adding size at the 24-year high is the largest exposure to exactly that tail.
  2. Keep the thesis, cut the size, and plan for a sharp one-way gap against you.
    Divergence describes the direction of travel but not the path. A smaller position that survives an intervention gap lets you stay in a thesis you still believe. This matches the course's "size for the volatility" and "sitting out is valid" rules for event risk.
  3. Reverse to short: intervention is obviously coming, so fade the move.
    Fading a trend because a government might act is a timing bet against the policy gap, with no trigger. The pair can be pushed down on the day and still go back up if the divergence is unchanged, so a full reversal risks being right about the event and wrong about the aftermath.
  4. Hold at the same size and wait for the Bank of Japan's next meeting.
    Reasonable if you already sized for a tail like this, but nothing in the setup tells you that the next BoJ meeting is when the risk lands. The Ministry of Finance does not wait for meetings.

What happened. Later on 22 September, Japan's Ministry of Finance intervened to buy yen, for the first time since 1998, and USD/JPY fell by about five yen, from roughly 146 to roughly 140, in a short burst. The pair then drifted back up as the policy gap persisted, reaching about 152 in October, when Japan intervened again. In December 2022 the Bank of Japan widened the band around its 10-year yield target, a de facto tightening, and the yen jumped by more than 3% in a day. The policy divergence was real and persistent, and it came with a policy-driven path risk the textbook framework does not price.

Sources: Federal Reserve, FOMC statement, 21 September 2022; Ministry of Finance, Japan, foreign exchange intervention operations. Yen levels are rounded; the Ministry's data gives the exact intervention amounts.

Section 10

Key Takeaways

✓Know the players. Committee composition, hawk/dove leanings and dissents matter. The Chair dominates, but a unified committee is more predictable than a divided one.
✓Language precedes action. Pivots are telegraphed through word changes ("transitory" to "elevated" to "soon be appropriate to raise"). Diff the statements: the diff is the signal.
✓Dots are not destiny. They are conditional projections, not commitments. The tradeable object is dots versus market pricing, read with the dispersion and the shift versus the last SEP.
✓Balance sheet = liquidity. Track net liquidity (balance sheet − TGA − RRP) weekly via H.4.1. Rising = risk tailwind; falling = headwind. QT can cause sudden stress when reserves get scarce.
✓Divergence drives FX. Monitor relative policy paths; the BoJ is the wildcard.
✓Patience is edge. First moves often reverse; wait for the press conference, check cross-asset confirmation, consider waiting days.
⚠These are framework claims, not validated rules. The statement is priced within half an hour, and "the first move reverses" has the same base rate as any big day. Stances, members and even regimes (YCC) change, so verify current data. Only the post-meeting dollar base rate has survived this desk's tests.
✗Do not trade the first spike or treat the dots as promises. Phase 1 is algorithmic and often wrong, and the dots have missed by hundreds of basis points.
Section 11

Self-test

Q1. A futures contract trades at 95.20 and the current effective rate is 4.58%. What does it say about the next meeting?
The implied rate is \(100 - 95.20 = 4.80\%\), which is 22bp above the current rate. Divided by a 25bp move that is 0.88, so the market prices a hike with roughly 88% probability. It is a simplified read (it ignores day-weighting inside the month), but it is the right order of magnitude, and it is the market's number you compare with the dot plot's median.
Q2. Why is the dot plot "not a forecast", and how should you use it instead?
Each dot is a conditional projection: where that participant thinks rates should be if the economy evolves as they expect. When the economy surprises, the dots move. The 2020-21 dots showed rates near zero for years, and rates reached 4.25-4.50% by the end of 2022. So use the shift versus the previous SEP, the dispersion, and above all the gap to market pricing.
Q3. The Fed's headline balance sheet falls during QT, yet net liquidity rises. How?
Net liquidity is balance sheet minus TGA minus RRP. If the Treasury spends down its TGA, or money-market funds move cash out of the RRP, those reduce the subtracted terms and add liquidity to markets. In the worked example the balance sheet fell by 0.05 while the TGA fell by 0.10 and the RRP by 0.10, so net liquidity rose by 0.15.
Q4. Why did the 17 September 2019 repo spike come as a surprise, and what does it teach about QT?
QE's effects build gradually, but QT can cause sudden stress when reserves become scarce, and the Fed often does not know where the pain point is until it hits it. In September 2019 the runoff had already been brought to an end (in August), yet overnight repo rates spiked well above the target range; the Fed added reserves through repo operations and bill purchases. The point: the threshold for "scarce" moves with the demand for reserves, so a single number such as $3 trillion is a rule of thumb, not a law.
Q5. The Fed hikes and the BoJ holds. What does the policy-divergence framework say, and what does the 22 September 2022 episode add?
The framework says USD/JPY rises as the rate differential widens, and in 2022 it did (115 to 150+). The episode adds path risk: the BoJ is the wildcard, and Japan's Ministry of Finance intervened on 22 September 2022 for the first time since 1998, pushing the pair down about five yen in a short burst before the underlying gap pulled it back up. Divergence gives direction, not a smooth path, so size for event risk.
Q6. When is it better to fade an FOMC move, and when to follow it?
Follow when the outcome truly surprised against pricing, the wording shifted materially, stocks, bonds and FX all confirm, the move survives the press conference and the macro backdrop supports it. Fade when the outcome was roughly as expected but the market overreacted, the move reversed in the press conference, cross-asset confirmation is missing, positioning was already extreme, or technicals show exhaustion. If neither set applies, sitting out is a valid decision.
