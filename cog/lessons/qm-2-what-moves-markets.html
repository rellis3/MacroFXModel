What Moves Markets, Part 1: The Flow Hierarchy and Rates

Who moves first and who inherits the lag: the chain from central banks down to credit, and how interest-rate gaps pull capital across borders, power the carry trade and set up its violent unwind. Part 1 of 2 of Lesson 2.

TL;DR
  • Policy shifts cascade through markets in a predictable order: central banks, then government bonds (hours to days), G10 currencies (days to weeks), equities (weeks to months) and corporate credit (months to quarters). Each step down adds lag and noise, and most retail trading sits at the bottom two rungs.
  • Interest rates are the price of money and capital chases the best risk-adjusted return. That is why a rate gap pulls on a currency, and why the carry trade that harvests the gap earns small steady returns and then loses years of them in days when it unwinds. A 10% yen rally against a 4.9% carry is about two years of carry gone.
  • Part 2 picks up from here: risk-on and risk-off regimes, central-bank liquidity, the institutional flow calendar and positioning data.
Section 01

Why this matters

This lesson, in two parts, names the forces that push prices, in the order they act. Most medium-term moves are the late echo of one of six things: a central-bank decision, an interest-rate gap, a switch of risk appetite, a change in the amount of money in the system, a calendar-driven institutional flow, or a crowded position being forced out. None is secret. The skill is knowing which is in play and where in the queue your market sits. Part 1 covers the first two (who moves first, and why rate gaps move money); part 2 covers the other four.

The lesson also keeps score on honesty. The course states its figures as exemplars and its relationships as frameworks, and this page keeps that status visible: it marks what is the course's claim and what is this page's own arithmetic. A few of the relationships, such as bond yields leading equities or net liquidity as a tailwind, can be tested and some have been; where a result exists the verdict box says so, and where none exists it says "not tested here".

💡
Plain-English intuition

Watch the weather map, not just the window

Central banks are the pressure systems. If you only watch stocks, you are looking out of the window at rain that was forecast days ago. Someone watching the weather map saw the front coming. The course's version: policy cascades through asset classes like dominoes, and most traders only see the last domino fall without noticing what tipped the first.

By the time policy reaches the stock you are watching, the people who read the bond market have been positioned for months. That is the retail disadvantage in one sentence: trading effects while institutions trade causes.

Section 02

The capital-flow hierarchy: who moves first

The course ranks five asset classes by how early they feel a policy change and how clean the information is there. The ranking is the lesson: before any position, check the levels above you.

Watch the formula build
A teaching model of the cascade: how much of the repricing has happened by day t
\[ \color{#fbbf24}{G(t)} = 1 - C(t) \]

Define the gap \(G(t)\): the share of the final repricing that has not happened yet. Right after the shock the gap is 1 (nothing priced) and in the end it is 0 (everything priced). \(C(t)\) is the share already done.

\[ \frac{dG}{dt} = \color{#fbbf24}{-\frac{G}{\tau}} \]

Assume each level closes a constant fraction of its remaining gap per unit of time. \(\tau\) is that level's time constant: small for bonds, large for credit. This is a modelling choice for teaching; the course gives lag ranges, not curves.

\[ C(t) = \color{#10b981}{1 - e^{-t/\tau}} \]

Solving gives the share priced after \(t\) days. After one time constant about 63% is done; after \(\tau\ln 10 \approx 2.3\,\tau\) days about 90% is.

C(t)share of the final repricing already done after t days (0 to 1)
τtime constant in days: how slowly this level catches up
e^(−t/τ)the part still to come; it shrinks by the same fraction every τ days

🧒 Explain like I'm 5

Drop a stone in a pond. The water right next to the stone moves first and moves a lot. Ripples reach the far bank later and smaller, mixed up with wind and ducks. The central bank is the stone, bonds are the water next to it, and the stocks you are watching are the far bank. If you only watch the far bank, you see the ripple after everyone near the stone already moved.

What the hierarchy implies, quantitatively

  • Lead-lag. The course says bond yields tend to lead equity returns by 3 to 6 months in rate-driven regimes (its exemplar is the 2022 hiking cycle), and that rate differentials lead FX moves by 2 to 8 weeks on average.
  • Regimes. During regime changes macro factors can explain the majority of cross-asset variance; in normal conditions idiosyncratic factors dominate more.
  • Signal-to-noise. The signal-to-noise ratio at the bond and rates level is materially higher than at the equity level for medium-term moves.
  • Crowding. The lower the level, the more participants, the more crowded the information and the smaller the edge.

Worked example

Rate-hike transmission, step by step

  1. The signal. The Fed announces +0.25%.
  2. Bonds, within hours. The 2-year yield jumps and bond prices fall. In the model above bonds are about 100% repriced by day 30.
  3. Currencies, days to weeks. Money flows into the dollar and it strengthens (95% repriced at day 30).
  4. Stocks, weeks to months. Higher borrowing costs and profits that are worth less today; growth stocks are hit hardest (39% at day 30).
  5. Credit, months. Refinancing costs rise, riskier bonds fall and defaults may increase (18% at day 30).

The percentages are this page's illustration of the order, not measured values.

Do this (as taught): before any position, check the levels above you. Trading equities, look at bond yields, curve slope and credit spreads first. Trading FX, look at rate expectations through Fed Funds futures, 2-year differentials and forward guidance. Trading anything, ask what the Fed, ECB or BoJ is doing and how it flows through to your asset class.

Section 03

Interest rates and capital flows

Interest rates are the price of money, and money flows to the highest risk-adjusted return. The core intuition: $1M earns 5% in country A and 1% in country B, so it flows to A. Multiply that by thousands of investors and the flow is what moves a currency. It explains the majority of medium-term FX and cross-border allocation moves in the course's account.

The carry trade

Borrow in a low-yield currency, invest in a high-yield one and pocket the difference: borrow yen at 0.1%, deposit dollars at 5%, and you collect about 4.9% a year as long as the exchange rate does not move against you. The course's decomposition: in calm periods the carry is roughly 80% of the return and spot moves roughly 20%, but during unwinds the volatility drag is negative and can exceed the carry.

Watch the formula build
The carry trade's return over a year, one term at a time
\[ \text{carry} = \color{#fbbf24}{i_H - i_F} \]

The headline: the high-yield rate \(i_H\) you earn minus the low-yield funding rate \(i_F\) you pay. At 5% and 0.1% that is 4.9%.

\[ R = \frac{1 + i_H}{\color{#fbbf24}{1 + x}} - (1 + i_F) \]

Now let the exchange rate move. If the funding currency strengthens by \(x\) against the high-yield one, each unit of funding currency you owe costs more of the high-yield currency to repay, so your deposit buys back less.

\[ R \approx (i_H - i_F) \;\color{#fbbf24}{-\; x} \quad\text{for small } x \]

For small moves this is carry minus the funding currency's rally. The approximation gets rough for big moves, which is exactly when it matters, so the calculator below uses the exact form.

\[ x^{*} = \color{#10b981}{\frac{1 + i_H}{1 + i_F} - 1} \]

The break-even: the funding-currency rally \(x^{*}\) that makes \(R = 0\). At 5% and 0.1% it is 4.90%. A rally bigger than that turns a year of carry into a loss.

i_H, i_Fannual rate in the high-yield currency (earned) and the funding currency (paid)
xappreciation of the funding currency against the high-yield one over the year (positive hurts the carry trader)
Rone-year return on the unlevered carry position, as a share of the amount borrowed

Predict first. With a 5% dollar rate and a 0.1% yen rate the carry is 4.9% a year. If the yen rallies 10% against the dollar in a few days, about how many years of that carry does the move erase? Lock in a guess, then set the yen move slider to +10%.

About 2 years. A 10% move divided by a 4.9% yearly carry is 2.04 years, which is why the course calls it picking up pennies in front of a steamroller. The exact one-year return at +10% is \(1.05/1.10 - 1.001 = -4.65\%\): the carry is gone and then some. Try +5% (close to break-even) and −10% (the yen weakens and the trade pays double).

Gold nuance

Gold follows real rates, not nominal ones

Gold responds primarily to real rates, the nominal rate minus inflation. If inflation rises faster than nominal rates, real rates fall and gold can rally even while the Fed hikes. So watch TIPS yields, not only nominal ones.

Case study — the 2022 rate shock

The hierarchy transmitting in real time

The Fed pivoted hawkish in late 2021 and then ran its fastest hiking cycle in 40 years. The course walks the cascade down its levels. The figures are the course's, rounded, and are one cycle, not a statistical result.

The course's lesson: those watching Level 2 (bonds) exited growth stocks 3 to 6 months before the bottom fell out, while those watching headlines bought the dip all the way down. That is hindsight on one cycle, the best case for the framework. It is also the case the framework was shaped by.

Desk test: nothing found A yield move does not tell you where the pair or index goes next. The forward coupling is null on this desk's data; the relationship is real only in the same bar. Rates and FX move together; one does not lead the other on the horizons tested. The course's "differentials lead FX by 2 to 8 weeks" is a longer horizon than the daily test covers.
Desk data: base rate The carry-unwind shape (yen firming while US yields rise). 35 episodes in 18 years, too few to score. USD/JPY was higher five sessions later 47% of the time, EUR/JPY 62% and AUD/JPY 47%. The shape can be described; nothing can be claimed about the week after.
Desk test: nothing found "2Y–10Y inversion is a recession signal." Over fifty years the inversion has ten independent episodes. A recession followed within 24 months in 6 of 10 (60%, 95% interval 31% to 83%), so four of ten had none, and the forward Nasdaq did not separate from a matched control at any horizon. The folk version reverses the conditional: most recessions follow inversions, but most inversions are not a call on the next two years.
Section 04

Key Takeaways

✓The hierarchy is not optional. Central banks, bonds, FX, equities, credit: information degrades at each level, so check the levels above before any position.
✓Rates drive the cross-border flow. Differentials explain most of the medium-term FX moves in the course's account; the 2-year yield is the one to watch. A carry trade earns a little steadily and gives it back in a rush: a 10% yen rally against a 4.9% carry is about two years of carry.
⚠The figures are exemplars. The lead-lags, the 0.7 to 0.9 USD/JPY link, the 6 to 18 month cycle lag and the 80/20 carry split are typical ranges from the course, not constants, and the cascade percentages are this page's own teaching model.
✗The mechanisms are real; the trades are not shown. A framework tells you what to look at. Whether rates lead equities and whether yields predict the next FX move are separate questions, and the course states them as lessons, not as measured results. On this desk's data each of those came back null; see the verdict boxes above.
Section 05

Self-test

Q1. Why does the course say to check the levels above your asset before taking a position?
Policy shifts cascade from central banks to bonds (hours to days), currencies (days to weeks), equities (weeks to months) and credit (months to quarters). Each step down adds lag, noise and crowding, so the higher levels carry the earlier and cleaner signal. In this page's teaching model, 30 days after a shock bonds are essentially fully repriced, currencies about 95%, equities about 39% and credit about 18%. If you trade equities and ignore bonds, curve slope and credit spreads, you are trading the lagged echo of something already visible one rung up.
Q2. A carry trade pays 4.9% a year, and the funding currency rallies 10% in a few days. What happens, and why is it called picking up pennies in front of a steamroller?
The exact one-year return is \(1.05/1.10 - 1.001 = -4.65\%\): the 10% move is about 2.04 years of carry, so a loss that arrives in days replaces the gain that took years to accumulate. Returns are steady until a risk event triggers mass deleveraging, and the unwind itself is mechanical: traders sell the high-yield holding, buy back the yen or franc they owe, safe-haven currencies spike, and risk assets fall together. That is why a sudden yen surge on a risk-off day is the unwind, not a separate event.
Q3. The dollar pays 5% and the yen 0.1%. How big a yen rally over the year makes the carry trade break even, and what does that tell you about the 10% rally in Q2?
The break-even is \(x^{*} = (1 + i_H)/(1 + i_F) - 1 = 1.05/1.001 - 1 = 4.90\%\). A funding-currency rally of 4.9% over the year turns the whole year of carry into zero, and anything larger is a loss. A 10% rally is roughly twice the break-even, which is the same fact as the 2.04 years of carry erased: the loss is the carry earned over a couple of years, delivered in days.
Q4. The Fed is hiking, yet inflation is rising faster than the nominal rate. Why might gold rise, and what should you watch instead of the nominal rate?
Gold responds mainly to real rates, the nominal rate minus inflation. If inflation rises faster than the nominal rate, the real rate falls, so the opportunity cost of holding gold falls with it and gold can rally even while the Fed hikes. That is why the course says to watch TIPS yields, not only nominal ones. The easy rule that hikes pressure gold holds only when the hikes raise real rates.
