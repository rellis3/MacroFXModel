Quantitative & Macro Foundations

Applied lesson notes on the plumbing of macro markets — signal vs. noise, the capital-flow hierarchy, central bank mechanics, fixed income, currencies, and cross-asset synthesis — kept as a study reference, not a validated trading system.

COURSE NOTES — applied practice, not from-scratch derivation ~3 min read Prereqs: Data Foundations
TL;DR
  • Markets are noisiest at short horizons and clearest at long ones — signal-to-noise improves with holding period, while transaction-cost drag compounds against you at high frequency, so the two effects stack against short-term trading.
  • Systematic validation (hypothesis → in-sample → out-of-sample → walk-forward → small live size) fails most ideas before capital is at risk; ad-hoc/intuition-based trading fails ideas with capital already deployed — a "double negative" of lost time and lost money.
  • Policy decisions cascade through a capital-flow hierarchy — central banks → government bonds → currencies → equities → credit — with information degrading and lag increasing at each step down; most retail activity happens at the noisiest, most crowded bottom rungs.
  • Central banks are readable: dual-mandate reaction functions, FOMC statement/press-conference/minutes language shifts, the dot plot (a conditional projection, not a forecast), and the balance sheet (QE/QT, net liquidity ≈ balance sheet − TGA − RRP) all telegraph policy before it happens.
  • Bond math is mechanical and leads the other asset classes: price and yield move inversely by definition, duration converts yield moves into price moves, the yield curve encodes recession expectations, and credit spreads plus real yields (TIPS) explain moves in gold, growth stocks, and FX that look mysterious from the equity side alone.
  • The cross-asset synthesis lesson's own verdict: understanding this framework is not the same as having an edge — markets are efficient at short horizons, and the institutional edge is data infrastructure, systematic implementation, longer time horizons, and the discipline to test before trading, not discretionary daily macro calls.
A note on the numbers in this file. The specific correlations, percentages and figures throughout these lessons are exemplar data representing typical historical ranges, as the lesson material itself states. Actual values vary by time period, market regime and measurement methodology. The relationships and mechanisms described are well-established; treat exact figures as illustrative until verified against current data.
The course

This course used to be one long page. It is now six lessons you can take one at a time, in order or by topic. Each has a summary at the top, a plain-English version, interactive charts, a self-test and a short slide-deck version (the Visual Guide).
